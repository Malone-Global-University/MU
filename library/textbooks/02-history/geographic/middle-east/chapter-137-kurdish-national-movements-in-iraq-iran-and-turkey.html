Chapter Opening

On 22 January 1946, in the Iranian town of Mahabad, Kurdish political leaders proclaimed a republic under the presidency of Qazi Muhammad, a judge and member of a prominent local family. Barely fourteen months later, on 31 March 1947, Qazi Muhammad was hanged in the same town. Between those two dates lay one of the most compressed and most remembered episodes in modern Kurdish history: a government that issued Kurdish-language publications, organized schools and a small administration, and collapsed within a year once the diplomatic circumstances that had sheltered it disappeared. Nearly three decades later, in March 1975, another Kurdish movement, the far larger insurgency led by Mustafa Barzani in Iraq, collapsed within days of a diplomatic agreement signed in Algiers between two governments that had never been its principal allies but had been its indispensable suppliers. Between those two collapses lies the subject of this chapter.

This chapter asks how Kurdish national movements developed under three different states, Iraq, Iran, and Turkey, between the end of the Second World War and 1975, and why common aspirations for cultural recognition, political rights, autonomy, or independence produced such different organizations, strategies, and outcomes. The Kurds were not a minor population on the margins of the region. They inhabited a continuous belt of mountains, foothills, valleys, and plains across the Taurus and Zagros ranges, a belt introduced geographically in Chapter 2 and traced historically through the Kurdish dynasties and principalities of Chapters 52, 56, and 69, the Kurdish-Ottoman frontier of Chapters 74 and 78, and the nineteenth-century destruction of the Kurdish emirates in Chapter 103. After 1920 that belt was divided by the borders of Turkey, Iraq, Iran, and Syria.

The central problem of the chapter is therefore not simply the absence of a Kurdish state. It is the historical relationship between Kurdish society and the modern territorial states that governed Kurdish-inhabited regions, and the different opportunities and constraints those states created. In Iraq, where the monarchy's fall in 1958 opened a constitutional space for Kurdish national rights, Kurdish politics developed into a sustained armed insurgency, interrupted by negotiations that promised autonomy and failed over territory, oil, and sovereignty. In Iran, the memory of the Mahabad republic became a founding national memory even as the monarchy suppressed Kurdish political organization at home and, at the same time, supplied the Iraqi Kurdish insurgents across the border. In Turkey, where the republic's definition of citizenship left little room for a public Kurdish identity, a different kind of politics emerged in the 1960s through socialist parties, students, intellectuals, and protest meetings about the economic neglect of the east.

A second theme is that Kurdish nationalism was never a single movement. The Kurdish Democratic Party of Iran, founded in Mahabad in 1945, and the Kurdistan Democratic Party of Iraq, founded in 1946, were separate organizations with overlapping histories and different commands. Within Iraq the movement was divided between Mustafa Barzani, whose authority drew on tribal, religious, and military leadership, and a politburo of urban intellectuals associated with Ibrahim Ahmad and Jalal Talabani. In Turkey Kurdish activists participated in a Turkish socialist party before they built their own organizations. This chapter treats those differences as evidence of political diversity rather than as failures of national unity, and it avoids presenting tribal leadership and modern nationalism as opposites.

A third theme is the role of outside powers. The Soviet Union's presence in northern Iran made the Mahabad republic possible and its withdrawal made the republic's survival impossible, yet Kurdish leaders had their own objectives and cannot be reduced to instruments of Soviet policy. In 1970 to 1975 the Shah of Iran, the United States, and Israel assisted Barzani's insurgency, each for reasons of its own, while the Shah continued to repress Kurdish nationalism inside Iran. This contradiction is not hypocrisy to be denounced but a pattern to be explained through interstate rivalry and regime security, and it is the key to understanding why the insurgency, once its supply lines were severed, fell apart in a matter of days.

The chapter's evidence is uneven, and students should read it with that in mind. Much of what is known about the Mahabad republic comes from a small number of contemporary observers, later memoirs, and Soviet, Iranian, British, and American diplomatic reporting. The 1958 constitutional provision, the 1966 Bazzaz initiative, the March 1970 manifesto, and the March 1974 autonomy law are documented, though the texts have been preserved and translated unevenly. For 1970 to 1975 the declassified American diplomatic and intelligence record, together with scholarly histories by Kurdish, Iraqi, Iranian, Turkish, and Western authors, allows an unusually detailed reconstruction of external involvement, and a far thinner reconstruction of the experiences of villagers, refugees, women, and rank-and-file fighters.

The chapter's chronology ends in 1975. Later developments, including the founding of the Patriotic Union of Kurdistan in June 1975, the Iranian Revolution and the Kurdish-Iranian confrontation that followed it (Chapters 144 and 145), the Iran-Iraq War and the Anfal campaign (Chapters 146 and 149), the emergence of the PKK (Chapters 149 and 154), and the autonomy established in Iraqi Kurdistan after 1991 (Chapter 151), belong to later chapters. They are not read back into the movements examined here.

Chapter 136 showed how Palestinian national movements built institutions and armed organizations without sovereign territory. Chapter 137 examines a related but distinct problem: how Kurdish movements sought recognition and territorial autonomy inside established states that resisted any division of sovereignty. The chapter then leads into Chapter 138, where another fragile arrangement, Lebanon's confessional order, begins to break apart.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Describe the geography, languages, and religious communities of Kurdish-inhabited regions and explain why Kurdistan is a historical-geographical designation rather than a political boundary.
  2. Explain the provisions of the Treaty of Sèvres concerning Kurdish autonomy and why they were never implemented before Lausanne superseded them.
  3. Analyze the establishment and collapse of the Republic of Mahabad, distinguishing Kurdish political initiative from Soviet strategy.
  4. Trace the course of Kurdish politics in revolutionary Iraq from the constitutional recognition of 1958 through the insurgency of 1961 and the Bazzaz initiative of 1966.
  5. Evaluate the divisions within the Iraqi Kurdish movement, particularly the rupture of 1964 between Mustafa Barzani and the politburo of the KDP.
  6. Compare the March 1970 autonomy agreement with the March 1974 autonomy law and explain why one was negotiated and the other unilaterally imposed.
  7. Explain the Kirkuk dispute and its connection to oil, demography, and territorial boundaries.
  8. Assess the roles of Iran, the United States, and Israel in the Iraqi Kurdish insurgency and the consequences of the Algiers Accord of March 1975.
  9. Describe Iranian Kurdish political activity between Mahabad and 1975, including the armed movement of 1967–1968, and the effects of the Shah's centralization and land reform.
  10. Examine the emergence of Kurdish political and cultural organization in Turkey during the 1960s, including the Eastern Meetings, the Workers' Party of Turkey, and the DDKO.
  11. Compare Kurdish nationalism in Iraq, Iran, and Turkey, evaluating geography, leadership, foreign intervention, social structure, and state policy.
  12. Distinguish national self-determination from territorial autonomy and recognize why the absence of a Kurdish state did not mean the absence of Kurdish political agency.

Historical Context

Kurdish-speaking communities appear in medieval Arabic, Persian, and Armenian sources and in the history of the Ayyubid, Seljuk, and Safavid worlds treated in Volumes IV and V. During the sixteenth-century Ottoman-Safavid contest (Chapters 78 and 74), many Kurdish chiefs became hereditary governors of frontier districts under Ottoman or Persian suzerainty, retaining considerable autonomy. The nineteenth-century centralizing reforms of both empires (Chapters 92, 94, and 103) removed most of those principalities, leaving tribal chieftains, religious shaykhs (particularly in Sufi orders such as the Naqshbandiyya and Qadiriyya), and urban notables as the principal intermediaries between Kurdish society and the state. The late Ottoman period also produced the first Kurdish publications, clubs, and associations, but little consensus on whether Kurdish aspirations lay in Ottoman decentralization, autonomy, or independence.

The defeat and dismemberment of the Ottoman Empire in World War I (Chapters 111 to 114) left Kurdish regions divided among a Turkish republic, the British-mandated Iraqi monarchy (Chapter 116), the French-mandated territories of Syria (Chapter 117), and Pahlavi Iran (Chapter 119). Each state pursued its own project of centralization and national integration. Turkey emphasized a unitary Turkish national identity. Iraq, a state with an Arab majority, a Shia plurality or majority among Arabs, and a Kurdish minority concentrated in the north, tried to define its national character with continuing uncertainty. Iran under Reza Shah emphasized Persian-language nationalism and tribal pacification. The British Mandate and the early Iraqi monarchy made rhetorical gestures toward Kurdish language and administration, but they were uneven and often not implemented.

The Second World War altered this arrangement. In August 1941 British and Soviet forces occupied Iran (Chapter 123), forcing the abdication of Reza Shah and weakening the central government's control over the northwest. Kurdish political activity there revived under conditions of Allied occupation, wartime shortage, and uncertain sovereignty. In Iraq, the Barzani chieftains had already challenged government authority in the early 1930s and again in 1943 to 1945. The new postwar order, with its rhetoric of self-determination in the United Nations Charter and the Soviet-American rivalry beginning to take shape, made Kurdish aspirations visible on an international stage, even though none of the great powers regarded a Kurdish state as a strategic priority.

By the time this chapter's narrative begins, in 1946, Kurdish nationalism had existed for some decades as a set of ideas, associations, and publications, but its organizational forms were new and fragile. This chapter follows them across three states during roughly three decades marked by decolonization, the Cold War, Arab nationalist revolutions, military coups, and the oil economy examined in Chapters 128 to 141.

Section 137.1

Kurdistan: Geography, Languages, Communities, and the Problem of Political Borders

The Kurdish-inhabited regions of the Middle East form a broad arc across the northern Fertile Crescent and the western Iranian plateau, introduced geographically in Chapter 2. In the west and north the arc follows the southeastern Taurus Mountains and the high plateau around Lake Van, in what is now southeastern and eastern Turkey. It continues south and east through the mountains and foothills of northern Iraq, across the Zagros ranges into western Iran, and includes smaller districts of northern Syria. The heads of the Tigris and its principal eastern tributaries, the Greater and Lesser Zab and the Diyala, rise in or cross these mountains, and their valleys supported the villages, orchards, wheat and tobacco fields, and pastures on which many Kurdish households depended. Cities such as Diyarbakır, Van, Mosul, Erbil, Sulaymaniyah, Kirkuk, Mahabad, and Sanandaj served as markets and administrative centers.

Mountains shaped political organization in familiar ways. They offered refuge and made centralized control expensive, and they hosted seasonal movement between lowland winter pastures and highland summer pastures. Yet they were also corridors, and cross-border trade, smuggling, marriage, and religious networks tied communities on both sides of what became international frontiers. Many families had relatives in two or three states. That fact would matter greatly after 1920, because the border became both a barrier and a resource for political movements, which could shelter, recruit, and trade across it when neighboring governments allowed.

Kurdish is a group of Northwestern Iranian languages, usually divided in scholarship into several principal varieties. Kurmanji, the northern variety, is spoken in most of Turkey's Kurdish regions, in Syria, and in the northern districts of Iraq such as Duhok. Sorani, the central variety, is spoken in the Iraqi districts of Sulaymaniyah and Erbil and in much of Iranian Kurdistan around Mahabad and Sanandaj. Southern Kurdish varieties are spoken in Kermanshah and Ilam, and Zaza and Gorani are sometimes classified as separate languages and sometimes as Kurdish varieties, a classification that is linguistically debated and also politically sensitive. The significance for this chapter is practical: Kurdish language policy, literacy, printing, and broadcasting developed differently in each state, and the major written standards of the mid-twentieth century were increasingly Sorani in Iraq and Iran and Kurmanji in Turkey and Syria, which affected which audiences each movement's publications could reach.

Kurdish religious communities were also diverse. The large majority were Sunni Muslims, many belonging to Sufi orders whose shaykhs commanded local authority. Substantial Shia communities lived around Kermanshah in Iran and in parts of Iraq, including the Faili Kurds of Baghdad and the eastern border regions. Alevi Kurds, a heterodox Muslim community with its own ritual and social organization, lived especially in central-eastern Anatolia. Yazidis lived in Sinjar and Sheikhan in Iraq and in smaller communities elsewhere. These affiliations did not simply predict political identity: religious leaders sometimes led nationalist movements, and some Kurds identified chiefly with other communities or with the states in which they lived.

It is also necessary to distinguish Kurdistan as a historical and geographical term from the sovereign borders of any state. Kurdistan has been used since the Seljuk period, and in modern usage it refers to regions where Kurds form a majority or substantial presence, with boundaries that Kurdish nationalists themselves drew differently. Those regions also contained Arabs, Turkmen, Assyrians, Armenians, Persians, Azeris, Jews until mid-century, and others. Kurdish national movements disagreed about proposed borders, and they disagreed about whether to seek independence, autonomy within existing states, or equal citizenship. The dispute over Kirkuk, treated in Section 137.10, is the most consequential example of how contested the question of territory could become.

These landscapes were never an exclusively Kurdish zone. In the northern Iraqi plains, Arab, Kurdish, Turkmen, Assyrian, and other communities used many of the same roads, irrigation systems, and town markets. Boundaries drawn around a proposed autonomous region could therefore affect families whose cultural and political identifications did not coincide with the majority in a district. The language of a homeland could be deeply meaningful without producing a universally agreed territorial map. A rigorous reading of Kurdish nationalism must begin with that difference between a cultural geography and a boundary that a government could administer.

The distinction between a region and an organization is equally important. The term peshmerga came to describe Kurdish fighters, but it did not designate one continuous army under a single command. Fighters in Barzani's Iraqi force, Kurdish activists in the Iranian highlands, and cultural organizers in Turkey belonged to different institutions. Their communications were facilitated by a sense of shared Kurdish identity and by cross-border kinship, yet state restrictions, language varieties, political ideologies, and local interests often mattered more immediately than the ideal of transnational unity. These differences help explain why events in one state could inspire Kurdish activists elsewhere without yielding a common strategy.

Section 137.2

From Ottoman and Persian Rule to Modern States: Sèvres, Lausanne, and the Kurdish Question

The Ottoman and Qajar empires had governed Kurdish regions through a combination of hereditary chiefs, military expeditions, and negotiated frontier arrangements (Chapters 74, 94, and 103). After the First World War the Ottoman collapse (Chapter 113) opened a short interval in which several outcomes seemed possible. Kurdish notables, some of them in Istanbul associations, others in tribal districts, held divergent opinions: some wanted British protection, some autonomy under a reformed Ottoman or Turkish government, and some independence. British officials, uncertain about their own commitments in Iraq, also differed over whether to set up a separate Kurdish administration or incorporate northern districts into an Arab-led state.

The Treaty of Sèvres of August 1920, signed by representatives of the Ottoman government but never implemented, placed Kurdish autonomy and possible independence explicitly on the postwar diplomatic agenda. Articles 62 to 64 provided for a commission in Istanbul to draft a scheme of local autonomy for predominantly Kurdish areas lying east of the Euphrates and south of the southern boundary of Armenia, and Article 64 provided that if, within a year, the Kurdish population in those areas demonstrated to the Council of the League of Nations that a majority wished independence and the Council judged them capable of it, Turkey would agree to carry out that recommendation. The provisions were conditional and procedural, not a grant of statehood. Article 62 did not include Mosul in the proposed initial autonomy territory, but Article 64 explicitly contemplated voluntary accession by Kurds in the Mosul vilayet to a future independent Kurdish state if the specified conditions were met. The treaty made no comparable provision for Iranian Kurdish regions. Moreover, the treaty was never ratified or put into effect. The Turkish nationalist movement under Mustafa Kemal rejected it (Chapter 114), and the Treaty of Lausanne of July 1923 replaced it with a settlement that contained no provision for Kurdish autonomy.

The Mosul question was resolved separately. The former Ottoman vilayet of Mosul, which included the oil prospects around Kirkuk and a large Kurdish population, was claimed by both Turkey and the British-mandated Iraq. A League of Nations commission investigated in 1925 and recommended that the province remain in Iraq under conditions that included respect for Kurdish interests, and the League Council decided accordingly in December 1925. An Anglo-Turkish-Iraqi treaty of 1926 confirmed the arrangement. Kurdish-inhabited northern Iraq thus became part of a state whose government was largely Arab and Sunni in its political leadership, and where Kurdish language, education, and administration were treated at first by assurances that were only partly honored. The 1932 Iraqi admission to the League was accompanied by promises that were similarly general.

The states that inherited Kurdish regions developed different policies. Republican Turkey, under the nationalist ideology treated in Chapter 115, defined citizenship through a Turkish national identity and responded to Kurdish risings, notably the uprising of Shaykh Said in 1925, with military force and legislation, and it restricted Kurdish education, associations, and publications from the 1920s (Section 137.17). In Iraq, Kurdish rebellions of the 1920s and 1930s, including those led by Shaykh Mahmud Barzinji and by the Barzani chiefs, were suppressed in part by British air power, a practice examined in Chapter 116. Iran under Reza Shah (Chapter 119) disarmed and relocated tribes and promoted Persian as the language of state, and Kurdish identity there was largely excluded from public life. These policies did not mean that Kurdish national consciousness disappeared; they meant that it developed in clandestine associations, in literature, and in the memories of earlier uprisings.

The purpose of this section is not to narrate interwar Kurdish history but to establish the institutional setting. When Kurdish leaders in 1946 looked to Sèvres as a precedent, they did so knowing that it had been unimplemented. When Iraqi, Turkish, and Iranian governments rejected autonomy they could point to Lausanne and the principle of the territorial integrity of their states. Every Kurdish movement of the following decades operated within this inheritance.

The language of international promises acquired a distinctive political afterlife. Kurdish petitions and later nationalist publications repeatedly invoked Sèvres, but it had never generated enforceable institutions. Its conditional clauses required decisions by outside bodies, and the Turkish nationalist victory removed the political conditions in which they might have operated. Lausanne, by contrast, became part of the implemented diplomatic framework for the Turkish Republic. The comparison illustrates a central methodological question: an international text may register a claim, but an unimplemented text and a functioning administrative settlement have radically different historical effects.

The League of Nations settlement over Mosul did not resolve the place of Kurdish people in the Iraqi state. British administrators had to reconcile Kurdish-language demands with the institutional development of the monarchy, while Iraqi nationalists resisted arrangements that appeared to diminish central authority. Kurdish political claims also competed with those of Turkmen and other communities in northern towns. When later Iraqi leaders invoked the territorial integrity of the republic, they drew on this inherited settlement; when Kurdish leaders invoked earlier assurances, they recalled promises that had not produced a secure system of representation. The conflict arose partly because both sides could identify precedents, but neither possessed a mutually accepted institutional framework.

Section 137.3

The Republic of Mahabad in 1946: Kurdish Nationalism and the Iranian-Soviet Crisis

When Soviet and British forces entered Iran in August 1941, the Allies divided the country into zones, and Soviet troops controlled the north, including the Azerbaijan provinces. Mahabad lay in a gap: the town was in the Kurdish-inhabited southern part of what became the Soviet zone's boundary region, where Iranian state authority had receded, and local officers, landowners, and tribal chiefs held much of the power. The Allied occupation, wartime shortages, and the departure of Reza Shah created conditions in which Kurdish political activity became possible again after two decades of repression.

In 1942 a group of young Kurdish men, including townsmen, teachers, and tradesmen, founded in Mahabad the Society for the Revival of Kurdistan, known by its Kurdish initials Komala JK. It was a secret nationalist association, and it gradually drew in members of notable families. Qazi Muhammad, a judge and religious figure from one of Mahabad's leading families, joined the movement and by 1945 had become its principal public leader. In August 1945 Komala was replaced by the Kurdish Democratic Party, founded in Mahabad with Qazi Muhammad as its president. The party's program, summarized in the primary source window below, called for autonomy within Iran, not independence, for the use of Kurdish in education and local administration, and for the development of the region.

In December 1945 the Azerbaijan People's Government, under Ja'far Pishevari and supported by the Soviet occupation, proclaimed itself in Tabriz. The Mahabad leadership could not ignore this development. Soviet officials had been in contact with Kurdish leaders, including by facilitating a visit of Kurdish figures to Soviet Azerbaijan, and the Soviet Union offered patronage that Kurdish leaders judged necessary for survival. On 22 January 1946, in a public ceremony, Qazi Muhammad announced the republic, with himself as president. It had a flag, a small cabinet, a newspaper in Kurdish, and educational and cultural initiatives that included the printing of Kurdish-language materials and the opening of schools in which Kurdish was used. It formed a small army, whose commanders included Mustafa Barzani, who had crossed into Iran with his followers after the failure of his 1943 to 1945 rebellion in Iraq, and tribal contingents of Iranian Kurdish tribes.

Soviet influence was real, but it cannot by itself explain the republic. The Kurdish Democratic Party had its own origins in Komala, its own social base in Mahabad's merchants, craftsmen, and clergy, and its own demand for cultural and administrative autonomy. Moscow regarded the northwest Iranian crisis chiefly as a lever to obtain an oil concession and to extend its influence in Iran, as the diplomatic confrontation at the new United Nations in early 1946 showed. In April 1946 Iran's prime minister Ahmad Qavam concluded an agreement with the Soviets that promised an oil company and the withdrawal of Soviet troops, which was completed by May. A Kurdish-Azerbaijani treaty of friendship in April 1946 tried to coordinate the two governments, but disputes between them about borders and the status of mixed districts exposed their weakness. Distinguishing Kurdish initiative from Soviet strategy is essential: neither alone explains the republic's creation, and Soviet disengagement doomed it.

The republic's internal base was also limited. It controlled the town of Mahabad and surrounding districts, but not the larger Kurdish regions of Iran around Kermanshah and Sanandaj, and some tribes, notably some of the large Dehbokri and Mangur confederations, were hostile or neutral. Its finances were thin. Its capacity to remain independent of the Soviet Union and Azerbaijan was constrained, as was its capacity to resist an Iranian army that, once Soviet forces left, could return.

Soviet sponsorship created political room, but it did not remove Kurdish agency or internal disagreement. Local merchants were concerned with trade and municipal stability; teachers and writers saw an opportunity to expand Kurdish-language publication; tribal chiefs calculated their relationship to the new administration in light of rivalries over territory and armed followers. The republic could issue a national proclamation, yet its effective authority depended on cooperation from local notables and on the absence of an Iranian military advance. In this sense it was at once a nationalist experiment, a local government, and an episode in the crisis of Iranian sovereignty after the Allied occupation.

The proclamation on 22 January 1946 was followed by efforts to teach and publish in Kurdish, give public office to sympathetic figures, and establish recognizable national symbols. These undertakings mattered even where implementation was limited: teachers and pupils encountered a language previously excluded from official education, and writers could address an imagined community beyond one town. The limitations were just as important. Many villages remained dependent on established landlords and chiefs; rural women and poorer households had limited influence over decisions made by urban political leaders. The documentary record is richer for speeches, party programs, and diplomatic reports than for the opinions of people outside Mahabad, and the difference must remain visible in any assessment of popular support.

Section 137.4

The Fall of Mahabad, Qazi Muhammad, and Mustafa Barzani's Soviet Exile

The republic's life depended on the diplomatic bargain between Moscow and Tehran. After the Qavam-Soviet agreement of April 1946 and the Soviet troop withdrawal in May, Iranian authorities slowly reasserted control. The Iranian parliament later rejected the Soviet oil agreement in 1947, but by then the strategic situation in northwest Iran had been reversed. In December 1946 the Iranian army advanced into Azerbaijan and, after the collapse of the Pishevari government, entered Mahabad. Qazi Muhammad, advised by some colleagues to resist and by others to seek terms, chose not to fight a hopeless battle and surrendered the town. Some tribal chiefs that had supported the republic changed sides, and in some regions local disputes were settled by new alignments with the government.

Qazi Muhammad was tried by a military court and hanged on 31 March 1947 together with two other members of his family, Sadr Qazi and Muhammad Husayn Saif Qazi. His execution made him, in Kurdish memory, the founding martyr of the republic. The Iranian government regarded the trial as the punishment of rebels, and some in the Kurdish political world blamed Qazi Muhammad's choices or his tribal allies. These differing assessments are part of the historical record and not simply a problem to be resolved.

Mustafa Barzani and his followers, perhaps in the low thousands including families and tribesmen, withdrew toward the Iraqi border, fought against Iranian and Iraqi forces in the spring of 1947, and eventually crossed the Aras (Araxes) into the Soviet Union. The long march through difficult terrain became a central episode in the Barzani legend. In the Soviet Union Barzani and his men lived in several places, under conditions about which the evidence is limited. Barzani remained in exile until after the Iraqi revolution of 1958. During his absence the Iraqi KDP, formed in August 1946 in Baghdad, with Barzani as president in absentia and with Ibrahim Ahmad later serving as its secretary-general, developed as an organization led by educated Kurds who worked in Iraqi cities and in the underground.

Mahabad's historical weight exceeded its duration for several reasons. For Kurdish nationalists it showed that a Kurdish government could issue a currency of ideas, write school books, and challenge the idea that Kurdish was merely a village dialect. It also provided a symbolic geography, in which Mahabad stood for the Kurdish capital that might have been. For analysts it demonstrated the vulnerability of a small nationalist government dependent on a patron whose priorities lay elsewhere. A republic that had flourished under Soviet cover could not survive when the Soviet Union traded its interest in the Kurds for its interest in Iranian oil.

Differing groups experienced these events differently. Kurdish political intellectuals often hoped for a modern educated administration. Tribal leaders weighed their advantage, and some feared that the republic would diminish their autonomy. Peasants saw the arrival and departure of armies and, in some places, a change of landlord. Soviet officials treated the republic as one piece of a larger regional strategy. The Iranian government saw an insurgency by a minority backed by a foreign power. This variety of perspectives is a warning against single-cause explanations.

The collapse was not simply a reversal imposed on passive Kurds. Some tribal leaders made arrangements with the returning Iranian authorities; others attempted to preserve their followers and property; still others remained loyal to the Mahabad leadership. These choices unfolded under severe inequalities of power, and later partisan histories sometimes classify every accommodation as betrayal. A historian should instead distinguish the local calculation made under military pressure from the larger question of whether the republic had developed institutions capable of surviving without outside protection. Its administrative reach was limited, and the Iranian government reasserted authority through troops, arrests, and the suppression of nationalist organizations.

Mustafa Barzani's subsequent flight made this an interconnected Iraqi-Iranian history. His followers' journey toward Soviet territory after the defeat became a formative part of their movement's collective memory, but the years abroad also imposed dependence on a foreign government. Exile preserved a core of experienced commanders, while interrupting normal family and village life and separating fighters from their political base in Iraq. The contrast between Mahabad's defeat and Barzani's eventual return in 1958 demonstrates why the survival of a movement cannot be inferred from the survival of any one government. Organizations, stories, personal networks, and military experience can continue even after an institutional project has been destroyed.

Historical Vocabulary
Self-Determination, Autonomy, Separatism, Sovereignty, Exile, and Irredentism

National self-determination is the principle that a nation should be able to decide its political status. Its meaning is contested, because it may imply independence, federation, or merely cultural and administrative rights. Autonomy means self-government over defined matters within a larger state, whereas sovereignty refers to ultimate authority over a territory and is normally retained by the state. Separatism describes movements that seek to leave a state, a term often applied by governments to movements that describe themselves as seeking rights. Irredentism is a claim to territory on grounds of ethnic or historical ties.

The Mahabad leaders, as described in Primary Source Window 1, sought autonomy and used the language of national rights. The distinction matters for the rest of the chapter, because the Iraqi, Iranian, and Turkish governments rejected autonomy partly by treating it as a step toward separatism, and many Kurdish leaders insisted that autonomy and national recognition were compatible with the unity of the state. Political exile, as in Barzani's years in the Soviet Union, removed leaders from their communities while allowing them to preserve prestige and ties with foreign governments.

Section 137.5

The Iraqi Revolution of 1958, Barzani's Return, and the Recognition of Kurdish National Rights

The overthrow of Iraq's Hashemite monarchy on 14 July 1958 by Brigadier Abd al-Karim Qasim and Colonel Abd al-Salam Arif (Chapter 133) created new expectations among Kurdish leaders. The monarchy had been closely tied to Britain, dominated by an Arab Sunni political elite, and hostile to Kurdish political parties. The revolution was greeted by many Kurds with hope, and Kurdish representation was visible at the top: the three-member Sovereignty Council included a Kurd, Khalid al-Naqshbandi, alongside a Sunni Arab and a Shia Arab.

The provisional constitution of July 1958 contained, in Article 3, a statement that the Iraqi entity rested on cooperation among all its citizens, that Arabs and Kurds were partners in the homeland, and that the constitution guaranteed their national rights within the Iraqi union. It was the first time a republican Iraqi constitutional text named Kurds as a national partner. Yet recognition of national rights in a provisional constitution is different from enforceable autonomy, because the text did not define institutions, territory, language policy, revenue, or the sharing of power. The question of how partnership would be translated into practice remained open.

Mustafa Barzani returned to Baghdad in October 1958 after eleven years in the Soviet Union, and he was received by the government and by Kurdish crowds, and a group of his followers returned later. The KDP, which had operated underground, was licensed in early 1960 and began publishing a Kurdish-language newspaper. Qasim, who faced pressure from Arab nationalists and communists, courted Barzani and the Kurds as a counterweight, and Barzani's forces helped to put down an army revolt at Mosul in March 1959 led by Arab nationalist officers.

The KDP at this time contained several currents. Mustafa Barzani, as president, represented the tribal, religious, and military leadership of the Barzan region and the personal loyalty it attracted. Ibrahim Ahmad, a writer and the party's secretary-general, led a more urban, educated wing concerned with party organization and social programs, and Jalal Talabani, then young, emerged as a political organizer among students and activists, with a base in Sulaymaniyah and its surrounding districts. These men agreed on the importance of Kurdish rights, but they differed on leadership, party discipline, tribalism, social reform, relations with the Iraqi left, and tactics. The partnership with Qasim concealed these tensions for a time.

The new republic opened political competition beyond the palace and the old military establishment. Communists, Arab nationalists, Baathists, officers, urban professionals, and Kurdish organizations differed sharply over what the revolution ought to produce. Kurdish activists welcomed the constitutional partnership language because it appeared to establish a new foundation for citizenship. Yet it contained no detailed rules governing a Kurdish legislature, tax authority, provincial police, or control over appointments. A constitutional principle could be cited in petitions and newspaper articles, but its effect depended on subsequent governmental decisions. This gap between recognition and implementation became a recurring feature of the Iraqi Kurdish question.

Barzani's return from the Soviet Union in October 1958 had both practical and symbolic effects. His prestige derived from his earlier tribal and military campaigns, his role in Mahabad, and his endurance in exile. Nevertheless, he returned to a political field that other Kurdish actors had been organizing during his absence. The KDP's activists included schoolteachers, journalists, intellectuals, and figures concerned with agrarian reform or the relationship between Kurdish nationalism and Iraqi democracy. Military loyalty to Barzani and allegiance to the party did not automatically coincide. A movement capable of mobilizing both village fighters and urban professionals also had to decide who would set policy, who would negotiate with Baghdad, and which kinds of social reform should take priority.

Educational policy shows the limits and possibilities of the opening. Kurdish-language schools and publications provided visible benefits for teachers, parents, and students; debates about their location and funding exposed the distinction between formal permission and public capacity. A rural school needed trained teachers, textbooks, transport, and a secure environment as well as legal recognition. Kurdish in government offices raised similar issues: how would courts hear testimony, how would officials be recruited, and which Kurdish written standard would be employed? These institutional questions were consequential because they converted the abstract claim of national rights into everyday encounters with government.

Section 137.6

From Cooperation to Insurgency: Qasim, Barzani, and the Kurdish Revolt of 1961

By 1960 relations between Qasim and the Kurdish leadership began to deteriorate. Qasim had no intention of conceding territorial or administrative autonomy and as his regime became more personal and more cautious about Kurdish demands, the content of Article 3 remained unfulfilled. Kurdish leaders pressed for recognition of Kurdish as an official language in Kurdish areas, for Kurdish administrators, for a share of oil revenues, and for the formal acknowledgment of a Kurdish region. Baghdad feared a precedent for fragmentation and, as Qasim's other alliances failed, he looked for ways to weaken Barzani.

Local conflicts amplified the dispute. The land reform law of 1958 threatened some tribal landholders while giving Barzani opportunities to extend his influence, and Qasim's government armed tribal rivals of Barzani, such as groups from the Zebari and Bradost areas, in order to contain him. Barzani's expansion into areas that were not his traditional base produced clashes between Kurdish tribes. The government also pressed Barzani's followers by pushing back against his authority in the Barzan region. These conflicts mixed national, party, agrarian, and tribal politics, and they illustrate why the insurgency cannot be described as purely a nationalist uprising or merely a tribal rebellion.

In September 1961 fighting escalated into open insurgency. Iraqi forces began operations and the air force bombed villages in Kurdish districts, and Kurdish fighters, the peshmerga, took to the mountains. The term, which means roughly those who face death, came to designate the Kurdish irregular forces that were organized by Barzani, by tribal contingents, and by KDP cadres. Their strength lay in knowledge of the mountains, local support, and the difficulty of conducting heavy operations in winter, and their weakness lay in limited arms, ammunition, and money. Baghdad's army and air force controlled the plains and roads, and the government tried to buy or arm rival chiefs, called collectively by Barzani's supporters "jash" (a pejorative for pro-government Kurdish militias).

The war had civilian costs. Villages were bombed or burned, and families fled to towns or to higher ground, and the insurgency also disrupted agriculture and trade. Evidence for casualties and displacement in the early 1960s is uneven and often partisan, so students should treat numerical claims carefully. What is clear is that, by the end of the Qasim period, the Kurdish question had become an armed conflict in which neither side could win quickly, and in which Baghdad's inability to suppress the insurgency weakened the regime.

The origins of the fighting cannot be reduced to a simple confrontation of Kurdish nationalism against Arab nationalism. Kurdish rural society also contained disputes over land tenure, taxation, tribal prestige, and the place of the central government in local economic life. Qasim's attempts to influence Kurdish chiefs and to play rival factions against one another interacted with older feuds. Some Kurdish landholders opposed state land redistribution, while poor cultivators might regard agrarian reform as more urgent than the leadership arrangements negotiated by notables. The rebellion drew strength from a shared opposition to central military intervention, but this did not erase conflicting class and local interests among its supporters.

The geography of combat favored neither side absolutely. Mountain passes and dispersed villages allowed guerrillas to evade a larger army, but movement across valleys depended on food, communications, ammunition, and the willingness of households to shelter fighters. The Iraqi army could concentrate artillery and aircraft against fixed settlements even when it could not hold every ridge. In prolonged conflicts the distinction between a fighter's supply system and a civilian family's subsistence became blurred, exposing villages to punishment and restricting access to markets. These pressures also shaped how military success was understood. Holding a mountain position did not necessarily provide secure schools, hospitals, or local civil administration.

Accounts of 1961 must be handled with caution because the outbreak was described differently by the Iraqi government, Barzani's supporters, rival Kurdish factions, and outside diplomats. Government sources often emphasized public order and tribal defiance, while insurgent sources highlighted broken constitutional promises and attacks on communities. Neither framework alone explains the shift from negotiation to prolonged war. It was the interaction of state centralization, unresolved national rights, local rural conflicts, and the loss of confidence in bargaining that transformed sporadic violence into a durable insurgency.

Section 137.7

Coups, Baathism, and the Kurdish War: Iraq from 1963 to 1966

On 8 February 1963 Baathist and nationalist officers overthrew Qasim, who was killed the next day (Chapter 133). The new government opened talks with Kurdish representatives, among them Jalal Talabani, but the two sides differed fundamentally. The Kurdish side asked for recognition of autonomy and for a territorial definition of the Kurdish area, including Kirkuk, and the new regime, committed to Arab unity and wary of the Kurdish demands, would offer only more limited decentralization. In June 1963 the Baathist government launched a major offensive. The Baathist regime was itself short-lived, and in November 1963 President Abd al-Salam Arif, an Arab nationalist officer, removed the Baathists from power.

Arif agreed to a ceasefire with Barzani in February 1964, but the political issues were unresolved, and fighting resumed in 1965. Arif's government was unwilling to accept territorial autonomy, and Barzani, who regarded his forces as the guarantee of Kurdish rights, hesitated to demobilize. Arif died in a helicopter accident in April 1966 and was succeeded by his brother Abd al-Rahman Arif. In May 1966 the Kurdish forces inflicted a significant defeat on Iraqi troops near Mount Handrin, in the Rawanduz area, an episode usually treated as an important demonstration of peshmerga capability.

Prime Minister Abd al-Rahman al-Bazzaz, a civilian lawyer and Arab nationalist, tried to combine negotiation with pressure. On 29 June 1966 his government issued a declaration, usually called the Bazzaz Declaration, which recognized Kurdish national rights within the framework of Iraqi unity and offered concessions on Kurdish language use in education and administration, on the employment of Kurds in public posts, on decentralization, and on the recognition of Kurdish cultural expression. It also pledged political accommodation, including amnesty. Contemporary U.S. diplomatic reporting treated the initiative seriously and recorded Barzani's reservations about implementation.

The declaration did not produce a durable settlement. Hard-line military officers opposed it, and the government was weak. Bazzaz was forced from office in August 1966, and the army and political elite did not accept the proposals. Barzani, while willing to treat the declaration as a basis, doubted that Baghdad would implement it. The episode taught Kurdish leaders that Iraqi governments could offer language and cultural concessions but resisted territorial and institutional guarantees, and it taught Iraqi officials that Kurdish military strength could not be defeated cheaply. The Arab-Israeli war of June 1967 (Chapter 135), shortly after, diverted Iraq's attention and resources.

The coup of 1963 did not create a stable new center of authority. The Baathists and their military allies initially cooperated, then competed for control of the state; Abd al-Salam Arif displaced the Baathists from power in November. Kurdish negotiators faced changing interlocutors whose commitments were vulnerable to another cabinet crisis or military intervention. A ceasefire announced by one leadership could be undermined by commanders or ministers with different priorities. This helps explain why apparent breakthroughs did not produce an enforceable division of power: the Iraqi state itself was divided over the authority to grant concessions.

The renewed military campaigns damaged much more than the positions held by peshmerga forces. Roads, livestock movements, seasonal planting, and village exchanges were disrupted, while officials could portray large areas as insecure and withdraw services. Both the state and the insurgency relied on local loyalties, so civilian communities were exposed to competing demands. Teachers and shopkeepers who remained in towns might encounter surveillance or pressure to demonstrate allegiance; families in border districts had additional incentives to seek refuge among relatives. These experiences help students see why the geography of war was also a geography of interrupted livelihoods rather than an abstract set of front lines.

Prime Minister Abd al-Rahman al-Bazzaz offered a more serious peace initiative in June 1966, including an amnesty, language provisions, and forms of local administration. Kurdish leaders accepted a basis for negotiation, but Bazzaz lacked the institutional backing to implement a lasting settlement and left office later that year. Contemporary diplomatic reporting recorded both the opening and the resistance to it among Iraqi officers. The pattern is instructive: the central problem was not a shortage of formulas that could be written on paper, but the absence of institutions trusted to enforce them and the resistance of powerful groups that feared losing security or territory.

Section 137.8

The Kurdish Democratic Party, Internal Rivalries, and the Return of Baathist Rule

The Kurdish movement in Iraq was never a politically homogeneous organization. Its leadership included tribal chiefs, shaykhs, military commanders, urban professionals, teachers, students, and former communists, and these groups held different views about land reform, party democracy, relations with Baghdad, and the proper methods of struggle. Barzani's authority combined hereditary prestige, the religious standing of the Barzani family in a Sufi tradition, military leadership proved in battle, and nationalist legitimacy. The politburo's intellectuals regarded the party as a modern organization whose decisions should not depend on one man.

The dispute reached a crisis in 1964. After the ceasefire with Arif, Barzani opposed the politburo's push to change policy and leadership, and at the party's congress at Qala Diza in July 1964 the two sides split. The politburo, led by Ibrahim Ahmad with Jalal Talabani, was driven into Iran by Barzani's forces, and in the following years it was divided and at times worked with Baghdad against Barzani. Historians treat the 1964 rupture as the origin of a division between Barzani's KDP and a rival grouping that, after 1975, took the form of the Patriotic Union of Kurdistan (Section 137.12). The rivalry was not simply tribal against modern: it also concerned personal power, strategy, and different social bases in the Kurdish north.

On 17 July 1968 the Baath Party returned to power in a coup that brought Ahmed Hassan al-Bakr to the presidency, with the young Saddam Hussein as the party's principal organizer of security (Chapter 133). The new regime lacked a broad base and needed to settle the Kurdish war so that it could consolidate power, and negotiations began in 1968 to 1969. Fighting continued in some areas and the Baathists applied pressure in the northern districts, while Barzani, with assistance reportedly arriving from Iran, negotiated from a position of military strength. The settlement of March 1970, examined in the next section, was thus the product of weakness on both sides.

Leadership rivalries should be analyzed without assuming that the participants belonged to neatly separated camps called tribal and modern. Barzani was a political negotiator who understood the significance of party legitimacy and foreign patronage. His intellectual opponents understood the need to maintain armed strength and contacts with rural populations. Their conflict concerned authority: who could speak for the KDP, whether a ceasefire could be concluded without collective consultation, and how to distribute influence across party officials, commanders, and civilian supporters. Personal distrust intensified substantive disagreements over negotiating strategy and the relationship of the movement to Iraqi left-wing politics.

The Kurdish public did not participate in these arguments on equal terms. Armed commanders controlled territory and could protect or intimidate activists; teachers and journalists were more likely to shape debate in towns; villagers often prioritized the security of crops, property, and family networks. Women took part in households that sustained armed movements and in developing urban education, but the leadership offices of rival factions remained overwhelmingly male. This imbalance makes the sources difficult to read: a party manifesto speaks for an organization, not automatically for every Kurdish household, and memoirs composed after the split may exaggerate their author's foresight or understate compromises.

After the Baathists returned in July 1968, they confronted a movement that was militarily established but politically divided. Baghdad could negotiate with Barzani while maintaining contacts with his opponents, and each Kurdish faction had reasons to distrust governmental sponsorship of rivals. The renewed Baathist state also possessed ideological ambitions to remake Iraqi society and expand central administrative reach. The independence of the Kurdish political field, therefore, depended not only on its armed capacity but also on whether it could resist becoming an instrument of conflicts within Baghdad's ruling coalition.

Section 137.9

March 11, 1970: The Iraqi-Kurdish Autonomy Agreement

The negotiations that produced the agreement of 11 March 1970 were conducted between Baathist leaders, principally Saddam Hussein, and Barzani's KDP leadership. The Baath had its own reasons for compromise. It had come to power by a coup, faced rivals in the army and on the left, was in dispute with Iran over the Shatt al-Arab, and needed to end a war that absorbed troops and resources. Barzani, for his part, wanted constitutional guarantees and a respite for a population that had lived with years of conflict, and he did not trust the regime.

The manifesto of 11 March 1970, announced as a government declaration with the KDP's assent, was a fifteen-point document. As summarized in standard sources, it recognized the Kurdish nation alongside the Arab nation as constituent peoples of Iraq, provided that Kurdish would be an official language together with Arabic in Kurdish-majority areas and the language of instruction there, promised Kurdish participation in government, including a Kurdish vice-presidency, and committed the state to the proportionate representation of Kurds in legislative and executive bodies. It promised the reunification of Kurdish administrative units, a census to determine which districts were Kurdish-majority, and a timetable of four years for the implementation of autonomous institutions. Kurdish ministers entered the cabinet, and the government agreed to a partial return of displaced populations and to measures for the development of the north.

For many Kurdish civilians the agreement was a moment of optimism. Fighting ceased, villagers returned where they could, Kurdish-language publication and education expanded, and political parties, including the Iraqi Communist Party, took part in public life. Yet the document left key matters undefined. It did not specify the boundaries of the autonomous area, it deferred them to a census whose terms were open to dispute, and it did not settle control of the army, police, oil, or finances in the north. The parties also understood the settlement differently: for Kurdish leaders it was a path toward genuine self-government, while for the Baath it was a political concession that would not impair the central state. Distrust persisted on both sides, and each continued to prepare for renewed conflict.

The agreement is best read clause by clause rather than as a simple promise of self-rule. It addressed the use of Kurdish in education and administration, representation in senior state positions, the political position of the Kurdish population, the administration of areas with Kurdish majorities, and the timetable for autonomy. These provisions had different legal and practical implications. A school-language undertaking could be acted upon locally, although budgets and teacher recruitment remained issues; an agreement about a vice-president depended on appointments at the center; the creation of autonomous institutions required legislation, demographic criteria, and decisions about territory. The degree to which a promise was self-executing therefore varied significantly.

The primary document should also be compared with Iraq's 1958 provisional constitution. Article 3 had described Arabs and Kurds as partners in the same homeland; the March 1970 settlement gave that political formula a much broader administrative content. This was an important advance, but it was not a constitutional transformation that guaranteed independent enforcement. The executive and security apparatus remained in Baathist hands. From Baghdad's perspective, recognition could help end an expensive war and secure the government's control of the north. From the KDP's perspective, it could secure an institutional foothold that military campaigns alone could not produce. Agreement on the words therefore concealed disagreement over the balance of power they would create.

The unsettled census question was more than a technical detail. It determined which towns, oil fields, agricultural districts, and populations would fall under Kurdish regional authority. A procedure acceptable to both sides would have required agreed boundaries, neutral enumerators, safeguards against coercion, and mechanisms for handling disputed results. None of those matters could be separated from the preceding years of fighting and displacement. The agreement illustrates a general problem of negotiated autonomy: rights expressed at the level of principle can fail if the same parties remain divided over who implements them and where they apply.

The agreement also recognized Kurdish student, youth, women’s, and teachers’ organizations within corresponding Iraqi national organizations. This provision shows that the parties understood national rights as involving civil society and education, not only a territorial administration. Its practical effect depended on the ability of such associations to function without arbitrary interference, and on the willingness of a centralized party-state to tolerate independent organizing.

Section 137.10

The Failure of Implementation: Kirkuk, Oil, Census Disputes, and Arabization

The 1970 settlement began to deteriorate within months. The central dispute concerned territory, and above all Kirkuk. The city and the oil fields around it, developed by the Iraq Petroleum Company since the 1920s and 1930s and among the largest sources of Iraqi revenue, lay on the edge of the Kurdish highlands. Kirkuk's population was mixed: Kurds, Arabs, Turkmen, and others, whose relative proportions had been contested in successive censuses and were politically contentious. For Kurdish nationalists Kirkuk was a Kurdish city whose inclusion in the autonomous region was essential, both for historical reasons and because oil revenue would make autonomy viable. For the Baath, giving the Kurds control of Kirkuk's oil was unacceptable, since oil was becoming the foundation of the state's power, and the regime was moving toward the nationalization of the Iraq Petroleum Company, which it carried out in 1972 (Chapter 141).

Other disagreements accumulated. The vice-presidency was not filled by a candidate acceptable to the KDP. The census meant to determine the boundaries of the Kurdish region was delayed, and the Kurdish side accused the government of manipulating it. Kurdish appointments to government posts and police positions in the north were contested. The Baath also pursued measures in mixed districts, including the settlement of Arabs and the relocation or displacement of Kurdish inhabitants, a policy later called Arabization, and from 1971 it expelled many Kurds of Shia faith, the Faili Kurds, by claiming that they were of Iranian origin. These deportations were not a Kurdish nationalist issue alone, since they affected a community of Baghdad merchants and workers, but they heightened Kurdish distrust of the regime's intentions.

Mutual suspicion intensified through assassinations and alleged plots. In 1971 an attempt was made on Mustafa Barzani's life through a delegation that included religious figures, and Kurdish leaders held the government responsible, although Baghdad denied involvement. Saddam Hussein, whose influence over security grew, increasingly directed the regime's Kurdish policy, which combined tactical concessions with a determination to weaken Barzani's autonomous power base. The Baath also sought to work with Kurdish rivals, including factions in the politburo tradition and the Communist Party, to dilute Barzani's claim to speak for the Kurdish people.

Regional developments heightened the stakes. In April 1972 Iraq signed a treaty of friendship with the Soviet Union, and the Baath nationalized the Iraq Petroleum Company in June 1972. Iran's Shah, wary of an Iraq closer to Moscow and richer in oil, was increasingly willing to support Barzani, and Barzani sought Western and Israeli aid. By 1972 the political settlement of 1970 was breaking down.

The demography of Kirkuk must be described at the appropriate scale. In figures from the 1957 mother-tongue census reproduced by Human Rights Watch, the city itself recorded 45,306 Turkic-speaking inhabitants, 40,047 Kurdish-speaking inhabitants, and 27,127 Arabic-speaking inhabitants. Figures for the surrounding province were different, with a larger Kurdish-speaking population outside the city. Mother tongue is not identical to national identity, and the territorial boundaries used for comparing the figures also matter. A claim about a city's demographic plurality cannot simply be transferred to an entire governorate, and a claim about historical presence cannot by itself settle who should govern a multiethnic territory. These distinctions made the census central to, rather than a solution for, the political dispute.

For a Kurdish autonomous government, Kirkuk represented more than symbolic possession. The city connected mountain and plain, provided access to markets and skilled employment, and sat near petroleum infrastructure critical to Iraq's revenues. For Turkmen residents, the same city carried long-standing cultural and political importance. Arab inhabitants, including established residents as well as those moved under government schemes, had interests that were not reducible to Baghdad's position. Assyrian and other smaller communities were also affected by changing local authority. Any settlement would have needed arrangements for language rights, municipal representation, policing, land ownership, and safeguards against domination by whichever group controlled regional institutions.

Demographic engineering deepened the impasse. The movement of Arab families into disputed districts, pressure on Kurdish residents, and the use of administrative reorganization changed what could be counted and who could safely contest official results. Kurdish leaders interpreted these measures as a deliberate attempt to remove territory from autonomy; the Baath government presented its measures through the language of public administration and national sovereignty. Later human-rights investigations documented coerced population movements, especially as the conflict intensified. Historians must separate policies demonstrably underway by 1975 from the much larger campaigns of destruction and forced relocation that followed in later decades, while recognizing that the earlier practices helped create their institutional framework.

Section 137.11

The Autonomy Law of 1974 and the Renewal of Iraqi-Kurdish Warfare

The four-year transition period of the 1970 agreement expired in March 1974. Negotiations in 1973 to 1974 failed to produce an agreed text on the boundaries and institutions of autonomy. On 11 March 1974 Baghdad promulgated, on its own authority, an autonomy law for a Kurdish region. The distinction between 1970 and 1974 is essential: the 1970 manifesto was a negotiated agreement, while the 1974 law was unilaterally imposed by the Iraqi government, and Barzani's leadership rejected it.

The law established a Kurdish Autonomous Region, centered on Erbil, Sulaymaniyah, and Duhok governorates, with a legislative council and an executive council, and it named Kurdish an official language alongside Arabic there. But its institutions were subordinate to the central government. The president of the executive council was appointed by the President of the Republic, and the law reserved foreign policy, defense, security, and the management of natural resources to the central state. The government used the 1957 census and its own administrative demarcations to define the region, excluding Kirkuk governorate and other disputed oil-bearing or mixed areas from the autonomous institutions; the distinction between the city, its surrounding district, and the broader governorate was central to the dispute. The KDP objected that Kirkuk was missing, that the autonomy was formal rather than effective, and that the structures were designed to be controlled by Baghdad.

Barzani rejected the law, Kurdish ministers left the government, and fighting resumed on a much larger scale in spring 1974. The Iraqi army, equipped with Soviet weapons and enlarged by the oil revenues of the 1973 price increases (Chapter 141), mounted a major offensive. The Kurdish forces, which by this time had grown into a larger force than the guerrilla bands of the 1960s with a more conventional structure, were supplied through Iran and, with Iranian artillery and air-defense support, held out through 1974. The fighting displaced large numbers of civilians, bombarded Kurdish towns and villages, and produced refugees who fled to Iran. By later 1974 and early 1975 Kurdish forces faced severe ammunition and logistical shortages, and the humanitarian situation was grave, although numbers of casualties and refugees in this period are uncertain and reported differently by the parties.

The significance of the 1974 law lies partly in the distinction between decentralization and meaningful autonomy. A provincial council whose powers could be withdrawn by Baghdad was not the same as a body that could make durable decisions on local education, budget allocations, appointments, or internal security. The law did create an official language and administrative framework within a defined region, but the government controlled the legal boundaries and reserved decisive powers for itself. Kurdish leaders were therefore responding not simply to the presence or absence of an autonomy label, but to the question of whether institutions could function independently of central command in matters central to daily life.

The government also had reasons to consider the 1970 negotiations politically exhausted. It had increased revenue from nationalized oil and benefited from diplomatic and military relations with the Soviet Union; it could contemplate a military outcome that had been more difficult in 1970. Kurdish commanders, meanwhile, were encouraged by supplies through Iran, but those supplies altered the nature of their battlefield obligations. A larger force using heavier weapons required continuous access to transport corridors, ammunition, fuel, medical assistance, and external liaison. Capabilities that made the insurgency more formidable could simultaneously make its independence of action more fragile.

Civilian exposure increased as military operations expanded. In the north, a family deciding whether to remain on its land had to consider harvest time, livestock, the accessibility of water, the presence of government forces, and the possibility of crossing the border. The burden of flight fell unevenly on children, elderly people, and households without transport or relatives elsewhere. Neither government war bulletins nor insurgent communiqués are sufficient to measure these consequences. Refugee accounts, demographic records, and later field investigations are necessary, and their figures must be dated carefully so as not to confuse the crisis of 1974–1975 with later displacement under the Baath regime.

Section 137.12

Iran, the United States, the Algiers Accord, and the Collapse of the Kurdish Rebellion in 1975

The Iraqi Kurdish insurgency was an international affair. Iran began to supply Barzani in the 1960s and substantially expanded its aid in the early 1970s, through arms, ammunition, medical help, and eventually artillery and military advisers. Israel had provided arms, training, and advisers to Barzani's forces since the 1960s, motivated by an interest in occupying Iraq's army, which had participated in the Arab-Israeli wars. And in 1972, after meetings between President Nixon, Henry Kissinger, and the Shah in Tehran in May, the United States agreed to provide covert financial assistance, channeled through Iran. The House Select Committee on Intelligence (the Pike Committee), whose findings were leaked in 1976, later reported covert expenditure on the order of sixteen million dollars over the program's life. American officials were skeptical that the Kurds could win and treated the program, in the language of the declassified record, as a means of keeping Iraq occupied, not as a commitment to Kurdish autonomy.

These external actors had different goals. The Shah wanted leverage against Baghdad, mainly over the Shatt al-Arab waterway, over which Iran claimed the thalweg (the midline of the deepest channel) as the boundary, and to prevent Iraq from becoming a Soviet-aligned regional power. Israel wanted to tie down Iraqi forces. The United States saw the program as part of its cooperation with the Shah and as a cheap way to pressure a Soviet-aligned government. None of them sought an independent Kurdish state, which would have destabilized Iran and Turkey as well as Iraq. Barzani, aware of this, pursued American and Israeli support partly to reduce his dependence on the Shah, but the Shah controlled the border across which supplies passed.

By early 1975 Iraq, whose military was larger and better equipped, and which had recently gained oil revenue, was pressing the Kurdish forces. Saddam Hussein concluded that he could end the insurgency by settling with Iran. On 6 March 1975, on the margins of an OPEC summit in Algiers, with the mediation of Algerian president Houari Boumédiène, Iran and Iraq announced an agreement, formalized in a treaty in June. Iraq accepted the thalweg as the boundary in the Shatt al-Arab, and the two states committed to control their common border and to stop interference in each other's internal affairs, which in practice meant that Iran would end its aid to the Kurdish insurgents.

The effect was immediate. Iranian supplies were cut off and Iraqi forces advanced as the front collapsed. Barzani and his commanders concluded that continued resistance without supplies would fail and ordered an end to organized fighting in March. Iran opened its border for a limited period so that fighters and civilians could cross, and an estimated 90,000 to 250,000 refugees fled to Iran according to a later U.S. diplomatic historical assessment, a broad range reflecting uncertain counts and changing movements, and many initially entered camps; others went back under an Iraqi amnesty or were dispersed. Barzani went into exile in Iran and later the United States, where he died in 1979. In the following years Baghdad relocated Kurdish populations from border zones and extended Arabization in the north.

The collapse was caused by more than one factor. The withdrawal of Iranian assistance was decisive, but the insurgency was already under strain from Iraq's military superiority, from shortages, from the dependence of a force built for mountain warfare on conventional supplies, and from political divisions among Kurds. In June 1975 a group of Kurdish figures, including Jalal Talabani, announced the formation of the Patriotic Union of Kurdistan, an organization that drew on the older politburo tradition and on younger activists critical of Barzani's leadership. Its later development belongs to Volume IX.

The chain of dependence ran through institutions rather than through a simple agreement between Kurdish fighters and Washington. Iranian authorities controlled border crossings and much of the route by which money and military supplies reached Barzani's organization. American assistance was coordinated with the Shah and was conditional on the broader strategic relationship with Tehran. Israeli assistance reflected Israel's regional security calculations, not an international guarantee of Kurdish sovereignty. The Kurdish leadership possessed its own diplomatic and military objectives, but none of the outside governments had committed itself to accepting whatever political settlement Kurdish representatives might prefer. This asymmetry was not fully resolved by the military resources it provided.

The documentary record is unusually revealing because American policymakers later described the operation in classified memoranda that have been published in the Foreign Relations of the United States series. Those memoranda illuminate external strategy, but they should not be mistaken for a comprehensive history of Kurdish decision-making. They are strongest for identifying the arguments of officials and the existence of a covert program. Kurdish memoirs, Iranian accounts, contemporary journalism, and refugee evidence are needed to assess expectations on the ground. Even the Pike Committee expenditure estimate reflects a particular investigative record rather than a final accounting of all Iranian, Israeli, and American support.

The Algiers settlement placed two questions in sharp contrast: the definition of a river boundary, on which Iraq and Iran could negotiate, and the rights of Kurdish communities, whose political leadership had no seat at the bilateral table. Iran's rapid withdrawal from the supply arrangement had military consequences, but it also revealed the limits of external sponsorship as a substitute for institutional guarantees. The ensuing refugee movement illustrates how the result of high-level interstate diplomacy could be experienced as emergency displacement by people who had not participated in the negotiations. A U.S. historical assessment gives a range of 90,000–250,000 people crossing into Iran; this should be read as an estimate for the crisis, not as a precise census of permanently displaced people.

Section 137.13

Iranian Kurdish Politics after Mahabad: Defeat, Memory, and Political Survival

After the destruction of the republic the Iranian government dismantled Kurdish political institutions, banned the Kurdish-language schools and publications, and arrested or exiled activists. Kurdish nationalism did not vanish. Former members of the Kurdish Democratic Party of Iran (KDPI), schoolteachers, students, poets, and others sustained networks that were partly underground and partly cultural, and the memory of Mahabad and of Qazi Muhammad became a rallying symbol. Kurdish exiles in Iraq, Europe, and the Soviet bloc kept contact with those at home.

The KDPI must be distinguished from the Iraqi KDP. The Iranian party was founded in Mahabad in 1945; the Iraqi party was established in 1946 through networks of Kurdish nationalists, with significant organization in Baghdad. They shared a name's resonance, some personnel, and mutual contacts, but they had different leaders, programs, and command structures. Iranian Kurdish objectives, usually expressed as democracy for Iran and autonomy for Kurdistan, differed from Barzani's emphasis on Iraqi Kurdish autonomy, and Barzani's dependence on the Shah made him a doubtful ally of Iranian Kurdish radicals.

In the 1960s and early 1970s a younger generation, many of them educated in Iranian cities or abroad, took leading roles. Abd al-Rahman Ghassemlou, an economist educated in Paris and Prague, emerged as an intellectual and organizer of the KDPI in this period and came to lead the party in the early 1970s. His leadership during and after the revolution of 1979 belongs to Chapters 144 and 145. At this point it is enough to note that the party's survival, despite repression, shows that Kurdish political organization persisted in Iran.

The years after Mahabad were not an empty interval between two revolutions. Activists adapted to conditions in which membership lists, newspapers, and public meetings exposed participants to surveillance. Political debate survived through informal personal contacts, handwritten and circulated texts, literary gatherings, student networks, and relationships with Kurdish communities in Iraq. Accounts of clandestine activity are necessarily incomplete: security records can identify people who were arrested without revealing the full extent of their support, while later activists' memoirs may frame loosely connected episodes as one continuous organization. A disciplined reconstruction distinguishes evidence of personal contact, the presence of a party committee, and the capacity to direct a sustained national campaign.

Mahabad's memory provided both encouragement and a source of disagreement. For some, it offered evidence that Kurdish could serve as a public and administrative language and that political symbols could help bind disparate districts. For others, its short life showed the risks of depending on international circumstances and of concentrating authority among urban notables. Kurdish nationalists could share reverence for Qazi Muhammad while disagreeing over whether to pursue reforms through Iranian constitutional politics, clandestine party-building, or renewed armed activity. The surviving movement included people whose commitments to Kurdish identity coexisted with socialist, Iranian democratic, or religious political frameworks.

Transnational ties did not guarantee mutual assistance. Iraqi and Iranian Kurdish organizations operated along the same frontier, yet their survival strategies could collide. Iranian activists seeking refuge in Iraqi-controlled districts sometimes confronted the fact that Barzani needed access to Iranian territory and supplies. Supporting an armed challenge to the Shah could therefore jeopardize the Iraqi insurgency's principal source of external assistance. This tension complicates later narratives that describe Kurdish solidarity as a unified chain of military cooperation; solidarity existed, but it was repeatedly constrained by the unequal bargaining power of neighboring states.

Historical Vocabulary
Political Underground, Centralization, Assimilation, and Repression

A political underground is a network of clandestine organizations that survive state prohibition through secret cells, publications, and personal ties. Centralization is the extension of state authority over regions and institutions previously governed by local elites or customary arrangements. Cultural assimilation refers to policies that encourage or compel minorities to adopt the language and identity of the dominant group, and political repression to measures such as arrests, censorship, and executions used to prevent organization. National autonomy in the Iranian Kurdish context meant a measure of self-government for Kurdistan within Iran. These concepts help explain why Iranian Kurdish politics were largely underground between 1947 and 1979.

Section 137.14

The Shah's Iran: Centralization, Land Reform, and Kurdish Society

After the restoration of the Shah's authority in 1953 (Chapter 129), the Pahlavi state, with the military, the gendarmerie, and SAVAK, the intelligence and security organization established in 1957, extended its administration into the provinces. The White Revolution of 1963 (Chapter 142) introduced land reform, the creation of rural development corps, literacy programs, and women's suffrage. In Kurdish districts the effects varied. Land reform reduced the holdings of some large landowners and tribal chiefs and gave some sharecroppers title, but it was implemented unevenly, and in many places landlords retained influence or compensated themselves in other ways. Agricultural laborers and landless households benefited less than smallholders.

Schools, roads, and clinics expanded, and migration to towns and to Tehran increased. Education was conducted in Persian, and Kurdish was excluded from schools and largely from publication. Some Kurds gained from state employment and from the expansion of education, and others were drawn into the Iranian national mainstream, while many experienced the state as a security presence. Merchants and landlords in towns such as Mahabad, Sanandaj, and Saqqez had different interests from tribal households and villagers, and from women, whose roles were affected by schooling, the reform of family law, and migration. The Shah's modernization did not eliminate regional inequality, and the western Kurdish provinces remained poorer than central Iran.

The important historical point is that modernization did not automatically produce cultural or political autonomy. Economic and educational change integrated Kurdish society into the Iranian state while leaving political exclusion intact, and the combination provided the educated recruits and the grievances for later Kurdish political activity. It would be a mistake to treat all Kurdish communities as sharing identical interests: Shia Kurds of Kermanshah, Sunni Kurds of Mahabad, and tribal and urban populations had different relationships to the state.

Land reform was experienced through existing systems of tenancy, tribal authority, and local credit. A household that acquired a legal claim to a small plot could still lack irrigation, livestock, storage, machinery, or access to inexpensive loans. In other communities, former landlords remained economically powerful because they controlled marketing networks or served as intermediaries with state officials. Seasonal pastoral households were affected differently from settled cultivators, and provincial policy did not operate uniformly across Sunni Kurdish districts, Shia Kurdish areas, and mixed towns. The social effects of reform therefore cannot be measured simply by the number of registered land transfers.

Education created both opportunity and tension. A young Kurdish speaker who learned Persian in school might gain access to universities, salaried employment, and national professional networks, while also encountering the expectation that Kurdish remain outside official instruction. For families, the choice to send daughters and sons to school depended on distance, cost, household labor requirements, and local attitudes as well as national reforms. Women could benefit from changes in literacy and legal status without acquiring equal influence in local political organizations. Village-to-city migration changed marriage patterns, remittances, and political communication, allowing new ideas to circulate between provincial towns, Tehran, and households that had remained in the countryside.

The state's own records require critical use. Development statistics may record a new road or classroom without showing who could use it, whether teaching was available in a child's first language, or how frequently a clinic was staffed. Security reports might label political discussion as separatism, while nationalist memoirs might understate the value that some residents placed on new services. Combining administrative evidence with local interviews and social history provides a less simplistic picture: modernization could simultaneously improve material opportunities, extend state supervision, and generate a generation of educated people who challenged cultural exclusion.

Everyday Life and Social History

Households, Land, and Language in Mid-Century Kurdistan

For most Kurds in the mid-twentieth century, politics reached daily life through land, grain, taxes, military service, schools, and the language of official business. Villages combined cultivation with herding, and many households relied on seasonal labor in towns or across borders. Smuggling across frontiers, tolerated or contested by states, supplemented incomes. War, whether the Iraqi conflict or border policing in Iran and Turkey, disrupted planting, markets, and families.

Evidence for these experiences is thinner than for leaders. Historians use oral histories, memoirs, anthropological studies, government reports, and the Kurdish press, all of which have biases. The experience of women, in particular, appears less in political documents than in studies of household economy, education, and later refugee testimony.

Section 137.15

The Iranian Kurdish Armed Movement of 1967–1968

In 1967–1968 a group of younger Iranian Kurdish activists attempted to renew armed struggle against the Shah's government; some historical accounts carry related operations and their aftermath into 1969. They were associated with the Revolutionary Committee of the Kurdish Democratic Party, a faction critical of the older leadership, which they regarded as passive. Among them were Ismail Sharifzadeh and Soleyman Mo'ini. Inspired by revolutionary movements elsewhere and by the Iraqi Kurdish insurgency, the group operated in mountainous areas around Saqqez, Baneh, Sardasht, Bukan, and Piranshahr, where it attacked gendarmerie posts, tried to recruit villagers, and sought to build a guerrilla base.

The movement's objectives combined Kurdish national rights with a leftist program of social reform, and it was divided over ideology and tactics. Its organizational capacity was limited, and it struggled to obtain safe havens: Barzani, dependent on the Shah, would not permit his territory to be used against Iran, and Iraqi sanctuary was uncertain. Iranian forces pursued the guerrillas with the army and gendarmerie, and by 1968 the movement had been suppressed, with several leaders killed or captured. It was smaller than the Iraqi insurgency, and its social base was narrower.

The episode is nonetheless valuable for two reasons. First, it corrects the false impression that Iranian Kurdish political activity disappeared between the fall of Mahabad and the revolution of 1979. Second, it shows the structural contrast with Iraq: where the Iraqi movement had a mountain hinterland, a large peshmerga force, and external supply, the Iranian movement faced a strong state, a policing apparatus, and the neighbor's refusal to host it.

The Revolutionary Committee grew out of frustrations within the Iranian Kurdish party milieu during the mid-1960s. Scholarly accounts place its organization in 1966 and trace armed or clandestine operations principally through 1967–1968, sometimes extending the account into 1969. Sharifzadeh, Soleyman Mo'ini, and associates sought a more active strategy than party figures who favored caution or alliance-building. Their project combined an effort to reestablish a Kurdish nationalist presence inside Iran with a critique of rural inequality and authoritarian monarchy. This was not a replica of Barzani's Iraqi organization: it lacked comparable territorial control, a mature supply network, and a leadership accepted across a broad coalition of tribes and towns.

Operational difficulties were fundamental rather than incidental. A guerrilla group seeking sustained presence in the mountains needed villages willing and able to provide information, food, and shelter. The Iranian security forces could draw on national police, gendarmerie, military units, and intelligence networks, while surveillance crossed the boundary between political association and ordinary household contact. A family suspected of helping activists risked interrogation or loss of livelihood. Such pressures discouraged open support even among people sympathetic to Kurdish-language demands. The absence of an available foreign sanctuary compounded the problem; Iraqi Kurdish commanders could not simply grant hospitality to a movement targeting the government on which their own forces increasingly depended.

The failure also reflected differences over political strategy. Was the central task to mobilize peasants for social reform, to restore a party underground, to obtain regional autonomy, or to provoke a broader Iranian opposition movement? These goals were related but not identical, and a small armed committee could not readily accomplish them all. The deaths and dispersal of leaders weakened immediate organization, yet memories of the campaign circulated among younger activists. The episode thus demonstrates both the limits of armed action in a highly policed setting and the way a militarily defeated movement could contribute to a later political vocabulary without causing later events directly.

Students should treat dates and labels carefully. The term "1967–1968 movement" is a conventional shorthand, not a claim that every supporting activity began or ended exactly at those years. Likewise, a source describing a "revolt" may be grouping together scattered attacks, recruitment efforts, and state operations whose scale differs from the Iraqi insurgency. Comparative analysis improves when the scale of armed conflict, rather than the similarity of its nationalist rhetoric, is measured explicitly.

Section 137.16

Iran's Kurdish Contradiction: Domestic Repression and Support for Iraqi Insurgents

The Shah's policy contained an apparent contradiction. In Iran his government banned Kurdish organization, censored Kurdish publications, and suppressed the armed movement of 1967 to 1968, yet it supplied Barzani against Baghdad. The contradiction is resolved by recognizing that Iranian policy was driven by interstate rivalry and regime security, not by support for Kurdish self-determination. Iraq under the Baath was a rival over the Shatt al-Arab, over leadership in the Gulf, and over ideology, and in the 1960s and 1970s the Shah regarded Kurdish insurgents as a means of keeping Iraq weak. He was careful to keep the insurgency under control, because a successful Kurdish autonomy in Iraq might encourage Iranian Kurds.

For Kurds the arrangement had real consequences. Families living near the border maintained kin networks that cut across state lines and were therefore watched by security services. Iranian Kurdish activists could be tolerated in Iraq and Iraqi Kurds in Iran only so long as they did not challenge their host. Separate Iranian and Iraqi organizations coexisted, and sometimes worked together, but their leaders did not control each other's patrons. It is therefore important to distinguish cooperation among Kurdish movements from the decisions of foreign governments that supported or restricted them.

The Algiers Accord of 1975 exposed the underlying logic. When the Shah obtained concessions from Baghdad, he abandoned Barzani in a few weeks. For Iraqi Kurds this was a decisive turning point. For Iranian Kurds it did not resolve the larger political question: the Kurdish demands for language, cultural rights, and autonomy in Iran remained unmet, and the Pahlavi state, which now had one fewer pressure on its western frontier, retained its repressive structure. Four years later the Iranian revolution would reopen the question under entirely different circumstances.

Iran's conduct becomes more intelligible when viewed as a policy of selective cross-border leverage. In Iranian territory, the central government treated autonomous Kurdish organization as a possible threat to its sovereignty and to the unity of a multiethnic state. Across the border, the same government considered Iraqi Kurdish armed pressure useful because it weakened a rival and increased Tehran's bargaining power over the Shatt al-Arab. The operative distinction was not ethnic affinity but location, command, and strategic value. Such an approach could change rapidly when the rival state offered diplomatic concessions that seemed more valuable than continued proxy conflict.

This policy forced a difficult choice on Kurdish organizers. Barzani could preserve access to Iranian ammunition and medical corridors by avoiding direct support for anti-Shah Kurdish operations. Iranian Kurdish activists could understand that choice as abandonment even while recognizing the precarious position of Iraqi fighters. Some intermarried border communities continued to maintain local forms of solidarity, but those forms were not equivalent to a political alliance among parties. The border operated as a corridor for some purposes, an instrument of surveillance for others, and an emergency route for civilians when fighting or repression intensified.

The case demonstrates why historians should not equate external military assistance with endorsement of national self-determination. A government may support minority insurgents in another state while denying analogous claims at home; a Kurdish movement may accept that support while retaining its own, different aspirations. The 1975 reversal illustrates the danger of collapsing these distinct interests into one apparent coalition. It also warns against judging participants solely by the subsequent outcome: Kurdish leaders facing immediate military pressures did not possess the information or freedom of action of later historians who can read the diplomatic archive.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Kurdish Aspirations from Mahabad to 1975

What Changed

  • The center of gravity of Kurdish political activity moved from Iranian Mahabad in 1946 to Iraqi Kurdistan in the 1960s and early 1970s.
  • Leadership shifted from urban notables and a small party to a large insurgency under a charismatic chief, and then to rival party factions.
  • The Iranian state's policy changed from suppressing Mahabad to using Iraqi Kurds as leverage against Baghdad, and then to ending that support.
  • In Turkey, Kurdish activism took legal and socialist forms in the 1960s that had not existed earlier.

What Stayed the Same

  • The demands for language rights, cultural recognition, and self-government, usually within existing states.
  • The dependence of Kurdish movements on neighboring states and great powers.
  • The refusal of the three states to concede territorial autonomy.
  • Divisions within Kurdish politics among tribal, party, regional, and ideological currents.

Why the pattern matters: The same aspirations produced different outcomes because the states, allies, and social structures around them differed.

Section 137.17

Kurds in Republican Turkey: National Identity, Language Policy, and Regional Inequality

The Turkish Republic, founded in 1923 (Chapters 114 and 115), defined its political community through citizenship and a Turkish national identity. Many officials regarded Kurdish ethnic distinctiveness as a threat to national unity, and public discourse often described Kurds as "mountain Turks" or avoided naming them at all. Within a few years of the republic's founding, the abolition of the caliphate in 1924, the closure of religious schools, and centralizing measures provoked the revolt of Shaykh Said in 1925 in the Diyarbakır region, which combined religious and nationalist motives and was suppressed, with Shaykh Said executed. The government responded with emergency legislation and military expeditions. A further revolt around Mount Ararat in 1927 to 1930, associated with the Kurdish nationalist organization Xoybûn, and the Dersim campaign of 1937 to 1938, which involved large-scale killing and displacement among Kurdish and Zaza-speaking Alevi communities and the execution of Seyid Riza, entrenched official suspicion. The scale and character of the Dersim violence remain debated by scholars, and are examined from different perspectives in Turkish and Kurdish historiography.

State policy toward Kurdish language and culture changed across periods and should not be described as uniform. From the mid-1920s, Kurdish schools, associations, and publications were banned or discouraged, and official policy encouraged the use of Turkish through education, military service, and settlement policy, including the resettlement of some Kurdish populations and the movement of others into the western cities. Kurdish names for places were replaced over time. In practice, enforcement varied by region and decade, and Kurdish was spoken widely in homes and villages. The legal framework was altered after 1960 by a new constitution and later by the military regime of 1980, a period beyond this chapter.

Socioeconomic conditions shaped political grievance. The eastern and southeastern provinces were poorer than western Turkey, with large landholdings in some areas, small peasant plots in others, limited industry, weak infrastructure, and lower literacy. Landlords, tribal chiefs, and religious leaders remained influential, and some cooperated with the state through party politics, notably after the introduction of multiparty elections in 1946 (Chapter 130). Kurdish rural communities, urban professionals, religious leaders, landlords, laborers, and officials experienced the republic differently. Migration to Istanbul, Ankara, and other cities increased from the 1950s and brought Kurdish students and workers into contact with the Turkish left, which proved to be decisive for the next stage.

The history of state policy is not captured by an opposition between Turkish-speaking cities and Kurdish-speaking villages. Large towns such as Diyarbakır and Van contained mixed social worlds in which officials, merchants, soldiers, teachers, and rural migrants interacted. In the countryside, local religious and landholding authorities could mediate elections and access to state services. Multiparty politics provided incentives for national parties to cultivate influential Kurdish local figures even while the official political language discouraged separate Kurdish national claims. Such arrangements could produce incorporation without recognition: voters and notables participated in Turkish institutions while the public legitimacy of Kurdish language and history remained contested.

The experience of Dersim requires particular care. State military operations in 1937–1938 involved killing, displacement, and coercive administrative transformation; Kurdish, Zaza, and Alevi identities among affected inhabitants were not interchangeable, and individuals described themselves in different ways. Estimates and retrospective political classifications vary. Scholarly disagreement about the most appropriate legal or historical terminology does not negate the substantial evidence of civilian suffering. Nor should the experience be retroactively treated as the direct beginning of every organization that emerged in the 1960s. Its importance for this chapter lies in the continuing family memories, fears of state coercion, and contested narratives of belonging that later activists inherited.

Migration changed the geography of politics. A student from an eastern provincial town might encounter other Kurdish speakers and left-wing activists for the first time in an Ankara dormitory, while factory workers gained experience in unions and political associations. These contacts encouraged a new interpretation of the Kurdish question as simultaneously a problem of regional investment, democratic representation, and linguistic exclusion. Yet the social constituency of university circles was not identical with that of rural villages, and the challenge of building durable links between them shaped later organization.

Section 137.18

The 1960 Constitution, Turkish Socialism, and the Reawakening of Kurdish Political Activity

The military intervention of 27 May 1960 overthrew the Democrat Party government (Chapter 130) and was followed by the 1961 Constitution, which guaranteed wider civil liberties, autonomous universities, trade unions, and a constitutional court, and which allowed a legal socialist party. Earlier, in 1959, a group of Kurdish students and intellectuals, remembered as "the 49," had been arrested for alleged Kurdish nationalist activity, which showed that expression was constrained, but the 1960s provided more space. Kurdish-themed periodicals and journals appeared in Turkish, and Kurdish students in Ankara and Istanbul formed circles.

The Workers' Party of Turkey (TİP), founded in 1961 and led from 1962 by Mehmet Ali Aybar, won fifteen seats in the 1965 elections under a proportional system, and it attracted students, trade unionists, and intellectuals, including Kurdish activists from the east. Some Kurds also founded a clandestine Kurdistan Democratic Party of Turkey in 1965, associated with Faik Bucak and Sait Elçi, which was inspired by Barzani's movement. Bucak was killed in 1966, and the party was weakened by repression and divisions. For most Kurdish activists in the 1960s, however, the leftist movement offered a more practical channel for open politics than a nationalist party.

Within this milieu, activists linked underdevelopment and national identity. They argued that the east suffered from neglect, feudal-like landholding, and the absence of investment, and that these inequalities were bound up with the denial of Kurdish language and culture. In 1967 a series of public rallies known as the Eastern Meetings (Doğu Mitingleri) was held in eastern towns including Silvan, Siverek, and Diyarbakır, with participation of students, workers, and local figures. They protested regional neglect and unequal treatment, and some participants called for recognition of Kurdish identity. The meetings were mostly framed in the language of development and equality and, with a few exceptions, did not demand independence. It is important to distinguish these different demands: economic investment, equal citizenship, cultural recognition, regional autonomy, and independence were not the same program, and activists disagreed among themselves.

The constitutional opening mattered because it was partial. Associations and publications could develop within a wider public sphere, but official recognition of Kurdish as a distinct national identity remained politically dangerous. Kurdish organizers therefore often used the language of eastern development, class inequality, village poverty, or constitutional citizenship. Such formulations were not necessarily disguises for one hidden independence program. Activists genuinely disagreed about whether social transformation would resolve national inequality, whether a specific Kurdish organization was necessary, or whether their cause could be pursued within a Turkish socialist party.

The Eastern Meetings made those questions public. Reports of rallies in Silvan, Diyarbakır, Siverek, Batman, Tunceli, and other locations in 1967 indicate a movement connecting students and intellectuals with local grievances. Public addresses about roads, jobs, schools, and discrimination could mobilize broader constituencies than a narrowly separatist manifesto. The meetings were also a form of political education: their organization required transport, leaflets, speakers, police negotiations, and communication among towns, thereby creating practical experience that lasted beyond any individual rally. Their significance should be measured by these networks and the demands articulated, not by projecting later armed organizations backward onto every participant.

The Workers' Party of Turkey created a platform on which discussion of inequality could be tied to a critique of national assimilation. Its parliamentary experience made that platform unusually visible, but it also exposed members to intense scrutiny. Within the Turkish left, disagreements arose over whether Kurdish claims should be understood as a national question in their own right or mainly as an expression of class relations and uneven development. The debate was substantive: the two readings implied different expectations about schooling, language recognition, and regional political institutions. It shaped the transition from broad left-wing participation toward Kurdish-specific associations at the end of the decade.

Section 137.19

The DDKO, Kurdish Political Organization, and Turkey's Military Intervention of 1971

In 1969 Kurdish students and intellectuals in Ankara, Istanbul, and eastern towns founded the Revolutionary Cultural Hearths of the East (Devrimci Doğu Kültür Ocakları, DDKO). It organized lectures, publications, and local branches, and campaigned against regional inequality and against restrictions on Kurdish culture. Its members included students, teachers, young professionals, and some workers, and it operated within the broader Turkish left, sharing platforms and sometimes personnel with TİP and with student organizations. Its political language was socialist and its demands were framed in terms of democratic rights and cultural freedom.

At its fourth congress in October 1970, TİP adopted a resolution that acknowledged the existence of a Kurdish people in eastern Turkey and condemned the pressures placed on them, treating the Kurdish question as one of national identity as well as of economic development. This was a significant departure for a legal Turkish party, and it was soon used against the party by its opponents.

The reaction came with the military memorandum of 12 March 1971, which forced the resignation of the government of Süleyman Demirel and led to martial law in several provinces. The authorities closed organizations, arrested activists, and tried leftist and Kurdish figures, and in July 1971 the Constitutional Court banned TİP, in part on the grounds of its Kurdish stance. DDKO members were prosecuted, and the organization was dissolved. A political amnesty in 1974 under the government of Bülent Ecevit released many prisoners and allowed a degree of reorganization.

The legacy of this period was the emergence of new Kurdish intellectual and political networks, with experience in organization, publication, and trial, and with a political vocabulary that linked national rights and social reform. The later foundation of the PKK in 1978 and the armed conflict that followed (Chapters 149 and 154) belong to a later chronology and must not be read back onto the DDKO or onto all Kurdish activists of this period, many of whom pursued legal and cultural strategies and some of whom would later oppose armed struggle.

The DDKO was important less because it commanded a large armed constituency than because it helped sustain a public political vocabulary and an intercity network. Meetings and publications created spaces in which Kurdish students could discuss regional history, economic policy, and the place of Kurdish identity within republican citizenship. Members had to navigate the fact that overt arguments for national rights could invite criminal prosecution, while a focus solely on regional poverty risked leaving language and cultural restrictions unaddressed. This strategic tension explains why activists moved between the idioms of socialist reform, democratic equality, and national recognition.

The TİP resolution of October 1970 provides unusually clear evidence that those questions had reached a legal party's highest deliberative forum. It recognized the existence of a Kurdish people in the eastern provinces and opposed assimilative pressures, connecting the national question to political and socioeconomic inequalities. It did not make every member of the party a Kurdish nationalist or establish one Kurdish political program. Its subsequent use in the Constitutional Court proceedings illustrates the line between the constitutional freedoms associated with the 1960s and the state's continued restrictions on the political expression of a distinct Kurdish nationhood.

The military memorandum of March 1971 narrowed that public space. Prosecutions, detention, and the dissolution of associations interrupted organizing and encouraged some activists to operate through informal or clandestine networks. The fact that certain prisoners were released after the 1974 amnesty did not restore the same political conditions that had existed in 1967. It did, however, leave behind graduates of an organizing experience who understood both the possibilities and vulnerabilities of legal public action. The later emergence of competing Kurdish currents should be explained from their own subsequent decisions rather than described as the inevitable product of DDKO or TİP.

Section 137.20

One People, Different Political Struggles: Kurdish Nationalism across Iraq, Iran, and Turkey

Between 1946 and 1975 Kurdish politics took three principal forms. In Iraq a movement with a mountain base, a mass following, and foreign supply fought repeated wars and negotiated autonomy. In Iran an episode of self-government was followed by repression and by underground organization. In Turkey an urban, student, and socialist politics grew in the 1960s under a state that denied separate Kurdish identity. The differences cannot be attributed to a single cause.

Geography mattered. The Iraqi mountains, close to a hostile neighbor willing to supply arms, made prolonged guerrilla war possible, while the Iranian and Turkish states had stronger control of their Kurdish regions. Leadership mattered: Barzani's combination of religious, tribal, and military authority gave the Iraqi movement cohesion and also created rivalries, while Iranian and Turkish activists operated in more constrained environments. Foreign intervention mattered most in Iraq, where it both enabled and doomed the insurgency, and it affected Mahabad before that. Social structure mattered: the persistence of tribal and landlord influence in Iraq and parts of Turkey contrasted with greater urbanization and state penetration in Iran. State policy toward language and organization defined the available forms of politics.

The Kurdish movements also differed in their relationship to national identity. Mahabad, the Iraqi KDP, the KDPI, and the Turkish activists shared a sense of Kurdish peoplehood but not a unified program. Some sought autonomy within existing states, some thought of a broader Kurdistan, and some framed their goals in socialist or democratic terms that tied Kurdish rights to the transformation of Turkey or Iran. Outside governments could support one Kurdish movement while suppressing another because they treated Kurds as instruments of interstate rivalry, not as bearers of national rights.

A useful comparison separates three dimensions that are often merged. The first is national aspiration: a commitment to Kurdish language, cultural recognition, political representation, or autonomy. The second is organizational form: a local republic, a party with a clandestine network, a guerrilla force, or a legal cultural association. The third is political opportunity: the degree of space afforded by constitutional rules, state capacity, social coalitions, and the policies of neighboring powers. Similar aspirations did not produce similar organizations when the political opportunities were so different. Nor did different organizations demonstrate that the people involved lacked a shared sense of belonging.

These movements also offer contrasting lessons about evidence. Mahabad is documented through proclamations, diplomatic reports, memoirs, and later recollection, which preserve elite voices more fully than village experience. Iraqi Kurdish history is illuminated by agreements, military and security records, foreign intelligence files, and personal testimony, each with its own incentives to justify decisions. Turkish Kurdish politics can be studied through court proceedings, socialist-party resolutions, pamphlets, newspapers, and the memories of association members. Comparative study requires changing the sources when the institutions under investigation change; no single archive gives an authoritative account of Kurdish society across all three states.

The chapter's terminal date is an analytical boundary rather than an end to Kurdish national history. The 1975 collapse exposed the fragility of an insurgency whose supply lines depended on another state's priorities, while the reorganization of Kurdish politics in Iraq was already beginning. In Iran, parties and memories survived repression; in Turkey, the accumulated experience of student and socialist organizing outlasted the repression of 1971. The historical achievement of this period cannot be measured exclusively by sovereignty. It also consists of the formation of political languages, institutions, alliances, and contested memories that subsequent generations inherited—and sometimes challenged.

Historical Debate

Why Did Kurdish National Movements Achieve Limited Success Before 1975?

The question: Was the limited success of Kurdish movements before 1975 determined mainly by the power of the states ruling Kurdish regions, by divisions within Kurdish politics, or by the decisions of outside powers?

Interpretation A — State power: Iraq, Iran, and Turkey had superior armies, administrations, and resources, shared an interest in preventing Kurdish autonomy, and cooperated when it suited them, as at Algiers. On this view Kurdish outcomes were set by a structure of states that was difficult to overcome.
Interpretation B — Kurdish divisions: Rivalries between Barzani and the politburo, between tribes, and between regions and parties weakened Kurdish bargaining power and allowed governments to divide Kurds, as in the use of pro-government militias and rival factions.
Interpretation C — External decisions: The Soviet withdrawal in 1946 and the Iranian withdrawal in 1975 were decisive, and external patrons used the Kurds for their own purposes and abandoned them when interests changed.

Evidence: Each interpretation can point to documented episodes. Most historians combine them, weighing them differently for different cases. In Mahabad external withdrawal was decisive, in the Iraqi 1964 split internal rivalries were central, and the final collapse in 1975 involved the withdrawal of aid within a context of state superiority.

Why it matters: The debate warns against treating Kurdish outcomes as the result of Kurdish failure alone or as the product of foreign betrayal alone.

Student task: Choose one case, Mahabad, 1964, or 1975, and explain how the three interpretations would analyze it differently.

The conclusion should separate three outcomes. The military collapse of the Iraqi insurgency in 1975 revealed the vulnerability of externally dependent armed movements. Kurdish political organization survived in Iran despite repression, and new Kurdish intellectual and political networks emerged in Turkey. The absence of Kurdish statehood did not mean the absence of Kurdish political institutions, national identity, or political agency: parties, newspapers, armed forces, administrations, schools, and networks existed, even where states denied them recognition.

Chapter Summary

Kurdish-inhabited regions form a mountain arc divided among Turkey, Iraq, Iran, and Syria. After the First World War the unimplemented Treaty of Sèvres, which had made Kurdish autonomy and conditional independence possible, was replaced by the Treaty of Lausanne, and Kurdish regions became parts of states with their own centralizing and national projects. Kurdistan remained a geographical and historical designation, not a political boundary.

The Republic of Mahabad, proclaimed in January 1946, was a landmark modern Kurdish nationalist government with a program of autonomy; it was not the first Kurdish experiment in self-government. It combined genuine Kurdish party organization with Soviet patronage and collapsed when Soviet troops withdrew, and its leader Qazi Muhammad was executed in March 1947. Mahabad became a founding memory and a lesson in the vulnerability of dependent nationalism.

In Iraq the revolution of 1958 recognized Arabs and Kurds as partners, and Barzani returned from exile. The recognition was not matched by institutions. Disputes over autonomy, landholding, and tribal rivalries led to the insurgency of 1961, and the peshmerga fought governments across coups and regimes. The Bazzaz Declaration of 1966 showed that Baghdad could offer cultural concessions but resisted territorial and institutional guarantees.

The Iraqi Kurdish movement was internally diverse. The rupture of 1964 between Barzani and the politburo led by Ibrahim Ahmad and Jalal Talabani established a division that shaped Kurdish politics for decades, and it reflected differences in leadership, social base, and strategy, not a simple opposition of tribalism and modernity.

The March 1970 agreement represented the high point of negotiated settlement, with recognition of Kurdish national rights, official language status, and a plan for autonomy. It collapsed over Kirkuk, oil, the census, Arabization, and mutual distrust. The unilateral law of March 1974 was rejected by Barzani, and war resumed on a large scale.

Iraqi Kurdish insurgency was an international affair. Iran, Israel, and the United States each assisted Barzani for their own reasons, and none sought a Kurdish state. The Algiers Accord of 6 March 1975, which settled the Iran-Iraq border dispute, ended Iranian aid and brought a swift collapse of the insurgency and large-scale displacement. The Patriotic Union of Kurdistan, founded in June 1975, was one product of the crisis.

In Iran, Kurdish politics survived underground after Mahabad. The Shah's centralization, land reform, and education integrated Kurdish society into the state without political autonomy. A small armed movement in 1967 to 1968 was suppressed. The Shah's support for Iraqi Kurds and repression of Iranian Kurds is explained by interstate rivalry and regime security.

In Turkey the republic's definition of national identity and the suppression of earlier revolts left little room for open Kurdish politics, until the constitution of 1961 and the rise of the socialist left created new opportunities. The Eastern Meetings, the DDKO, and the TİP's fourth congress linked regional inequality to national recognition, and the repression after March 1971 shut the space again, though the networks persisted.

Comparing the three cases shows that outcomes were shaped by geography, leadership, foreign intervention, social structure, and state policy. The Iraqi case produced sustained insurgency and negotiation, the Iranian case produced repression and underground survival, and the Turkish case produced a legal and socialist politics that was then repressed.

Between 1946 and 1975 Kurdish movements demonstrated political resilience and organizational capacity. They were constrained by the competing territorial interests of the three states, the internal diversity of Kurdish politics, and the instability of foreign assistance. The collapse of 1975 did not extinguish aspirations for autonomy, equality, and recognition.

Chapter Reflection

Chapter 137 matters because it shows how a national movement can exist without a state and how states shape the movements within them. The Kurdish case is not a footnote to Arab, Turkish, or Iranian nationalism but a parallel history in which the same pressures, modernization, centralization, Cold War rivalry, and oil, produced different political forms.

The larger historical processes are familiar from earlier chapters: the post-Ottoman state system, the Cold War alignments of Chapters 128 to 134, and the oil economy of Chapter 141. Kirkuk shows how oil could convert a territorial dispute into a question of state survival, and the Algiers Accord shows how a border agreement could end an insurgency in days.

Several structures continued after 1975: the territorial claims of Kirkuk, the divisions between Kurdish parties, the dependence on neighbors, the Turkish debate over identity, and the Iranian state's suspicion of Kurdish organization. Volume IX examines the Iranian revolution, the Iran-Iraq War, the Anfal campaign, the PKK, and the autonomy of Iraqi Kurdistan after 1991.

Chapter 138 turns to Lebanon, another state where communal politics and external involvement would collide. The Kurdish case and the Lebanese case together show how fragile the post-colonial state system was, and how much of the region's history in the 1970s was made at its borders.
