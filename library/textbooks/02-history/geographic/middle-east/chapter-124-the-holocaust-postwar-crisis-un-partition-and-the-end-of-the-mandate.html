The Holocaust, Postwar Crisis, UN Partition, and the End of the Mandate

A History of the Middle East: From Ancient Civilizations to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

On 11 July 1947 a former river steamer left the French port of Sète carrying roughly 4,500 Jewish men, women, and children, most of them survivors of the Nazi persecution. A week later, on 18 July, Royal Navy vessels intercepted her off the coast of Palestine. The ship had been renamed Exodus 1947, and her passengers were not going to be allowed to land. After a violent boarding in which three people were killed, they were transferred to other ships and sent back toward France, and then, after weeks of standoff, to the British zone of occupied Germany. Few episodes of the postwar years compressed the problems of this chapter so tightly: refugees with nowhere to go, a mandatory government enforcing immigration limits, a Jewish movement that wanted both rescue and sovereignty, an Arab majority that regarded the whole arrangement as an imposition, and an international audience that was watching.

The Exodus affair belongs to a larger story that this chapter tells from the end of the Second World War in 1945 to the legal termination of the British Mandate for Palestine at midnight between 14 and 15 May 1948. It is a story about how a humanitarian emergency, two competing national movements, the weakening of an empire, and the new diplomacy of the United Nations combined to end thirty years of British rule without producing an agreed settlement. The chapter's central question is how a crisis involving refugees, competing national claims, imperial responsibility, and international diplomacy developed into the collapse of British rule and the outbreak of civil war in Palestine.

The chapter begins with the Holocaust, but only as far as is necessary to understand what it meant for Palestine. The murder of approximately six million European Jews destroyed the largest Jewish communities in the world and left several hundred thousand survivors in a Europe that was devastated, often hostile, and mostly closed to them. A general history of the Holocaust belongs elsewhere. Here the question is narrower: how that catastrophe altered the urgency of Jewish demands for refuge and statehood, the way those demands were received by governments and publics, and the pressure they placed on the Mandate.

It would nevertheless be a serious distortion to tell this as the story of Jewish refugees and British officials alone. In 1945 Palestine had about 1.2 million Arab inhabitants and roughly 600,000 Jews. Palestinian Arabs had their own society of villages, towns, ports, courts, newspapers, trade unions, and political parties, and their own claim to independence, which had been articulated since the First World War and defended in the great revolt of 1936–1939. That revolt had been crushed, and its aftermath left Palestinian political life fragmented and its leaders exiled or constrained. The Palestinian position in 1945–1948 cannot be understood as a mere reaction to Jewish immigration; it was a claim of a majority population to self-government in the country it inhabited.

Britain, for its part, emerged from the war victorious but financially exhausted, committed to a Labour program of domestic reconstruction, and anxious about its strategic position in Egypt, the Gulf, India, and the wider Muslim world. Palestine had become a costly and dangerous liability. A garrison on the order of 100,000 troops faced a Jewish insurgency, Arab opposition, and a stream of illegal immigrant ships, while Washington pressed London to admit Jewish refugees. The British government attempted a series of compromises and then, in February 1947, handed the problem to the United Nations.

The UN Special Committee on Palestine recommended partition, and on 29 November 1947 the General Assembly adopted Resolution 181 (II) by a vote of 33 to 13, with 10 abstentions. Yet the resolution was a recommendation, not a self-executing settlement. Palestinian Arab leaders and the Arab states rejected it; the Jewish Agency accepted it in principle with reservations; Britain declined to enforce it; and no international force was created to impose it. Within days, violence between Arabs and Jews escalated into the first phase of a civil war, and British authority eroded week by week until the Mandate lapsed.

The chapter therefore resists two simplifications. The creation of a Jewish state was not an inevitable consequence of the Holocaust, and Palestinian dispossession was not an incidental by-product of diplomacy; both outcomes emerged from choices, alternatives, and failures of compromise that can be reconstructed from the evidence. Violence and displacement began before May 1948, and this chapter acknowledges them, but the full military and demographic history of the war belongs to Chapter 125. Chapter 123 treated the Middle East during the Second World War; Chapter 124 is the political and humanitarian crisis that follows; and the end of the Mandate is the threshold across which Volume VIII begins.

The twenty sections that follow are organized in five divisions: the Holocaust and the refugee crisis (124.1–124.4); the crisis of British rule (124.5–124.8); the diplomacy of inquiry and referral (124.9–124.12); the Partition Plan itself (124.13–124.16); and the slide from civil war to British withdrawal (124.17–124.20). Throughout, documents are identified by the institution that produced them, and official proposals are distinguished from what was actually implemented.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how the Holocaust and the postwar displaced-persons crisis changed the urgency and international reception of Jewish demands for refuge and statehood in Palestine.
  2. Distinguish the varied experiences, aspirations, and destinations of Jewish survivors rather than treating them as a single, uniform population.
  3. Analyze British policy after 1945, including the 1939 White Paper, immigration quotas, and the security, financial, and imperial calculations behind them.
  4. Evaluate Palestinian Arab society, leadership, and national aspirations, including the legacy of the 1936–1939 revolt and the reasons for opposition to partition.
  5. Compare the Jewish Agency, Haganah, Irgun, and Lehi in their objectives, methods, and relations with one another and with Britain.
  6. Trace the diplomatic sequence from the Harrison Report through the Anglo-American Committee, the Morrison–Grady proposals, and Britain's referral of Palestine to the United Nations.
  7. Describe the work and recommendations of UNSCOP and the differences between its majority and minority plans.
  8. Explain why the United States, the Soviet Union, and the Arab states took the positions they did on partition, distinguishing governments from popular opinion.
  9. Analyze the territorial, economic, and constitutional provisions of Resolution 181 (II) and the status of the resolution as a recommendation.
  10. Identify the practical obstacles that prevented the Partition Plan from creating two functioning states.
  11. Reconstruct the early stages of the civil war, civilian hardship, and British withdrawal while distinguishing them from the wider war treated in Chapter 125.
  12. Evaluate competing historical interpretations of British motives, the role of the Holocaust, and the fairness and feasibility of partition.

Historical Context

This chapter draws on earlier material in Volume VII and before. Chapter 102 examined the origins of Zionism and Jewish migration to late Ottoman Palestine; Chapter 112 examined wartime British commitments, including the Balfour Declaration of 1917; and Chapters 120 through 122 examined the Mandate's government, Palestinian nationalism and the Arab Revolt of 1936–1939, and the institutions of the Yishuv, the organized Jewish community of Palestine. Chapter 123 examined the Middle East during the Second World War. Students need only the following reminders.

Britain received the Mandate for Palestine from the League of Nations, with effect from 1923, and incorporated into it the commitment to facilitate a Jewish national home while safeguarding the civil and religious rights of the non-Jewish communities. Jewish immigration, land purchase, and institution-building expanded under the Mandate, particularly after the Nazi seizure of power in 1933. Arab opposition, expressed through strikes, delegations, and violence, culminated in the general strike and rebellion of 1936–1939. In May 1939, as war approached, Britain issued a White Paper that restricted Jewish immigration and land purchase and promised an independent Palestine within ten years, a policy Zionists regarded as a betrayal at the moment of Europe's greatest peril.

During the war, the Yishuv supported Britain against Nazi Germany while opposing the White Paper; Palestinian Arab leadership was divided and weakened, and the Mufti, Hajj Amin al-Husayni, spent the war in the Axis sphere. By 1945 the Mandate remained legally in force, but the underlying contradiction in its terms had grown sharper, and the world to which it belonged, a world of imperial trusteeship under the League of Nations, had been replaced by the United Nations, the Cold War, and the rising power of the United States and the Soviet Union.

Section 124.1 · Division I — The Holocaust and the Postwar Jewish Refugee Crisis

The Holocaust and the Destruction of European Jewish Communities

The Holocaust was the systematic, state-organized murder of European Jews by Nazi Germany and its allies and collaborators between 1941 and 1945, preceded by years of persecution after 1933. When Germany invaded the Soviet Union in June 1941, mobile killing units known as Einsatzgruppen began mass shootings of Jewish civilians. Ghettos, deportations, and killing centers followed, including Chełmno, Bełżec, Sobibór, Treblinka, Majdanek, and Auschwitz-Birkenau. The Wannsee Conference of January 1942 coordinated the administration of deportations already underway. Approximately six million Jews were murdered, close to two-thirds of the European Jewish population of the 1930s, with the heaviest losses in Poland, the Baltic states, and the occupied Soviet territories. Other groups, including Roma, disabled people, Soviet prisoners of war, and political opponents, were also persecuted and killed, in policies related to but distinct from the genocide of the Jews.

This chapter does not narrate the Holocaust; it asks what the catastrophe meant for Palestine. Three consequences stand out. First, it destroyed the demographic heartland of the Jewish world. The Jewish communities of Poland, Lithuania, and neighboring regions had been centers of religious learning, Yiddish culture, and political movements of every kind, Zionist and anti-Zionist, socialist and Orthodox. Before the war Zionism had been one current among several, and many European Jews had looked to emancipation, socialism, or religious tradition rather than emigration. The murder of millions, including the Bundist and other constituencies that had opposed Zionist premises, changed the balance of Jewish political life.

Second, it created a population of survivors for whom return was often impossible. Homes had been seized, communities erased, and in some places returning Jews met hostility and violence. Third, it shaped how Jews and many outside observers judged the failure of rescue. The Évian Conference of 1938 and the restrictive immigration policies of many states, including Britain's limits on Palestine after 1939, left a bitter memory. In the Yishuv, news of the extermination reached the public in 1942–1943, and the community debated what it could and could not have done; about 30,000 Jews from Palestine served in the British forces, and the Jewish Brigade, formed in 1944, later helped survivors in Europe.

Evidence for the Holocaust is overwhelming and diverse: German administrative records, the proceedings of the Nuremberg trials that began in November 1945, photographs and film taken at liberated camps such as Bergen-Belsen in April 1945, survivor testimony, and the physical remains of the sites. The postwar public confronted these revelations as they considered Palestine. That does not mean the Holocaust automatically produced the Jewish state, an argument examined critically in Section 124.4. It means that after 1945 Jewish claims reached governments and publics in a changed moral climate, while Palestinian Arabs, who bore no responsibility for the crime, found themselves facing the consequences of a European catastrophe in which they had played no part.

The genocide also shattered the institutional geography of Jewish public life. Where interwar Warsaw and Vilna had supported newspapers, political parties, schools, charitable societies, and religious institutions, survivors frequently encountered destroyed buildings and absent neighbors. A surviving individual might have been released from a camp with no reliable information about relatives; another had escaped to the Soviet interior and returned after the war only to discover that an entire household had been murdered. These different routes of survival mattered. They shaped languages spoken in displaced-persons centers, decisions about rebuilding families, and trust in governments that had either persecuted Jews or failed to protect them. Historians reconstruct these experiences through survivor interviews and correspondence as well as official records; the silence of the murdered must not be mistaken for evidence that their prewar alternatives to Zionism were unimportant.

The international refugee system was designed around the assumption that most displaced Europeans would eventually go home. For many surviving Jews, however, repatriation did not provide a viable answer. Postwar European borders were shifting, governments were rebuilding, property restitution was contested, and antisemitic hostility persisted. Jewish families could sometimes reunite only by crossing borders illegally. The destruction of communities in central and eastern Europe therefore had consequences that outlasted Germany's defeat. The problem was neither simply the lack of transportation nor a momentary shortage of camps; it was the collapse of a social world in which return, even when physically possible, might mean isolation, dispossession, and danger.

There was no single political response to this collapse. Some survivors joined Zionist associations and prepared to immigrate to Palestine. Others worked for renewed Jewish life in Europe, sought admission to the United States or Latin American countries, or organized around religious and socialist projects. It would be historically careless to assume that all Holocaust survivors shared one political ideology. Zionist organizers nevertheless acquired an unusual capacity to turn the desire to depart into a collective campaign through youth organizations, agricultural training centers, and transport networks. The difference between refugee preference and organized political representation is central: one concerns what individual people wanted; the other concerns which institutions could speak forcefully on their behalf.

Palestinian Arabs also interpreted the devastation of Europe within their own political framework. Sympathy for victims of Nazi persecution did not automatically imply consent to increased immigration into a country where Arab inhabitants already feared losing political control. Their leaders argued that Europeans and Americans should assume responsibility for admitting refugees rather than transfer the consequences of European antisemitism to Palestine. Zionists answered that only a self-governing Jewish homeland could offer durable security where reliance on other governments had failed so catastrophically. These were distinct arguments about justice and sovereignty, not different accounts of whether the genocide had occurred. The crisis of 1945 exposed a tragic problem of overlapping claims for which no state had produced an accepted solution.

Section 124.2 · Division I

Liberation, Displaced Persons, and the Search for Refuge

At the end of the war, millions of displaced persons, forced laborers, prisoners of war, and concentration-camp survivors were scattered across Germany, Austria, and Italy. Most non-Jewish displaced persons were repatriated within months. Many Jewish survivors, however, could not or would not go home. Some found their houses occupied; some feared the antisemitism that persisted in Eastern Europe. On 4 July 1946, a mob in the Polish city of Kielce killed dozens of Jews, a pogrom that triggered a large wave of departures from Poland and demonstrated that return was dangerous. By 1947, roughly a quarter of a million Jewish displaced persons were living in camps and urban centers in the American, British, and French zones of Germany and Austria and in Italy.

In summer 1945 President Truman sent Earl G. Harrison, dean of the University of Pennsylvania Law School, to inspect conditions in the camps. His report, submitted in August, criticized the treatment of Jewish survivors, many of whom remained behind barbed wire and in poor conditions under military administration, and it recommended that 100,000 of them be admitted to Palestine. The report prompted changes in American military policy, including the recognition of Jews as a distinct group in the camps, and helped bring the Palestine question to the president's desk.

Primary Source Window

The Harrison Report, August 1945

Creator: Earl G. Harrison for President Truman · Date: submitted August 1945; publicly reported that autumn · Type: Official investigative report

Verified document reference: Earl G. Harrison, Report on Jewish displaced persons, August 1945 (archival description and original report)

Context: Truman, under pressure from Jewish organizations and from officers who had witnessed the camps, asked Harrison to inspect the treatment of displaced persons, especially Jews, in the American zone.

Paraphrase (not a verbatim quotation): Harrison described Jewish survivors as living in crowded, inadequate, and sometimes guarded facilities, still deprived of their liberty and dignity, and he argued that the Jewish displaced persons should be treated as a distinct category. He reported that most of them wished to leave Germany and that Palestine was the destination of choice for many, and he urged that 100,000 immigration certificates for Palestine be issued as a first step.

Analysis: The report is an official humanitarian assessment that links survivors' conditions to a specific immigration proposal. Its influence lay in its source, an American investigator reporting to the president, and in its framing of Palestine as a solution to a refugee problem.

Limitations: Harrison reported what he saw in a limited number of camps over a short time, and his generalization about survivors' preferences should be read alongside evidence that many sought emigration to the United States, Britain, and elsewhere. Quoting passages from the text should be done only from the published report itself.

Survivors were not a uniform group. They included Zionists trained on agricultural training farms, members of religious communities, former partisans, Bundists, and many who simply wished to rebuild a family in a safe country. Some preferred the United States, Canada, or Australia, but restrictive immigration laws, including American national-origins quotas, made that difficult; Truman's directive of December 1945 eased admission only within existing limits, and a broader American Displaced Persons Act came only in 1948. For many survivors Palestine thus became the most available destination, and the Zionist movement the most organized advocate for their migration. Britain's monthly quota, set at 1,500 immigrants from late 1945, was a fraction of the demand.

Camp life itself produced new communities. Survivors married, had children, opened schools, established newspapers, staged theater, and formed committees to represent their interests to occupation authorities. The camps were also political spaces, where emissaries from Palestine recruited, trained, and organized the clandestine movement described in the next section.

Administration of the camps was itself a political arena. In the American occupation zone, Jewish committees sought autonomy over food distribution, education, religious observance, and the representation of residents to military and humanitarian authorities. The United Nations Relief and Rehabilitation Administration, later followed by the International Refugee Organization, faced enormous logistical responsibilities and conflicting national priorities. Housing shortages, epidemics, rationing, and the scarcity of trained personnel could make nominal liberation feel like another period of confinement. Jewish self-government in the camps challenged an early tendency to classify residents merely by citizenship, a system that could obscure the distinct dangers facing Jews from countries where antisemitism remained strong.

The Harrison Report became influential because it was not simply another petition by an interested movement. Truman had authorized an outside investigator to assess actual conditions, and Harrison criticized circumstances he regarded as incompatible with the promises of liberation. His findings helped shift American military administration toward separate arrangements for Jewish displaced persons, though improvements came unevenly. British officials interpreted the requested immigration certificates in a different frame: accepting a large immediate influx, they argued, would disrupt the fragile equilibrium in Palestine and expose an overstretched imperial administration to more violence. A proposal that appeared to American officials as urgent humanitarian relief could thus appear to British officials as an immediate security and diplomatic liability.

Survivors created public institutions as well as private lives. Camp representatives organized congresses, welfare societies, vocational courses, libraries, amateur theater, and newspapers. Weddings and births in displaced-persons camps were both personal occasions and signs that a population officially described through displacement was reconstructing a future. These activities were not a substitute for adequate housing or mobility; nor did they eliminate disagreement about destinations. They show why the expression 'displaced persons' is a legal and administrative category, not a complete description of individuals who carried languages, professions, beliefs, and political memories into the postwar years.

The movement known as Brichah, or flight, linked events in Eastern Europe to port cities on the Mediterranean. Organizers guided Jews from Poland and other countries through transit routes in Austria and Italy, sometimes using forged documentation or crossing borders without permission. The route depended upon local officials, charitable aid, transport capacity, and the willingness of families to endure renewed uncertainty. Each stage involved decisions about children, relatives left behind, property that could not be recovered, and the risks of detention. Such choices rarely fit a neat division between voluntary migration and forced flight: survivors exercised agency, but they did so under sharply constrained conditions.

The numerical record requires care. A peak population of roughly 250,000 Jewish displaced persons in postwar European camps is a stock count, whereas the number who passed through a particular transport route is cumulative. The two should not be added. Nor should the figure for Jewish people who sought Palestine be treated as a measure of all survivors' beliefs. The historical question is how the convergence of a large displaced population, organized migration, and restrictive admission policies transformed humanitarian relief into a conflict over governing authority in Palestine. Everyday Life and Social History

Daily Life in a Displaced-Persons Camp

Displaced-persons camps were often former military barracks or institutions, administered first by Allied armies and UNRRA and later by the International Refugee Organization. Residents received rations, which they supplemented through barter and the black market, and lived in crowded rooms often shared by several families. Despite this, camps developed a dense civic life: children attended Hebrew or Yiddish schools, young people joined youth movements, couples married in large numbers, and births rose markedly.

Historians use camp newspapers, oral histories, administrative records, and photographs to reconstruct this world. These sources reveal both resilience and the psychological injuries of the survivors. They also show differences: religious survivors reestablished yeshivot, others pursued vocational training, and political groups competed for the loyalty of the young. The camps were neither simply places of despair nor simply laboratories of Zionist mobilization; they were societies in suspension.

Historical Vocabulary
Displaced Persons and She'erit ha-Pletah

The term displaced person was an administrative category used by the Allied authorities and UNRRA for people uprooted by the war and unable to return home or settle elsewhere. For many years, Jews were not treated as a separate category within it. After the Harrison Report, military authorities in the American zone recognized Jewish survivors as a distinct group and provided separate camps.

She'erit ha-Pletah, usually translated “the surviving remnant,” was the survivors' own self-description and became a political symbol. It carried a claim: that the remnant had a collective voice in deciding its future. Historians use it carefully, because it can obscure the individuality of survivors and the many different decisions they made about where to live.

Section 124.3 · Division I

Jewish Migration, Aliyah Bet, Cyprus, and the Exodus 1947

Organized immigration outside British limits was not new. During the 1930s, Zionist and Revisionist groups had arranged ships carrying Jews from Europe, and the Mossad LeAliyah Bet, created in 1938 under the Haganah, coordinated the effort. After 1945 the scale and visibility changed. Within Europe, the Brichah (“flight”) movement, assisted by Jewish Brigade veterans, emissaries from Palestine, and relief agencies such as the American Jewish Joint Distribution Committee, helped survivors cross borders toward Mediterranean ports in Italy and France. Authorities in several countries, sometimes sympathetic and sometimes simply overwhelmed, did not obstruct the movement. The Mossad LeAliyah Bet purchased and refitted surplus vessels, and the voyages, called Aliyah Bet (“immigration B”), began in earnest.

The Royal Navy intercepted most of the ships. Until August 1946, intercepted passengers were often taken to a detention camp at Atlit in Palestine; thereafter Britain deported them to camps on Cyprus, which it hoped would deter further voyages and avoid exhausting the Palestine quota. The Cyprus camps eventually held roughly 50,000 people. They were crowded and guarded, but also became centers of Zionist training and of community life; children were born there, and the Jewish Agency supplied teachers and aid. Britain released people according to the monthly quota, so the camps were effectively a waiting room for legal immigration.

The Exodus 1947 was the best-known voyage because it was timed to coincide with the presence of UNSCOP in the region and because Britain chose to respond in a way that provoked international criticism. She sailed from Sète on 11 July 1947 with about 4,500 passengers and was intercepted by British warships on 18 July. In the boarding, three people died. The passengers were not taken to Cyprus but placed on three vessels and returned to France. French authorities declined to force them off, and the passengers refused to disembark voluntarily. After weeks anchored at Port-de-Bouc, the British sent them to Hamburg, in occupied Germany, where they were removed ashore by force.

The decision to return Holocaust survivors to German soil gave the affair great symbolic power. The Zionist leadership understood the propaganda value, and many historians argue that the episode damaged Britain's standing and strengthened the position of those arguing that its Palestine policy was unsustainable. Others caution that its direct effect on UNSCOP and the General Assembly is difficult to measure. What is clear is that the incident made visible the tangled relationship between humanitarian suffering and political strategy: the Jewish Agency sought to rescue survivors, to demonstrate the failure of British restrictions, and to build momentum for a state, and these goals were intertwined, not separable.

Illegal immigration was conducted through a remarkable network extending from inland European transit centers to French, Italian, and Balkan ports. Zionist emissaries, seamen, former soldiers, camp leaders, and local supporters arranged documents, food, vessel purchases, and embarkation under intense pressure. Many vessels were unsuitable for their intended passenger loads. Crowding, inadequate sanitation, rough seas, and the threat of interception created serious risks even before any confrontation with the Royal Navy. The movement was thus both a rescue effort for people whose options were narrow and an organized challenge to British immigration law. Calling the voyages 'illegal' describes the Mandate government's position, not the ethical verdict of the historian.

The British detention policy illustrates how a limited quota became a multiplying crisis. Each intercepted voyage created a new group of people whose immediate needs the government still had to meet. Deportation to Cyprus allowed London to maintain its restrictions without concentrating every detainee in Palestine, yet it created visible camps administered under British authority. A figure such as roughly 50,000 detainees processed through the Cyprus camps across 1946–1949 records the cumulative flow, not the number held at one time. Prisoners arrived and departed on different schedules, and births in the camps added another dimension to their changing population. The camps embodied both detention and the persistence of schools, religious life, and political organization.

Exodus 1947 shows the international dimensions of that policy more clearly than any routine interception. Passengers were transferred from the intercepted ship at Haifa and then taken to Port-de-Bouc in France, where they resisted disembarkation. The subsequent movement to Hamburg in the British zone of Germany provoked sharp criticism because Holocaust survivors were being returned to German soil after liberation. The visual and symbolic force of the event was unusually strong, but it did not by itself determine UNSCOP's decision or the General Assembly vote. Publicized images, observers' reports, survivors' testimony, and political campaigning reinforced one another in a larger international argument about whether British administration remained defensible.

Different actors interpreted the same voyage differently. British authorities emphasized their obligation to enforce immigration regulations and feared encouraging further sailings. Jewish organizers stressed that people with few viable destinations were being excluded from the one place where an organized community had prepared to receive them. Palestinian leaders warned that every admission altered the country's demographic balance without the consent of its majority population. French officials, confronted with the ship's return, had their own legal and diplomatic calculations. The affair was a vivid international incident precisely because each participant recognized a different obligation and none controlled the entire sequence.

An analytical account must also distinguish the Mediterranean route from the wider pattern of Jewish migration. Many displaced survivors hoped to leave for North America, and some succeeded once quotas and administrative practices changed. The choice of Palestine could be shaped by family ties, Zionist beliefs, language networks, or the availability of a ship rather than a settled conviction about constitutional design. Aliyah Bet and the Cyprus camps demonstrate how movement across borders was converted by states and organizations into an argument over sovereignty. They also show how a policy intended to restrict immigration could, by producing dramatic public confrontation, undermine confidence in the government's ability to continue governing.

Historical Vocabulary
Certificates, “Illegal” Immigration, and Aliyah Bet

Under the Mandate, legal Jewish immigration was governed by certificates issued according to quotas set by the government. After the 1939 White Paper, the number of certificates was strictly limited. Immigration outside this system was called “illegal” by the Mandate government. Zionists generally used the term Aliyah Bet and regarded the restrictions as unjust and incompatible with the Mandate's purpose.

Historians put the word “illegal” in quotation marks or explain it, because the legality of the White Paper itself was contested: the League of Nations' Permanent Mandates Commission had judged it inconsistent with the Mandate in 1939. The vocabulary of the period thus embeds a political argument, which students should recognize.

Section 124.4 · Division I

The Holocaust's Political Consequences for Zionism

In May 1942 an American Zionist conference at the Biltmore Hotel in New York adopted a program demanding that Palestine be established as a Jewish commonwealth, with unrestricted immigration under Jewish control. Ben-Gurion, who had concluded that Britain would not honor the Mandate's promise of a national home and that the center of Zionist politics must shift to the United States, was its principal advocate. The Biltmore Program committed the movement publicly to statehood before the extent of the Holocaust was fully known, and it was adopted by the Zionist Actions Committee in November 1942.

After 1945 the survivors' plight gave the demand for statehood greater emotional and diplomatic power. The Jewish Agency presented immigration as a humanitarian necessity and the Jewish state as the only guarantee against future catastrophe. American Zionist organizations, led by figures such as Abba Hillel Silver, mobilized public opinion and pressed Congress and the White House. At the 22nd Zionist Congress at Basel in December 1946, the movement did not re-elect Chaim Weizmann as president, signaling a rejection of his gradualist approach and strengthening Ben-Gurion, who took charge of defense matters in the Jewish Agency Executive.

Yet Jewish opinion was not unanimous. The Ihud group, with Judah Magnes and Martin Buber, advocated a binational state. Hashomer Hatzair, a left-wing Zionist movement, also favored binationalism until 1947. Some Orthodox groups opposed a secular state, and the American Council for Judaism rejected Jewish nationalism altogether. The Revisionists and the Irgun opposed any partition of the land. Survivors themselves, as shown above, held differing views. The Holocaust strengthened Zionism, but it did not produce a single Jewish position.

The 1942 Biltmore Program marked a change in the language and strategic horizon of mainstream Zionist leadership. Earlier declarations had often emphasized a Jewish national home within the British Mandate framework, while Zionist factions debated the timing and shape of future sovereignty. The demand for a commonwealth sharpened the question: would Palestine be a place of protected immigration under imperial rule, or would the organized Jewish community exercise the powers of a state, including control over admission? The program did not erase earlier divisions, and wartime British policy had not yet collapsed, but it provided a vocabulary that postwar Zionist diplomats could bring directly to Washington, London, and international institutions.

Political strategy developed along more than one track. The Jewish Agency sought influence through negotiations with governments and the emerging United Nations, while the Haganah prepared personnel, intelligence, and defensive infrastructure. The Histadrut linked economic organization with the labor movement and settlement, and Jewish fundraising abroad sustained many of these efforts. At the same time, Irgun and Lehi members argued that Britain could be driven from Palestine through armed pressure. These competing methods reflected disagreements not only about tactics but about the kind of society that should emerge afterward. Labor, religious, liberal, socialist, and Revisionist currents had differing positions on class relations, territorial boundaries, and relations with the Arab population.

The claim that the Holocaust 'caused' Israel is inadequate as a historical explanation. It overlooks institutions, settlement networks, armed forces, financial organizations, and diplomatic lobbying built long before 1939. It also overlooks Britain's retreat from colonial commitments and the strategic calculations of the United States and Soviet Union. Yet saying that Israel's institutional foundations predated the genocide must not minimize the genocide's extraordinary impact on urgency, political legitimacy, and the international reception of Jewish demands. A serious account separates the existence of a national movement from the circumstances that changed its prospects of achieving statehood.

The surviving European Jewish population did not simply transfer its political preferences to the Jewish Agency. Camps contained members of competing parties, religious tendencies, and associations; some survivors distrusted the manipulation of their suffering by governments or movements. Zionist advocacy nevertheless acquired a powerful humanitarian register because there were real people who had lost families and homes and had spent years awaiting admission somewhere. Political legitimacy here operated on two levels: the individual rights of refugees to safety and the collective Zionist argument that only sovereignty could make such safety durable. Those claims were related, but not identical.

Binationalist proposals deserve attention because they establish that partition was not the only imaginable response. Judah Magnes, Martin Buber, and others feared that exclusive sovereignty would institutionalize conflict and advocated forms of shared political power. They lacked the electoral and organizational strength of the main Jewish Agency leadership, especially under the pressure of postwar migration. Palestinian Arab leaders generally demanded majority self-government rather than parity between two national collectives, which they believed would convert a minority movement into an equal constitutional partner. The failure of binationalism therefore cannot be reduced to the absence of idealism; it involved incompatible definitions of democratic legitimacy, national rights, and the authority to control future immigration. Section 124.5 · Division II — Palestine and the Crisis of British Rule

Britain's Mandate Obligations and the Immigration Question

Britain's Palestine policy in 1945 rested on contradictory legal and political commitments. The Mandate and the Balfour Declaration promised a Jewish national home; the 1939 White Paper, approved by Parliament in May 1939, limited Jewish immigration to 75,000 over five years, after which further immigration would require Arab consent, restricted land transfers, and promised an independent Palestine in ten years with safeguards for both communities. The Permanent Mandates Commission had considered the White Paper inconsistent with the Mandate, and Zionists regarded it as a betrayal. Arab leaders, meanwhile, rejected it as insufficient, because it still permitted immigration and delayed independence.

The new Labour government of July 1945 had in earlier party statements expressed sympathy for Zionism, which led many Zionists to expect a reversal. Instead, Prime Minister Clement Attlee and Foreign Secretary Ernest Bevin maintained the White Paper quotas, allowing only a limited number of certificates each month. Their reasons were practical. Britain depended on good relations with Arab governments and with Muslim opinion across the empire, including India. It was negotiating the future of its position in Egypt and its treaties with Iraq and Transjordan. It relied on Middle Eastern oil and on communications through the Suez Canal. It was financially weak and dependent on American credit, which made it sensitive to US opinion yet reluctant to bear the cost of Palestine alone.

Britain also feared that large-scale immigration would provoke Arab violence and that its limited forces could not control both communities. The government told itself that the Jewish refugee problem was European and international and could not be solved by Palestine alone, a view that Zionists considered an evasion. In Britain itself, public opinion grew weary of the cost, and the Jewish insurgency, described below, hardened attitudes. The result was a policy defined less by principle than by an effort to avoid being forced by one side or the other.

The Mandate's constitutional dilemma was built into its origins. Britain had received international authority to administer Palestine while facilitating a Jewish national home and safeguarding the civil and religious rights of its existing non-Jewish communities. The Mandate did not offer a clear route to representative self-government that both national movements considered legitimate. For the Palestinian Arab majority, a system that enabled large-scale immigration without securing majority sovereignty appeared fundamentally undemocratic. For Zionists, any constitution that gave the majority unrestricted power to halt Jewish immigration threatened the national home itself. British officials repeatedly proposed advisory councils or staged constitutional reforms, but a shared governing compact did not develop.

The 1939 White Paper was one answer to the crisis produced by the revolt of 1936–1939. It envisaged an independent Palestine within ten years, substantial limits on Jewish immigration, and controls on certain land transfers. Its immigration program allowed 75,000 entrants over five years, including 25,000 refugees in addition to annual allowances, after which further Jewish immigration required Arab consent. When war and the refugee emergency changed the context, Jewish leaders judged the policy intolerable; Arab leaders often regarded even its proposed concessions as incomplete or insufficiently enforced. The historical point is that the White Paper represented a policy shift, not the complete realization of either side's political program.

The Labour government's refusal to abandon the restrictions shocked those who had expected sympathetic declarations made before the election to become immediate policy. Ernest Bevin and Clement Attlee balanced domestic reconstruction, military budgets, relations with Washington, and commitments across an empire whose Middle Eastern position depended on Egypt, the eastern Mediterranean, Iraq, and the Gulf. They feared that a dramatic concession on immigration would provoke opposition not only inside Palestine but among Arab governments whose support Britain still needed. These strategic concerns did not eliminate moral responsibility toward refugees; they help explain why officials who acknowledged the humanitarian crisis could nevertheless defend restrictive entry.

Britain's problem was also administrative. Maintaining garrisons, policing bombings and sabotage, staffing immigration and customs offices, and protecting infrastructure consumed resources as British voters demanded housing, welfare, and economic recovery at home. Every new emergency measure could impose short-term control while reducing political consent. Officials were caught between demands from the Jewish Agency for admission and statehood and demands from Palestinians for independence and limits on immigration. British forces were not politically neutral in every policy decision, but their institutional priority increasingly became an orderly exit rather than the construction of a new legitimate order.

Contemporaries frequently treated imperial withdrawal as evidence of simple exhaustion. The situation was more specific. Britain remained a major power and retained substantial military assets, but keeping Palestine at the expense of other strategic priorities became increasingly difficult to justify. The Mandate was therefore not ended by one decisive defeat. It unraveled through a combination of constitutional impasse, escalating attacks, adverse publicity, financial burdens, and the inability to secure agreement from either national movement. Section 124.6 · Division II

Palestinian Arab Society, Leadership, and National Aspirations

In 1945 Palestinian Arabs, Muslim, Christian, and Druze, numbered about 1.2 million, roughly two-thirds of the population. Most lived in villages, where agriculture, olive and fruit cultivation, and herding remained central; a substantial minority lived in towns such as Jaffa, Haifa, Jerusalem, Nablus, Hebron, Acre, and Nazareth. Citrus exports from Jaffa and the coast linked Arab agriculture to world markets. The war brought employment on British projects and in military service, and also inflation and shortages. Education had expanded, newspapers such as Filastin and al-Difa' circulated widely, trade unions and women's organizations existed, and municipal councils administered towns. Palestinian society was dynamic and differentiated, not defined by its leadership alone.

Politically, the suppression of the 1936–1939 revolt had been devastating. The British dissolved the Arab Higher Committee in 1937, exiled or arrested many leaders, confiscated weapons, and used harsh measures against villages. Estimates of the human cost vary; the historian Rashid Khalidi has estimated that around ten percent of the adult male population was killed, wounded, imprisoned, or exiled. The revolt also exposed divisions within the Arab community, including violence among rival factions. The Mufti, Hajj Amin al-Husayni, spent the war in the Axis sphere, where he engaged in propaganda for Germany. This damaged his standing internationally and gave critics a lasting argument, and historians debate the extent of his actual influence on Nazi policy. His wartime role should be understood as one feature of a leadership under extreme pressure, not as a characteristic of Palestinian society.

In November 1945 the Arab League reconstituted the Arab Higher Committee as the Palestinian political leadership, and a successor body, the Arab Higher Executive, followed in 1946. Jamal al-Husayni presided while the Mufti, who escaped from French custody and reached Egypt in 1946, was barred from Palestine. Opposing factions, notably those associated with the Nashashibi family, remained outside, and some activists, such as Musa al-Alami, pursued development and diplomatic work through the Arab Office and agricultural projects. A National Liberation League of Arab communists and left-wing labor activists also existed. The leadership as a whole lacked a unified organization, a clear military structure, and a strong financial base.

Palestinian opposition to partition rested on several grounds: that the majority population had a right to govern the country; that Jewish land ownership, about six to seven percent of the total, did not justify giving Jews more than half the territory; that the UN had no authority to override the Mandate's population; and that partition would leave a large Arab minority inside the Jewish state. These arguments were advanced by leaders and echoed among much of the Arab public, but they were not the only voices. Some Palestinians quietly favored accommodation, and King Abdullah's ambitions offered an alternative path for part of the country. The task of historians is to recognize both the strength of the national claim and the limitations of the organizations that represented it.

Palestinian society in the 1940s was not a unified rural population waiting for decisions made elsewhere. The country contained sharply different regional economies. Coastal cities linked merchants and laborers to maritime trade, citrus exports, and British military markets. Nablus and Hebron were centers of local commerce and social networks extending into the hill country. Villages combined grain cultivation, orchards, livestock, market exchange, and seasonal labor, while Bedouin communities in the south relied on patterns of grazing and cultivation poorly captured by simple maps of permanent settlement. These differences affected how households understood land, political security, and the possibility of partition. A boundary that looked abstract to negotiators might separate a village from its market, fields from water, or family branches from one another.

Education and public debate had also expanded during the Mandate. Newspapers, student associations, professional organizations, religious institutions, and municipal networks articulated local and national priorities. Women contributed to charities, demonstrations, education, fundraising, and political mobilization, even where formal leadership bodies remained overwhelmingly male. Teachers and civil servants helped sustain a literate public sphere alongside religious and landed notables. Palestinian nationalism must therefore be understood as both an elite diplomatic claim and a wider network of civic engagement. Its institutions were less centralized than those of the Yishuv, but that disparity should not be confused with a lack of Palestinian political consciousness.

The defeat of the 1936–1939 revolt was critical in 1947. British military operations, arms confiscations, executions, imprisonment, and exile had reduced the capacity to coordinate armed resistance. Arab political leadership also suffered from rivalries among major families, regional interests, and disagreements about tactics. Amin al-Husayni's prominence did not make him representative of every Palestinian, and wartime political choices, including his collaboration with Nazi Germany, must be addressed without imputing them to the entire Arab population. Other leaders pursued different strategies, and a substantial number of Palestinian Arabs served in British wartime organizations. Generalizations about a single 'Arab position' conceal these differences.

Diplomacy was made harder by the absence of an elected, broadly legitimate representative institution capable of negotiating on behalf of all Palestinian Arabs. The Arab Higher Committee and related bodies sought recognition as the voice of the national movement, but their composition and methods did not solve the problem of political fragmentation. The Arab League gave the Palestinian issue wider visibility, yet governments in Cairo, Damascus, Baghdad, and Amman disagreed over interests and leadership. King Abdullah's ambitions in the central hill country were especially different from the demand for a sovereign Palestinian Arab state. Neither Palestinian leaders nor other Arab governments had a single plan for dealing with the collapse of British authority.

The case against partition was grounded in demographic and constitutional arguments. Palestinian Arabs formed a majority of the country's inhabitants and claimed the right to independence in the territory as a whole. Zionists answered that Jews had historical ties and internationally recognized national-home claims, as well as urgently unmet refugee needs. Land ownership and sovereignty were not synonymous, but ownership patterns mattered to both sides' perceptions of legitimacy. Arab leaders saw proposals to place large Arab populations under Jewish sovereignty as contrary to self-determination. Zionist leaders saw exclusive Arab-majority rule as a denial of Jewish collective security. A full historical explanation takes both arguments seriously while recognizing that the people living in the territory did not enjoy equal organizational or international influence. Sources: 1

Section 124.7 · Division II

The Yishuv, Jewish Political Organizations, and Armed Resistance

The Yishuv in 1946 numbered about 600,000, roughly a third of Palestine's population. It had developed self-governing institutions during the Mandate: the Jewish Agency, which represented the Zionist movement to the Mandate authorities and organized immigration and settlement; the Vaad Leumi (National Council), which administered community affairs; the Histadrut labor federation, which was both a trade union and an economic enterprise; and a network of agricultural settlements, schools, a Hebrew press, and industries that had grown during the war. Political life was diverse, including labor Zionists, who dominated the Jewish Agency, General Zionists, religious parties, Revisionists, communists, and advocates of binationalism.

Three armed organizations operated. The Haganah, the main underground defense force, was under the authority of the Jewish Agency's leadership, and its full-time strike force, the Palmach, had been formed in 1941. The Irgun Tzva'i Leumi (Etzel), associated with the Revisionist movement, had been led by Menachem Begin since 1943 and declared a revolt against British rule in February 1944. Lehi (the Fighters for the Freedom of Israel, called by the British the Stern Gang) was a smaller group that broke away from the Irgun and carried out assassinations, including that of Lord Moyne in Cairo in November 1944. During 1944–1945 the Haganah cooperated with the British against the Irgun in a campaign later called the Season.

Relations were uneasy. The Jewish Agency's leaders sought to use limited force to demonstrate that Britain could not rule Palestine without Jewish consent, while Begin and Lehi sought to expel Britain by escalating violence. The King David Hotel bombing, described in the next section, ended the coalition, and the Haganah withdrew from joint action. Disagreement among the three over legitimacy and restraint would shape the Yishuv's political life through 1948.

The armed organizations were embedded in a broader mobilization. Immigration, illegal and legal, brought hundreds of thousands of new members; the Jewish Agency's Mossad LeAliyah Bet organized transport; Haganah representatives secretly procured weapons abroad; and veterans of the British army and the Jewish Brigade contributed military experience. The Yishuv's institutions, unlike those of the Palestinian Arabs, were centralized and funded, a difference that would matter greatly after the partition vote.

Institutional capacity was an important historical variable. The Jewish Agency negotiated externally, the Vaad Leumi administered recognized communal affairs, and the Histadrut maintained labor and economic networks that connected urban workers with agricultural settlements. Jewish schools, medical services, cooperatives, taxation, and voluntary associations had created routines of administration long before anyone could proclaim independence. Their effectiveness differed by region and social class; private capital and private employers also played major roles. Nonetheless, the existence of overlapping institutions meant that a political decision in the Jewish leadership could be translated into staffing plans, communications, fundraising, and logistical preparation more readily than in the Palestinian Arab national movement of the late 1940s.

Military capacity was not simply a matter of the number of weapons. The Haganah developed command structures and local units tied to settlements and urban neighborhoods, while Palmach formations provided a more mobile force with extensive field experience. Jewish veterans of the British armed services contributed technical knowledge, though their later roles varied. Procurement agents sought weapons abroad and investigated opportunities to manufacture or repair them locally. These preparations were accelerated by fears of renewed communal violence and the possibility of invasion once Britain departed. They also reflected the aspiration to control territory, not merely to defend isolated settlements; that strategic distinction became more consequential in spring 1948.

The Irgun and Lehi occupied different political positions. Revisionist ideology influenced the Irgun, which undertook bombings and raids against British facilities and at times attacked Arab civilians. Lehi was smaller, more radical, and willing to use assassination as well as attacks on colonial authority. Coordination among the three armed organizations in the Jewish Resistance Movement was real but temporary. The King David Hotel bombing and disputes about the risks of mass civilian casualties deepened divisions. Haganah leaders subsequently emphasized their own independent political accountability while still operating outside British law. It is misleading either to present all Jewish organizations as one military chain of command or to suggest their actions were unrelated to the broader struggle over statehood.

Internal debates also concerned the desired character of any new state. Labor Zionists emphasized collective organization and a socialist language of national construction, but they were not uniform about borders or Arab rights. Religious parties sought accommodation for Jewish religious institutions and practice. Revisionists challenged the territorial and diplomatic caution of the Agency leadership. Communists and binationalists offered other interpretations of national equality and the future of the country. These differences did not disappear under external pressure, though the common objective of ending British restrictions and securing a Jewish political future often brought competitors into practical alliance.

One measure of state-building is the ability to respond to a crisis in more than one sector at once. When fighting increased after November 1947, the Jewish leadership could organize neighborhood defense, ration transport, collect contributions, deploy medical services, and negotiate abroad through relatively integrated bodies. Palestinian municipalities and village committees performed essential functions as well, but the national organizational structure had been badly damaged and received inconsistent external support. The disparity helps explain later outcomes; it does not imply that the outcomes were predetermined or that civilian losses were a simple reflection of administrative efficiency.

Section 124.8 · Division II

Violence, Emergency Regulations, and Britain's Loss of Authority

Armed Jewish resistance to British rule began in earnest in late 1945. On the night of 31 October to 1 November the Jewish Resistance Movement sabotaged railways, coastal vessels, and other targets in coordinated attacks. In June 1946 the Palmach destroyed bridges linking Palestine to neighboring countries in what became known as the Night of the Bridges (16–17 June). The British responded on 29 June in Operation Agatha, sometimes called Black Sabbath, in which troops arrested about 2,700 people, including prominent Jewish Agency officials, and searched the Agency's headquarters for evidence linking the Haganah to the attacks.

On 22 July 1946 the Irgun bombed the King David Hotel in Jerusalem, which housed the Mandate Secretariat and part of the British military headquarters. A bomb concealed in milk churns in the basement exploded, causing a large part of the south wing to collapse. Approximately 91 people were killed, including British, Arab, and Jewish employees and visitors, and dozens more were wounded. The Irgun said it had telephoned warnings; British officials disputed this or said the warnings were inadequate, and the sequence of events remains debated. The Jewish Agency condemned the attack, though documents later showed that the Haganah leadership had been aware of the plan.

The British response combined security operations and legal measures. Under Defence (Emergency) Regulations, the authorities imposed curfews, conducted cordon-and-search operations, used military courts, and detained suspects without trial. Neighborhoods in Jerusalem and other towns were fenced into fortified security zones, nicknamed “Bevingrads.” In March 1947 martial law was proclaimed in parts of the Tel Aviv area. Executions and floggings of Irgun members led to reprisals; in July 1947, after three Irgun members were executed, the Irgun hanged two British sergeants, Clifford Martin and Mervyn Paice, and their bodies were found hidden in a grove. The killings provoked anti-Jewish riots in several British cities and hardened opinion in Britain against the Jewish insurgents. In May 1947, the Irgun had breached the walls of Acre prison and freed prisoners.

These events had political consequences beyond the casualties. They eroded the Mandate government's authority by demonstrating that it could not protect its own headquarters or maintain control without heavy force. They also raised the cost to Britain, whose troops numbered on the order of 100,000, in money, in lives, and in domestic politics. They damaged the reputation of the Jewish cause among some British and American observers, even as others saw British repression and the detention of refugees as discrediting British policy. And they shifted attention from the justice of the White Paper to the question of order.

The violence described here was primarily directed at British institutions, though Arabs and Jews were among the victims. It should be distinguished from the Arab-Jewish civil war that began after the partition vote, treated in Section 124.17. Palestinian Arab leaders, for the most part, did not conduct a comparable armed campaign against Britain in 1945–1947, in part because of the damage of the earlier revolt. This changed in late 1947, when Arab-Jewish violence became the dominant pattern. Civilians experienced these months as curfews, searches, bombings, and fear, whatever their community.

The struggle against British rule unfolded in an international atmosphere deeply affected by the war. Jewish armed activists expected that attacks on communications, installations, and symbols of government would make the Mandate more expensive to administer and persuade London that continued rule was unsustainable. British officers believed that tolerating such attacks would encourage wider challenges throughout the empire. This conflict made railways, bridges, police posts, prisons, and government buildings strategic targets. It also turned ordinary journeys and workdays into potential emergencies for civilians and employees who had no role in directing policy.

Operations on the night later remembered as the Night of the Bridges in June 1946 illustrated the importance attached to transport infrastructure. Attacking bridges linking Palestine to neighboring territories served military and symbolic purposes: it could impede British movement while signaling that insurgents could disrupt an imperial network. The British mass arrests known as Operation Agatha, or Black Sabbath, followed in late June, targeting Jewish Agency offices and political figures as well as arms caches. These actions intensified an argument among Zionist leaders about whether armed pressure was helping their diplomacy or jeopardizing sympathetic international opinion. Their security dilemma was different from that of British authorities, but it was no less real.

The King David Hotel attack in July should be taught with precision. The Irgun planted explosives in the wing used by British administrative and military offices; the explosion killed 91 people, including Arabs, Jews, and Britons. Disputes about warnings and responsibility were central to subsequent political arguments, but they do not change the fact that a large number of people died in an attack on an occupied building. British authorities treated the bombing as terrorism, and many Jewish political figures publicly condemned it. Historians must distinguish the organization's stated military objective from the action's effects on employees, visitors, and other civilians.

Security operations further strained relations between the government and the communities it ruled. Broad emergency powers allowed detention without ordinary criminal proceedings, house searches, movement restrictions, and censorship. Such measures sometimes disrupted underground operations, but they also created resentment and helped portray imperial government as increasingly coercive. British officials were not simply unwilling to enforce law; they faced active resistance, uncertain intelligence, and political instructions that shifted as London reconsidered its future commitments. These conditions did not make abuses inevitable, and evidence about particular incidents must be assessed individually.

The cycle of violence also distorted wider political debate. Newspapers abroad could focus on spectacular bombings and executions while paying less attention to discussions about citizenship, agriculture, municipal finance, and the prospects of Arab-Jewish power sharing. In Palestine, anxiety about retaliation reinforced communal separation. Businesses reconsidered deliveries across neighborhoods; bus routes and public events required precautions; people who spoke both Arabic and Hebrew could find their social position increasingly difficult. Before civil war began in late 1947, the struggle over British authority had already degraded the everyday trust upon which a peaceful constitutional transition would have depended.

Section 124.9 · Division III — International Diplomacy and the Search for a Settlement

The United States, Britain, and the Anglo-American Committee of Inquiry

Palestine became an Anglo-American problem because the refugee question was inseparable from American public and political opinion. President Harry Truman, who succeeded Franklin Roosevelt in April 1945, was moved by the Harrison Report and by pressure from Jewish organizations, members of Congress, and some of his advisers, while the State Department and Pentagon worried about Arab and Muslim opinion, oil, and the Soviet Union. On 31 August 1945 Truman wrote to Prime Minister Attlee urging the admission of 100,000 Jewish displaced persons to Palestine. Attlee and Bevin replied that the issue could not be separated from the wider Palestine problem and that Arab reactions had to be considered.

On 13 November 1945 Bevin announced in the House of Commons that Britain and the United States would form a joint committee to examine the situation of Jews in Europe and to make recommendations on Palestine. The Anglo-American Committee of Inquiry, with six American and six British members and co-chairs Judge Joseph Hutcheson and Sir John Singleton, took evidence in Washington and London, visited displaced-persons camps in Europe, and heard witnesses in Cairo and Palestine. Its report, signed in April 1946, was unanimous.

Truman publicly welcomed the call for 100,000 certificates but did not endorse the rest of the report. Attlee told Parliament that the transfer of such a number could not proceed until the illegal armies were disarmed and a plan for the cost and security was worked out. Truman thus emphasized the humanitarian element and Britain emphasized security and order. The episode illustrates how the two governments, allied elsewhere, treated Palestine as a field of different priorities: Washington responded to domestic opinion and refugee relief; London, to the realities of governing Palestine and managing relations with the Arab world. The failure of the committee to produce a joint program set the pattern for the following year.

The Anglo-American Committee of Inquiry was an institutional response to a problem neither government could solve alone. American policymakers confronted pressure from Jewish organizations, public outrage over the Holocaust, and demands for a humanitarian response. Britain controlled immigration into Palestine and carried the costs of maintaining order; Washington could influence policy but did not administer the territory. The committee therefore combined an investigation of European refugee conditions with an examination of Palestine's political, social, and economic circumstances. Its mandate was unusual because conditions on the European continent and the constitutional future of a Middle Eastern territory were formally linked in one inquiry.

Committee members visited displaced-persons centers, heard testimony from Zionist and Arab representatives, and examined economic claims about Palestine's capacity to absorb immigrants. They found no consensus about the ultimate political settlement. Their recommendation for the admission of 100,000 Jewish survivors was strikingly clear; their opposition to the immediate establishment of either an Arab or a Jewish state reflected an attempt to avoid turning humanitarian relief into a final constitutional ruling. They also urged measures against inflammatory propaganda and called for safeguards against dispossession. One can understand these recommendations only by reading the report as a package rather than extracting the immigration number as its sole purpose.

For many Zionists, the 100,000 figure represented a welcome recognition of urgent needs but not an adequate substitute for sovereign control over further admission. For Palestinian Arab representatives, the recommendation threatened to change the demographic balance even while the report formally rejected immediate Jewish statehood. The committee attempted to reconcile these positions through continued government and gradual development, but that approach depended upon a degree of cooperation that its own hearings showed to be absent. Each side worried that provisional arrangements would become permanent in a way that favored the other.

The dispute between London and Washington was also about responsibility. American leaders could advocate substantial immigration without having to deploy troops to protect ports, roads, and settlements. British officials asked whether the United States was willing to share the fiscal and military burdens of implementation. Truman and his advisers did not speak with one voice: humanitarian arguments, electoral calculations, strategic planning, and concern for Arab relations competed within the administration. The public difference between the two allies revealed that international sympathy did not automatically translate into an agreed operational program.

The committee's report became a starting point for further diplomacy rather than a settlement. It showed that an Anglo-American investigation could make recommendations but lacked authority to create institutions, impose boundaries, or ensure acceptance. This limitation foreshadowed the United Nations crisis: external bodies could clarify alternatives and add legitimacy to a proposal, but implementation still required force, political consent, or both. The unresolved question was who would be responsible when neither community accepted an arrangement and the mandatory government no longer wished to remain. Sources: 1

Section 124.10 · Division III

Failed Negotiations and Britain's Decision to Refer Palestine to the United Nations

After the Anglo-American Committee, Britain and the United States tried again. In July 1946 a joint cabinet committee, with Herbert Morrison for Britain and Henry Grady for the United States, devised a plan of provincial autonomy: Palestine would be divided into Arab and Jewish provinces with extensive local powers, under a central government retaining British control over defense, foreign affairs, and Jerusalem and the Negev, and 100,000 Jewish immigrants would be admitted over a period of time. Neither Jews nor Arabs accepted. Zionists regarded it as a denial of statehood; Arab leaders rejected the continued immigration and the denial of independence. President Truman, under domestic pressure, declined to support it, and in a statement on the eve of Yom Kippur in October 1946 he expressed sympathy for a viable Jewish state in an adequate area, a statement that dismayed the British.

London then convened conferences with delegations from the Arab states and, at times, Palestinian Arab representatives, while the Jewish Agency, whose leaders had been arrested in Operation Agatha and whose Congress rejected participation at first, remained largely absent from the early sessions. Arab proposals envisaged an independent unitary Palestine. In early 1947 Bevin put forward a plan for five years of British trusteeship leading to independence. Both sides rejected it.

On 2 April 1947 Britain asked the UN Secretary-General to place the question on the agenda of the General Assembly. A special session met from 28 April to 15 May and created a committee of inquiry, UNSCOP. The Arab states proposed that the Assembly consider ending the Mandate and declaring Palestine independent, but this was not adopted as the agenda item.

The Morrison–Grady plan illustrates a constitutional approach that is often misdescribed. It did not propose an immediately independent federation of equal constituent states. Instead, it envisaged provincial autonomy in predominantly Arab and Jewish areas while retaining decisive central powers under British authority, with Jerusalem and the Negev kept under direct central control. Officials hoped provincial governments would give both communities some control over local affairs while postponing the hardest question of sovereignty. The planned admission of Jewish refugees would be supervised within this continuing imperial framework. That arrangement differed sharply from the UNSCOP minority proposal of an independent federal Palestine, even though both used provinces or subnational political units.

The plan failed because it asked both national movements to trust a temporary arrangement whose future direction they feared. Arab leaders objected to continuing Jewish immigration and refused a form of government that denied the immediate sovereignty of the majority. Zionist leaders rejected provisions that left ultimate authority and immigration control outside Jewish hands and preserved large regions under central administration. The British also failed to secure firm support from the Truman administration. A compromise can be technically elaborate yet politically unviable when its concessions affect the very issues each party regards as non-negotiable.

The London conferences of 1946–1947 exposed how difficult formal negotiations had become. Delegations disagreed about who represented Palestine, whether to speak with the Jewish Agency directly, and which constitutional proposals might even be discussed. Arab governments could support Palestinian national claims in public while privately disputing priorities. The Jewish Agency retained a strong international apparatus but was weakened by arrests and tensions over armed insurgency. British mediators occupied a difficult position: they were expected to bring the sides together while their own authority and policies were contested. Meetings could occur without producing a negotiating framework recognized as legitimate by all parties.

By February 1947 Britain had decided that further bilateral talks were unlikely to resolve the dispute. Referral to the United Nations served several purposes at once. It offered a public acknowledgment of failure, moved responsibility toward a wider international body, and allowed London to evaluate eventual withdrawal without immediately choosing the terms of independence. It did not transfer sovereignty or terminate the Mandate on the day of referral. Britain continued to police the territory and regulate immigration while the United Nations organized an inquiry. Students should distinguish the diplomatic decision to internationalize the issue from the later legal date on which British responsibility ended.

There was a deeper institutional irony. The League of Nations had entrusted Britain with the Mandate, but its successor organization was being asked to design a settlement at precisely the moment when British consent to enforce that settlement was uncertain. The United Nations possessed a Charter and an expanding diplomatic role, yet no independent army and no straightforward means of compelling the parties to accept its proposals. The history of 1947 is therefore a test of the limits of a new international organization as much as the history of an exhausted colonial administration. The problem did not disappear because it moved from London to New York. Sources: 1, 2

Historical Debate

Why Did Britain Refer Palestine to the United Nations?

The question: Was the referral an admission of failure, a tactic to shift responsibility, or an attempt to secure a favorable outcome?

Interpretation A — Exhaustion and inability to find a solution: Some historians emphasize that Britain faced insurgency, financial strain, American pressure, and the rejection of every proposal, and that referral was the only remaining option.
Interpretation B — A maneuver: Others argue that Bevin expected the UN to fail to agree on a plan, leaving Britain free to retain a role or at least to withdraw without blame, and that British officials hoped to preserve influence with Arab states.
Interpretation C — Imperial retrenchment: A broader view places the decision within Britain's wider withdrawal, including its departure from India in August 1947, and sees Palestine as a burden to be shed as part of a reassessment of imperial commitments.

Evidence: Evidence includes cabinet minutes, Foreign Office memoranda, and statements by Bevin, Attlee, and Colonial Secretary Arthur Creech Jones. Historians such as Wm. Roger Louis have examined British policy in the postwar Middle East in detail, and interpretations vary according to the weight given to each document.

Why it matters: The debate affects how one judges British responsibility for the later violence and whether Britain acted to facilitate or merely to escape.

Student task: Evaluate which interpretation best explains Britain's refusal to enforce partition in 1947–1948.

Section 124.11 · Division III

UNSCOP and the Competing Plans for Palestine

The United Nations Special Committee on Palestine was created on 15 May 1947. It consisted of eleven states: Australia, Canada, Czechoslovakia, Guatemala, India, Iran, the Netherlands, Peru, Sweden, Uruguay, and Yugoslavia. The great powers were excluded to avoid the appearance of bias. The chairman was Emil Sandström of Sweden. The Committee visited Palestine from mid-June to July 1947, toured towns and settlements, heard British officials and Jewish Agency representatives, and visited Lebanon, where it met Arab League officials and Arab governments. The Arab Higher Committee boycotted UNSCOP, arguing that the question of independence was not a matter for inquiry. The Committee later visited displaced-persons camps in Germany and Austria and drafted its report in Geneva.

The majority plan envisaged a Jewish state including eastern Galilee, the coastal plain from Haifa southward, and much of the Negev, and an Arab state including western Galilee, the hill country, and part of the southern coast, with Jerusalem under UN administration and Jaffa as an Arab enclave. The UNSCOP plan assigned the Jewish state a larger share of the land than the final version adopted by the Assembly. An important feature was that the two states would be linked by an economic union, an arrangement meant to recognize the interdependence of the economies. The plan envisaged a transitional period during which Jewish immigration would continue, with a figure of 150,000 over two years proposed for the Jewish area.

The minority plan would have created a federal state with a Jewish state and an Arab state within it, a unitary capital in Jerusalem, and a transitional period of three years. Its authors believed it would respect Arab claims to sovereignty over the whole while permitting Jewish self-government in a portion. The principles invoked in both plans included national self-determination, demographic reality, and the protection of refugees; they were not easy to reconcile, because the Arab majority claimed the whole country as its own, while the Jewish minority claimed a right to sovereignty in at least part of it.

UNSCOP's membership was deliberately drawn from countries that were not the principal wartime powers or the principal neighboring governments. This design was intended to give the inquiry a degree of independence, but independence of membership did not remove the unequal access of the contending parties. Zionist institutions prepared detailed evidence, escorts, and programs for committee visits. Palestinian Arab leadership largely boycotted official cooperation, contending that the Committee's mandate presupposed a question of whether an Arab-majority country could be divided rather than recognizing its right to independence. Committee members still encountered Palestinian society and Arab arguments, but the absence of equivalent official testimony affected the inquiry's public record. Neither a boycott nor extensive participation can be evaluated without asking what each side hoped the committee would recognize as legitimate.

The visit involved more than hearings in Jerusalem. Representatives examined farms, industries, towns, religious sites, and administrative records; members also traveled to Europe to inspect the displaced-persons situation. This mobility reinforced the connection between postwar refugee emergency and Palestine's constitutional future. It also produced conflicting impressions. Some members were impressed by the Yishuv's organized infrastructure and capacity to receive immigrants. Others stressed the status of the Arab majority and the dangers of dividing mixed economic and geographic systems. A report that reflects these different emphases should not be treated as if its authors had discovered a single indisputable technical answer.

The majority proposal for partition emerged from the judgment that competing demands for national self-government were too far apart to reconcile within a unitary state. It attempted to secure a Jewish state with room for immigration, an Arab state reflecting Palestinian national claims, and an international Jerusalem to protect religious interests. Economic union was not an ornamental afterthought: it was a practical response to overlapping railways, ports, currency, customs, and water systems. In a densely interconnected territory, diplomatic separation would otherwise have threatened the daily operation of commerce and public services. The majority plan thus combined political division with economic integration, a tension that became acute once armed violence expanded.

The minority federal proposal, supported by representatives of India, Iran, and Yugoslavia, sought a different balance between national autonomy and common citizenship. It envisaged a central authority with Arab and Jewish constituent units and safeguards for communal interests, aiming to avoid the transfer of mixed populations into competing exclusive sovereignties. Advocates believed this would better acknowledge Palestinian majority rights and the common territorial economy. Critics questioned whether such a federation could work when the parties had not agreed on immigration, security, the distribution of power, or the legitimacy of a common state. The minority proposal was a genuine alternative, not a simple refusal to confront the problem.

UNSCOP's report was submitted on 31 August 1947, but it did not settle the debate. It set out models for the General Assembly, whose committees then revised the majority proposal. The report's population tables depended on figures and classifications assembled under the Mandate, including categories of settled residents and Bedouin populations that required separate accounting. The evidence was therefore administrative as well as political. Readers should distinguish what surveyors and statistical officers claimed to have measured, what committee members inferred from those measurements, and what the governments of proposed states would actually have had authority to do. Maps of a recommended arrangement were not records of existing sovereign borders. Sources: 1

Section 124.12 · Division III

The United States, Soviet Union, Arab States, and International Opinion

The great powers, absent from UNSCOP, were decisive in the General Assembly. President Truman supported partition in the autumn of 1947, motivated by sympathy for Jewish refugees, domestic political pressure, and the belief that a settlement was necessary, though officials in the State Department and the military worried that partition could inflame the Arab world, jeopardize oil interests, and require American troops. The American position was therefore supportive but divided, and it was not accompanied by a commitment to enforcement.

The Soviet Union surprised many observers. In a speech on 14 May 1947 at the special session, Soviet representative Andrei Gromyko emphasized the suffering of the Jewish people during the war and the failure of Western European states to protect them, and said that if a single binational state proved impossible, partition should be considered.

The Arab states opposed partition. Egypt, Iraq, Syria, Lebanon, Saudi Arabia, Yemen, and Transjordan were members of the Arab League, founded in March 1945, which coordinated policy on Palestine. Meetings at Bludan in Syria in June 1946 and later in Cairo emphasized rejecting partition and preparing to assist the Palestinians. Governments differed in motive and capacity. Egypt, the largest, sought leadership of the Arab world; Iraq and Transjordan, ruled by Hashemite monarchs, competed with Egypt and Saudi Arabia; Syria and Lebanon, newly independent, faced fragile politics; and Saudi Arabia, with strong American ties, objected but did not prepare for war. Popular sentiment in the Arab world was strongly sympathetic to the Palestinians and hostile to partition, and the Palestinian cause became an arena of domestic politics, which constrained governments.

Governments were also militarily weak and politically divided. King Abdullah of Transjordan, whose Arab Legion was the most effective Arab force and was commanded by the British officer John Bagot Glubb, publicly opposed partition but had long envisaged annexing the Arab portion of Palestine; he held private meetings with Zionist representatives, including Golda Meyerson (Meir) on 17 November 1947. These contacts show that the divide was not simply between a united Arab camp and a united Zionist camp. Each side contained rival interests and strategies.

American support for partition was shaped by competing forces within the Truman administration. The president responded to the plight of Jewish survivors and to a political campaign conducted by Zionist and sympathetic American organizations. State Department and military officials, however, feared that an imposed settlement would endanger relations with Arab governments, expose Western interests to instability, and make commitments that the United States was unwilling to enforce. The presence of these disagreements is important: a favorable American vote did not mean that every branch of government had adopted a coherent plan for administering Palestine or guaranteeing its borders. Washington's changing position in early 1948 would expose this gap between diplomatic approval and practical commitment.

The Soviet calculation was different. Andrei Gromyko recognized the severity of Jewish suffering in Europe and discussed both a shared Arab-Jewish state and, if coexistence could not be achieved, partition. Moscow also had reasons to welcome the weakening of British influence in the eastern Mediterranean. Support for partition did not imply agreement with American policy across the region. Soviet diplomacy could combine an argument about Jewish self-determination with a strategic expectation that decolonization would unsettle an imperial rival. Later Czechoslovak arms transfers to Jewish forces occurred within this broader international environment, but Soviet support should not be described as a simple undertaking to determine every military event in Palestine.

Arab governments shared a public rejection of partition, but their goals were not interchangeable. Egypt worried about Palestine's proximity and domestic political opinion; Syria and Lebanon saw the issue through security and national solidarity; Iraq had its own regional ambitions and military constraints. Transjordan's King Abdullah pursued a dynastic and territorial strategy that could bring him into conflict with Palestinian independent-state advocates as well as with other Arab rulers. The Arab League provided a forum for common declarations and some military coordination, yet political distrust repeatedly impeded joint planning. Arab public opinion was neither irrelevant nor a substitute for cabinet decisions, military logistics, and disagreement over the future of Palestinian institutions.

Political debate also extended well beyond governments. Jewish communities in the United States, Europe, and the Middle East disagreed about immigration, statehood, and the safety of minorities. Arab newspapers and associations argued that the same United Nations that endorsed national self-determination elsewhere could not legitimately allocate territory without its inhabitants' consent. Latin American governments received sustained diplomatic approaches and reached differing decisions based on foreign policy interests, domestic constituencies, and judgments about humanitarian need. The result was not a simple regional contest between 'West' and 'East': the General Assembly vote was assembled from a diverse coalition that did not share a single rationale.

Diplomatic conversations were conducted under severe asymmetries. The Jewish Agency had offices, representatives, funds, and access to politicians in major capitals. Palestinian leaders had longstanding claims and local legitimacy but suffered from organizational fragmentation and dependence on governments whose priorities varied. British representatives controlled existing administrative information yet increasingly disclaimed the obligation to impose whatever settlement the UN might approve. Each actor therefore entered the international arena with a different mixture of authority and constraint. Understanding the partition vote requires studying who could speak, who could promise implementation, and who would bear the consequences if promises failed. Sources: 1

Section 124.13 · Division IV — The United Nations Partition Plan of 1947

Partition with Economic Union: Territory, Jerusalem, and Statehood

Resolution 181 (II) recommended the termination of the Mandate as soon as possible and no later than 1 August 1948, with the armed forces of the mandatory power withdrawing in stages; the creation of independent Arab and Jewish states, to come into existence two months after the evacuation and no later than 1 October 1948; and a special international regime for the city of Jerusalem under the UN Trusteeship Council. A five-member UN Palestine Commission (Bolivia, Czechoslovakia, Denmark, Panama, and the Philippines) would supervise the transition and the establishment of provisional councils of government.

The territorial provisions assigned the Jewish state about 55 to 56 percent of Mandate Palestine, comprising eastern Galilee, the coastal plain from near Haifa to south of Tel Aviv, and most of the Negev, including access to the Gulf of Aqaba. The Arab state received western Galilee with Acre, the hill country of Samaria and Judea, and a coastal strip from south of Ashdod to the Egyptian border, including Gaza and part of the Negev near Egypt. Beersheba lay in the Arab state. Jaffa, a predominantly Arab city, was to be an enclave within the Jewish state. Each state would consist of three principal segments joined at points of contact, which required special arrangements for transit. Jerusalem, together with Bethlehem and surrounding villages, was designated a corpus separatum, to be governed by the UN for ten years, after which residents would vote on its future.

The economic union provisions were extensive. The two states and Jerusalem would form a customs union with a common currency, joint operation of railways, interstate highways, postal, telephone, and telegraph services, ports, and irrigation, and a Joint Economic Board of representatives of each state and three foreign members appointed by the UN. The Plan required each state to adopt a constitution guaranteeing equal rights regardless of religion, language, or sex, protecting holy places, and ensuring that expropriation of property would involve compensation. Residents of each state would become its citizens, with a right to opt for citizenship of the other within a year, and the document specified guarantees concerning minorities.

The population figures require precise definitions. The UNSCOP majority report estimated that the proposed Jewish state included 498,000 Jews and 407,000 settled Arabs and others, with approximately 90,000 additional Bedouins identified separately. The proposed Arab state contained 10,000 Jews and 725,000 Arabs and others; Jerusalem contained 100,000 Jews and 105,000 Arabs and others. These are the committee’s late-1946-based estimates for its majority proposal, not a census of sovereign states, and the final General Assembly boundaries differed in some respects.

The plan's geographical logic needs to be examined at the level of districts rather than through two solid colors on a classroom map. The proposed Jewish state combined the coastal region including Tel Aviv and Haifa, eastern Galilee, and much of the Negev. The proposed Arab state occupied much of the central hill country, western Galilee, and the Gaza coastal area, while the predominantly Arab municipality of Jaffa was designated a separate Arab enclave. Jerusalem and its surrounding area, including Bethlehem, would be administered under an international regime. These territories were not straightforward contiguous blocks, and access between them depended upon transit arrangements that made the proposed borders particularly vulnerable to conflict.

Land area, land use, and land ownership must not be conflated. The proposed Jewish state received a majority of the territory, often approximated as 55 to 56 percent, but much of that land lay in arid southern districts, including extensive Bedouin grazing areas. The Negev was therefore neither 'empty' land nor agricultural land of the same productive character as the irrigated coastal plain. Palestinian Arab objections drew attention to the fact that Jewish-owned land comprised only a small fraction of Palestine's total area. Zionist supporters emphasized plans for settlement and development as well as the anticipated need to admit refugees. Neither a map of land ownership nor a comparison of square kilometers settles the question of sovereign entitlement by itself, but each clarifies what the protagonists considered unjust or necessary.

Jerusalem created a distinct challenge. Its religious importance extended to Jews, Christians, and Muslims internationally, and its population contained mixed and separated neighborhoods with a long history of interdependence and tension. A special regime was intended to protect access to holy places and avoid exclusive control by one national state. Yet the city also depended upon roads through surrounding districts and had large populations whose political preferences diverged. The proposed international administration would need police, revenue, housing policy, and a secure supply network; calling the city a corpus separatum did not create those capabilities. A plan that could not maintain access to Jerusalem risked failure even before broader state institutions were established.

Economic union tried to reconcile politically separate governments with shared material systems. A Joint Economic Board, customs and currency cooperation, transport services, and provisions for revenue redistribution would have required daily negotiation. Haifa's harbor and refinery, Jaffa's citrus trade, rail links, electrical infrastructure, and water schemes could not be divided neatly along political lines without significant disruption. The plan recognized the problem but assumed that institutions could be built under UN oversight during a short and contested transition. Its sophistication was therefore also its vulnerability: the more services that had to be shared, the more damaging it became when neither side trusted the other and security deteriorated. Section 124.14 · Division IV

Zionist Acceptance, Palestinian Rejection, and Competing Arguments

The Jewish Agency accepted partition in principle, while voicing reservations about the boundaries, particularly the status of Jerusalem, whose Jewish population would be outside the Jewish state, and the exclusion of western Galilee. For Ben-Gurion and the mainstream leadership, the key was that the Plan recognized a Jewish state with control of immigration and a territory that included the coastal plain and the Negev. Many leaders regarded the borders as provisional and expected to have to defend them. The Irgun and Lehi rejected partition on principle, claiming the entire land, while the left-wing and binationalist groups had divided views.

The Arab Higher Committee and the Arab governments rejected partition entirely. Their arguments combined principle and practicality. They contended that the Arab majority had a right to self-determination in the whole of Palestine; that the UN had no authority to partition a country against the will of its majority; that the Jewish minority, which owned a small share of the land, was being allocated a majority of the territory; and that partition would place hundreds of thousands of Arabs under Jewish rule. Some also argued that Palestinians should not bear the cost of a European crime. The Arab states' alternative was a unitary state with minority protections.

Supporters of partition replied that the competing claims could not be reconciled in a single state without perpetual conflict; that Jewish refugees required a haven; that the Negev, a large share of the allocated land, was sparsely populated; and that the Plan guaranteed minority rights. Both sets of arguments rested on genuine principles, and each side could cite the right of self-determination while defining the relevant unit differently.

Reactions spread across the region. In several Arab countries, Jewish communities faced violence after the vote, including riots in Aleppo and Aden in December 1947 that killed dozens and destroyed property. Earlier, in November 1945, anti-Jewish riots in Cairo and Tripoli had occurred. These events, discussed more fully in Chapter 126, show how the Palestine conflict reverberated in the lives of Jews in the Arab world, whose communities were distinct from European Jews and the Yishuv.

The Jewish Agency's acceptance was neither unqualified enthusiasm for every boundary nor an embrace of all the plan's obligations in perpetuity. Mainstream Zionist leaders welcomed international recognition of the principle of Jewish statehood and judged that accepting an imperfect proposal offered an opportunity that rejection might destroy. At the same time they disliked the internationalization of Jerusalem, debated access to some settlements, and considered how future immigration would transform the proposed state's demographic profile. The relationship between tactical acceptance of a diplomatic plan and subsequent territorial decisions should be investigated rather than assumed. Acceptance of a recommendation was a political act; compliance with every boundary would have required developments that did not occur.

Palestinian opposition likewise cannot be understood only as hostility to Jewish national aspirations. Representatives argued that the inhabitants of Palestine, most of whom were Arabs, should determine their country's political future under the principle of majority self-government. They objected that an international assembly whose membership did not include an independent Palestinian state had recommended transferring sovereignty over substantial Arab-populated areas to a Jewish state. Many Palestinian families also feared that boundaries would affect homes, land, employment, and legal status. Political leaders opposed additional immigration under circumstances in which they lacked control over the country's future. These arguments should be documented even where they were expressed through diplomatic boycotts or uncompromising public language.

Supporters of partition responded that a single majoritarian state could deny Jews national rights and leave survivors dependent on authorities they did not trust. They argued that international recognition of a national home and decades of settlement had created a Jewish political community with claims of its own. For them, the prospect of a Jewish state was inseparable from control of immigration and defense. Palestinian spokespeople challenged the conclusion that humanitarian responsibility for European refugees entitled other governments to divide their country. Both positions turned on different understandings of the relationship between individual rights, collective national rights, and the authority of international organizations to settle disputes.

The debate included alternatives that were politically serious but lacked a winning coalition. A democratic unitary Palestine, a federal state, provincial autonomy under trusteeship, and binational power sharing each implied different answers to questions of representation and immigration. In the abstract, compromise schemes could distribute offices, ministries, and voting rights. In practice, each national movement feared that arrangements called temporary would put it at permanent disadvantage. The lack of a trusted coercive or constitutional authority to guarantee mutual rights was as consequential as the ideological distance between the proposals. National self-determination was invoked by multiple parties, but not in forms that could readily coexist within a single territory.

Regional effects quickly made the dispute more dangerous. Demonstrations and violence against Jewish communities in some Arab countries after the partition vote are historical events in their own right; they cannot be excused as inevitable expressions of politics, nor can they be treated as evidence that every Arab government followed the same policy. Jewish residents of Aden, Aleppo, Baghdad, Cairo, and elsewhere lived in societies with distinct political and legal histories, and their experiences should not be collapsed into those of European refugees. At the same time, attacks on Jews outside Palestine intensified many Zionists' conviction that only sovereign refuge could ensure security. Thus an international vote about one territory sharpened anxieties and divisions across the broader Middle East. Sources: 1, 2

The question: Was the Partition Plan a reasonable compromise, or did it favor one side, and could any alternative have worked?

Interpretation A — A reasonable compromise: Defenders argue that the plan responded to the irreconcilable claims of two national movements, that the Negev allocation reflected future immigration needs, and that no alternative would have secured agreement.

Section 124.15 · Division IV

The UN General Assembly Vote of 29 November 1947

The General Assembly considered the UNSCOP report in an Ad Hoc Committee chaired by Herbert Evatt of Australia, which set up subcommittees on the partition plan and on the Arab proposal for a unitary state. The partition plan was revised, shifting Beersheba and some of the Negev to the Arab state and making Jaffa an Arab enclave. Supporters needed a two-thirds majority of those voting in the plenary, and the vote was delayed by a few days while sponsors sought to secure it. Both Zionist representatives and American officials lobbied delegations, and some delegates later complained of pressure. Historians weigh these pressures alongside the independent calculations of the governments concerned.

On 29 November 1947, at Flushing Meadows, New York, the Assembly adopted Resolution 181 (II) by 33 votes to 13, with 10 abstentions and one member absent. Those in favor included the United States, the Soviet Union, France, Poland, Czechoslovakia, and a number of Latin American states. Those against included the Arab states and several Asian states, including India, Iran, Pakistan, Turkey, and Afghanistan, as well as Greece and Cuba. The abstainers included the United Kingdom, China, Argentina, Mexico, Ethiopia, and Yugoslavia. In Jewish communities there were celebrations; in Arab communities, mourning and anger, and the Arab Higher Committee called a three-day general strike.

The vote had great moral and political significance. It gave international sanction to the idea of a Jewish state and later served as an element of the legal argument for Israel's legitimacy. But a General Assembly resolution is a recommendation, and the Assembly had no armed forces. The resolution asked the Security Council to implement it, but the permanent members had not agreed on enforcement, and Britain, the mandatory power, had said it would not impose a settlement. International endorsement was therefore not the same as enforceable political control.

The General Assembly's Ad Hoc Committee on the Palestinian Question was more than a formal stage before an expected result. Delegates debated the committee reports, legal issues about the UN's authority, proposed amendments, the question of how territory would be administered, and the prospects of protecting minority rights. Supporters of partition sought a two-thirds majority among votes cast, excluding abstentions. Opponents sought either an alternative constitutional plan or a postponement. Delegations from smaller and medium-sized states thus held leverage disproportionate to their geographic distance from Palestine. Their votes mattered because international recognition depended on procedures as well as the arguments advanced by the major powers.

A first scheduled vote was delayed while supporters lobbied wavering delegations. The final roll call on 29 November included 33 votes in favor, 13 against, and 10 abstentions, with one delegation absent. Claims about pressure exerted on particular countries have been part of later polemic, but the existence of lobbying is not itself proof that the vote was invalid. Historical scrutiny asks which governments changed positions, what reasons they gave, and whether contemporary diplomatic records substantiate specific accusations of coercion. A responsible textbook can discuss vigorous diplomacy without assuming that all small-country choices were purchased or imposed.

The United States and Soviet Union voted on the same side despite being rivals in the emerging Cold War. Their convergence was limited to a particular proposal and rested on different strategic assumptions. Britain abstained, signaling the gap between the state that still governed the territory and those that urged its partition. India and Iran supported a federal alternative and opposed the adopted plan; Turkey also voted against partition. The vote therefore cut across simple categories such as capitalist versus communist, European versus non-European, or colonial versus anticolonial. National interests, refugee politics, and conceptions of self-determination produced a coalition that was real but fragile.

Public reactions revealed that passage in New York did not translate into consent on the ground. Crowds in Jewish communities celebrated the prospect of sovereignty, while Palestinian Arab communities protested a decision they regarded as an imposed division of their country. Arab leaders called a strike, which became part of the sequence in which street clashes and armed attacks multiplied. Feelings of relief and fear could coexist within each population: a Jewish family might celebrate prospective refuge while worrying about nearby Arab hostility, and a Palestinian household could reject partition while fearing the risks of open conflict. The news arrived into already polarized political institutions and fragile neighborhoods.

Resolution 181 recommended a political process. It did not instantly terminate the Mandate, grant automatic admission to the United Nations, establish two administrations, or provide a force to ensure the transfer of authority. The Security Council was asked to take necessary steps, but the legal and operational gap between endorsement and implementation remained enormous. This distinction is fundamental for interpreting the following months. Students should learn to separate the date of an international vote from the date when institutions actually govern territory, and to ask what happens when an international design depends upon actors who either oppose it or refuse to enforce it. Section 124.16 · Division IV

The Practical and Political Obstacles to Partition

Adopting a partition resolution did not create two functioning states. The territory the Plan divided was not demographically or economically separable. Jerusalem, Haifa, Jaffa, Tiberias, Safed, and Acre had mixed or adjoining populations. In the countryside, Jewish settlements, including isolated ones such as the Etzion bloc south of Jerusalem and a series of outposts in the Negev, lay within territory assigned to the Arab state or on routes that crossed Arab areas, while hundreds of Arab villages lay within the Jewish state. The lines on the map cut across the settlement pattern.

The Plan also relied on economic interdependence. The citrus industry of Jaffa, the port and refinery of Haifa, the railways, the currency, the banks, and the postal and telephone systems were integrated. A customs union and joint economic board might have maintained this, but only with cooperation that neither the Arab leadership nor, in some respects, the Jewish leadership was prepared to give. The administration of the Mandate, staffed largely by British officials with Arab and Jewish subordinates, would disappear with withdrawal, and neither community had an institutional replacement for the whole country.

Security was the central problem. The Plan envisaged that the UN Palestine Commission would take control of the transition, and that each state would form a militia, but Britain refused to allow the Commission to enter or to transfer authority until it left. The Security Council had no force at its disposal, and the great powers were reluctant to commit troops. The Arab League declared it would resist. The Jewish Agency had an organized military and administrative framework, while the Palestinian Arab leadership did not. There was, in short, no agreed mechanism for peaceful implementation, and the plan's success depended on cooperation among actors who rejected it or doubted it.

Several structural features of the Mandate also shaped what followed. The police force, divided along communal lines, could not be relied on by either side. Arms were widely, if unevenly, distributed. The roads, particularly the route from the coast to Jerusalem, passed through Arab-populated hills, making Jewish Jerusalem vulnerable. And the demographic reality that the Jewish state would contain a large Arab minority meant that even if the Plan had been accepted, intercommunal tension would have been acute. These obstacles help explain why the diplomatic outcome of November 1947 turned so rapidly into violence.

The partition plan assumed that political boundaries could be translated into functioning institutions in an exceptionally short time. It required provisional councils, police and militias, financial administration, customs arrangements, and protection of religious sites. Yet Britain's existing administration remained in place until withdrawal, and it rejected the idea of using British forces to impose partition. The UN Palestine Commission could not simply occupy an office and inherit a working government: it needed access to officials, records, premises, and public cooperation. Without these, the creation of the proposed Arab and Jewish states was a constitutional aspiration rather than an executable administrative schedule.

The proposed geography multiplied the difficulties. Roads from the coastal plain to Jerusalem crossed areas with different populations and competing political objectives. Haifa served a mixed urban economy and an international harbor; Jaffa was an Arab port city adjacent to predominantly Jewish Tel Aviv; elsewhere villages and settlements were interspersed. Even if administrators had drawn an agreed boundary, laborers would have needed to travel to workplaces and farmers to markets. Commercial contracts, water access, irrigation, electricity, railway timetables, and public health services could not be suspended without consequences. The plan's economic union represented an attempt to solve this problem, but a shared economy requires stable enforcement and some measure of mutual confidence.

The protection of minority communities was an equally substantial undertaking. Resolution 181 envisaged constitutional guarantees for religious freedom, language, education, nondiscrimination, and access to holy places. These principles were especially important because hundreds of thousands of Arabs would reside in the proposed Jewish state and some Jews would reside in the proposed Arab state. Rights on paper cannot protect a family if nearby armed groups threaten it or if the local administration collapses. National leaders disagreed over whether international guarantees would have real force, and the absence of effective transitional authority made even well-designed clauses vulnerable. The rights of populations assigned against their wishes to new sovereignties became an urgent practical problem.

The proposed Jerusalem administration posed particularly demanding logistical questions. A UN authority would have had to police holy places, secure communications with the rest of Palestine, collect revenue, and negotiate access to food and fuel. The city was connected to the coastal plain by routes passing through contested towns and villages, and its population depended on regular deliveries. Security problems along a few kilometers of road could affect tens of thousands of civilians. Internationalization as an idea did not supply vehicles, escorts, trained police, or a reliable chain of command. As fighting intensified, the concept became progressively harder to realize.

By spring 1948 the central issue was no longer whether the plan contained a detailed institutional blueprint; it was whether any institution possessed the will and force to implement that blueprint. The Jewish leadership prepared to govern what it could hold. The Palestinian Arab leadership opposed partition but lacked the capacity to operate an alternative government across the territory. Britain prioritized withdrawal, and the Security Council did not provide a mechanism to enforce the General Assembly recommendation. These choices interacted. The failure of partition was neither a clerical oversight nor an inevitable result of geography alone, but the collapse of a political design whose essential conditions had not been secured. Section 124.17 · Division V — Civil War, British Withdrawal, and the End of the Mandate

The Outbreak of Arab-Jewish Civil War

Violence began within hours of the vote. On 30 November 1947, Arab gunmen attacked Jewish buses near Lydda and elsewhere, killing several passengers, and clashes spread. On 2 December, after the Arab strike, a crowd looted and burned shops in the Jewish commercial center of Jerusalem. Jewish groups retaliated; the Irgun and Lehi carried out bombings in Arab areas, and the Haganah conducted raids. On 30 December, an Irgun bomb thrown at Arab workers outside the Haifa oil refinery killed several, and Arab workers inside then killed dozens of Jewish employees; on 31 December, in reprisal, Haganah forces attacked the villages of Balad al-Shaykh and Hawassa, killing numerous villagers. On 4 January 1948, a Lehi bomb struck the Jaffa municipal building, killing and wounding many.

The fighting during these early months was largely irregular. On the Arab side, local committees and irregular units operated, including the Army of the Holy War organized around Abd al-Qadir al-Husayni in the Jerusalem area and Hasan Salama near Jaffa, and, from January 1948, the Arab Liberation Army, a volunteer force under Fawzi al-Qawuqji organized under Arab League auspices. They attacked roads and settlements and disrupted traffic to Jerusalem. On the Jewish side, the Haganah, the Palmach, and the other organizations defended settlements and convoys, escorted supplies, and conducted reprisals. Isolated bloc settlements and convoys suffered heavily; in January a party of thirty-five Haganah fighters was killed while trying to reach the Etzion bloc.

British forces remained responsible for order but intervened unevenly. In some cases they protected convoys or separated combatants; in others, they stood aside or withdrew from positions as part of their evacuation. As spring approached, the Jewish forces moved from defense to offensive operations, a transition associated with the adoption in March 1948 of Plan Dalet, whose interpretation is debated and is examined in Chapter 125. The bombing of the Jewish Agency's Jerusalem headquarters on 11 March, the Ben Yehuda Street bombing in February, the death of Abd al-Qadir al-Husayni at al-Qastal in April, the Deir Yassin massacre on 9 April, and the attack on a medical convoy near Sheikh Jarrah on 13 April belong to this phase of the conflict and are treated in the following chapter. What matters here is that by spring 1948 a civil war between the two communities, with its own dynamics, was well under way before any Arab army crossed the border.

The first phase of the civil war combined attacks on transport with violence within and between towns. Road vehicles were vulnerable because passengers could be identified as belonging to one community, and control of traffic had consequences for food, medicine, and commercial supply. Bombings and shootings in Jaffa, Jerusalem, and Haifa heightened fear and prompted retaliatory operations. The Haifa refinery killings in December 1947 and the subsequent attack on nearby Arab villages show how violence could spread across workplaces and residential areas. Responsibility for particular attacks belongs to the perpetrators, even where their actions were framed as revenge. The logic of retaliation punished people who had not committed the original act.

Arab armed organization was fragmented. Local forces defended villages and quarters, while the Army of the Holy War associated with Abd al-Qadir al-Husayni became important in the Jerusalem region. The Arab Liberation Army, recruited under Arab League auspices, entered parts of Palestine in early 1948 but operated under leadership and political arrangements not identical to those of Palestinian local forces. Volunteers did not constitute the regular armies that would enter after the Mandate ended. Relations among commanders, local notables, and neighboring governments were sometimes strained by conflicting aims. Material limitations, training differences, and unreliable coordination affected operations and civilian protection.

The Haganah initially emphasized defense of Jewish settlements and lines of communication, though its operations increasingly included raids and efforts to control strategically important areas. The Palmach provided mobile formations and experienced officers. Irgun and Lehi conducted separate actions, including attacks that killed civilians, and did not operate under an identical political command. As the war progressed, the Haganah's greater logistical resources and national chain of command became increasingly important. This difference in organizational capacity should be analyzed alongside the changing balance of arms and manpower, not turned into a moral explanation of victory or defeat.

The road war around Jerusalem was decisive for civilian life. Jewish neighborhoods in the city depended upon convoys from the coast, and Arab forces repeatedly attacked or obstructed routes through the hills. Jewish attempts to open and secure the road, including operations in early April, were therefore both logistical and territorial. Local villages near the road were drawn into fighting as armed forces sought high ground, junctions, and secure passages. A convoy's success or failure could determine whether bakeries obtained flour or medical institutions received supplies. Warfare was embedded in geography and the material needs of a city, not only in declarations issued by leaders.

The escalation in spring 1948 marked a turning point. Plan Dalet, adopted by the Haganah in March, set out military preparations for controlling strategically important areas and communications amid the expected British departure and possible intervention by neighboring armies. Historians disagree over its relationship to a general policy of expulsion; documentary evidence supports the fact that its operational provisions could authorize the destruction of villages and removal of inhabitants in specified circumstances. It is therefore misleading to describe the plan simply as either an unambiguous master order for every expulsion or a document unrelated to Palestinian displacement. Its practical implementation has to be studied through the operations that followed, local orders, and what happened to civilians. Sources: 1

Section 124.18 · Division V

Civilians, Displacement, and the Breakdown of Everyday Life

Civilians bore the weight of these months. Commerce between Arab and Jewish areas, which had been substantial, collapsed. Transport slowed as roads became dangerous, food supplies to Jerusalem grew uncertain, banks and offices closed, schools suspended classes, and municipal services deteriorated. Curfews and checkpoints restricted movement, and neighborhoods organized their own watches. Families sent children away from exposed areas, and Jewish authorities evacuated women and children from some isolated settlements and from Jerusalem.

Displacement began before May 1948. From December 1947, members of the Arab upper and middle classes, especially in Jaffa, Haifa, and Jerusalem, who had the means and connections, left for Lebanon, Egypt, Transjordan, or the Arab-held interior, often expecting to return when the fighting ended. Rural families left villages near the front lines. Estimates of the number who had left by the end of March 1948 vary and are commonly given in the range of several tens of thousands up to about 100,000. The departure of elites weakened the cohesion and financial and administrative capacity of Palestinian towns, and the uneven capacity of communities to protect themselves deepened the crisis. The Jewish community, with its centralized institutions and mobilization, was more able to organize defense and relief, though it too suffered.

Before May 1948, armed conflict had already undermined ordinary civilian routines. Schools closed or operated irregularly, wages were lost when travel became unsafe, and shopkeepers reduced inventories because deliveries could not be guaranteed. In mixed towns, the withdrawal of customers across communal lines meant that a conflict over sovereignty became an immediate economic crisis for households. Municipal authorities struggled to maintain sanitation, street lighting, policing, and public health. Landlords, tenants, port workers, farmers, domestic servants, and small traders suffered in different ways. A history told only through battlefield positions would miss the accumulated decisions that led people to leave their homes or stay in increasingly dangerous surroundings.

Class shaped the earliest departures. Some wealthier Palestinian families could move to Lebanon, Egypt, or inland towns with relatives and savings, often expecting a temporary absence. Their departure deprived cities of capital, professional expertise, and connections to municipal institutions. Poorer families had fewer alternatives and could be trapped in neighborhoods affected by gunfire, shortages, or unemployment. In villages, the timing of movement depended on proximity to fighting, local leadership, available transport, and whether neighboring communities offered refuge. These were not uniform decisions made by an undifferentiated population; agency existed alongside fear, compulsion, and rapidly deteriorating security.

By April and early May, military operations produced new forms of displacement on a larger scale. Residents fled bombardment and street battles, some were expelled by armed forces, and others left after nearby communities were attacked or because political and administrative authority had disintegrated. The Deir Yassin killings of 9 April, carried out by Irgun and Lehi attackers with Haganah involvement in the wider operation, had a powerful effect on fear and rumor far beyond the village itself. Historians disagree about precise casualty figures and aspects of command responsibility, but the massacre and its psychological impact are not inventions. Accounts of individual departures must distinguish direct expulsion, flight from combat, fear of imminent violence, and the breakdown of services, while recognizing that these pressures could overlap.

The fall of Tiberias in April, the Haganah's capture of Haifa after intense fighting later that month, the collapse of Arab positions around Jaffa, and the seizure of Safed in May were watershed events. They affected the future of entire urban communities, not merely strategic dots on a map. British actions varied by location and moment; some officers sought evacuations or ceasefires, while the broader administration was retreating. In Haifa, Jewish authorities publicly urged some Arab residents to remain, yet this did not reverse the mass departure amid fear and combat. Historians examine such episodes through military orders, municipal records, British reports, contemporary photographs, and the testimony of those who stayed or fled. No single proclamation explains every family decision.

Jewish civilians likewise suffered from attacks on transport, shelling, deaths, and severe shortages, especially in the Jerusalem area and isolated settlements. The capacity of Jewish institutions to mobilize medical relief and convoy protection mitigated some risks but did not eliminate the dangers of siege and war. Recognizing these experiences does not require equating their cumulative consequences with those of the Palestinian displacement already under way. A balanced historical account identifies harms precisely and follows their unequal scale, location, and duration. By the time Britain departed, the Palestinian refugee crisis had begun; it did not wait for regular Arab armies to enter on 15 May. Everyday Life and Social History

Living through the Winter of 1947–1948

A resident of Jerusalem in January 1948 had to plan daily life around uncertainty: the bus might not run, a neighborhood might be closed off, bread or fuel might be scarce, and a checkpoint might intervene between home and work. Jewish residents of the western neighborhoods and the Old City's Jewish quarter, Arab residents of neighborhoods such as Katamon and Talbiyeh, and British officials living in fortified zones experienced the city differently, but all lived with the possibility of bombing or sniping.

In Jaffa and Tel Aviv, adjoining but increasingly separated, merchants lost customers and workers lost jobs. In rural districts, farmers faced harvest and marketing problems. Women worked as nurses, couriers, teachers, and, in the Jewish case, in military auxiliaries; they also managed households under rationing and uncertainty. Sources for this experience include diaries, memoirs, oral histories, municipal records, newspapers, and consular reports. They show that civilians were not mere spectators but active in survival, evacuation, and defense, while also revealing how much of the burden fell on those with the least power to protect themselves.

Section 124.19 · Division V

British Withdrawal and the Collapse of Mandate Administration

In December 1947 Britain announced that it would end the Mandate and withdraw by 15 May 1948. The government had declared in the UN that it would not enforce the Partition Plan against the wishes of either community, and it refused to share administration with the UN Palestine Commission before the end of the Mandate, which allowed the Commission only to arrive shortly before the final departure. It also declined to hand over to either side the defense of areas it was leaving in an orderly manner. In February 1948 the Commission reported to the Security Council that the Arab opposition and the lack of an armed force made its task impossible.

The withdrawal proceeded in stages. British forces withdrew from locations in a manner that reflected their own logistics and priorities, often leaving police stations, forts, and strategic positions to be seized by whichever Arab or Jewish forces were nearest. Haifa, where British troops left the city in April 1948, was a case in which departure was followed rapidly by Jewish forces gaining control. British forces continued to hold the port of Haifa and certain areas until 30 June 1948, but Mandate governance in the ordinary sense, including courts, the postal service, the railways, and the civil service, was ceasing by degrees.

The UN General Assembly met again in a second special session from 16 April and, on 14 May, adopted a resolution appointing a UN mediator, later Count Folke Bernadotte, as the Mandate ended. The Security Council had meanwhile established a truce commission. The legal responsibilities of Britain were clear until midnight on 14 May, but its capacity to carry them out had collapsed. Competing administrative authorities emerged: the Jewish Agency and the Vaad Leumi prepared a skeleton government, which became the People's Council and a smaller executive, to take over services; Palestinian Arab leaders had municipal and local committees and the Arab Higher Committee but nothing like a prepared national administration.

The British withdrawal was not a single transfer ceremony in which one government handed keys to another. Troops, police, administrators, engineers, and customs officers departed through different routes on different timetables. Some establishments closed before the formal end of the Mandate; other military positions were held temporarily to protect evacuation. British officials faced immediate decisions about records, weapons, police stores, prisons, hospitals, and land offices. Each choice could alter local power balances after departure. The official principle of not imposing partition did not make the effects of evacuation neutral in practice, and British decisions must be evaluated with the constraints and knowledge available to officials at the time.

The withdrawal also exposed a basic weakness in the United Nations plan. The Palestine Commission was supposed to oversee transition and help establish provisional governing councils, yet it could not obtain the authority, secure access, and cooperation necessary to do so. Britain declined to administer a prolonged transition under UN direction or use its armed forces to establish new state institutions. The Commission reported obstacles to the Security Council, while the security situation on the ground deteriorated. A new international body could issue reports and seek guidance but could not replace policemen, port workers, and district officials who were leaving their posts. The problem was operational, not simply a disagreement about constitutional language.

British personnel did not all understand withdrawal in the same terms. Some regarded departure as a welcome end to a dangerous and unpopular assignment; others worried about abandoning communities and colleagues who had worked alongside them. Arab Palestinians could view the retreat as the end of colonial tutelage while fearing the immediate collapse of order. Jewish leaders prepared to inherit or seize administrative functions while worrying about attacks on transport and the entry of neighboring armies. None of these experiences proves that British departure was planned to benefit one side. It shows why a government can claim to be exiting a conflict while the manner of its exit becomes part of that conflict's history.

Institutional continuity varied widely. Some banking, municipal, and commercial operations continued under local management; others were interrupted by insecurity or the loss of personnel. Political organizations tried to requisition facilities, control checkpoints, secure food stores, and recruit staff. In towns where public offices closed, civilians might no longer know which authority could issue a travel document, settle a dispute, or protect property. Such administrative uncertainty made rumors more dangerous and could accelerate flight even in places without a major battle that day. Legal termination on 15 May had been announced in advance, but the erosion of everyday governing capacity was cumulative.

The regional context grew more ominous as withdrawal proceeded. Arab governments had publicly rejected partition and were considering military intervention, while debating how far to commit forces and under whose political direction. The Yishuv prepared civil government and a more consolidated armed command. Britain tried to protect its departing troops and avoid being drawn into the military outcome, even as ongoing fighting made disengagement difficult. The absence of a jointly accepted transitional authority meant that the last weeks of the Mandate were simultaneously a retreat of imperial sovereignty and a struggle to determine who would govern territory after it vanished. Section 124.20 · Division V

May 1948: The End of the Mandate and the Threshold of a Regional War

In March 1948, with fighting escalating and the Security Council unable to agree on measures, the United States proposed that the implementation of partition be suspended and that a temporary UN trusteeship be established. On 19 March Ambassador Warren Austin told the Security Council that partition could not be carried out by peaceful means. The reversal, which reflected State Department concerns and the lack of means to enforce the plan, caused consternation among Zionists and was resisted by Truman. It highlighted the fact that the principal backer of partition was unwilling to enforce it.

The Jewish leadership proceeded to prepare for statehood. The Jewish Agency and the Vaad Leumi created a People's Council and a People's Administration to take over functions as the British left. On 12 May the People’s Administration considered the security and diplomatic choices facing it; a familiar account of a narrow six-to-four vote on whether to declare independence is not established by the surviving minutes. A recorded five-to-four vote concerned whether borders should be specified in the declaration, and historians dispute the later recollections. The Zionist leadership understood the risk of invasion by Arab states, procured arms, including from Czechoslovakia, and mobilized the Haganah. Ben-Gurion and others believed that statehood should be proclaimed at the moment of British departure.

The Palestinian Arab situation was grave. Towns such as Tiberias, Haifa, Jaffa, and Safed had fallen or been evacuated in April and May, and by mid-May estimates of Palestinians displaced generally range from a quarter to a third of a million. The Army of the Holy War had lost its commander, Abd al-Qadir al-Husayni, and local forces were scattered. The Arab Higher Committee had limited authority. The Arab League governments, divided and reluctant, decided under public pressure to intervene when the Mandate ended. King Abdullah of Transjordan met privately with Golda Meyerson shortly before the Mandate ended, commonly dated to 10–11 May. Accounts of the conversation and its precise timing differ, and participants’ later recollections require comparison with contemporary evidence. His territorial ambitions and regional pressures complicated efforts to reach an understanding.

High Commissioner Sir Alan Cunningham left Jerusalem on 14 May and departed from Haifa. The Mandate terminated at midnight between 14 and 15 May 1948. On the afternoon of 14 May, in Tel Aviv, Ben-Gurion read the declaration of the establishment of a Jewish state, to be called Israel, to take effect at the end of the Mandate; the United States extended recognition within minutes, and the Soviet Union soon afterward. The following day, forces from Egypt, Transjordan, Syria, Iraq, and Lebanon entered the country, beginning the interstate phase of the war.

The legal end of the Mandate was thus not the culmination of a negotiated settlement but the moment when an unimplemented international proposal was overtaken by a new state and a wider war. The partition resolution had set a date by which two states would come into existence; instead, one state was proclaimed, an Arab state did not appear, Jerusalem was not internationalized, and the borders would be determined by the fighting. These developments, and the Palestinian catastrophe known as the Nakba, are the subject of Chapter 125 and of Volume VIII.

The May 12 deliberations of the People's Administration are frequently narrated as a dramatic six-to-four vote on whether to declare independence. That familiar story is not securely established by the surviving minutes. Historian Martin Kramer, examining contemporaneous documents and later recollections, argues that no such vote on the declaration itself took place; the recorded five-to-four vote concerned whether the declaration should specify borders. The evidence should therefore be presented as a dispute over the reconstruction of a crucial meeting, not as an established narrow majority for statehood. Deliberations did address security, international pressure, and the practical consequences of proclamation. But distinction between remembered drama and documented procedure is part of responsible institutional history.

Diplomatic uncertainty also surrounded King Abdullah of Transjordan. Golda Meyerson, later known as Golda Meir, met him secretly in May, after earlier contacts, in an effort to assess his intentions. Accounts vary over the meeting's precise date and over the language each participant used; later memoirs cannot substitute automatically for contemporary documentation. Abdullah's ambitions in the Arab-populated central hills did not coincide fully with those of Palestinian independence advocates or other Arab governments. His Arab Legion was more organized than many irregular forces, but Jordanian objectives and British connections created their own constraints. The meeting belongs to the history of contested regional diplomacy, not to a simple story in which the entire approaching war was planned by one secret agreement.

By mid-May the military and humanitarian situation had changed dramatically from November. Arab communities in Tiberias, Haifa, Jaffa, and Safed had suffered major departures or loss of political and military control. Historical estimates of the number of Palestinians already displaced before 15 May differ because evidence was incomplete, dates of counting vary, and not every movement was formally recorded. A range of roughly a quarter-million to more than 300,000 is often cited for the pre-interstate-war phase, but should be treated as an estimate rather than a definitive census. The extent and causes of this displacement require fuller analysis in Chapter 125. For Chapter 124, the critical fact is that mass displacement had already begun before the armies of neighboring states crossed the frontiers.

On May 14 the Jewish People's Council announced the establishment of the State of Israel, effective upon the end of the Mandate; Britain ended its mandatory responsibility at midnight between May 14 and May 15. The United States recognized the provisional government on a de facto basis on May 14; the Soviet Union's recognition followed. The United Nations General Assembly, meanwhile, adopted Resolution 186 (S-2) on May 14, creating the framework for a mediator rather than implementing partition. Folke Bernadotte would be appointed later, on May 20, and should not be described as already in office on the day of independence. As regular forces from neighboring Arab states entered the war, the conflict acquired an interstate dimension. The legal ending of British rule thus coincided with the beginning of a new political and military phase, but not with an agreed settlement of sovereignty, minority rights, or the fate of displaced people. Historical Vocabulary
Partition, Trusteeship, Federation, and Binationalism

Partition envisaged separate sovereign Arab and Jewish states. Trusteeship meant temporary administration under an international arrangement. Federation created a single independent state with constituent Arab and Jewish units, as in the UNSCOP minority proposal. Provincial autonomy, as proposed in the Morrison–Grady plan, retained paramount British authority and did not provide immediate sovereign federation. Binationalism referred to forms of shared political power advocated by Ihud and some others; Soviet diplomacy initially considered a common state but was not identical to Ihud's program.

Each model answered the question of sovereignty differently, and each depended on the consent of communities that held mutually exclusive claims. Students should avoid treating any one as obviously workable or obviously doomed; the history shows why each failed to command agreement.

Cause and Effect

Why the Mandate Ended Without a Settlement

Long-term causes

The contradictory commitments of the Mandate; the growth of two national movements; the unresolved legacy of the 1936–1939 revolt; Britain's declining imperial capacity.

Short-term causes

The Holocaust and the refugee crisis; immigration restrictions; Jewish insurgency; American pressure; the failure of the Anglo-American proposals and the London conferences.

Immediate effects

Referral to the UN; Resolution 181; Arab rejection; outbreak of civil war; the erosion of British authority.

Longer-term consequences

The proclamation of Israel; the intervention of Arab states; the Palestinian refugee crisis and the failure of a separate Arab state to appear (Chapters 125–127).

Historical complexity: No single cause explains the outcome. Humanitarian need, national aspiration, imperial retreat, and the absence of enforcement all interacted.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how a humanitarian crisis, competing national movements, imperial retreat, and international diplomacy ended the British Mandate for Palestine without a settlement. The Holocaust destroyed most of European Jewry and left several hundred thousand survivors in displaced-persons camps, many unable to return home and facing restrictive immigration policies elsewhere. Palestine became the destination of choice for many and, through the Zionist movement, the focus of an organized effort to move them.

Britain's response was shaped by strategic interests in the Arab and Muslim world, financial weakness, and security concerns. It retained the 1939 White Paper quotas and intercepted clandestine immigrants, deporting them to Cyprus. The Exodus 1947 affair showed the political costs of this policy. The Jewish Agency, with its institutions and armed organizations, pursued immigration, state-building, and pressure on Britain, while the Irgun and Lehi pursued more radical violence, culminating in the King David Hotel bombing and the killing of British sergeants.

Palestinian Arabs, about two-thirds of the population, sought independence and an end to Jewish immigration and land transfers. Their political leadership had been weakened by the suppression of the 1936–1939 revolt, by exile, and by divisions, and it was constituted anew under Arab League auspices. Its rejection of partition rested on arguments about majority self-determination, land, and the authority of the UN, though its boycott of UNSCOP limited its voice.

Anglo-American diplomacy produced the Harrison Report, the Anglo-American Committee, and the Morrison–Grady proposals, each of which failed because neither Zionists nor Arab leaders accepted the whole package and because the United States and Britain had different priorities. In February 1947 Britain referred the matter to the United Nations, which created UNSCOP. Its majority proposed partition with economic union, and its minority a federal state.

The General Assembly adopted Resolution 181 (II) on 29 November 1947 by 33 votes to 13 with 10 abstentions, with American and Soviet support despite their rivalry. The Arab states and Palestinian leaders rejected it; the Jewish Agency accepted it with reservations. The resolution's provisions on territory, Jerusalem, economic union, and minority protections were elaborate, but it was a recommendation without an enforcement mechanism.

Practical obstacles, including mixed towns, scattered settlements, economic interdependence, and the absence of a prepared administration, ensured that adopting the resolution did not create two states. Violence began immediately and escalated into a civil war, with irregular forces, the arrival of the Arab Liberation Army, and attacks on convoys and civilians. Civilians experienced the collapse of commerce, services, and security, and displacement began among Palestinian Arabs before May 1948.

Britain announced its withdrawal and refused to enforce the plan or to cooperate fully with the UN Commission. The American proposal for trusteeship in March 1948 revealed that even the principal sponsor hesitated. Mandate administration disintegrated, and the Jewish leadership, with prepared institutions, proclaimed a state as the Mandate ended, while the Palestinian leadership lacked an equivalent structure.

Historians interpret these events differently. They debate the weight of the Holocaust, British motives, the fairness and feasibility of partition, and the responsibilities of each actor. The chapter has avoided treating either the creation of Israel as inevitable or Palestinian dispossession as incidental, and has stressed alternatives, choices, and the structural weaknesses of the international framework.

The Mandate legally ended at midnight between 14 and 15 May 1948. The partition proposal remained unimplemented; a Jewish state had been proclaimed; Arab states were preparing to enter; and the Palestinian Arab society, already experiencing displacement, was about to face the central catastrophe of its modern history. Chapter 125 takes up this story.

This project asks you to investigate one city or district between November 1947 and May 1948 using sources produced by different institutions.

  1. Choose a locale. Select Jerusalem, Haifa, Jaffa and Tel Aviv, Tiberias, or the Negev settlements.
  2. Gather sources. Locate British administrative records, Jewish Agency and Haganah materials, Palestinian newspapers and memoirs, UN documents, and consular reports.
  3. Reconstruct a chronology. Establish a dated sequence of events, distinguishing documented facts from claims and later memory.
  4. Examine civilian experience. Trace how food, security, commerce, and movement changed for different communities.
  5. Write findings. In a 4–6 page essay, explain what the sources show, where they conflict, and what is uncertain, using cautious language.

Library resources: UN documents (including the text of Resolution 181 and the UNSCOP report), the U.S. Holocaust Memorial Museum Encyclopedia, parliamentary debates, memoirs, oral histories, and the MGU History Library's regional reference materials.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has traced the final three years of British rule in Palestine, a period in which a humanitarian emergency, two national movements, and a weakening empire met a new international organization unable to enforce its own recommendation. It illustrates how large processes, the destruction of European Jewry, decolonization, and the early Cold War, intersected with local societies whose members had their own aims and fears.

The chapter also shows how institutions shaped outcomes. The Yishuv entered 1948 with centralized political and military institutions, while Palestinian society, despite its vitality, had been weakened by the suppression of the revolt, the exile of leaders, and divisions. This asymmetry, together with Britain's withdrawal, helps explain events that Chapter 125 narrates.

The questions raised here persisted: the status of Jerusalem, the fate of refugees, the borders of states, and the relationship between international legitimacy and enforcement. They recur in Volume VIII and in the later chapters on Oslo, the occupation, and the wars of the 2020s. Understanding how the Mandate ended, and what alternatives were considered, allows students to read those later events as the products of historical choices rather than fate.

Chapter 124 closes Volume VII at the legal end of the Mandate, with the partition proposal unimplemented and a new state and a regional war emerging. Its contribution to the history of the Middle East is to show that this threshold was not a single decision but the cumulative outcome of refugee crisis, imperial retreat, competing sovereignty claims, and the limits of international diplomacy.
