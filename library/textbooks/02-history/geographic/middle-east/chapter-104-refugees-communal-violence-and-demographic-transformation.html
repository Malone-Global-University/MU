Part XXIII — Nationalism, Communal Politics, and New Political Identities

Refugees, Communal Violence, and Demographic Transformation

A History of the Middle East: From Ancient Civilizations to the Twenty-First Century · Principal period: c. 1840–1914, with earlier background

Chapter Opening

In July 1860 the Christian quarters of Damascus burned, and in the same city a group of Algerian exiles led by Abd al-Qadir al-Jaza'iri opened their houses and his own residence to Christian neighbors seeking refuge. Four years later, along the eastern shore of the Black Sea, tens of thousands of Circassians waited at coastal landing places for ships that would carry them to Ottoman ports such as Trabzon and Samsun. The two scenes belong to different landscapes and different wars, yet they belong to one historical process: in the middle decades of the nineteenth century, violence, imperial expansion, and state policy moved people, destroyed communities, and created new ones across the Ottoman world, and in doing so reorganized the political geography of the region.

This chapter joins two transformations that are usually told separately. The first is the violence of 1860 in Mount Lebanon and Damascus, in which thousands of people, most of them Christians, were killed, many more were displaced, and an Ottoman and European response produced a new administrative order for Mount Lebanon. The second is the displacement of Crimean Tatars, Circassians, Chechens, Nogais, and other Muslim populations by Russian imperial expansion, followed by the mass arrival of Muslim refugees from the Balkans after 1877–1878, and the Ottoman government's attempts to receive, register, and settle them. Taken together, these developments changed who lived where, how governments classified and governed their subjects, and what religious and communal identities meant in public life.

The chapter begins from a methodological premise that governs the whole textbook: religious difference, taken alone, is not an explanation. Muslims, Christians, Druze, and Jews had lived in shared markets, neighborhoods, and political arrangements across the Ottoman Levant for centuries, and their relations had included cooperation, rivalry, and conflict. The violence of 1860 requires an explanation grounded in the specific circumstances of the nineteenth century: new tax and land relations, European commerce, the language of reform and equality, the weakening of old hierarchies, competing foreign patronage, and the political mobilization of religious communities. The chapter's first two divisions reconstruct those circumstances and the events themselves, including the acts of protection and rescue that sit alongside the killing.

The second premise concerns refugees. The Muslim refugees whom the Ottoman state called muhacir were not an undifferentiated mass. Crimean Tatars, Circassians, Chechens, Dagestanis, Nogais, Abkhazians, Bulgarian and Rumelian Muslims, Bosniaks, and Cretan Muslims had different languages, social structures, economic resources, and relations with the places where they arrived. Many had suffered devastating losses before arrival, including hunger, epidemic disease, and the destruction of their villages. At the same time, the Ottoman government did not treat them only as objects of charity. Refugee settlement served fiscal, agricultural, military, and territorial purposes, and settlement affected the people already living in the chosen districts, including peasants, Bedouin pastoralists, townspeople, and local officials.

The historical sources require care. For 1860, they include Arabic chronicles and memoirs, consular and diplomatic correspondence, missionary reports, Ottoman investigative records, and later European narratives that often reflected the sympathies of their authors. For the Caucasus and the Black Sea, they include Russian military and administrative reports, Ottoman registers, consular reports, and later refugee memory, and the figures for deaths and migration remain estimates with wide ranges. For settlement, the most revealing evidence often consists of land registers, tax records, and local petitions, which only recently have been studied systematically. This chapter therefore attributes numerical claims, distinguishes counting methods, and treats disputed classifications such as genocide as questions of historical interpretation and legal definition rather than as slogans.

The chapter's argument is that nineteenth-century demographic change was not merely a consequence of conflict. It also became a means through which imperial governments reorganized territory, extended their authority, shaped communal institutions, and influenced the formation of political identities. Russia's conquest of the Caucasus depended on removing populations as well as defeating armies. The Ottoman state used refugee settlement to populate frontiers and cultivate land while building a new administrative apparatus. After 1860, the European powers and the Ottoman government built a communal representative structure in Mount Lebanon that gave religious community a formal political role. None of these outcomes was inevitable, and none determined later events, but each left durable institutions, settlements, and memories.

The chapter is organized in five divisions of four sections each. Division I examines the Ottoman Levant, Mount Lebanon's society, and the crisis that culminated in the Kisrawan peasant revolt. Division II reconstructs the violence of 1860 and the reconstruction of Ottoman Syria. Division III turns to Crimea, the Caucasus, the Circassian expulsions, and the Ottoman Refugee Commission. Division IV follows the refugees into Anatolia, the Balkans, Syria, the Golan, and Transjordan, and into the everyday life of resettled communities. Division V examines Balkan refugees, the methods by which population is counted, and the region in 1914. Boundaries with neighboring chapters are deliberate: the Armenian massacres of the 1890s belong to Chapter 100, Kurdish politics and the Hamidiye to Chapter 103, Zionist settlement to Chapter 102, the Balkan Wars of 1912–1913 to Chapter 107, and the mass violence and displacement of World War I to Chapter 111.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Describe the communal, administrative, and economic organization of the mid-nineteenth-century Ottoman Levant and Mount Lebanon.
  2. Explain how the Egyptian occupation, the Ottoman restoration, and the dual qaimaqamate shaped political relations among Maronites, Druze, and other communities.
  3. Analyze the Kisrawan peasant revolt of 1858–1861 as a crisis of social hierarchy and political representation.
  4. Reconstruct the chronology and principal episodes of the 1860 violence in Mount Lebanon and Damascus while distinguishing their differing causes.
  5. Evaluate the roles of Ottoman authorities, local elites, foreign consuls, and European governments in the violence and its aftermath.
  6. Explain the provisions and significance of the 1861 Organic Regulation and the 1864 revisions establishing the Mount Lebanon mutasarrifate.
  7. Trace Russian expansion in Crimea and the Caucasus and the displacement of Crimean Tatars, Circassians, Chechens, and other Muslim populations.
  8. Assess historians' characterizations of the Circassian expulsions, distinguishing established acts from disputed legal and historical classifications.
  9. Describe the Ottoman Refugee Commission and the development of imperial resettlement policy.
  10. Compare refugee settlement in Anatolia, the Balkans, the Golan, and Transjordan, and its effects on established communities.
  11. Interpret primary and archival evidence, including consular reports, legal regulations, and land registers, with attention to authorship and limitation.
  12. Evaluate how nineteenth-century population movements shaped the Middle East of 1914 without treating later outcomes as inevitable.

Historical Context

Earlier chapters established the setting. The Ottoman Empire, examined as a transregional imperial system in Volume V, governed its Arab provinces through a combination of provincial governors, local notables, tax farmers, religious courts, and recognized religious communities, and the eighteenth century had strengthened provincial households and notable families (Chapters 74, 86, and 88). Chapter 91 traced Muhammad Ali's rule in Egypt and the Egyptian occupation of Syria from 1831 to 1840, which this chapter recalls only as background to Mount Lebanon. Chapter 92 examined the Tanzimat, the reform decrees of 1839 and 1856, and the effort to create a more centralized, bureaucratic state with legal equality among Ottoman subjects. This chapter examines one set of consequences of that reform, not the program itself.

The economic background appears in Chapter 96. Steamships, European banks, foreign merchants, and the expansion of cash crops integrated the Levantine coast into global markets. In Mount Lebanon, raw silk for European, especially French, mills became a principal export, and Beirut grew as a commercial port. Consular protection and the capitulations gave European governments influence over particular Ottoman communities, and Christian merchants and brokers, who often had European connections, gained in some sectors while craft producers and some landed notables faced pressure. Chapter 101 described the intellectual world of the Nahda, including the schools, presses, and missions that were growing in Beirut and elsewhere, and Chapters 100 and 103 showed how other Ottoman communities also confronted reform, centralization, and violence in these decades.

The Russian Empire constitutes the second strand. Chapter 89 recounted Russian expansion on the Black Sea, including the annexation of Crimea in 1783, and Chapter 95 examined the Crimean War and the Eastern Question. Those chapters treat the diplomatic and military history. This chapter asks what that expansion meant for the Muslim populations whose lands were conquered, and what it meant for the Ottoman Empire that received many of them. The Ottoman state had long accommodated migrants, but in the nineteenth century the number of arrivals, the speed of their movement, and the state's new administrative capacity made refugee policy a distinct field of governance.

The chapter also depends on the broader demographic reality of the region. The Ottoman Empire's inhabitants were Muslim and non-Muslim, speakers of Turkish, Arabic, Greek, Armenian, Kurdish, Bulgarian, Circassian languages, and many others, and no simple map of religion or language corresponded to the empire's political structure. Many villages and neighborhoods were mixed. Between the 1830s and 1914, wars, conquests, and policies altered that mixture in ways that later nationalists, states, and historians would interpret in competing ways. The task of this chapter is to reconstruct how those changes occurred and what they meant to the people who experienced them.

Division I · Sections 104.1–104.4

Ottoman Reform, Communal Relations, and the Origins of Violence

The first division reconstructs the world in which the crisis of 1860 became possible. Its purpose is to reject explanations based on supposedly timeless religious hostility and to show how economic change, administrative reform, local politics, and foreign patronage altered the meaning of communal belonging.

Section 104.1

The Ottoman Levant: Peoples, Provinces, and Communal Relations

In the middle of the nineteenth century the Ottoman Levant was governed through provinces whose boundaries changed repeatedly. The old eyalets of Damascus, Sidon, Tripoli, and Aleppo were reorganized during the Tanzimat decades, and the Vilayet Law of 1864 and its application in Syria in 1865 created a larger Vilayet of Syria centered on Damascus, followed later by a separate Vilayet of Beirut in 1888. Jerusalem acquired a separate administrative status through reorganization beginning in 1872, formalized more firmly by 1874. These changes show an Ottoman state experimenting with how to govern a region of mountains, coastal cities, steppe margins, and agricultural plains. Mount Lebanon, a mountain district rising behind the Mediterranean coast between Beirut and Tripoli, occupied an unusual place within this structure, because its tax-collecting notables had long exercised substantial local authority under the supervision of Ottoman governors.

The region's population was religiously diverse. Sunni Muslims formed the majority in the inland cities of Damascus, Aleppo, Homs, and Hama and in many coastal towns. Shia Muslims, known locally as Metwalis in some districts, lived in southern Lebanon and the Biqa'. The Druze, a community whose faith had developed from Ismaili Islam, lived in the Shuf, the Matn, the Gharb, and in the Hawran and southern Syrian highlands. Christians belonged to several churches: the Maronites, who were Eastern Catholics, were concentrated in the northern and central mountain; the Greek Orthodox (Rum Orthodox) were scattered through coastal towns, Damascus, Zahle, and the Hawran; the Greek Catholics (Melkites) were strong in Zahle and Damascus; and smaller Syriac, Armenian, and Protestant congregations were also present. Jewish communities lived in Damascus, Aleppo, Safed, Tiberias, Jerusalem, and Beirut, with a long history outlined in Chapter 84.

These communities did not live in separate worlds. They shared markets, craft guilds, caravan networks, and in many places neighborhoods or village spaces. Local courts, including Islamic sharia courts, served Christians and Jews as well as Muslims in commercial and some legal matters, and religious communities had their own institutions for marriage, inheritance, education, and charity. Religion mattered to status, taxation, and legal capacity, but it did not automatically determine economic position or political loyalty. Christian peasants worked for Christian landlords; Druze peasants worked for Druze lords; Muslim and Christian merchants cooperated in the same trade; and wealthy families of every religion cultivated patronage relations with governors and notables.

Historians therefore distinguish several layers of identity: religious belief and practice; formal membership in a recognized community; class position; local or village allegiance; allegiance to a notable house or faction; and, increasingly, the language of communal representation promoted by foreign governments, missionaries, and reformers. Before the nineteenth century these layers coexisted without producing a single dominant axis of political conflict. In Mount Lebanon especially, factions had often cut across religious lines, and Druze and Maronite notables had allied and fought in shifting combinations. What changed after 1830 was not the existence of religious difference but the extent to which political institutions, tax obligations, foreign protection, and representation came to be organized around it.

Evidence for this world comes from many kinds of source. Ottoman court registers and tax surveys document landholding and legal practice. Local chronicles such as those written by Maronite and Druze notables, clergy, and townsmen record events and factions. Consular reports and missionary letters, while invaluable, are written from particular perspectives, often with a European interest in the fate of Christians. Later memoirs introduce hindsight. A historian must read each kind of source for what the author could observe and what the author wanted a particular audience to believe.

Beirut illustrates the changing environment. A modest port in 1800, it grew rapidly as the commercial outlet for Mount Lebanon silk and for Damascus trade, attracting merchants, missionaries, consuls, and migrants from the mountain. Its growth created a new urban Christian and Muslim middle class and a new arena for foreign influence. Damascus, by contrast, remained a great inland Muslim-majority city with powerful notable families, a large Christian quarter, and long caravan connections that European steam navigation was beginning to reshape. The two cities would experience the crisis of 1860 in different ways, and the contrast is part of what this chapter explains.

Section 104.2

Mount Lebanon: Landholding, Notables, and the Druze-Maronite Political Order

Mount Lebanon was a district of steep valleys, terraced slopes, and numerous villages, organized politically through a hierarchy of notable families. At the apex stood, from the late seventeenth century to 1841, the emirs of the Shihab family, who collected taxes for Ottoman governors and exercised authority over the mountain's subordinate lords. The Shihabs were of Sunni origin in the earlier period, and members of the family later became Maronite Christians, a reminder that dynastic politics did not follow simple confessional lines. The most famous Shihab ruler, Bashir II, governed from 1788 until 1840 and played a central role in the events recounted in the next section.

Beneath the emir were the muqata'jis, hereditary lords who held a tax district (muqata'a) through the iltizam system, a form of tax farming described in Chapter 83. Prominent muqata'ji houses included the Druze Janbalats, Arslans, Abu Nakads, and Talhuqs, and the Maronite Khazins, Hubayshes, and Dahdahs. A muqata'ji collected taxes and dues from peasants, remitted a fixed sum upward, retained a portion, and exercised local judicial and social authority. Many muqata'jis were Druze, but Maronite and other Christian notables held districts too, particularly in Kisrawan and the north. The system thus combined seigneurial relations with Ottoman fiscal structure, and it conferred political privileges on a narrow elite that included members of different religious communities.

Most of the population consisted of cultivators who worked land they held under various tenures, paying taxes, rents, and customary dues, including labor obligations and gifts, to their lords. Silk, produced from mulberry-fed silkworms in village households, became the principal commercial crop. European demand, especially from the silk mills of Lyon, expanded production through the early nineteenth century, drew villagers into the market, and increased the importance of merchants, brokers, and creditors, many of them Christian and many based in Beirut. Peasants who produced silk could earn cash, but they were also vulnerable to price fluctuations, debt, and the claims of lords who sought to capture a share of the increased income.

Population movement also changed the mountain's composition. Over the eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries, Maronite peasants migrated south from the north into the Shuf and other districts traditionally under Druze lords, partly in response to opportunities for land and labor on Druze estates. Mixed Druze-Maronite districts became common in the central and southern mountain, a development with long-term consequences, because it put communities with different religious leaderships and different relations to their notables under the same local authorities. Maronite clergy and the patriarchate, strengthened by links with Rome and France, developed greater social influence and a growing capacity to speak for the community.

Economic change intersected with political privilege. Peasants and clergy increasingly questioned the dues and authority of lords, and some lords struggled with debt and competition for resources. Christian merchants and the clergy gained in influence, while Druze notables, whose communities were smaller, felt their traditional primacy threatened. European consuls and missionaries added another layer, offering patronage to particular communities: France traditionally supported the Maronites, Britain developed ties with the Druze, Russia with the Greek Orthodox, and Austria with the Catholics. These associations were not total, and individual notables sought support wherever it was available, but they meant that local rivalries could become matters of international concern.

By the 1840s, then, class relations, political privilege, and communal identity were becoming entangled. A dispute over dues might appear as a quarrel between peasants and lords, between communities, or between rival factions of the same community, depending on who described it. Reforms that proclaimed equality among Ottoman subjects raised questions about the status of the muqata'jis and the meaning of equal representation. The next section turns to the political events that made those questions urgent.

Section 104.3

Egyptian Occupation, Ottoman Restoration, and the Reorganization of Mount Lebanon

The story of Egyptian rule in Syria belongs primarily to Chapter 91, but its consequences for Mount Lebanon must be recalled. In 1831 the forces of Muhammad Ali, commanded by his son Ibrahim Pasha, invaded Syria, and Bashir II allied himself with the Egyptians. Egyptian administration introduced new taxation, conscription, and disarmament measures, and it relied on Bashir to extend its authority into the mountain. Reform-minded in some respects, the occupation was also heavy-handed and costly, and it provoked revolts, including a major rising in 1838 in the Hawran and among the Druze. In the course of these events, communities were armed, rearmed, and disarmed, and local grievances were sharpened rather than resolved. Some Christians had initially welcomed Egyptian rule because it promised greater equality, while many Druze and Muslims resented it, and the political alignments that resulted would shape the following decades.

In 1840 Britain, Austria, Russia, and Prussia intervened to compel Egypt's withdrawal, with the support of the Ottoman government and of rebellious Lebanese. Bashir II was forced into exile. The Ottoman government, with European backing, installed Bashir III Shihab, but he proved unable to command the mountain's notables, and the Ottomans deposed him in 1842. The end of Shihab rule removed the single institution that had held the mountain's hierarchy together, and the Ottomans did not initially have an adequate replacement. The attempt to impose direct Ottoman administration in 1842 provoked opposition and left the Druze and Maronite notables competing for position under governors who did not command the full confidence of either.

Fighting in 1841 between Druze and Maronite forces, notably around Dayr al-Qamar, was an early expression of this instability. The causes were mixed: Maronite clergy and Christian merchants were asserting greater claims, Druze lords feared losing authority, notables of both communities sought to restore their positions, and Ottoman officials did not act consistently. European consuls provided arms, advice, or diplomatic support to the communities with which they were associated. The ensuing arrangements, brokered under European and Ottoman supervision, did not stabilize the mountain.

In 1842–1843 the Ottomans, with Austrian and other European influence, adopted a solution known as the dual qaimaqamate. Mount Lebanon was divided into two administrative districts along the Beirut-Damascus road, a northern district under a Maronite qaimaqam and a southern district under a Druze qaimaqam, both subordinate to the Ottoman governor in Sidon. The design assumed that the mountain could be divided into predominantly Christian and predominantly Druze zones. In practice the population was mixed, and the arrangement placed many Maronites in the southern district under Druze authority and some Druze in the northern district under a Maronite qaimaqam, without clear protections for either. The plan therefore treated religious community as the basis of territorial administration while leaving the underlying mixture of populations intact.

Renewed fighting in 1845 demonstrated these weaknesses. The Ottoman minister Shekib Efendi, sent to Syria, issued regulations in 1845 that supplemented the qaimaqamate by creating councils in each district with representatives of the principal communities, including a judge and an advisor for each. These councils were an early step in institutionalizing communal representation, and they also gave Ottoman officials an opening to supervise the mountain more closely. Yet the underlying disputes over taxation, land, and authority were unresolved.

Meanwhile, the Tanzimat introduced the principle of legal equality among Ottoman subjects. The Gülhane decree of 1839 promised security of life, property, and honor to all subjects irrespective of religion, and the Reform Edict of 1856, issued after the Crimean War and under strong European pressure, restated equality in much more detail, addressing taxation, military service, courts, schools, and access to office. These reforms were uneven in their application and met with varied responses. Many Christians welcomed the promise of equality but felt it was not honored; many Muslims, and many Druze, saw equality as an erosion of established hierarchies and as an instrument of European influence. The reforms thus offered a new political vocabulary, equality, rights, representation, and subjecthood, which all communities could use, with differing aims.

By the mid-1850s the dual qaimaqamate had not resolved the fundamental questions. Who could tax whom? Which lords exercised judicial authority? Could peasants claim representation against their lords? How should mixed districts be governed? The compromise arrangements held, uneasily, because no group was strong enough to alter them decisively and because Ottoman governors preferred to manage rivalries than to settle them. The first major challenge came, not from a conflict between communities, but from within the Maronite community itself.

Historical Vocabulary
Communal Representation, Confessionalism, and Sectarianism

Communal representation is the arrangement by which political bodies include delegates of recognized religious communities as such. Confessionalism describes a political system that allocates offices or seats by community, as in the council established for Mount Lebanon in 1861 and later in the Lebanese state (examined in Chapters 117 and 138). The term sectarianism is used by historians in several ways, and this textbook uses it with care. Ussama Makdisi has argued that modern sectarianism, understood as the political organization of religious difference through law, administration, and rhetoric, was produced in the nineteenth-century Ottoman reform era and its interactions with European power, and was not simply an inheritance of older religious antagonism. Whatever the weight one gives to that argument, the practical point for students is that sectarian identity needs to be explained historically, by identifying the institutions, interests, and events that gave it political force, and cannot serve as an explanation in itself.

Section 104.4

The Kisrawan Peasant Revolt, 1858–1861, and the Breakdown of the Old Order

Kisrawan, a district in northern Mount Lebanon north of Beirut, was predominantly Maronite and had been governed for generations by the Khazin family, Maronite notables who held the muqata'a and exercised wide authority over the peasants. By the 1850s the Khazin shaykhs collected taxes and dues, including customary gifts and labor obligations, from a population that included silk producers, villagers, and clergy with their own landholdings. The Khazins' authority had a religious dimension: they were prominent Maronite patrons and had relations with the Patriarchate. They were also lords whose privileges the Reform Edict of 1856 and the language of equality seemed to call into question.

Village organization and petitions began in the autumn of 1858; the movement became an overt revolt in early 1859, when village delegates selected Tanyus Shahin, a farrier or muleteer from Rayfun, as their leader. The date of the insurgency therefore must not be reduced to a supposed uprising in January 1858. The peasants demanded an end to specific exactions and privileges and, in the most radical phase of the revolt, expelled the Khazin shaykhs from the district and established a form of peasant self-government under local committees and representatives. This was an extraordinary development in a society organized by hereditary hierarchy: cultivators had asserted political authority over a district and forced notables to flee.

The rebels used the language of reform. They invoked the promise of equality in the Ottoman edicts, appealed to Ottoman officials and to the principle that subjects were entitled to just treatment, and framed their demands in terms of rights rather than only as acts of rebellion. Historians such as Ussama Makdisi have stressed that this appropriation of reform vocabulary by peasants shows that equality and representation were not abstractions imposed from above but became weapons in local struggles over land, labor, and authority. The rebels also had to deal with the Maronite clergy, whose position was difficult. The Church had its own landholdings and links to the notables, but many clergy were of peasant background and sympathized with grievances, and the Patriarch sought to restore order without condoning violence.

The revolt spread. Peasants in Matn and in the Druze-ruled southern districts, many of them Maronite and Greek Catholic cultivators, began to question their own lords' dues and authority, and Druze peasants in some areas also had grievances. The revolt thus unsettled the mountain's wider order, not only Kisrawan's. For the Druze notables, who ruled many Christian tenants, the example was alarming: a successful peasant uprising in the north threatened the basis of their own authority in the south. The Ottoman authorities, for their part, were ambivalent. Some officials found in the revolt an opportunity to weaken the notables and extend direct government; others feared disorder.

By 1859 the insurgents had expelled Khazin lords and established a peasant commonwealth, or jumhuriyya, supported by elected village representatives. This was not a final settlement with the Khazins: their attempts to restore authority continued, and peasant self-government endured through the crisis of 1860. In March 1861 Yusuf Karam, acting with support from Ottoman authorities and established elites, defeated Shahin's supporters at Rayfun. Thereafter a reconciliation was arranged, but the old system of hereditary privilege was not fully restored. The movement should therefore be dated from its beginnings in 1858 through its suppression and negotiated aftermath in 1861. The movement left many grievances unresolved and created a heightened sense of insecurity among notables of both communities. It also showed that Maronite peasants and Maronite lords had interests that diverged, and that the Church's claim to speak for the community was contested from below. The conventional picture of Maronites and Druze as two blocs confronting each other is therefore too simple; both communities contained deep social divisions, and a revolt that began as a Maronite peasant challenge to Maronite notables was part of the context of the war that followed.

The relationship between the revolt and the violence of 1860 is a matter of historical interpretation. No one argues that the revolt caused the war in a direct, mechanical sense. Yet it destabilized the mountain's social hierarchy, strengthened the fears of Druze notables, intensified the competition for authority, and shaped the way Christians and Druze interpreted threats. The violence of spring 1860 should be read as emerging in an environment in which the political order had already been shaken from within. The next division turns to that violence.

The Violence of 1860 and the Reconstruction of Ottoman Syria

The second division is the chapter's central case study of communal violence, civilian displacement, foreign intervention, and institutional reorganization. It treats Mount Lebanon and Damascus separately, because they had different causes, before considering rescue, refuge, and the political settlement that followed.

Section 104.5

The Mount Lebanon War of 1860: Communal Mobilization, Massacres, and Destruction

The fighting of 1860 began in the spring in the mixed districts of the central mountain. By late May there were clashes in the Matn, between Druze and Maronite villagers, and attacks and reprisals by both sides soon spread south and east into the Shuf, the Gharb, the Jezzine district, and the slopes toward the Biqa' and Mount Hermon. Contemporary reports disagree about who attacked first in particular places and about the degree of planning. Maronite and Druze accounts, and those of consuls sympathetic to one side or the other, assigned blame differently. What can be established is that armed bands from both communities mobilized, that villages were burned, and that over the following weeks the Christian population, particularly in the southern and mixed districts, suffered by far the heavier toll in lives and property.

The Druze fighters were led by notables, including members of the Janbalat, Arslan, and Abu Nakad houses, who mobilized retainers and peasants. Said Janbalat was among the most prominent leaders. Maronite fighters were less centrally organized, and the community's clergy, notables, and peasants did not act under a single command. Druze strategy relied on mobility, knowledge of the terrain, and in some places on coordination with Muslim irregulars from outside the mountain. A frequently repeated explanation holds that the Druze leaders, fearing loss of their position after the Kisrawan revolt, acted pre-emptively against Christian communities in their districts. Historians treat the claim cautiously: the evidence for coordinated planning at the highest level is debated, whereas the mobilization of local forces and the willingness of local notables to use violence is well documented.

Several episodes became emblematic. At Hasbaya, in the southeastern slopes of Mount Hermon, Christians were reportedly given Ottoman protection in the government building in early June, but the Druze attackers entered and many were killed. At Rashaya, in the same region, Christians were attacked around the same time. At Zahle, a large, prosperous Greek Catholic and Maronite town on the edge of the Biqa' valley whose commerce linked the mountain to Damascus, a force of Druze and allied fighters attacked and destroyed much of the town in mid-June after earlier fighting in which the Christians had initially held their own. At Dayr al-Qamar, the old seat of the Shihab emirs and a town with a large Christian population, hundreds, by some accounts more than a thousand, were killed after the Ottoman garrison there failed to protect them. Dozens of other villages were destroyed or abandoned in these weeks.

The role of Ottoman authorities was crucial and has been intensely debated. Ottoman governors and officers commanded troops in the region, and in several of the worst episodes those troops were present. At Hasbaya and Dayr al-Qamar they disarmed or concentrated Christians and then failed to prevent massacres, or, according to some accounts, allowed attackers to enter. The governor in Beirut, Khurshid Pasha, was later accused of negligence and complicity, and the Ottoman government under European pressure prosecuted him and other officials. Whether the failures reflected deliberate policy, local collusion, incompetence, divided command, or a combination remains a matter of interpretation. What the evidence supports is that the state's agents did not provide the protection that the reform decrees had promised, and that European observers and many local Christians concluded that the state had abandoned them.

Casualty figures must be treated with great caution and separated by location. In Mount Lebanon, several scholarly reconstructions put the deaths above ten thousand, whereas contemporary estimates and later histories of the Damascus massacre separately describe thousands more, some estimating as many as ten thousand in Damascus. The figures vary considerably by author, district, dates counted, and whether immediate killings or later deaths from disease, hunger, and displacement are included; they should not be combined into a single apparently precise total. Accounts also describe hundreds of damaged or destroyed villages and widespread displacement. Druze people were killed in the initial fighting and reprisals, but the documented direct civilian toll fell overwhelmingly on Christian communities. The historical claim that the violence was catastrophic is secure; a precise aggregate death count is not. See the analyses of Leila Tarazi Fawaz and Ussama Makdisi, and Najwa al-Qattan’s discussion of the Damascus evidence (source overview).

The violence was not simply a Druze-Christian division. Many Druze, Muslims, and others sheltered Christian neighbors, some Druze notables opposed the killing or sought to protect particular villages, and Christians in some districts organized their own defenses and in places attacked Druze villages. Accounts of "the Druze" or "the Maronites" as monolithic actors flatten these differences. The more precise historical statement is that political leaders and armed bands in each community mobilized along communal lines, that the instruments of the state failed or participated, and that civilians, overwhelmingly Christian, paid the price.

Section 104.6

The Damascus Massacre of July 1860: Urban Crisis and Communal Violence

The violence in Damascus began on 9 July 1860, and over the following days the Christian quarter of Bab Tuma, along with other Christian neighborhoods, was attacked, looted, and burned. Churches, convents, schools, and private houses were destroyed, and a large number of Christians were killed. Several foreign consulates were also attacked or sacked. Estimates of the dead commonly range from a few thousand to over five thousand, with figures uncertain for the same reasons as in Mount Lebanon. Thousands of survivors found shelter in the citadel under Ottoman protection or in the houses of Muslim neighbors, or fled the city. Contemporary observers stressed the speed with which the violence spread and the apparent inaction of the local garrison.

The causes of the Damascus violence differ from those of the mountain war. Damascus was a large, predominantly Muslim city with a substantial Christian minority, powerful Muslim notable families and religious scholars, and an economy centered on crafts and caravan trade. Over the previous decades several developments had altered its social balance. European commerce, particularly in textiles, had undercut some local craft producers, many of them Muslim, while Christian and Jewish merchants and brokers with foreign connections often prospered. The Reform Edict of 1856 had promised equality, including ending the distinction that had subordinated non-Muslims in public life, and many Damascene Muslims resented what they saw as an imposed change in status and the growing presence of European consuls and protégés. Many Christians, in turn, had higher expectations that were not met.

Events in the mountain contributed. Refugees from the Lebanese war, including destitute Christians from Zahle and the Biqa', reached Damascus and heightened tensions, and news of the war, including accounts of Christian attacks on Muslims and Druze, circulated in the city. The Ottoman governor, Ahmad Pasha, was accused by European consuls and later by an Ottoman investigation of failing to restrain the crowd and, according to some, of encouraging or tolerating the violence. A general atmosphere of insecurity, a weakened garrison, and a population of unemployed or marginal men provided material for mobs. Historians argue about the weight of each factor. Some emphasize economic dislocation and resentment of Christian prosperity, others the political shock of the Reform Edict, others the failures and possible complicity of officials, and others the specific actions of local leaders and instigators.

The Damascus violence and the mountain war were connected in contemporary perception, and European governments quickly treated them as parts of a single "Syrian massacre," a framing that shaped the response. But the differences matter. In the mountain, the conflict was embedded in a rural society of notables, peasants, and competing factions, and armed communities fought one another under rival leaders. In Damascus, the principal violence was committed by urban crowds and irregulars against a largely unarmed minority, in a context of tension over status and economic change, and under the eyes of a local government that failed to act. Treating them as identical obscures both.

Damascene Muslims were not uniform in their response. Some of the city's leading scholars, notables, and officials condemned the violence or sheltered Christians, and the next section examines the best-known examples. Others took part or profited from the looting. The social composition of the perpetrators is itself a subject of study: contemporary reports describe crowds drawn from several quarters, including poor residents, irregulars, and in some accounts people from the surrounding region. The consequences for Muslim-Christian relations in Damascus were lasting, though not uniform. Christians suffered enormous losses of life and property, many left the city, and trust between communities was damaged. At the same time, personal relations of protection and obligation created in those days persisted in family memory.

Damascus also illustrates the limits of the Ottoman state's authority in 1860. The empire's reform program promised a central government that could guarantee security, yet in July the garrison and civil authorities proved unable or unwilling to protect a major city's minority. The Ottoman government's later response, and the punishment of Ahmad Pasha and other officials, was partly an effort to restore the credibility of that promise and to forestall further European intervention. The following sections trace both the human responses to the crisis and the political outcome.

Section 104.7

Rescue, Refuge, Displacement, and Civilian Survival in 1860

Casualty statistics describe the scale of 1860 but not its texture. For tens of thousands of people the violence meant flight, loss of home and livelihood, uncertainty about missing relatives, and dependence on others for survival. Families from burned villages in the Shuf and Matn moved toward Beirut, Sidon, and the Mediterranean coast, or across the mountains to Damascus and the Biqa'. Villagers from the Zahle region sought safety in towns and in the mountains. After the Damascus violence, survivors fled the city or remained in cramped refuges. The mountain's silk-producing economy was disrupted, farms and mulberry groves were abandoned or destroyed, and a season's work was lost. Hunger and disease followed, and contemporary reports estimated that many more people died from privation than from direct violence, though such estimates are imprecise.

Beirut was the principal refuge. Its population swelled with displaced villagers, and its consuls, missionaries, merchants, and charitable bodies organized relief. British, French, American, and other committees collected funds and distributed food, clothing, and shelter, and Beirut's Christian and Muslim notables took part. Out of the relief efforts came new institutions, including schools for girls and orphans established by foreign women's organizations; the British Syrian relief effort associated with Mary Bowen Thompson led to a network of schools that served displaced families. Relief did not reach everyone, was sometimes distributed through confessional and patronage networks, and left rural recovery to the longer and harder work of rebuilding homes and fields.

In Damascus, protection took many forms. The best-known example is that of Abd al-Qadir al-Jaza'iri, the Algerian scholar and former leader of resistance to the French conquest of Algeria, who had lived in Damascus since 1855 after French authorities released him from detention. When the violence began, he and his Algerian followers, who were armed and disciplined, sheltered Christians in his residence and escorted many to the citadel, where Ottoman troops held others. Accounts, including those of European consuls and Damascene Christians, attribute to him the protection of thousands, with figures in the range of several thousand frequently cited, though exact numbers are uncertain. He subsequently received honors from European governments, including France, which was the very power he had fought. His conduct was not an isolated act. Other Muslim notables, religious scholars, and ordinary neighbors also sheltered Christian friends, clients, and employees, and many of the rescues occurred through existing personal ties.

Interpreting Abd al-Qadir's role requires care. He acted out of what he and his contemporaries described in terms of religious and ethical duty to protect those under threat, and also as a man of considerable standing who could command respect and had the means to resist the mob. European governments celebrated his actions, in part because they reinforced a narrative of the civilizing influence of protection, and some Damascenes had mixed feelings about the attention. These complexities do not diminish the rescue. They illustrate that the history of 1860 includes acts of protection rooted in the social and religious norms of the same city where the violence occurred, and that the same Islamic and communal vocabulary could be used to justify both atrocity and rescue.

Women, children, and families bore specific burdens. Widows and orphans required support, and many families had lost the men who held property and earned incomes. Contemporary reports record that women and children were taken into households during the violence, and the Ottoman authorities and European consuls pressed for their recovery and return. Family networks, including relatives in Beirut and abroad, remittances, and the Church's charitable institutions, were crucial to recovery. Returning home was often difficult: villages had been burned, property claims were complicated, and fear of renewed violence lingered. Some families did not return, and the mountain's population geography shifted as Christians resettled in or near Beirut, in the Biqa', and elsewhere.

Economic consequences followed. The Ottoman and European authorities required compensation for Christian losses, which was assessed and in part levied on Muslim and Druze communities, a measure that itself created resentment. The reconstruction of Damascus's Christian quarter proceeded over the following years, partly funded by indemnities and relief funds. In the longer term the events contributed to increased Christian emigration, from the mountain to Beirut, Egypt, and eventually the Americas, though the major waves of Lebanese emigration belong to the later nineteenth century and had multiple causes, including economic changes treated in Chapter 96.

Taken together, these experiences show that civilian history cannot be reduced to a record of victims and perpetrators. People organized refuge, negotiated with attackers, appealed to officials, relied on relatives, and rebuilt. They also, in places, took revenge or exploited others' losses. The historian's task is to recover that range of conduct without erasing either the atrocity or the acts of courage and compassion that accompanied it.

Section 104.8

Fuad Pasha, European Intervention, and the Mount Lebanon Mutasarrifate

The scale of the violence and the outcry in Europe, where the press and governments portrayed the events as a massacre of Christians, produced an immediate Ottoman and European response. Fearing foreign intervention, the Ottoman government sent Fuad Pasha, one of the leading Tanzimat statesmen and then foreign minister, to Syria with extraordinary powers. Fuad arrived in Beirut in July 1860 and proceeded to Damascus, where he established a special tribunal, arrested and tried officials and notables, and ordered executions. The Ottoman governor of Damascus, Ahmad Pasha, was among those condemned and executed, as were a number of other officials and individuals implicated in the violence. Khurshid Pasha and other officials were also tried. Fuad imposed collective indemnities and ordered reconstruction. Druze notables, including Said Janbalat, were also tried and condemned. Fuad's measures were severe, and some contemporaries and historians have observed that they served to demonstrate Ottoman sovereignty and pre-empt a larger European role, as well as to deliver justice.

Europe nevertheless intervened. In August 1860 the powers, led by France, agreed to send a French expeditionary force to Syria, with a limited mandate and duration. About six thousand French troops landed at Beirut that month under General Beaufort d'Hautpoul. Their presence reflected Napoleon III's desire to demonstrate France's role as protector of Catholics and to enhance his prestige, and it provoked British suspicion of French ambitions. The powers established an international commission in Beirut, with representatives of Britain, France, Austria, Prussia, Russia, and the Ottoman Empire, to investigate the events, determine responsibility and compensation, and propose a new administrative structure for Mount Lebanon. The French force remained until June 1861, a period during which it had a limited military role, and it withdrew once the new regime was in place.

The commission's deliberations revealed the powers' differing interests. France favored an arrangement that would protect Maronites and its own influence, possibly including a single Christian governor with considerable autonomy. Britain, wary of a French-dominated Lebanon and sympathetic to the Druze and to Ottoman integrity, preferred to limit autonomy. Russia supported measures benefitting Orthodox Christians, and Austria supported Catholics. The Ottomans sought to preserve sovereignty and avoid a precedent for foreign tutelage. The eventual agreement was a compromise among these positions, and it was embodied in the Organic Regulation (Règlement organique) promulgated on 9 June 1861.

The Organic Regulation created the autonomous Ottoman district known as the Mount Lebanon mutasarrifate. It was governed by a mutasarrif—an Ottoman Christian chosen from outside Mount Lebanon and appointed by the Sublime Porte—whose authority derived directly from Istanbul. The first governor was Daud Pasha, an Armenian Catholic Ottoman official. Under Article 2 of the regulation dated 9 June 1861, the twelve-member central administrative council consisted of two representatives each from six communities: Maronites, Druze, Greek Orthodox, Greek Catholics, Sunni Muslims, and Shia Muslims (called Metualis in the French text). It advised the governor and had fiscal functions. The text provided for six administrative districts, local administration and security, abolition of the hereditary privileges of the muqata'jis, legal equality and fiscal arrangements. These provisions expressed an Ottoman commitment to provincial order alongside an international diplomatic compromise (1861 and 1864 original French texts).

The revised Organic Regulation of 6 September 1864 retained the twelve-member council but changed its confessional composition to four Maronites, three Druze, two Greek Orthodox, one Greek Catholic, one Sunni Muslim, and one Shia Muslim. The change, unlike the equal six-by-two allocation in 1861, was defended as reflecting the population and landed interests of different districts. The revised provisions also reorganized administrative districts and confirmed arrangements for the governor’s term and appointment. The distinction between the 1861 and 1864 council arrangements is crucial: it demonstrates that communal representation was negotiated and revised rather than fixed from the outset. The revised settlement formed the foundation of Mount Lebanon’s government until World War I, and historians such as Engin Deniz Akarlı have examined both its achievements and its constraints in The Long Peace. It excluded Beirut, Tripoli, Sidon, and much of the grain-producing Biqa', leaving the mountain dependent on wider commercial and agricultural connections.

The legal status of Mount Lebanon must be stated precisely. It remained under Ottoman sovereignty and was not an independent state. Historical Debate I

Inherited Sectarian Hostility or Nineteenth-Century Political Transformation?

The question: Was the violence of 1860 the expression of long-standing religious antagonism among Christians, Druze, and Muslims, or the product of specific nineteenth-century developments?

Older and popular interpretations — enduring communal antagonism: Nineteenth-century European observers and some later narratives described the conflict as a manifestation of old confessional rivalry, often depicting Druze and Maronites as traditional enemies, or Muslims as fanatically hostile to Christians. These accounts shaped European policy and public opinion and have persisted in popular explanations. Their weaknesses are the evidence of earlier cooperation, the cross-cutting alliances among Druze and Maronite notables, and the absence of comparable violence for long periods.
Ussama Makdisi and related work — the production of modern sectarianism: Makdisi argues in The Culture of Sectarianism that the violence was bound up with Ottoman reform, European intervention, and the elaboration of new political discourses in which religious identity became the language of politics. On this view, communal identity as a political organizing principle was produced, not simply inherited, in the nineteenth century, and the 1858 Kisrawan revolt and the 1860 war are part of that process.
Leila Fawaz and social-economic interpretations: Fawaz's An Occasion for War examines both Mount Lebanon and Damascus, emphasizing social and economic conditions, the effects of European commerce and the reforms on different groups, local politics, and the failures of Ottoman authority. Her approach allows for the specific circumstances of each place and treats the violence as a result of interacting local and international pressures.

Evidence: All serious interpretations rely on the same range of sources, from consular reports to local chronicles, and differ chiefly in their weighting of religion, class, state failure, and foreign involvement.

Why it matters: The chapter does not claim that a single explanation accounts for all events. It shows that religious identities carried new political meanings under identifiable circumstances: reform language, changing tax and land relations, rivalry for foreign patronage, and weak state protection. Religion mattered, but as part of a historical configuration and not as a timeless cause.

Section 104.9

Crimea, Russian Imperial Expansion, and the Displacement of Muslim Communities

The Crimean Khanate, a Muslim Turkic state ruled by the Giray dynasty and long under Ottoman suzerainty, was annexed by the Russian Empire in 1783, as recounted in the diplomatic and military context of Chapter 89. For the Crimean Tatars the annexation meant the end of their state and the reorganization of their society under Russian law. The Tatar nobility, the mirzas, were incorporated into the Russian nobility on certain conditions, Muslim religious endowments (waqfs) were gradually subjected to state control and secularization, and land was granted to Russian nobles and settlers. The Tatar peasantry found their access to land, their religious institutions, and their traditional legal arrangements changing, and the pressure on their position increased as Russian and other settlement of the peninsula and the northern Black Sea steppe expanded.

Emigration from Crimea to Ottoman lands began almost immediately and continued in waves. Some left in the 1780s and 1790s; further departures occurred after the Russo-Ottoman wars and at times of heightened pressure, including around 1812. The largest wave of the early nineteenth century followed the Crimean War of 1853–1856, when Russian authorities suspected Tatar loyalty, military operations disrupted the peninsula, and rumors and pressures encouraged departure. Estimates for the number of Crimean Tatars and associated Nogais who left in the years around 1860 vary considerably, with figures of well over a hundred thousand frequently cited and higher totals proposed by some scholars. Because Russian and Ottoman officials counted differently and because many left informally, no figure should be treated as exact.

Historians distinguish among kinds of movement. Some emigrants were expelled under direct compulsion. Others left under pressure, including confiscation of land, suspicion of disloyalty, and fear of conscription or religious pressure, in a situation in which staying was possible but costly. Others fled warfare. Many left voluntarily to join co-religionists in Ottoman territory, motivated by religious solidarity and by the Ottoman government's invitation. The categories blur, and sources from the two empires describe the same movements in different terms: Russian officials often portrayed emigration as voluntary, while Tatar accounts and some Ottoman records stress pressure and loss. The history of Muslim displacement from the Russian sphere cannot be reduced to a single formula, and this chapter uses terms such as expulsion, flight, and coerced emigration deliberately.

The Nogais, a Turkic pastoralist people of the steppe north of the Black Sea, experienced parallel pressures. The Russian state's settlement of the steppe, its policies toward pastoral nomads, and the wars of the era undermined their way of life, and tens of thousands moved into Ottoman territory in the 1850s and 1860s, many settling in Dobruja and Anatolia. For all these communities, the Ottoman Empire appeared as the natural refuge: it was a Muslim state, the sultan was also caliph in Ottoman self-presentation, and the government had cultural and political ties to the Crimean Tatars dating to the era of Ottoman suzerainty.

Ottoman policy toward these arrivals developed over time. In the early period the state received migrants informally, but the scale of arrivals in the mid-nineteenth century, together with the Ottoman government's own interests in settling uncultivated land, led to more systematic approaches. An imperial decree of 1857 on immigration offered land and temporary exemptions to settlers, and the arrival of Crimean Tatars after the war accelerated administrative experimentation. These developments set the pattern for the larger Circassian crisis discussed in the following sections, and they show that Ottoman refugee policy was already forming before 1860.

The Crimean case also reveals how the Black Sea, as a connective waterway discussed in Chapter 2, shaped refugee movement. The migrants crossed by sea, and ports on the Black Sea coast, such as Varna, Constanța, Sinop, Samsun, and Trabzon, became reception points. Steamship companies and sailing vessels carried them. The movement thus depended on commercial and maritime networks that also served trade, and it had consequences for those port cities, whose populations had to absorb arrivals and, in some cases, epidemic disease.

Section 104.10

The Russian Conquest of the Caucasus and the Destruction of Local Autonomy

The North Caucasus was a mountainous region of many peoples, languages, and political forms. Along the northwestern slopes toward the Black Sea lived the Circassians, or Adyghe-speaking peoples, who included the Shapsug, Abzakh, Natukhai, Bzhedug, and others, and the Ubykh, who spoke a distinct language. Abkhaz and related peoples lived farther southeast along the coast. In the central and eastern North Caucasus lived the Kabardians (who were Circassians in language but historically in a distinct political setting), Chechens, Ingush, Ossetians, Karachay, Balkars, and the many peoples of Dagestan. These peoples were differentiated by language, religion, social structure, and politics: some societies had princes and nobles, others were organized through assemblies of free villagers, and Islam had spread unevenly, with some groups, such as the Ubykh and parts of Circassia, partly Muslim and partly holding earlier religious practices, and others, such as the Dagestanis and Chechens, strongly connected to Sufi orders. It is essential not to treat all these peoples as a single "Caucasian Muslim" population.

Circassians were not unknown to the Ottoman world. Circassian soldiers and administrators had served in the Mamluk state of Egypt (discussed in Chapter 65), and the commerce in Circassian slaves had long connected the Caucasus to Ottoman and Egyptian elites, a trade that the Russian authorities and Europeans condemned and that diminished in the nineteenth century. The Ottoman government had sovereignty claims over the Black Sea coast, and the Treaty of Adrianople of 1829, which ended a Russo-Ottoman war, transferred Ottoman claims to the Russian Empire. The Circassians, who had not been consulted and did not regard themselves as Ottoman subjects in any ordinary sense, rejected the transfer. Russian expansion thus met resistance on the coast as well as in the mountains.

Russian conquest of the Caucasus proceeded over decades. Campaigns in the early nineteenth century established fortified lines and Cossack settlements on the northern edges of the mountains, and resistance movements arose. In the eastern Caucasus, Imam Shamil led a Chechen and Dagestani state and army that resisted for many years until his surrender at Gunib in 1859. With the east largely subdued, Russia turned its forces to the northwest. From about 1860 under commanders such as Nikolai Evdokimov, with the strategic direction and support of Minister of War Dmitry Milyutin, Russian forces systematically advanced into Circassian territory. They built roads and forts, cut forests, destroyed villages and crops, and pushed populations toward the plains, where they would be under Russian supervision, or toward the coast, from which they could leave.

Russian officials had several objectives. They sought to secure the new Black Sea coast and the borderland, to eliminate a centre of resistance that had defied them for decades, to settle the land with Cossacks and other settlers loyal to the state, and to fulfill a vision of a consolidated imperial frontier. Evidence from Russian administrative and military reports shows that officials considered the removal of the mountain population as a means of achieving these ends, and that they pressed communities to choose between submission and resettlement on Russian terms or departure. Some officials welcomed the departure of Muslims to the Ottoman Empire, and the scale of removal went well beyond what military necessity alone required.

Circassian societies were not passive. Their resistance was organized through alliances of villages and tribal assemblies, with support from some Ottoman and British interests who saw advantage in opposing Russia. British agents and journalists visited the coast in the 1830s, and a few Europeans and Poles fought alongside the Circassians. But the Circassians lacked a unified state, and foreign support did not materialize in the form of decisive intervention. By 1863–1864 their military position had collapsed, and the Russian conquest of the western Caucasus was declared complete in May 1864 with a ceremony at Krasnaya Polyana (the Circassian Qbaada) in the mountains.

The conquest affected other peoples differently. Chechens and Dagestanis had been defeated earlier and experienced Russian rule, repression, and in some places further migration, including a significant movement of Chechens to the Ottoman Empire in the mid-1860s, partly in response to the post-Shamil settlement and Russian policies. Abkhazians, after an uprising in 1866, were also affected by deportations and emigration. Nogais, Karachays, and others experienced varying pressures. The overall effect was a significant reduction of the Muslim populations of the northwest Caucasus and a transformation of the region's demography, and the next section examines the culminating events.

Section 104.11

The Circassian Expulsions of 1864 and the Humanitarian Catastrophe

In the spring of 1864, as Russian forces completed their advance, tens of thousands of Circassians and associated peoples gathered along the Black Sea coast and in the lowlands, awaiting ships to carry them to the Ottoman Empire. Many had lost their homes and crops to Russian military operations; others had been compelled to leave under threat. They came with whatever they could carry: livestock, tools, bedding, and household goods were often left behind or sold for nominal sums. Reports from the coast describe crowded and unsanitary camps where hunger, exposure, and disease spread, and where families waited for passage.

The transport was chaotic. Ships of various sizes, Ottoman, Russian-chartered, and European-owned vessels, carried the migrants across the Black Sea to ports such as Trabzon, Samsun, Sinop, Varna, and Constanța. Many vessels were badly overcrowded, with passengers packed in holds and on decks. The voyages exposed people to storms and to the contagious diseases circulating in the camps, and some vessels reportedly foundered. Families were separated in the confusion of embarkation and arrival. At the Ottoman ports, authorities and local residents faced the arrival of thousands of destitute and sick people for whom shelter, food, and medical care were lacking. Epidemic disease, including typhus and smallpox, killed large numbers in the camps and during onward movement, and the host cities themselves suffered outbreaks.

Numbers are among the most contested aspects of this history. Estimates for the Circassian departure of the early to mid-1860s run into the hundreds of thousands, and broader estimates for North Caucasian Muslim migration to Ottoman territory from the 1850s to the early twentieth century run higher, with scholars proposing totals above a million. Mortality estimates vary widely, from the tens of thousands to figures several times larger, and they depend on assumptions about pre-1864 population, the number of arrivals, and deaths in the camps and on the journey. Russian statistics, Ottoman registers, consular estimates, and later scholarly reconstructions all have limitations: they count different things at different times, and they were produced for administrative or political purposes. Students should treat any single figure as an estimate and ask how it was derived.

Historians agree that the expulsion involved the forced removal or flight of a very large part of the Circassian population under conditions of extreme hardship and that Russian policy was central to it. They differ about the appropriate characterization. Many scholars describe the process as ethnic cleansing, meaning the forcible removal of a population from a territory. Some scholars, including Walter Richmond in The Circassian Genocide, argue that it constitutes genocide, pointing to deliberate military policy, the destruction of the means of life, and the scale of death. The Georgian parliament formally recognized the events as genocide in 2011, while the Russian government rejects that characterization. Other historians question whether the evidence of intent to destroy the Circassians as a people, as the legal definition of genocide requires, has been established, while not disputing the severity of the displacement and mortality. The questions involved are about legal and historical classification and about the interpretation of intent, and they are separate from the established fact of mass forced removal.

Relief efforts were limited. The Ottoman government, which had invited the migration in principle and had established refugee machinery in 1860, was overwhelmed by the scale and speed of arrivals. It provided transport in some cases, food rations, and temporary shelter, and it instructed provincial authorities to receive the migrants, but resources were inadequate. British and other consuls reported on conditions, and charitable appeals reached Europe, yet foreign relief was small relative to need. Local Muslim and Christian residents of the port cities and their hinterlands provided shelter and help in many cases, and also bore the burdens of epidemics and shortages. Conditions varied by port and by year.

For the refugees, the exodus was a catastrophe whose memory remained central to Circassian communal identity. Survivors spoke of the loss of homeland, the deaths on the journey, and the difficulties of arrival, and these memories became part of oral tradition and later political discourse among Circassian diaspora communities in Turkey, Syria, Jordan, and elsewhere, as well as among those who remained in the Caucasus. The Ubykh, whose homeland was on the coast, left almost entirely, and the Ubykh language later became extinct in the late twentieth century, a long-term linguistic consequence of dispersal. The question of how to remember and name the events continues to shape politics, a point to which Chapter 162 and later volumes return only insofar as relevant to the region's contemporary geopolitics.

Historical Vocabulary
Naming Mass Violence and Displacement

Historians use several terms for forced population movement and mass violence, and each has a specific meaning. Forced migration is the broad category of movement under compulsion or threat. Deportation and expulsion refer to removal by state action. Ethnic cleansing, a term that entered wide use in the late twentieth century, describes the forcible removal of an identified population from a territory, often through violence or intimidation, and it is widely applied retrospectively by historians to events such as the Circassian expulsions. Genocide has a defined meaning in the 1948 UN Genocide Convention: specified acts committed with intent to destroy, in whole or in part, a national, ethnical, racial, or religious group as such. Because genocide depends on a finding of intent, and because the term is also used in political and memorial discourse, its application to historical cases is often debated. A responsible student of history identifies what happened, who acted, who suffered, and what evidence supports each claim, before turning to the question of classification.

Section 104.12

The Ottoman Refugee Commission and the Development of Imperial Resettlement Policy

The Ottoman government established a refugee commission in 1860, in response to the growing arrivals from Crimea and the Caucasus. Known as the Muhacirin Komisyonu, it was charged with coordinating the reception, registration, transport, provisioning, and settlement of refugees, the muhacir (from the Arabic muhajir, one who migrates). The word carried religious resonance, recalling the early Muslim migration (hijra), and it helped frame the refugees as Muslims who had come to the abode of Islam, a framing that mattered to Ottoman legitimacy and to the refugees' own understanding of their displacement. The commission was later reorganized, and provincial and local committees were created to manage arrivals. Regulations issued over the next decade, culminating in a comprehensive refugee regulation in the mid-1860s, set out the rights and obligations of the refugees and the responsibilities of officials.

The commission emerged from earlier initiatives rather than a single original refugee law. Instructions to officials in the Silistra region in May 1856 dealt with the settlement and aid of Crimean migrants; the broader 1857 Ottoman Immigration Law set out an immigration framework; and the 1860 Muhacirin Komisyonu coordinated expanding relief and resettlement operations. Regulations and exemptions varied by period, geographic jurisdiction and household circumstances. It is not accurate to speak of an authenticated all-purpose 1866 regulation without identifying a specific dated document. Hamed-Troyansky documents the 1857 framework, the 1860 commission and its interaction with the 1858 Land Code in the Transjordan case (study and documentary references).

The administrative work was substantial. Officials needed to register arrivals, identify their communities of origin, determine where they could be settled, and arrange transport, often over long distances and rough terrain. Refugees were provided with rations for a period, and the state offered land, seed, and sometimes tools or livestock, along with temporary exemptions from taxes and military service for a number of years. Land allocation required surveys and registration, using the cadastral framework created by the Land Code of 1858, and conflicts over titles were common. The state also had to coordinate with provincial governors, local councils, and in places with religious institutions and charitable donors who contributed funds and goods. By the 1860s and 1870s this effort had created a recognizable bureaucratic field of migration management.

The commission's work was also fiscal and political. The state's finances were strained, and the cost of relief competed with other needs, a pressure that became acute in the years leading to the financial crisis of the 1870s discussed in Chapter 96. Provincial governments carried much of the burden, and they relied on local communities to host and support arrivals. This produced tensions, since receiving communities, including long-established villagers, townsmen, and pastoralists, faced competition for land, grazing, and resources, and they resented the privileges, such as tax exemptions, that refugees received. At the same time, refugee settlement offered the state advantages: it brought uncultivated land into production and thereby promised increased revenue, it placed loyal populations on frontier and insecure routes, and it strengthened the empire's Muslim character in areas of mixed population.

Historians therefore emphasize that refugee policy served multiple purposes. Humanitarian obligations were real: sultans and officials publicly acknowledged a duty to assist Muslim co-religionists, charitable campaigns were organized, and some officials worked hard to reduce suffering. Fiscal motives, including the desire to expand the tax base, were evident. Military and security objectives were visible in the decision to settle some groups along strategic roads, borders, and areas inhabited by nomads or restive populations. Territorial objectives, including the consolidation of Ottoman control over regions of weak authority, were also present. These purposes did not always conflict, but they sometimes did, and the next division examines how they interacted on the ground. Research by Kemal Karpat, Vladimir Hamed-Troyansky, and others has shown how migration became an increasingly centralized field of imperial policy, and how refugee administration was a testing ground for Ottoman state capacity.

The refugee administration also had effects on the state's relationship with its subjects. By registering people and land, assigning them to places, and regulating their movement, it extended the state's knowledge and control. The practice of classifying people as muhacir, as distinct from other subjects, created a new administrative category with legal consequences. Over time, refugees and their descendants became, in Ottoman and later Turkish and Arab contexts, communities with distinctive identities, such as the Circassian, Chechen, Tatar, and Bosniak communities that persist in the modern Middle East. The refugee commission thus helped shape both the empire's administrative structure and the social landscape of the region.

Historical Vocabulary
Muhacir, Refugee Commission, and Resettlement

The Ottoman term muhacir (plural muhacirin) designated Muslim migrants or refugees who entered the empire from lands that had passed under non-Muslim rule or from regions of conflict. The term did not distinguish between voluntary and coerced departure, and it was applied to Crimean Tatars, Circassians, Balkan Muslims, and others. The refugee commission was the central body established in 1860 to administer reception and settlement. Imperial resettlement refers to the state's allocation of refugees to particular places, a practice that served humanitarian, fiscal, military, and territorial purposes. Because the Ottoman state defined its obligations primarily toward Muslims, the category left out displaced non-Muslims, whose treatment depended on other institutions and circumstances. Historians use the term muhacir because it is the language of the sources, while also analyzing how that language shaped policy.

Division IV · Sections 104.13–104.16

Resettlement, Land, and the Transformation of Local Societies

The fourth division explains what happened after refugees arrived. It follows them into Anatolia, the Black Sea region, the Balkans, Syria, the Golan, and Transjordan, and examines how new settlements changed existing communities and how displaced people rebuilt their lives.

Section 104.13

Refugee Settlement in Anatolia, the Black Sea Region, and the Ottoman Balkans

The Ottoman government distributed refugees across a wide geography. The first destinations were the Black Sea ports and their hinterlands. From Trabzon and Samsun, Circassians and others were directed inland into the valleys and plains of northern and central Anatolia, including districts around Samsun and Çarşamba, Sivas, Tokat, Amasya, Kayseri, and the Sakarya and Düzce regions toward the northwest. Others were settled in the Balkans, particularly in the Danubian provinces of northern Bulgaria (Vidin, Lom, Ruse, and neighboring districts) and in Dobruja, where Crimean Tatars and Nogais concentrated. After 1864 the state also began to send refugees to Syria and the southern provinces, discussed in the next section.

Placement followed several logics. Officials looked for land they regarded as underused, including marshy lowlands, forested areas, and the margins of pastoral zones, and they preferred locations along roads and routes where a loyal agricultural population could secure communications. They also sought to avoid concentrations of refugees large enough to overwhelm local communities, though the shortage of suitable land and the pressure of arrivals often overrode such intentions. In regions with mixed populations, such as the Danubian provinces, resettlement of Muslim refugees alongside Bulgarian and other Christian villagers shaped local relations and became a point of political tension, and the effects of these placements would be felt in the Balkan crisis of 1875–1878.

The conditions of initial settlement were harsh. Refugees often arrived in poor health, and mortality in the first winters was high. Housing was improvised: families lived in tents, huts, caves, or shared quarters in existing villages while building their own homes. Food supplies depended on the rations the state could provide, which were frequently delayed or insufficient, and on assistance from neighbors. Settlement in low-lying, marshy areas exposed people to malaria and other diseases. Land registration was often slow, so that families worked fields without secure title, and disputes arose over boundaries, with the state's allocations sometimes overlapping the claims or traditional use rights of local villagers or pastoralists. Many refugees lacked the tools, seed, and animals to cultivate their land, and the state's assistance was uneven.

Over time many settlements stabilized. Circassian and Tatar refugees, who included skilled farmers, horse breeders, and artisans, founded new villages or expanded existing ones, introduced or extended crops and techniques, and connected to markets. Tatar and Nogai farmers in Dobruja and Anatolia brought experience of steppe agriculture; Circassians contributed horse-breeding and mounted skills, as well as agriculture and woodworking. Some settlements became centers of local commerce. The refugees also acquired roles in the state's security apparatus: Circassians, in particular, were recruited in large numbers to irregular units and gendarmerie, and their reputation as warriors influenced official thinking about where to settle them.

The consequences for neighboring communities varied. In some places refugee settlement increased cultivation and trade and led to cooperation. In others, villagers lost access to land and pasture that had been informally used, and tensions grew. The most serious conflicts occurred where refugees had been settled among Christian populations, such as in parts of the Balkans and in some districts of Anatolia, where complaints of seizure of land, theft, and violence appear in consular and local records, as well as accusations that refugees and irregulars attacked Christian villagers. Historians caution that such accounts were often politically charged, and that conflict was not universal; but the pattern of friction between refugees and established populations, and between refugees and Ottoman officials who struggled to restrain them, is documented.

Other factors complicated these settlements. Ottoman officials sometimes moved refugees a second time, because land proved unsuitable, because of local opposition, or because of military or political needs. The loss of the Danubian provinces to Bulgaria's autonomy in 1878 forced many Circassians and Tatars there to flee again into Ottoman territory, a pattern of repeated displacement that was a major feature of nineteenth-century refugee life. Such double migration helps explain why some Circassian settlements in Syria and Transjordan were founded by people who had first been settled in the Balkans.

Section 104.14

Circassians and Chechens in Ottoman Syria, the Golan, and Transjordan

From the 1860s the Ottoman government directed substantial numbers of Circassians and, later, Chechens and other North Caucasians to Ottoman Syria. In the Golan (Jawlan), settlement began around Quneitra and neighboring villages in the 1860s and 1870s, and Circassian and Chechen villages grew there alongside Druze, Arab, Turkmen, and other communities. In the Hawran and the Damascus region, further settlements appeared. East of the Jordan River, in the district historically linked to the Balqa and the Hawran frontier, Circassians established settlements at Amman beginning in 1878, at Wadi al-Sir in 1880, at Jerash in 1884, at Naʿur in 1901 and at al-Rusayfa in 1904. Chechens established distinct communities at al-Zarqaʾ (1902), al-Sukhna (1905) and Sweileh (1906). These dates, established from Ottoman land and court records, should not be merged into a generic 1878 wave. The overall settlement pattern created a chain of villages along the edge of the steppe, linking the Golan, the Hawran, and the eastern Jordan plateau.

These were not empty lands. The Transjordanian and Golan steppe margins were inhabited by Bedouin tribes who grazed their flocks seasonally and by village cultivators; towns such as Salt, linked by merchants and religious institutions to Damascus and Palestine, were centers of local commerce. Amman itself, a site of ancient Philadelphia with Roman and earlier remains, was largely a ruin before 1878, used as seasonal grazing and as a watering place, though it was not devoid of earlier history or of occasional use. The Circassian settlers used the ruins as a source of stone, rebuilt houses, and farmed the valley's springs and fields. The idea that Amman was founded from nothing by refugees is incorrect, and the careful historical statement is that Circassian settlement transformed the site into a permanent agricultural and commercial town.

Vladimir Hamed-Troyansky's research, drawing on Ottoman land registers, Circassian testimony, and other sources, shows how the settlers' relationship to land, commerce, and the state shaped Amman's development. The Circassians received land from the state and registered it, farmed grain and other crops, raised animals, and, as the settlement grew, engaged in trade with Salt, Damascus, and the Hijaz. They also sold or leased land to merchants, including those from Salt and from Damascus, and borrowed capital, and many individuals and families who began as refugees moved into positions as landholders, small traders, and local notables. The arrival of the Hijaz Railway, which reached Amman in 1903 and Medina in 1908, accelerated the town's growth, and Circassians took part as contractors, laborers, and suppliers, while the railway gave Amman a new economic function as a grain-market and transport node.

Relations among the settlers, Bedouin communities, villagers, and the state were complex. The Ottoman authorities settled the Circassians partly to extend agriculture and to create loyal communities able to assist in maintaining security on a frontier where the government's control was incomplete. Bedouin tribes, such as segments of the Bani Sakhr and the Adwan, whose pasture and water arrangements were affected by the new villages, sometimes raided or contested the settlers, and the Circassians in turn defended their villages, a pattern recorded in Ottoman reports. Over time, negotiated arrangements also developed: payments for protection, shared use of water and grazing in certain seasons, intermarriage, commercial exchange, and alliances. Neither "conflict" nor "harmony" adequately describes these relations. They varied by place and period, and they changed as the state, the economy, and the settlers' strength changed.

Quneitra provides a comparison. Its Circassian settlement, begun in the 1860s and 1870s, interacted with a landscape of Druze, Arab, and other villages and with pastoralists of the Golan plateau, and it became the district's administrative and market center. Research on the Ottoman Jawlan shows that refugee settlement occurred within established village and pastoral societies, with continuing negotiation over land and resources, and not in an empty territory. Differences between Amman and Quneitra illustrate how local geography and economy influenced outcomes: Amman's connection to the railway and to Salt's merchants and its location as a regional crossroads gave it greater scope for later growth, while Quneitra's position on a frontier plateau with a pre-existing network of villages shaped a more regionally embedded settlement.

The Chechens, who generally settled somewhat later and in smaller numbers, formed distinct communities with their own Sufi networks and customary law, and they too took on roles in agriculture and local security. Different groups of North Caucasians retained separate languages, social structures, and sometimes rivalries, and Ottoman officials were aware of the distinctions even as local populations might refer to all of them as "Circassians" (Sharakisa). Students should keep these distinctions in mind and avoid collapsing different groups into one.

Used with other sources, they show how refugees became property-holding participants in local commerce and how the state's registration of land created legal structures that influenced later conflicts over ownership.

Section 104.15

Land, Pastoral Communities, Agricultural Expansion, and Local Conflict

The economic effects of resettlement depended on how land, water, and labor were allocated. The Ottoman Land Code of 1858 and subsequent registration campaigns sought to establish individual or family title to agricultural land, to ensure that taxes could be collected from identifiable holders, and to bring cultivation under state oversight. Refugee allocations were part of this framework. In many places, however, the land granted to refugees was already used by others under customary arrangements that were not fully recognized in law: villagers who held land communally and redivided it periodically, and pastoral groups who grazed seasonally on commons. The collision between registered individual title and customary use was a source of conflict across the empire, and refugee settlement sharpened it.

Pastoralists were particularly affected. Bedouin and semi-nomadic groups depended on access to pasture and water in varying seasons, and the settlement of agricultural villages along the steppe margin restricted movement, closed off springs, and reduced grazing. Some tribes adapted by cultivating, combining herding with farming, and later by acquiring land themselves; others resisted. The state's policy of "settling" nomads, a theme of Ottoman policy in this period discussed in Chapter 83 for earlier centuries, reinforced the impetus to expand cultivation. Refugee settlement was therefore one component of a broader shift in which the agricultural frontier moved eastward and pastoral spaces contracted.

Taxation shaped local attitudes. Refugees' temporary exemptions from taxes and conscription meant that existing villagers bore a greater share of fiscal burdens in the short term, and the end of exemptions, when tax obligations began, could be difficult for refugee households with limited resources. The state's collection of tithes and other taxes in newly settled areas also required local intermediaries, creating opportunities for officials, tax farmers, and merchants. In some districts, refugee communities developed relationships with these intermediaries and gained influence, while in others they were exploited by moneylenders and by officials who took bribes.

Records document a range of relations between refugees and neighbors. There were cases of cooperation: shared use of wells, exchange of produce for livestock, hiring of labor, marriage alliances, and joint defense against raiders. There were also violent conflicts over land, water, and livestock, and incidents of raiding and retaliation recorded in Ottoman correspondence and in local memory. Some conflicts reflected the specific vulnerability of newly arrived groups with no kin networks, while others reflected the readiness of Circassian and Chechen communities, many with strong martial traditions, to defend their holdings. Ottoman officials often responded by mediating, by deploying troops, or by incorporating the settlers into security arrangements.

A central historical question concerns whether resettlement always strengthened central authority. In some respects it did: the state extended registration and taxation into frontier areas, created settlements loyal to the sultan, and used refugee communities as a source of gendarmes and irregular troops. In other respects it created new local power centers. Circassian leaders, with their own followings, landholdings, and ties to officials, acquired influence in their districts, sometimes acting as intermediaries between the state and Bedouin or villagers, and sometimes bargaining with the state from positions of local strength. In Amman, merchants and landholders of Circassian and other origins became notables in the town's emerging society. The pattern is therefore not one of straightforward centralization, and it varied by place.

The economic effects were substantial in aggregate. New villages brought land under cultivation, increased grain production, and fed growing markets in Damascus and Beirut, and later the Hijaz Railway's transport of grain; some historians link the expansion of cultivation to the broader commercial agriculture of the late nineteenth century analyzed in Chapter 96. At the same time, the costs were borne unevenly: pastoralists and some villagers lost access to resources, refugees themselves suffered high mortality and poverty, and the state's investment, in a period of financial strain, was limited. The comparison of Amman, where settlement fed a rapidly growing market town, with Quneitra, where it joined an already dense network of villages, shows how the same policy yielded different results.

Section 104.16

Refugee Communities, Religious Institutions, Language, and Everyday Life

After arrival, refugees had to rebuild the institutions that organized daily life. Family and kin networks were the foundation: households reconstituted themselves from survivors, widows and orphans were absorbed into extended families, and marriage within and across refugee communities helped reconstruct social networks. Among Circassians, customary law (known as adat or khabze) continued to regulate marriage, hospitality, conflict resolution, and honor, though it adapted to Ottoman legal frameworks, and the rituals of village assemblies and elders' councils provided continuity. Among Chechens, Sufi brotherhoods, notably the Qadiri and Naqshbandi orders, served as religious and social organizations that sustained community discipline and identity in exile.

Religious practice both connected refugees to their Ottoman neighbors and preserved distinct traditions. Most North Caucasian refugees were Muslims, mainly Sunni, and they quickly built mosques and sought imams and teachers; Ottoman religious institutions, including endowment resources and the provincial religious administration, supported some of this. Differences remained between Caucasian customs and those of local Muslims, and Ottoman and Arab neighbors sometimes regarded the newcomers as unfamiliar. Crimean Tatars, with a long tradition of religious education, contributed teachers and scholars to Anatolian and Balkan communities.

Language was a central issue. Circassian and Chechen refugees spoke languages unrelated to Turkish or Arabic, and the first generation largely retained them at home while learning Ottoman Turkish or Arabic for dealings with officials and merchants. In Syria and Transjordan, Circassians increasingly used Arabic in public life, though the Adyghe and Kabardian languages continued in the family and community and survive in the region today. Schooling varied: Ottoman state schools were expanding in the late nineteenth century, and some refugee children attended them, gaining literacy in Ottoman Turkish and later Arabic, while others received only religious instruction. Children raised in the empire often experienced a different identity from their parents, with different linguistic repertoires and loyalties, and many later served in Ottoman schools, the army, or the bureaucracy.

Women's responsibilities were substantial. In newly settled villages, women worked in agriculture and animal husbandry, maintained the household, and sustained community life, and in the absence of men lost to war or to military service they often managed property. Evidence from land and court records indicates that Muslim women, including refugee women, could hold, inherit, and transfer property under Islamic law, and the case from Amman discussed above suggests that they took part in commercial transactions. Marriage practices adapted: bride-price and the importance of family reputation continued, while new marriages crossed lines of origin as communities integrated. Daily work of spinning, weaving, cooking, and care for the ill and old formed the continuous background to the political history.

Military service and employment connected refugees to the state. Circassians, Chechens, and others served in Ottoman gendarmerie and irregular formations, and some rose in the army and administration, a pathway to security and status. Others found work in agriculture, crafts, and commerce, and the Hijaz Railway provided employment in construction and operations. These roles reinforced the refugees' ties to the state but also made them agents of it in their dealings with neighboring populations, which affected how others perceived them.

Memory of displacement shaped identity. Oral traditions, songs, and family stories preserved accounts of the lost homeland and the journey, and community associations, which multiplied in the late Ottoman and later periods, gave these memories institutional form. At the same time, new local loyalties developed alongside the inherited ones: refugees' children identified with the villages and towns where they were raised, with their Ottoman citizenship, and eventually with the successor states in which they found themselves. The double character of this identity, rooted in a lost Caucasian homeland and in a new Middle Eastern home, is a hallmark of these communities and a reminder that the refugees became part of the region's society rather than remaining outsiders to it.

Section 104.17

The Russo-Ottoman War of 1877–1878 and Muslim Refugees from the Balkans

The Russo-Ottoman War of 1877–1878 followed the Balkan crisis that began with the Herzegovina uprising of 1875 and the Bulgarian April Uprising of 1876, whose suppression by Ottoman irregular forces killed many Bulgarian civilians and provoked an international outcry, as noted in Chapter 95. Russia declared war in April 1877, and its armies, aided by Romanian and Bulgarian volunteers and by Serbian and Montenegrin forces, advanced through the Balkans and into eastern Anatolia. Fighting in the Caucasus and the Balkans devastated civilian life. The Treaty of San Stefano in March 1878 imposed harsh terms, which the Congress of Berlin in June and July 1878 revised, creating an autonomous Bulgarian principality, an Ottoman province of Eastern Rumelia, and a reorganization of territory that reduced Ottoman holdings in the Balkans and transferred Kars, Ardahan, and Batum in the Caucasus to Russia.

The war produced one of the largest refugee movements of the century. As Russian and allied forces advanced, hundreds of thousands of Muslims, including Turks, Bulgarian-speaking Muslims (Pomaks), Tatars, Circassians, and others, fled or were expelled from Bulgaria and neighboring regions, and many died of violence, exposure, hunger, and epidemic disease. Estimates of the numbers displaced vary widely: scholars have proposed figures from several hundred thousand to over a million for the war and its immediate aftermath, and the lower and higher figures reflect differences in sources, in the period counted, and in the definition of "refugee." Justin McCarthy's studies of Muslim displacement in the Balkans have emphasized the enormous scale of loss and have been influential, while other historians have criticized aspects of his methods and conclusions, and no figures should be treated as precise.

The violence of the war was not one-sided. Muslim civilians suffered from attacks by Russian troops, Bulgarian irregulars, and local militias, and the withdrawal of Ottoman authority left them exposed. Christian civilians also suffered: Ottoman irregulars, including Circassians and Bashi-bazouks, had been implicated in violence against Bulgarians in 1876 and again during the war, and Christian villagers were displaced or killed in the fighting. Bulgarian, Serbian, Greek, and other non-Muslim populations in contested zones experienced displacement and loss. Acknowledging all these experiences is necessary for an accurate history, and it does not require equating them or ignoring differences in context, responsibility, and scale.

The refugees' routes took them toward the coasts and toward Istanbul, Edirne, Salonica, and the Anatolian interior. They traveled by foot, wagon, rail, and ship, and many gathered in overcrowded cities, mosques, schools, and other public buildings. Istanbul's population swelled with arrivals, with epidemics and food shortages afflicting both refugees and residents, and the government and charitable bodies struggled to respond. A refugee committee and relief funds, along with contributions from Muslim communities and foreign sympathizers including some in Britain, provided aid. The state, already facing a financial crisis and the diplomatic aftermath of defeat, lacked resources, and the refugee administration built since 1860 was pushed to its limits.

The arrival of these refugees reinforced the Ottoman state's emphasis on Anatolia and the Arab provinces as the empire's core. Many were settled in western and central Anatolia, in the Marmara region, and in parts of Syria and Iraq. The loss of the Balkan provinces, including areas with substantial Muslim populations, changed the empire's demographic balance: Muslims now formed a larger proportion of its remaining subjects. This shift influenced Hamidian policy, discussed in Chapter 98, and contributed to the political climate in which pan-Islamic ideology and Ottoman concern for Muslim solidarity gained force. The refugees' stories of loss at the hands of Christian states also shaped attitudes, with consequences for relations with Christian subjects that later chapters examine.

The refugee movements of 1877–1878 also link to the Circassian settlements of the Levant. Many Circassians who had been settled in the Danubian provinces after 1864 were displaced again, and the Ottoman government directed many of them to Syria and Transjordan, where the foundation of Amman and other villages in 1878 belongs to this chain of displacement. The historical connection illustrates how separate events, the Caucasian conquest, the Balkan war, and the settlement of the southern Levant, formed a single sequence in the experience of individual families.

Section 104.18

Bosniaks, Balkan Muslims, and Later Nineteenth-Century Refugee Movements

The Congress of Berlin authorized Austria-Hungary to occupy and administer Bosnia-Herzegovina, which remained formally Ottoman until Austria-Hungary annexed it in 1908 (a development belonging to Chapter 105's crisis context). The occupation of 1878 met armed resistance, and over the following decades many Bosnian Muslims, the Bosniaks, emigrated to Ottoman territory. Their motives varied: some left from fear of the new regime, others from economic hardship, from the pressures of land and tax reforms, or from a wish to live under Muslim rule; and the Austro-Hungarian administration at times restricted and at times tolerated departure. Bosniak migrants settled in Anatolia, Syria, and elsewhere, and their communities, including in Syria and later Jordan and Turkey, preserved distinct identities.

Other Balkan Muslims moved as territories changed hands. Muslim populations in Thessaly, ceded to Greece in 1881, in parts of Serbia and Montenegro after 1878, and in Bulgaria and Eastern Rumelia continued to leave in subsequent years, driven by insecurity, loss of land, discriminatory pressure, and the attraction of Ottoman lands. The scale was smaller than in 1877–1878, but cumulatively large. These movements were not uniform. Some regions saw large-scale emigration, others small, and some Muslim communities remained and adapted to the new states. Historians stress this variation and caution against assuming that all Balkan Muslims left.

Crete offers another case. The island, an Ottoman possession with a Christian Greek-speaking majority and a substantial Muslim minority, many of them Greek-speaking, experienced repeated insurrections in the nineteenth century, notably in 1866–1869 and in 1897–1898. During the crisis of the late 1890s, communal violence, the intervention of European powers, and the establishment of an autonomous Cretan State under international supervision in 1898 led thousands of Cretan Muslims to leave, many for Anatolia, Syria, Egypt, and Libya. Their descendants formed communities in cities such as Izmir, Ayvalık, Latakia, and Tripoli, and some retained the Cretan Greek dialect. Cretan Muslims therefore join Bosniaks and others as populations whose homeland ceased to be Ottoman and who became part of the empire's refugee society.

The refugee groups differed in social background, language, economic resources, and settlement experience. Bosniaks included landholders, officials, and urban professionals as well as peasants, and many arrived with some capital or skills. Crimean Tatars included an educated intelligentsia that later played a role in the Ottoman press and politics. Circassians included former notables and warriors alongside farmers. Cretan Muslims often had urban commercial backgrounds. These differences shaped their adaptation: those with education or capital integrated more quickly into urban and administrative life, while rural and poorer refugees faced longer struggles. The Ottoman state's category of muhacir covered all of them, but their lived experiences varied.

Taken together, these successive movements contributed substantially to the Muslim population of Anatolia and parts of the eastern Mediterranean. Scholars such as Kemal Karpat have emphasized that the cumulative immigration of the nineteenth century, drawn from the Crimea, the Caucasus, and the Balkans, changed the composition and character of Anatolia and the empire's Muslim population, and affected the later Turkish Republic. The chapter does not reproduce the story of the Balkan Wars of 1912–1913, which produced a further, massive refugee crisis and which belong to Chapter 107, but it is important to see that the 1912–1913 catastrophe came after more than a century of earlier displacements and that the Ottoman state had built administrative machinery for managing them.

Section 104.19

Population Registers, Settlement Maps, Communal Institutions, and Demographic Evidence

Reconstructing the demographic history of the late Ottoman Empire depends on sources that were produced for administrative purposes and that count different things. The Ottoman state conducted a census in 1831 aimed at males for taxation and conscription, and more systematic registration followed in the later decades. The census of 1881/82–1893 and the later census of 1905/06 recorded population by religious community and by sex, and the 1914 census recorded further categories. Provincial yearbooks (salnames) published statistical and administrative information about provinces and districts. Refugee commission registers recorded arrivals and settlement. Land registers, tax records, and court records document landholding, taxation, and legal transactions. Consuls, missionaries, and travelers produced their own estimates, and religious communities kept registers of baptisms, marriages, and deaths.

Each source has limitations. Ottoman censuses classified the population primarily by religion and not by language or ethnicity, so that Muslims were treated as a single category even though they spoke many languages, and non-Muslims were classified by religious community. Women and young children were undercounted in some enumerations, and nomadic and mountain populations were difficult to count, with administrators relying on estimates. Registration was linked to taxation and conscription, which gave households reasons to avoid counting. Refugee registers recorded people at the moment of arrival or assignment and did not follow those who left, died, or moved. Consular and missionary estimates reflected the interests and access of their authors, and communities themselves sometimes inflated figures for political reasons.

It is also essential to distinguish what is being counted. The number of refugees arriving at a port is not the number registered for resettlement, which is not the number who survived the first winters, which is not the number of permanent settlers or their descendants. Counting by household, by individual, by adult male, or by tax unit yields different results. Mortality in transit and in the first years of settlement is poorly documented, and double counting occurs when refugees moved between districts or were resettled a second time. These differences account for much of the divergence in published estimates of the scale of Muslim migration and of Circassian mortality, and they are reasons to attribute every figure to its source and method.

Communal institutions and population categories changed through state administration. The Ottoman classification of subjects by religious community, the system sometimes called the millet system, was not a fixed ancient arrangement but a set of practices that developed and was reformed in the nineteenth century, with councils and regulations that defined communal authority (see Chapters 84 and 92). Refugee administration created a new category, the muhacir, with distinct legal status. The Mount Lebanon regulations of 1861 established confessional representation in a formal political institution. Censuses and yearbooks made religion a standard category of statistical knowledge. Through these practices the state, and later the communities themselves, learned to think of the population as a set of enumerated communities, a mode of thinking that later nationalisms could adopt and adapt.

Settlement maps and demographic reconstructions allow historians to see how migration altered regional patterns. The creation of Circassian villages along the Syrian steppe margin, the concentration of Tatar and Nogai settlements in Dobruja and Anatolia, the growth of Muslim population in western Anatolia, the changing composition of Beirut and the mountain after 1860, and the loss of Muslim populations in the Balkans can all be traced through registers and maps. Modern historians, using geographic information systems and archival databases, have refined these reconstructions.

A methodological caution is necessary. Historical population figures are sometimes invoked in modern political arguments to claim entitlement to territory or to legitimate or delegitimate communities' presence. This chapter's evidence shows that regional demographic geography changed substantially in the nineteenth century, but it does not follow that any particular historical distribution confers a modern political or territorial right. Historians reconstruct population change in order to understand how societies were transformed, and they treat the use of such reconstructions as arguments in contemporary politics as a separate matter requiring its own analysis.

Historical Debate II

Was Ottoman Refugee Resettlement Humanitarian Assistance or Imperial State-Building?

The question: How should historians interpret the Ottoman state's reception and settlement of Muslim refugees: as welfare for co-religionists in distress, or as a strategy of population management and state consolidation?

Interpretation A — Humanitarian and religious obligation: Evidence supports the view that Ottoman rulers and many officials regarded assistance to Muslim refugees as a duty. The state invited migrants, paid for transport and rations, granted land and temporary tax exemptions, and encouraged charitable fundraising. Sultans and officials publicly framed their policy in religious and moral terms, and local Muslim communities provided help. On this view, the arrangements were an extension of state welfare and a response to real suffering.
Interpretation B — Strategy of state-building and population management: Other evidence emphasizes that settlement was directed toward the state's interests: expanding cultivation and revenue, populating frontier and insecure areas, securing roads and later railways, placing martial communities near restive populations, and reinforcing Muslim presence in strategic or mixed districts. Registration and land allocation extended the state's knowledge and control. On this view, refugee policy was a tool of centralization and territorial consolidation.
Synthesis — both purposes operated at once: Much recent scholarship, including work by Hamed-Troyansky and others on refugees and their property, argues that welfare and state-building were intertwined and that both must be studied. The two purposes sometimes reinforced one another, as when productive settlement helped refugees and increased revenue, and sometimes conflicted, as when strategic placement meant unsuitable land, or when state priorities exposed refugees to conflict with neighbors. The outcomes also depended on local conditions and on the refugees' own agency in negotiating land, work, and protection.

Evidence: The same archival records, decrees, registers, and provincial correspondence support different emphases, depending on whether the historian focuses on relief provisions or on placement and control.

Why it matters: The debate affects how historians understand the Ottoman state, the later demographic geography of Anatolia and the Levant, and the relationship between humanitarianism and imperial power more generally.

Section 104.20

The Middle East in 1914: Displacement, New Communities, and the Legacy of Imperial Population Politics

Comparing the Ottoman Levant, Anatolia, and the Black Sea region of 1840 with the same regions in 1914 reveals a transformed human geography. In 1840, Mount Lebanon was governed by an emirate resting on a hierarchy of Druze and Maronite notables; by 1914 it was an autonomous mutasarrifate with an administrative council representing recognized communities, an Ottoman-appointed Christian governor, and a European guarantee. In 1840, the Syrian steppe margins east of the Jordan and in the Golan were thinly settled and largely used by pastoralists; by 1914 there were Circassian, Chechen, and other villages from Quneitra to Amman and beyond, a railway line, and growing market towns. In 1840, the northern Black Sea steppe and the western Caucasus were populated by Crimean Tatars, Nogais, Circassians, and others; by 1914 those populations had been largely removed or reduced, and their descendants lived across Ottoman lands. In 1840, Muslims formed a large share of the Balkan population of the Ottoman Empire; by 1914 much of the Balkans lay outside Ottoman control, and many Balkan Muslims had relocated to Anatolia.

The legacy of 1860 has several layers. First, it left a political settlement in Mount Lebanon that, whatever its limitations, brought decades of relative stability and administrative development under the mutasarrifate, as Akarlı and others have argued. Second, it entrenched the principle that religious community could be a basis for political representation, a precedent that shaped the institutions of the mandate period and the Lebanese state, as Chapters 117 and 138 explain. Third, it altered the relation between Ottoman authorities and European governments, setting a precedent for international involvement in Ottoman internal affairs on behalf of Christian communities, which affected Ottoman perceptions of minorities and foreign powers and which Chapters 98 and 100 note in connection with later crises. Fourth, it left memories of violence and rescue that communities carried forward, including the memory of Muslims who protected Christians.

The legacy of the refugee movements is equally broad. The Ottoman state built an administrative apparatus for migration and settlement that grew in capacity and ambition. It learned to register populations, allocate land, and move people, tools that it would use again, with very different consequences, in the era of World War I, which Chapter 111 examines. The refugees themselves formed new communities, with their own institutions, languages, and memories, that became enduring elements of the societies of Turkey, Syria, Jordan, and other successor states. The demographic geography of Anatolia, with its large populations of Caucasian, Crimean, and Balkan descent, and the human geography of the southern Levant, with settlements such as Amman, are products of this nineteenth-century history.

The Ottoman response combined welfare, security, economic development, and political calculation. The state assisted Muslim refugees out of religious and moral commitment, and it used settlement to bring land under cultivation, secure frontiers, and enhance its control. After 1860, it combined punishment of perpetrators, restoration of order, and acceptance of foreign supervision in Mount Lebanon with an attempt to preserve sovereignty. These actions reflected a state that was simultaneously weakened by war, debt, and foreign pressure and strengthened by new administrative capacities. The same duality appears in the Hamidian era examined in Chapter 98 and in the constitutional politics treated in Chapter 105.

The chapter's larger argument is that the region's religious and ethnic geography was substantially shaped by nineteenth-century events, not simply inherited from antiquity. The distribution of Circassians, Chechens, Crimean Tatars, Bosniaks, and Cretan Muslims across the Middle East, the composition of Mount Lebanon and Beirut after 1860, the loss of Muslim populations in the Balkans and their arrival in Anatolia, and the removal of many Caucasian Muslims from their homelands all resulted from specific wars, policies, and decisions made by identifiable governments and communities. Recognizing this prevents the projection of present-day distributions into the distant past, and it shows how modern identities, including the political meaning of communal belonging, were formed in historical circumstances.

These transformations influenced later conflicts and political movements without determining them. The experience of Muslim refugees from Christian-ruled lands affected Ottoman and later Turkish attitudes to security and minorities, a point that later chapters must treat carefully and with evidence. The confessional institutions of Mount Lebanon influenced Lebanese politics, but did not make the civil war of 1975 inevitable. Circassian and Chechen communities in Syria and Jordan acquired roles in the security services and local society of later states, but their trajectories depended on later events. The historian's task is to trace connections while recognizing contingency, a principle that guides the transition to Chapter 105, which examines the Young Turk Revolution of 1908 and the constitutional politics of an empire whose population had been profoundly reshaped.

Everyday Life and Social History

Displacement, Household Survival, Settlement, Education, and Employment

For displaced households, history was experienced through immediate needs. After 1860, families from the mountain sought food and shelter in Beirut, relied on relatives and relief committees, and waited to learn whether silk groves and houses survived. After 1864 and 1878, refugee families carried what they could, endured hunger and disease, and faced the work of building houses, clearing and plowing land, acquiring animals, and obtaining title. Mortality among children and the elderly was high in the early years, and survival often depended on kin networks and on neighbors' hospitality.

Over time households stabilized. Women worked fields, tended animals, processed food, and maintained homes, and many ran households alone after war and disease took men. Children attended religious schools, Ottoman state schools, or missionary and charitable schools, such as those that grew in Beirut after 1860, and acquired literacy and new languages. Employment spread from farming to crafts, trade, military and gendarmerie service, and railway work. Sources for these lives are scattered: registers record household heads and landholdings, court records preserve disputes and inheritance, and oral tradition and family memory preserve the stories that official records omit. Historians must read across these sources to recover the experience of ordinary people, including the many whose names appear nowhere.

Imperial War, Displacement, Settlement Policy, and Communal Institutions

Long-term causes

Russian imperial expansion; Ottoman territorial retreat; Tanzimat reform and its tensions over equality; economic integration with Europe; changing landholding and tax relations; European patronage of religious communities.

Short-term causes

The Crimean War; the Kisrawan revolt and fear among Druze notables; the failures of Ottoman authority in 1860; Russia's final western Caucasus campaign of 1860–1864; the Balkan crisis and war of 1875–1878.

Immediate effects

Thousands killed and displaced in 1860; punishment of officials and French intervention; mass Circassian and Balkan Muslim refugee flows; epidemic disease and emergency relief; formation of the refugee commission and the Mount Lebanon mutasarrifate.

Long-term and regional consequences

Confessional representation in Mount Lebanon; expansion of Ottoman administrative capacity; new refugee communities in Anatolia, Syria, and Transjordan; transformation of frontier economies; altered Muslim and Christian demographic balances.

Historical complexity: These processes were interconnected but not identical. Violence in Lebanon was not caused by Russian expansion, and refugee settlement was not caused by the 1860 events. Their connection lay in the common Ottoman context of reform, imperial pressure, and the state's efforts to govern a changing population.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Religious Identity, Local Communities, and Demographic Organization

What Changed

  • Religious community became a formal basis of political representation in Mount Lebanon.
  • The Ottoman state developed a standing administrative apparatus for migration and settlement.
  • The Muslim populations of the northern Black Sea steppe and the northwestern Caucasus were largely removed or reduced, and new Caucasian, Tatar, Bosniak, and Cretan communities formed in Ottoman lands.
  • The agricultural frontier of Syria and Transjordan advanced eastward, with new villages and towns such as Amman.

What Stayed the Same

  • Mixed neighborhoods, markets, and villages remained common, and many individuals continued cooperating across communal lines.
  • Notable families, local elites, and kin networks continued to mediate between communities and the state.
  • Ottoman sovereignty persisted, and the empire continued to rely on negotiation with local powers.
  • Pastoral and agricultural economies continued, though in altered balance.

Why the pattern matters: Students should recognize both the depth of change and the persistence of older structures. The nineteenth century created new political meanings for community without erasing older ties, and without making later conflicts inevitable.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that nineteenth-century demographic transformation in the Ottoman world was not only a consequence of conflict but also an instrument by which imperial governments reorganized territory, extended authority, shaped communal institutions, and influenced political identities. It joined two histories that are often separated: the communal violence of 1860 in Mount Lebanon and Damascus, and the displacement of Muslim populations from Russian-conquered and Balkan lands and their resettlement by the Ottoman state.

Division I showed that the Ottoman Levant was a mixed society in which religious difference coexisted with shared markets, neighborhoods, and political arrangements. In Mount Lebanon, a hierarchy of Druze and Maronite notables ruled peasants through the muqata'ji system, and the growth of silk and European commerce altered economic relationships. The Egyptian occupation and Ottoman restoration destroyed the Shihab emirate and led to the dual qaimaqamate, which tied administration to religious community without resolving conflicts over land and taxation. The Kisrawan revolt of 1858–1861, in which Maronite peasants challenged Maronite notables, showed that the order was already breaking down from within.

Division II reconstructed the violence of 1860. In Mount Lebanon, armed Druze and Christian groups mobilized, and the greatest losses fell on Christian civilians in places such as Hasbaya, Zahle, and Dayr al-Qamar, while Ottoman officials failed to protect them. In Damascus, an urban crowd attacked the Christian quarter in July in a context of economic change, resentment of reform, and failed policing. The two episodes had different causes. Casualty estimates require separate reckoning for Mount Lebanon and Damascus: some scholarly reconstructions place deaths in the mountain above ten thousand, with thousands more reported in Damascus. Exact figures vary by source and cannot responsibly be merged into one unqualified total; many also suffered death from hunger and disease.

Civilian experience included flight, refuge, relief, and rescue. Abd al-Qadir al-Jaza'iri and other Muslims protected Christians, demonstrating that the same society produced both atrocity and protection. The Ottoman government, through Fuad Pasha, punished officials and notables and restored order, while France sent an expeditionary force and the powers negotiated the Organic Regulation of 1861, revised in 1864. The mutasarrifate remained under Ottoman sovereignty, with a non-local Christian governor, a council of twelve initially divided equally among six communities in 1861 but reapportioned in 1864, and an international diplomatic guarantee, and it institutionalized communal representation.

Division III turned to Russia's expansion. Crimean Tatars and Nogais emigrated after the annexation of 1783 and in large numbers around the Crimean War. Russia's conquest of the Caucasus, finally pressed in the northwest between 1860 and 1864, involved the destruction of villages and the removal or flight of most of the Circassian population. Ships carried hundreds of thousands to Ottoman ports under conditions of hunger and disease, with large but uncertain mortality. Historians agree on forced removal and mass suffering and disagree about classification as ethnic cleansing or genocide. The Ottoman government, which had created a refugee commission in 1860, developed regulations and practices for reception and settlement.

Division IV followed the refugees to their new homes. They were placed in Anatolia, the Black Sea region, the Balkans, the Golan, and Transjordan, often in poor conditions, and they built villages, farmed land, entered commerce, and served the state. In Amman and Quneitra, settlement interacted with established villagers and Bedouin populations, producing cooperation and conflict and, in some cases, new local power. Resettlement did not simply strengthen central authority. Refugee communities rebuilt family, religious, linguistic, and educational life, and their children grew up as Ottoman subjects with layered identities.

Division V examined the Balkan refugee crisis of 1877–1878, which displaced very large numbers of Muslims and affected other communities as well, and later movements of Bosniaks, Thessalian and Cretan Muslims, and others. It then discussed the evidence by which historians reconstruct population change, noting the limits of censuses, registers, and consular estimates, and the danger of using historical demographic figures to claim modern political entitlements. The chapter closed with a comparison of 1840 and 1914.

The two debates organized the chapter's interpretive questions. On 1860, the chapter showed that neither an appeal to inherited sectarian hatred nor a single social explanation is sufficient, and that religious identities acquired new political meaning in particular nineteenth-century circumstances. On refugee policy, it showed that Ottoman resettlement was both an act of assistance and a strategy of state-building, and that the two purposes interacted.

Across these histories, the Middle East of 1914 differed substantially from that of 1840. Its demographic geography included new refugee communities, altered balances of Muslim and Christian populations, a reconstructed Mount Lebanon, and an Ottoman administration more able to register, move, and govern people. These changes were products of identifiable wars, policies, and choices, and not an unchanging inheritance.

The chapter's legacy for later volumes is real but not deterministic. Communal representation in Lebanon, the experience of displacement in Ottoman and Turkish memory, the administrative tools of migration management, and the presence of Caucasian, Balkan, and Cretan communities in successor states all shaped subsequent events. The chapter prepares for Chapter 105, which turns to the Young Turk Revolution and the constitutional politics of an empire whose population and institutions had been reshaped by the processes examined here.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Muhacir
Ottoman term for Muslim migrants or refugees entering the empire, including Crimean Tatars, Circassians, Balkan Muslims, and others.
Muqata'ji
Hereditary lord of a tax district (muqata'a) in Mount Lebanon, holding tax-collection rights and local authority.
Iltizam
Tax farming, the system by which the right to collect taxes was assigned in return for payments to the state.
Qaimaqamate
The dual administration of Mount Lebanon established in 1842–1843, with a Maronite qaimaqam in the north and a Druze qaimaqam in the south.
Mutasarrifate
The autonomous Ottoman district of Mount Lebanon created in 1861 and governed by a mutasarrif, a Christian Ottoman official from outside the mountain.
Organic Regulation
The 1861 statute (revised in 1864) establishing the mutasarrifate, its governor, administrative council, and legal framework under European guarantee.
Tanzimat
The Ottoman reform era beginning in 1839, aimed at centralization, legal equality, and administrative modernization.
Islahat Fermani
The Reform Edict of 1856, restating the equality of Ottoman subjects regardless of religion.
Communal representation
A political arrangement in which recognized religious communities are represented as such in governing bodies.
Confessionalism
A system allocating political offices or seats by religious community.
Sectarianism
A term for the political organization and mobilization of religious difference, whose historical origins and character are debated; it is not a self-explanatory cause.
Millet
An Ottoman term for a recognized religious community, whose institutions and legal arrangements changed substantially over time.
Capitulations
Treaty privileges granted to foreign states and their nationals within the Ottoman Empire, which expanded in the nineteenth century into extensive foreign legal and commercial protection.
Consul
A foreign government's representative in an Ottoman city, who reported events and often acted as patron of particular communities.
Kisrawan revolt
The 1858–1859 peasant uprising led by Tanyus Shahin against the Khazin notables in northern Mount Lebanon.
Ethnic cleansing
The forcible removal of an identified population from a territory, widely used by historians to describe the Circassian expulsions.
Genocide
Acts committed with intent to destroy, in whole or in part, a national, ethnical, racial, or religious group, as defined in the 1948 Genocide Convention; its application to the Circassian case is debated.
Forced migration
Movement of people under compulsion, threat, or the destruction of their means of life.
Refugee commission
The Ottoman body (Muhacirin Komisyonu, 1860) administering refugee reception, registration, and settlement.
Imperial resettlement
State allocation of refugees or other populations to chosen places for humanitarian, fiscal, military, and territorial purposes.
Land Code of 1858
Ottoman law regulating categories of land tenure and registration, the framework for refugee land allocation.
Adyghe (Circassian)
The collective name for a group of North-West Caucasian peoples and their languages, including Shapsug, Abzakh, and Kabardian communities.
Ubykh
A coastal Caucasian people with a distinct language who left almost entirely in the 1860s; the language became extinct in the late twentieth century.
Crimean Tatars
Turkic-speaking Muslim population of Crimea, whose khanate Russia annexed in 1783.
Nogais
Turkic pastoralist people of the northern Black Sea steppe, many of whom emigrated to Ottoman lands.
Bedouin
Pastoral and semi-pastoral Arab tribal communities of the Arabian and Syrian steppe, whose seasonal grazing and water use were affected by agricultural expansion.
Congress of Berlin
The 1878 conference that revised the Treaty of San Stefano and reorganized the Balkans, with large demographic consequences.
Census (Ottoman)
State enumeration of population, classified mainly by religion and sex, serving taxation and conscription.
Salname
An Ottoman provincial yearbook with administrative and statistical information.
Hijaz Railway
Railway reaching Amman in 1903 and Medina in 1908, tied to the growth of settlements in Transjordan.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter closes Part XXIII by showing that modern political identities emerged not only through intellectual movements, nationalism, and religious institutions but also through the physical movement of populations, the reorganization of territory, and the struggle for security and belonging after displacement. Mount Lebanon's confessional council, the villages of Circassians on the Syrian steppe, and the Muslim refugee quarters of Anatolian towns were all consequences of decisions made by governments and of the responses of ordinary people to catastrophe.

The larger historical processes involved are the expansion of the Russian Empire, the contraction of Ottoman territory, the growth of state administrative capacity, the integration of the region into European commerce and diplomacy, and the transformation of religious community into a political category. These processes continued beyond 1914. The Ottoman state's management of displaced populations would reappear, in far more destructive forms, in the war years examined in Chapter 111, and the experience of communal violence and foreign intervention would inform the construction of the mandate system and the Lebanese state in Chapters 117 and 138.

The chapter also underlines continuing structures: the role of notables as intermediaries, the persistence of mixed communities, the contest over land and water between settled and pastoral populations, and the use of demographic knowledge as an instrument of government. At the same time, it shows the limits of determinism. Neighbors sheltered neighbors; refugees became farmers, merchants, soldiers, and townspeople; and communities negotiated new relationships under pressure. Later outcomes were not preordained.

Chapter 105 turns to the Young Turk Revolution of 1908 and the constitutional politics of an empire whose population, institutions, and political vocabulary had been profoundly altered. To understand that revolution, and the Balkan Wars and world war that followed, students must carry forward the lessons of this chapter: that the human geography of the Middle East was made historically, that states used population as a tool of governance, and that the people who endured displacement and violence were agents of their own histories as well as subjects of imperial policy.
