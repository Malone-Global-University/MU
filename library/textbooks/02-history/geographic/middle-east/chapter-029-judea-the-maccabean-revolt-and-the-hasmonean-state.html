Judea, the Maccabean Revolt, and the Hasmonean State

A History of the Middle East: From Ancient Civilizations to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

During the crisis of 168–167 BCE, a Seleucid garrison occupied a fortified stronghold in Jerusalem called the Akra — a fortification whose precise location remains one of the most disputed problems in the historical topography of ancient Jerusalem — while the altar of the Temple itself, according to Jewish tradition, had been rededicated to a foreign cult. Within a few years, the same city would see that altar purified and its sacrifices restored by men who had begun as a small armed band in the Judean hill country. Within a century, the descendants of that band would rule a Jewish kingdom stretching from the Mediterranean coast to the Jordan River and beyond, commanding a standing army, minting their own coinage, and claiming both the high priesthood and the diadem of a Hellenistic monarch. No single explanation accounts for this transformation, and no single word — "revolt," "religion," "nationalism," or "dynasty" — captures the whole of it.

Chapter 28 examined the Seleucid Empire as an imperial system stretching from Syria to Iran, a vast and difficult territory governed through cities, military colonies, local elites, and negotiated authority rather than uniform centralized rule. Judea, a small hill-country district in southern Syria-Palestine with Jerusalem as its principal city, was one province among many within that empire, and for most of the third century BCE its internal politics attracted little attention from the wider Hellenistic world. This chapter narrows the focus dramatically, from an empire of many peoples and provinces to a single province whose internal crisis, in the 160s BCE, produced one of the best-documented and most consequential episodes of ancient Jewish history: the Maccabean Revolt and the century of Hasmonean rule that followed it.

The temptation in telling this story is to flatten it into a conflict between two fixed camps — a faithful, traditional Judaism resisting an alien, corrupting Hellenism. That framing, inherited in part from the triumphant later memory of the revolt itself, does not withstand close examination of the evidence. Jews throughout the Hellenistic eastern Mediterranean, including many who remained fully committed to the Torah, the Jerusalem Temple, and Jewish communal life, participated in Greek language, education, commerce, and civic institutions to varying degrees, just as non-Jewish populations throughout the Seleucid world did. The crisis that erupted in Jerusalem in the 170s and 160s BCE arose not from an abstract clash of civilizations but from a specific and complicated interaction: competition among Jewish elite families for control of the high priesthood and its accompanying wealth and authority, the Seleucid dynasty's own fiscal and military pressures under King Antiochus IV, and a set of religious restrictions imposed on Jewish practice in Jerusalem that had no clear precedent in ordinary Seleucid governance and that ancient sources explain in strikingly different ways.

What followed the crisis was equally complicated. The armed resistance associated with the priestly family later called the Hasmoneans, and especially with Judas Maccabeus, began as a movement to restore Temple worship and defend Jewish religious practice against what its participants experienced as an unprecedented assault. It did not begin as a movement for full political independence, and its early leaders did not initially aim to overthrow Seleucid rule as such. Only gradually, through years of warfare, negotiation, and the exploitation of dynastic conflict within the Seleucid royal house itself, did the movement's goals expand from religious restoration to effective self-government and, eventually, to a territorial state ruled by the same family that had led the original resistance.

That state, which modern historians call the Hasmonean state after the family name Hasmonaeus, would in time look very different from the movement that produced it. Its later rulers combined the high priesthood with dynastic political authority, commanded professional armies, expanded Judea's borders through conquest, and in some cases compelled conquered populations to adopt Jewish practice. These developments generated new and serious divisions within Jewish society over the legitimacy of combining priestly and political power, over how Jewish law should be interpreted and by what authority, and over whether the Hasmonean dynasty itself continued to represent the values that had animated the original revolt. Some of the religious and political groupings that would matter enormously in later Jewish history, and that appear repeatedly in the chapters ahead, took recognizable shape during this same Hasmonean century.

This chapter also depends on a body of evidence that requires careful, critical reading rather than simple acceptance. The two principal narrative sources, the books called 1 Maccabees and 2 Maccabees, were composed by different authors, for different purposes, within different literary traditions, and neither is a neutral eyewitness record. The book of Daniel, composed in roughly this same period, refracts the crisis through apocalyptic vision rather than straightforward narrative. The first-century CE Jewish historian Flavius Josephus wrote more than two centuries after the events he describes, drawing on earlier sources not always identifiable today. Later rabbinic tradition, composed centuries afterward, added its own distinctive account of the festival of Hanukkah that differs in emphasis from the earliest sources. This chapter treats each of these sources as historical evidence to be examined rather than as transparent windows onto the past, a method already introduced in Chapter 4 and essential to responsible treatment of ancient Jewish history throughout this textbook.

Finally, this chapter situates Judea's crisis and transformation within the wider Hellenistic and Seleucid world examined in Chapter 28, without re-narrating that empire's general history. Judea's fate in this period cannot be separated from Seleucid dynastic instability, from the empire's repeated wars, from Ptolemaic Egypt's continuing interest in the southern Levant, and from the gradual weakening of Seleucid central authority that both enabled and constrained Hasmonean ambitions. The chapter that follows this one, Chapter 30, turns to Parthia, Armenia, Nabataea, and the Roman frontier; the chapter after that, Chapter 31, returns to Judea under Roman rule and the destruction of the Second Temple. This chapter's task is to explain what happened between those two larger stories — how a religious and political crisis in a small Seleucid province produced first a revolt, then an independent Jewish state, and finally new internal divisions whose consequences would outlast the Hasmonean dynasty itself.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Describe the transition of Judea from Ptolemaic to Seleucid rule after the Battle of Panium (conventionally dated to 200 BCE, though recent numismatic and epigraphic reassessment favors 198 BCE) and explain how that transition affected local government and taxation.
  2. Explain the varied and overlapping forms that Hellenistic cultural participation took among Jews in this period, avoiding a simple division between "Hellenized" and "traditional" Jews.
  3. Analyze the political, economic, and religious significance of the Jerusalem Temple and the high priesthood within Judean society.
  4. Identify the major elite factions and figures involved in the struggle over the high priesthood in the 170s BCE, including Onias III, Jason, and Menelaus.
  5. Explain how Antiochus IV's dynastic, military, and fiscal circumstances intersected with Jerusalem's internal conflicts to produce the crisis of 168–167 BCE.
  6. Evaluate competing historical explanations for the religious restrictions imposed on Jewish practice under Antiochus IV, distinguishing ancient source claims from modern historical debate.
  7. Trace the course of the Maccabean uprising from Mattathias's initial resistance through Judas Maccabeus's campaigns to the rededication of the Temple in 164 BCE.
  8. Distinguish the earliest accounts of the Temple's rededication from later rabbinic traditions associated with the festival of Hanukkah.
  9. Explain how the movement associated with Judas, Jonathan, and Simon expanded from religious restoration toward effective political independence.
  10. Describe the institutional development of the Hasmonean state, including its combination of high-priestly and dynastic political authority.
  11. Analyze the territorial expansion carried out by John Hyrcanus, Aristobulus I, and Alexander Jannaeus, and the methods and consequences of that expansion.
  12. Explain the emergence of Jewish political and religious diversity, including Pharisees, Sadducees, and other groups, in relation to Hasmonean rule.

Historical Context

Chapter 26 examined Alexander's conquest of the Near East and the political fragmentation that followed his death, and Chapter 28 examined the Seleucid Empire that emerged from that fragmentation as the dominant power across Syria, Mesopotamia, and much of Iran. Judea, together with the rest of the southern Levant, did not initially fall under Seleucid rule. For roughly a century after Alexander's death, the region belonged instead to the Ptolemaic kingdom of Egypt, examined in Chapter 27, which administered it as a frontier province valued for its agricultural revenue, its strategic position along the coastal route between Egypt and Syria, and its access to Mediterranean and overland trade.

Ptolemaic rule over Judea, like Ptolemaic rule over Egypt itself, combined a Greek-speaking royal administration with substantial continuity in local institutions. Jerusalem's high priest, drawn from a hereditary priestly lineage descended from Zadok and traditionally called the Oniad family after several priests named Onias, functioned as the principal local authority responsible for collecting taxes, maintaining order, and representing the Jewish community to the royal government, in a pattern broadly similar to how other Hellenistic kingdoms governed through temple-based or civic elites in various provinces. The Ptolemaic government taxed Judea's agricultural produce, and surviving papyri from the Ptolemaic administrative archive known as the Zenon papyri document Ptolemaic officials traveling through the region, negotiating with local landowners, and managing tax farming arrangements, providing valuable contemporary evidence for Judea's economic integration into the wider Ptolemaic system even before the crisis this chapter examines.

This arrangement changed after the Battle of Panium, examined in Chapter 28, in which the Seleucid king Antiochus III decisively defeated Ptolemaic forces — an engagement conventionally dated to 200 BCE in older scholarship, though recent numismatic and epigraphic reappraisal has favored a date of 198 BCE — and brought Judea and the rest of Coele-Syria under Seleucid rule. Antiochus III, seeking to secure the loyalty of his new subjects, issued a charter, preserved in summary by Josephus, granting the Jewish population of Jerusalem certain privileges: permission to live according to their ancestral laws, tax relief for a period following the transition, and financial support for Temple sacrifices and the maintenance of the Temple precinct. This charter illustrates a pattern already familiar from earlier chapters on Hellenistic kingship: successor kings frequently negotiated with local religious and civic institutions rather than imposing uniform administration, and Judea's transition to Seleucid rule initially followed this negotiated pattern rather than any wholesale transformation of local government.

The stability of this arrangement, however, depended on two conditions that would not hold for long: continued cooperation among Jerusalem's priestly elite, and continued Seleucid capacity to honor negotiated privileges without needing to extract additional revenue or assert additional control over wealthy provincial temples. Both conditions came under strain during the 170s BCE, for reasons rooted partly in internal Jerusalem politics and partly in the Seleucid Empire's own difficulties, examined further in Chapter 28, including the costly Roman-imposed indemnity following Antiochus III's defeat at Magnesia in 190 BCE and the empire's continuing rivalry with Ptolemaic Egypt over control of the southern Levant. This chapter begins where that context leaves off, examining how these pressures combined with Jerusalem's own internal elite competition to produce the crisis, revolt, and eventual state formation that are this chapter's central subject.

Section 29.1

Judea under the Ptolemies and Early Seleucids

For roughly a century following Alexander's death, Judea existed as a modest inland district within the Ptolemaic province of Coele-Syria, a term Hellenistic geographers applied broadly to the southern Levant. Ptolemaic administration governed Judea chiefly through fiscal extraction: royal officials assessed and collected taxes on agricultural production, particularly grain, oil, and wine, often through tax-farming arrangements in which wealthy local figures or syndicates purchased the right to collect taxes in exchange for a fixed payment to the crown, retaining any surplus they could extract as profit. The Tobiad family, prominent Jewish landowners with estates east of the Jordan, illustrate this pattern, though the evidence for them comes from two distinct bodies of material that should not be conflated: Josephus, writing more than two centuries later, tells an elaborate narrative of Tobiad tax-farming fortunes under Ptolemaic rule, while the independent, contemporary Zenon papyri, a Ptolemaic administrative archive, separately attest dealings between Ptolemaic officials and the Tobiad family in Transjordan. Read together, these two independent evidence bases indicate how Jewish elites could occupy influential positions within a Greek-speaking imperial administration without abandoning Jewish identity or practice, even though Josephus's fuller narrative cannot simply be assumed to be accurate in every detail merely because the Tobiads' general prominence is independently attested.

Jerusalem itself, though far smaller and less prominent than the great Hellenistic capitals examined in Chapter 27 and Chapter 28, functioned during this period as the religious and administrative center of Judean life, organized around the Temple and its hereditary high priesthood. The high priest, drawn from the Zadokite or Oniad line, served simultaneously as the chief religious officiant responsible for the Temple cult and as the community's principal representative to the Ptolemaic government, a dual role that made the office both spiritually significant and politically consequential. This arrangement resembled, in general structure though not in specific content, the way other Hellenistic kingdoms often governed through existing temple institutions and local elites rather than through uniform provincial bureaucracies, a pattern already discussed in connection with Ptolemaic Egypt in Chapter 27.

The Battle of Panium, now generally dated to 198 BCE (traditionally 200 BCE), examined from the Seleucid imperial perspective in Chapter 28, transferred Judea from Ptolemaic to Seleucid control as part of Antiochus III's broader conquest of Coele-Syria. According to a document preserved by the first-century CE historian Josephus and generally regarded by modern historians as substantially reliable in its main outlines, Antiochus III issued a charter for Jerusalem shortly after the conquest. The charter reportedly granted the Jewish population permission to live according to their ancestral laws, provided funds from royal revenue to support Temple sacrifices and the maintenance of Temple buildings, exempted the Jerusalem senate, priests, and Temple scribes from certain taxes, and granted the wider population temporary tax relief to ease the transition to Seleucid rule. Antiochus III's charter should be read in the context of Seleucid practice generally: successor kings frequently offered such privileges to newly acquired cities and temples as a means of securing loyalty at minimal immediate cost, a strategy that assumed continued cooperation from the local elite receiving the privileges.

This arrangement initially preserved substantial continuity in Judean self-government under the high priest Onias III, who is described by ancient sources, particularly 2 Maccabees, in favorable terms as a pious and effective leader who maintained good relations with the Seleucid administration. The stability of the arrangement, however, rested on conditions that would not hold indefinitely. The Seleucid Empire, as examined in Chapter 28, faced substantial fiscal pressure following its defeat by Rome at Magnesia in 190 BCE and the subsequent Treaty of Apamea, which imposed a heavy indemnity on the Seleucid treasury and constrained Seleucid military capacity in Anatolia. These pressures increased the imperial court's interest in extracting additional revenue from wealthy provincial institutions, including well-endowed temples such as the one in Jerusalem, a pressure that would soon intersect with internal Judean elite competition in ways that neither Antiochus III's charter nor Onias III's leadership had anticipated.

Section 29.2

Hellenistic Jewish Society before the Revolt

Before examining the crisis of the 170s and 160s BCE, it is necessary to establish a point that later tradition, and much popular retelling, has often obscured: Jewish engagement with Hellenistic culture in this period was neither uniform nor inherently a departure from Jewish religious commitment. Greek had become, by the second century BCE, a significant language of administration, commerce, and elite communication throughout the eastern Mediterranean, including among Jewish communities both in Judea and, even more extensively, in the large Jewish diaspora communities of Alexandria, Antioch, and other Hellenistic cities examined further in later chapters on Jewish diaspora history. Many Jews, including priests and other figures who remained deeply committed to Temple worship and Jewish law, adopted Greek names alongside Hebrew ones, engaged in Greek-language commerce and diplomacy, and were familiar with Greek civic and educational institutions without regarding this familiarity as incompatible with Jewish identity.

The wealthy Tobiad family, already mentioned in Section 29.1 as prominent tax farmers under Ptolemaic rule, illustrates this pattern clearly: the independent Zenon papyri documenting Ptolemaic dealings with the family in Transjordan show fluent participation in Ptolemaic Greek-language administration alongside continued identification as Jews. Archaeological and numismatic evidence from Judea in this period likewise shows Greek artistic and architectural influence appearing alongside continued Jewish religious practice, rather than displacing it. This textbook's treatment of Hellenistic culture throughout Volume II has repeatedly emphasized that Hellenization, discussed as a general phenomenon in Chapter 26, took highly variable local forms and rarely produced uniform cultural transformation; Judea in the early second century BCE fits this broader pattern rather than standing as an exception to it.

What changed in the 170s BCE was not the sudden arrival of Greek cultural influence into a previously untouched Judean society, but rather a specific and contested attempt by one faction of Jerusalem's priestly elite to reorganize the city's civic institutions along more explicitly Greek lines, and the escalating conflict that this attempt provoked among rival Jewish elites and, eventually, with the Seleucid crown itself. Understanding this distinction matters because it prevents students from mistaking a specific political and institutional conflict, rooted in elite competition and crown intervention, for a general clash between two static and mutually exclusive cultural systems. As the following sections show, the crisis emerged from particular decisions made by particular actors under particular pressures, not from an inevitable collision between "Judaism" and "Hellenism" understood as fixed, opposing essences.

Historical Vocabulary
Hellenization and Its Limits

Hellenization refers to the spread of Greek language, institutions, artistic styles, and modes of civic and educational organization throughout the territories conquered by Alexander and ruled by his Hellenistic successors, examined generally in Chapters 26 through 28. Historians once described this process, especially in Jewish history, as a relatively uniform and often coercive cultural transformation pitting Greek culture against local traditions. Since the later twentieth century, however, most historians of the Hellenistic Jewish world, following the influential reassessment associated with scholars such as Martin Hengel and subsequent critics and refiners of his work, have emphasized that Hellenistic cultural influence and continued adherence to Jewish law and Temple worship were not mutually exclusive, and that Jews across the eastern Mediterranean combined these elements in widely varying ways.

This textbook therefore avoids describing individuals or groups in this period simply as "Hellenizers" versus "traditionalists," a binary that ancient polemical sources, particularly 2 Maccabees, sometimes encourage but that does not withstand careful historical scrutiny. Instead, this chapter treats Hellenistic cultural participation as a widespread and variable feature of Jewish life in this period, and treats the specific crisis of the 170s and 160s BCE as arising from a particular set of political, institutional, and religious circumstances rather than from cultural Hellenization as such.

Section 29.3

Jerusalem, the Temple, and the High Priesthood

To understand why control of the high priesthood became so fiercely contested in the 170s BCE, students must first understand what the office actually controlled. The Second Temple, rebuilt after the Babylonian exile examined in Chapter 20 and Chapter 22, functioned as the sole legitimate site of Jewish sacrificial worship, the physical and symbolic center of Judean religious life, and, simultaneously, one of the wealthiest institutions in the province. The Temple received tithes, offerings, and dedicated funds from Jews throughout Judea and, increasingly, from diaspora Jewish communities across the Hellenistic world who sent contributions to Jerusalem despite living at great distances from it. This wealth was stored partly within the Temple treasury itself, making the Temple an object of interest not only to pious donors but also to cash-strapped rulers, a vulnerability that would become directly relevant to the crisis examined in Section 29.5.

The high priest presided over this institution and, in doing so, exercised authority that extended well beyond ritual matters. As the community's recognized representative to the Seleucid crown, the high priest negotiated taxation, represented Judean interests in disputes with neighboring populations and administrations, and functioned, in effect, as the closest approximation to a head of Judean civil government that existed under Hellenistic rule. The office was hereditary within the Zadokite priestly line, tracing legitimacy back to the priesthood established under the united monarchy and reorganized after the Babylonian exile, discussed in Chapter 20; this hereditary claim to legitimacy mattered enormously to how Jews in this period understood proper religious and communal authority, and its disruption in the 170s BCE, examined in Section 29.4, therefore represented far more than an administrative personnel change.

Because the high priesthood combined religious legitimacy, political representation, and control over substantial Temple wealth in a single office, competition for that office inevitably combined religious, political, and economic stakes in ways that are difficult to separate cleanly. A candidate for the high priesthood who could offer the Seleucid crown a larger payment, or promise more effective cooperation with royal fiscal demands, possessed a significant practical advantage regardless of that candidate's standing within traditional priestly succession, a vulnerability inherent in a system where distant Hellenistic kings, rather than any purely internal Jewish process, held ultimate authority to appoint or confirm the high priest. This structural vulnerability, rather than any single individual's ambition or any abstract cultural conflict, created the conditions in which the rival bids for the high priesthood examined in the next section became possible.

Section 29.4

Jewish Political Diversity and Elite Competition

The crisis that led to the Maccabean revolt began not with a Seleucid decree imposed on a united Jewish population, but with internal competition among rival members of Jerusalem's priestly elite for control of the high priesthood, competition that drew Seleucid royal intervention deeper into Judean affairs at each stage. This section examines that competition directly, because understanding it is essential to avoiding the oversimplified narrative, discussed in Section 29.2, of a unified Jewish resistance against a unified Hellenizing threat.

According to 2 Maccabees, our fullest narrative source for this period, tensions began during the high priesthood of Onias III, when a Temple official named Simon, involved in a dispute over Temple finances, reported to the Seleucid governor of Coele-Syria that the Temple treasury held funds that could be seized for royal use. Whatever the precise accuracy of this specific episode, it illustrates a broader dynamic: internal Judean factional disputes were, by this period, already being carried to Seleucid authorities for adjudication, a pattern that made external royal power an active participant in internal Jewish politics rather than a distant and uninvolved sovereign.

Matters escalated significantly when Jason, a brother of Onias III who had adopted a Greek name in place of his Hebrew name Yeshua or Joshua, offered Antiochus IV a substantial payment in exchange for the high priesthood, displacing his own brother, and requested royal authorization to establish a gymnasium and register the citizens of Jerusalem as citizens of a Greek-style polis, apparently to be called Antioch-at-Jerusalem. Ancient sources present this request as a decisive and provocative act, and 2 Maccabees in particular frames it in strongly critical terms; modern historians generally treat the episode as evidence of a specific political strategy by one elite faction to secure both office and civic privileges advantageous to Jerusalem's upper class, rather than as an attempt to abolish Jewish religious practice, which Jason's own regime does not appear to have restricted. Jason's high priesthood therefore illustrates the complexity discussed in Section 29.2: a priest from a legitimate priestly family, still overseeing ordinary Temple worship, simultaneously pursuing an ambitious program of Greek civic reorganization.

Jason's position proved no more secure than his brother's had been. Within a few years, a man named Menelaus, whose priestly lineage is itself uncertain in the surviving textual tradition — 2 Maccabees identifies his brother Simon with the tribe of Benjamin in some manuscript readings, and recent scholarship has argued Menelaus may not have been of Aaronite descent at all — but who in any case did not possess the traditional Zadokite/Oniad claim to the office, outbid Jason for the high priesthood with an even larger payment to Antiochus IV, and Jason fled into exile. Menelaus's appointment marked a further escalation, since it placed the high priesthood, for the first time in the sources' account, in the hands of a figure lacking the hereditary legitimacy that had previously underpinned the office's authority, a departure that ancient sources treat as deeply troubling regardless of their other disagreements. According to 2 Maccabees, Menelaus resorted to selling Temple vessels to raise funds owed to the crown and was implicated in the killing of the deposed Onias III, episodes that, whatever their precise factual accuracy, reflect a widely shared ancient perception that the high priesthood had become dangerously entangled in financial corruption and violent factional competition.

This sequence of events — payment-based appointment, displacement of hereditary claimants, financial exploitation of Temple resources, and violence among rival factions — created deep instability in Jerusalem before Antiochus IV's direct military intervention, examined in the next section, had even occurred. Historians differ on how much weight to assign to this internal Judean factional competition, as against Antiochus IV's own initiative, in explaining the crisis that followed; this textbook treats both as necessary and interacting causes, a theme developed further in the Cause and Effect feature later in this chapter.

Section 29.5

Antiochus IV and the Crisis in Judea

Antiochus IV Epiphanes, who became Seleucid king around 175 BCE, ruled an empire still recovering from the costly indemnity imposed after the Treaty of Apamea, examined in Chapter 28, and still contesting control of the southern Levant and, at times, Egypt itself with the Ptolemaic kingdom. These circumstances made the Seleucid crown receptive to the kind of competitive bidding for provincial offices described in Section 29.4, since such payments offered a relatively convenient source of revenue, and they also made Antiochus IV attentive to the strategic and political stability of border provinces such as Judea, which lay along routes of potential conflict with Egypt.

In 169 BCE, Antiochus IV launched a campaign against Ptolemaic Egypt, examined further in Chapter 27, achieving substantial early success. According to 1 Maccabees, Antiochus IV entered Jerusalem in connection with this campaign and plundered the Temple treasury and vessels, an act the source describes with considerable alarm. The ancient narrative sources do not present a single, easily harmonized chronology of the events that followed: 1 Maccabees connects the Temple plunder directly to this first Egyptian campaign without dwelling on Jason's movements, while 2 Maccabees instead places a rumor of Antiochus IV's death, Jason's attempted armed recovery of the high priesthood from Menelaus, and Antiochus's subsequent punitive action against Jerusalem in connection with developments associated with the king's later, second Egyptian campaign in 168 BCE. Rather than attempting to merge these into a single uncontested sequence of two separate Jason-led coups, students should recognize that 1 and 2 Maccabees preserve different chronological presentations of a crisis that unfolded, in outline, across 169–167 BCE.

Antiochus IV's second Egyptian campaign, in 168 BCE, ended when Roman diplomatic intervention, in the episode later known as the "Day of Eleusis," forced him to withdraw from Egypt entirely. Some ancient and modern historians connect this forced withdrawal to Antiochus IV's subsequent harsher treatment of Jerusalem, though the precise causal link remains a matter of historical inference rather than direct ancient testimony. Upon Antiochus IV's return from Egypt, renewed unrest in Jerusalem, associated by 2 Maccabees with Jason's attempt to retake the high priesthood by force, prompted direct Seleucid military intervention. According to 1 Maccabees, Seleucid forces under a royal officer stormed Jerusalem, killed a substantial number of inhabitants, plundered the city, and established a fortified Seleucid stronghold, the Akra, whose precise location relative to the Temple remains disputed among historians of Jerusalem's topography, staffed partly by Seleucid troops and partly by Jews sympathetic to Menelaus's faction and to closer alignment with the Seleucid crown. This garrison would remain a symbol of foreign and factional domination over Jerusalem for the following decades, and its eventual capture by Simon in 141 BCE, examined in Section 29.9, would mark one of the clearest milestones of the movement's eventual success.

It was in this context of military intervention, garrisoning, and continuing factional conflict that Antiochus IV, in 167 BCE, issued the religious restrictions examined in the following section — restrictions that ancient sources agree were exceptional and unprecedented in scope, whatever their precise motivation. The crisis in Judea by this point had already passed through several stages of escalation: elite competition for the high priesthood, competitive royal appointment undermining hereditary legitimacy, Temple plundering, renewed factional violence, and direct Seleucid military occupation. The religious decrees of 167 BCE arrived as the culmination of this sequence rather than as an isolated or unprovoked act, though this observation explains the timing and context of the decrees without by itself settling the harder historical question of why Antiochus IV chose this particular and unusual response.

Section 29.6

Religious Restrictions and the Problem of Motive

According to 1 Maccabees, 2 Maccabees, and the book of Daniel, Antiochus IV, in 167 BCE, issued decrees prohibiting central elements of Jewish religious practice in Jerusalem and, apparently, in the surrounding towns of Judea: the regular Temple sacrifice was suspended, the observance of the Sabbath and Jewish festivals was forbidden, circumcision of infants was prohibited under penalty of death, possession of Torah scrolls was criminalized, and, most dramatically, an altar to a foreign deity, referred to by 1 Maccabees as "the abomination of desolation," a phrase drawn from and echoing similar language in the book of Daniel, was reportedly erected on top of the Temple's own altar, with sacrifices offered upon it, including, according to some ancient accounts, the sacrifice of pigs, an animal forbidden in Jewish dietary law. Ancient sources describe Jews in Jerusalem and the surrounding towns being compelled, on pain of death, to participate in sacrifices to this foreign cult and to violate other specific requirements of Jewish law.

These measures, as ancient and modern historians alike agree, were extraordinary by the standards of ordinary Hellenistic imperial governance. Seleucid kings elsewhere in their empire generally tolerated, and often patronized, the diverse religious traditions of their many subject peoples, a pattern already discussed in Chapter 28 in connection with Seleucid religious policy generally. No comparable large-scale attempt to suppress an entire subject population's religious practice by law is well attested elsewhere in Seleucid history. This has made the motivation behind the 167 BCE decrees one of the most extensively debated problems in the history of the Hellenistic Jewish world, a problem this chapter addresses directly in the Historical Debate feature later in this section, rather than resolving prematurely here.

Ancient sources themselves offer differing explanations. 2 Maccabees presents the persecution largely in theological terms, as divine punishment permitted because of Jewish sin, later redeemed through faithful resistance and martyrdom; it also emphasizes Menelaus's active collaboration in promoting the decrees to secure his own position. 1 Maccabees presents the crisis more in political terms, describing Antiochus IV's decree as part of a broader royal policy, applied throughout his kingdom, that all his subjects should become "one people" by abandoning their distinctive ancestral customs, with Jerusalem's crisis presented as an especially severe instance of this general policy rather than as a uniquely anti-Jewish measure. The book of Daniel, composed in its final form during or very shortly after these events, frames the persecution in apocalyptic terms as the culminating act of a proud and blasphemous king whose downfall is divinely assured, offering a theological interpretation rather than a political explanation of Antiochus's motives.

Modern historians have proposed several non-mutually-exclusive explanations, examined more fully in the Historical Debate feature below: that the decrees emerged from Menelaus's own initiative as a means of eliminating rival claims to legitimacy rooted in traditional practice; that Antiochus IV, having been forced by Rome to withdraw from Egypt and needing to reassert authority and extract resources from a troublesome province, responded to continued Jerusalem unrest with an unusually harsh assertion of royal control that happened to take a religious form because Jewish identity and Temple practice were so closely intertwined with Judean political loyalty; or that Antiochus IV, whose epithet "Epiphanes" ("god manifest") reflects distinctive personal religious pretensions, pursued a genuinely ideological program of religious unification that Jerusalem's crisis made a convenient test case. No single explanation commands unanimous scholarly agreement, and this chapter treats the question as a genuine and instructive historical debate rather than a settled matter.

Historical Debate

Why Did Antiochus IV Restrict Jewish Religious Practice?

The question: Why did Antiochus IV impose religious restrictions on Jewish practice in Jerusalem in 167 BCE, a measure without clear precedent elsewhere in Seleucid governance? Historians continue to debate whether the decrees originated primarily from local Judean actors, from Antiochus IV's own political calculations, or from a genuinely religious or ideological royal program.

Interpretation A — The crisis originated primarily in local Judean politics: Some historians argue that Menelaus and his faction, having gained the high priesthood through royal favor rather than hereditary legitimacy and facing continued opposition rooted in traditional Jewish practice, encouraged or requested Seleucid intervention against that traditional practice as a means of eliminating rivals and consolidating a Hellenizing faction's control over Jerusalem. On this view, Antiochus IV acted less as an independent religious persecutor than as an outside power drawn into, and manipulated by, an internal Judean factional struggle.
Interpretation B — The decrees reflected Antiochus IV's political and fiscal response to a rebellious province: Other historians emphasize Antiochus IV's own position: forced by Rome to withdraw from Egypt, facing continued Jerusalem unrest that he may have perceived as outright rebellion connected to Ptolemaic sympathies, and needing to reassert control and extract resources from a wealthy but troublesome province, Antiochus IV responded with an exceptionally harsh assertion of royal authority that took a religious form primarily because Jewish identity, law, and Temple worship were so tightly bound up with Judean political loyalty in this period, making religious suppression appear to be an effective method of political pacification.
Interpretation C — The decrees reflected a genuine royal religious or ideological program: A smaller number of historians, taking seriously 1 Maccabees's description of a broader royal policy of cultural unification and Antiochus IV's own distinctive religious self-presentation as "Epiphanes," argue that the Jerusalem decrees expressed a genuine, if unusual, royal ideological commitment to religious and cultural unification across the kingdom, of which Judea's crisis became an especially visible and severe test case.

Evidence: All three interpretations draw on the same core ancient narratives — 1 Maccabees, 2 Maccabees, and Daniel — but weigh the reliability, bias, and emphasis of each source differently, and combine this textual evidence with differing assumptions about ordinary Seleucid governing practice elsewhere in the empire.

Why it matters: How historians answer this question shapes how they interpret the entire Maccabean revolt — as primarily a defensive response to an essentially internal Jewish factional crisis exploited by outside power, as a response to a specific and unusual act of imperial religious persecution, or as resistance to a broader royal ideological program. This textbook does not adopt a single interpretation as certain, but treats the interaction of local Judean politics and Seleucid imperial pressure, discussed throughout Sections 29.4 and 29.5, as the most historically responsible starting point, while acknowledging that Antiochus IV's precise personal motivations remain genuinely uncertain.

Student task: Using the evidence summarized above, evaluate each interpretation's strengths and weaknesses, and explain which combination of factors you find most historically persuasive, being careful to distinguish ancient sources' own theological framing from modern historical explanation.

Section 29.7

Mattathias, Judas Maccabeus, and the Beginning of Revolt

According to 1 Maccabees, armed resistance to the 167 BCE decrees began in the town of Modiin, in the Judean hill country northwest of Jerusalem, when a priest named Mattathias, of the priestly family later called Hasmonaean after an ancestor named Hasmon, refused a royal officer's demand that he offer a pagan sacrifice, killed both a Jew who stepped forward to comply and the royal officer himself, and fled into the hills with his five sons — John, Simon, Judas, Eleazar, and Jonathan — calling on others who remained committed to "the covenant" to join him. This episode, whatever its precise historical detail, marks the narrative beginning of organized armed resistance to the decrees, as distinct from the earlier factional competition and unrest already examined in Sections 29.4 and 29.5.

1 Maccabees records that, in the early days of the uprising, a separate group of Jews who had withdrawn to the wilderness rather than comply with the decrees was discovered on the Sabbath by Seleucid forces; when the group refused to take up arms or even block the entrances to their hiding places in observance of the Sabbath, Seleucid troops killed them. In response to this massacre, Mattathias and his companions decided that self-defense would be permitted even on the Sabbath, a significant and pragmatic legal decision with long-term consequences for later Jewish law regarding the preservation of life. Only after this decision, according to 1 Maccabees, did a group called the Hasidim, generally translated "the pious ones" and described as men "devoted to the law," join Mattathias's forces. The precise relationship between the massacred Sabbath observers and the Hasidim who later joined Mattathias is not stated explicitly in the source and should not be assumed; what the sequence does show clearly is that the movement, from its earliest days, involved genuine debate over how strictly religious law should be applied even during a struggle undertaken in law's defense, a complexity easily lost in later simplified retellings.

Mattathias died within roughly a year of beginning the resistance, and leadership passed to his son Judas, who became known by the epithet Maccabeus, traditionally interpreted as meaning "the Hammer," though the epithet's exact derivation is uncertain and remains debated among philologists; the entire movement and the biblical books recounting it take their conventional modern name from this epithet. Judas proved a capable military commander, winning a series of engagements against Seleucid forces sent to suppress the uprising, including victories described in 1 Maccabees at Beth-Horon and Emmaus against forces considerably larger and better equipped than his own. These early victories relied heavily on Judas's use of the Judean hill country's difficult terrain, on ambush tactics suited to a smaller and more mobile force, and on the Seleucid Empire's own preoccupations elsewhere in its territory, which limited the resources Antiochus IV could immediately commit to suppressing what may initially have appeared to Seleucid authorities as a containable local disturbance rather than a serious threat to imperial control.

By 164 BCE, after further victories, including a significant engagement at Beth-Zur against a larger Seleucid force under the general Lysias, Judas and his forces controlled sufficient territory around Jerusalem to move against the Temple itself, still occupied and, according to Jewish sources, defiled by the altar and practices imposed in 167 BCE. This military success set the stage for the Temple's recapture and rededication, examined in the next section, an event that both ancient and modern accounts treat as the revolt's first great triumph, even though, as later sections show, it did not yet mark the end of the conflict or the achievement of full political independence.

Section 29.8

Temple Rededication and the Meaning of Hanukkah

In late 164 BCE, according to 1 Maccabees, Judas and his forces regained control of the Temple Mount, though the adjacent Akra fortress, discussed in Section 29.5, remained in Seleucid and pro-Menelaus hands for nearly another two decades. Judas ordered the defiled altar dismantled and a new altar built in its place, had the Temple building itself cleansed and its furnishings restored or replaced, and rekindled the Temple's sacred lampstand. 1 Maccabees reports that the rededication and resumption of sacrifice were celebrated for eight days beginning on the twenty-fifth day of the Jewish month of Kislev, deliberately matching the date on which the foreign cult had reportedly first been installed three years earlier, and that Judas and the community established an annual eight-day commemorative festival, later known as Hanukkah, meaning "dedication."

2 Maccabees provides an independent account of the same rededication, connecting the newly instituted festival explicitly to the earlier Israelite festival of Sukkot, or Tabernacles, which the community had reportedly been unable to observe properly during the period of persecution, and framing the eight-day commemoration partly as a delayed celebration of that festival. This detail illustrates a broader point already emphasized in this chapter's opening: 1 Maccabees and 2 Maccabees, while agreeing on the basic fact and approximate date of the Temple's rededication, differ in their literary framing and theological emphasis, a difference students should treat as valuable historical evidence about how different early communities remembered and interpreted the same event rather than as a simple contradiction to be resolved.

Neither 1 Maccabees nor 2 Maccabees, nor any source from this period, mentions the story most familiar from later popular observance of Hanukkah: that a small quantity of ritually pure oil, sufficient by rights for only a single day, miraculously burned for the full eight days required to prepare new oil. This tradition appears for the first time many centuries later, in the rabbinic compilation known as the Babylonian Talmud, whose final compositional form is generally dated by scholars to somewhere in the range of the fifth through seventh centuries CE, though the precise compositional history remains debated, in a passage that itself acknowledges the earlier military and political framing of the festival while adding this specific miracle narrative. Modern historians generally treat the oil-miracle tradition as a later rabbinic elaboration. Some scholars have suggested this elaboration may reflect later rabbinic ambivalence about celebrating a military and political victory associated with a priestly dynasty, the Hasmoneans, whose later rule, as Section 29.12 will show, became deeply controversial among some Jewish religious authorities; other scholars point to additional or alternative processes in the longer evolution of Hanukkah's commemorative meaning. This remains an area of ongoing scholarly discussion rather than a single settled explanation. This distinction between the earliest, largely military and political accounts of the Temple's rededication and the later, primarily miraculous rabbinic tradition provides an instructive case study, developed further in the Primary Source Window later in this chapter, of how historical events can be commemorated in ways that shift considerably over time in response to later religious and political concerns.

The rededication of the Temple in 164 BCE achieved the movement's original, most immediate religious objective: the restoration of legitimate sacrificial worship and the removal of the specific decrees examined in Section 29.6. It is important for students to recognize, however, that this achievement did not by itself end the conflict or establish Judean political independence. The Akra fortress remained under hostile control, Seleucid forces remained present in the region, and Judas's movement, as the following section shows, continued fighting for nearly two more decades before securing anything resembling full self-government. Treating 164 BCE as the story's conclusion, as popular retellings sometimes do, obscures the considerably longer and more gradual process by which religious restoration eventually became political independence.

Section 29.9

From Religious Revolt to Political Independence

Following the Temple's rededication, Judas Maccabeus continued military campaigns, described in 1 Maccabees, against neighboring populations in Idumea, Ammon, Gilead, and Galilee whom the sources describe as hostile to the Jewish communities living among them, campaigns that combined continued defense of the movement's gains with the protection and, in some cases, relocation of scattered Jewish communities beyond Judea's immediate core. Judas also pursued diplomatic contact beyond the region, and 1 Maccabees records an embassy sent to Rome around 161 BCE seeking a formal treaty of friendship, an early instance of the pattern, examined more fully in later chapters on Rome's expanding involvement in the eastern Mediterranean, of regional actors seeking Roman recognition and support against more immediate imperial neighbors, without this contact yet implying any substantial Roman military commitment to Judean affairs.

Judas was killed in battle at Elasa in 160 BCE against a large Seleucid force under the general Bacchides, a defeat that 1 Maccabees presents as a serious setback for the movement, which briefly lost significant ground before rallying under Judas's brother Jonathan. Jonathan pursued a more gradual and diplomatically opportunistic strategy than his brother had, benefiting substantially from a prolonged succession struggle within the Seleucid royal house itself, examined further in Chapter 28's treatment of later Seleucid dynastic instability. Rival claimants to the Seleucid throne, competing for support wherever they could find it, offered Jonathan increasingly significant concessions in exchange for his backing: around 152 BCE, the claimant Alexander Balas appointed Jonathan as high priest, formally uniting, for the first time since the crisis began, effective military leadership of the resistance movement with the office whose earlier corruption, examined in Section 29.4, had helped precipitate the entire crisis. Jonathan was later treacherously killed by another Seleucid claimant in 143 BCE, and leadership passed to the last surviving brother, Simon.

Simon achieved the most decisive successes of the movement's political struggle. In 142 BCE, exploiting continued Seleucid dynastic weakness, Simon secured from the Seleucid king Demetrius II a formal exemption from tribute payments, an act 1 Maccabees treats as marking the effective removal of "the yoke of the Gentiles" from Judea, language reflecting the movement's own understanding that this concession represented substantive, if not universally or permanently secure, political independence. In 141 BCE, Simon's forces finally captured the Akra fortress, discussed since Section 29.5 as a persistent symbol of foreign and factional domination over Jerusalem, an achievement 1 Maccabees describes as accompanied by public celebration, since it removed the last significant garrison of hostile forces from the city itself.

These achievements culminated in 140 BCE in a formal assembly of "the priests and the people and the elders of the land," described in 1 Maccabees, which confirmed Simon as high priest, military commander, and civil leader ("ethnarch") of the Jewish people, declaring these offices to belong to Simon and his descendants "forever, until a trustworthy prophet should arise." This declaration, inscribed on bronze tablets and displayed publicly according to the source, marks the formal transition from an emergency resistance leadership to what modern historians recognize as the establishment of a hereditary dynastic state, the subject of the following section. The qualifying phrase "until a trustworthy prophet should arise" is itself historically significant, since it suggests that even this founding declaration of hereditary Hasmonean authority acknowledged some degree of provisional or contested legitimacy, a tension that would resurface, as Section 29.12 shows, throughout the following century of Hasmonean rule.

Section 29.10

The Formation of the Hasmonean State

The assembly of 140 BCE, examined at the end of the preceding section, marks the institutional foundation of what modern historians call the Hasmonean state, named after the family's ancestor Hasmon, though the family itself, and the Jewish tradition generally, more often referred to Simon's line as the house of the Maccabees or simply by individual rulers' names. This new political order combined several distinct sources of authority that had previously existed separately, or had not existed at all, within Judean government: the high priesthood, with its inherited religious legitimacy and control over Temple wealth, discussed in Section 29.3; military command over a now substantial and increasingly professionalized armed force, built up over decades of continuous warfare; and a form of hereditary civil leadership, expressed through the title ethnarch under Simon and, as the following section shows, eventually through the more explicitly Hellenistic royal title of king under his successors.

This combination of offices in a single hereditary line represented a genuine institutional innovation within Judean history, though the innovation should be stated precisely. As Section 29.3 explained, the high priesthood had long combined religious leadership with a significant degree of political representation; the office was never purely religious in a narrow sense. What was new under the Hasmoneans was not the combination of religious and political functions as such, but rather the fusion of the high priesthood with explicit hereditary dynastic succession, standing professional military command, sovereign state authority over taxation and foreign relations, and, eventually, the formally Hellenistic title of king, all vested in a single family line whose claim to the priesthood itself, as the following paragraph notes, could not be clearly grounded in Zadokite descent.

Some ancient and modern observers have noted the tension inherent in this combination: the high priesthood traditionally required descent from the Zadokite priestly line, a qualification the Hasmonean family, though certainly priestly, could not straightforwardly claim, since surviving evidence does not establish a clear Zadokite genealogy for Mattathias's family. This tension, largely unaddressed in the movement's own triumphant early accounts, would become an important source of later religious controversy, examined further in Section 29.12.

Simon's successors expanded the state's institutional apparatus significantly. John Hyrcanus, Simon's son, who ruled from 134 to 104 BCE, maintained and expanded the standing army, in part through the employment of foreign mercenary troops, a practice attested by Josephus and consistent with wider Hellenistic military practice examined in Chapter 28. Hyrcanus also issued coinage in his own name, using the paleo-Hebrew script associated with the pre-exilic monarchy in a deliberate assertion of continuity with ancient Israelite kingship, even though he did not yet claim the royal title itself. This combination of religious office, military command, minted coinage, and effective sovereign authority over internal administration and foreign relations placed the Hasmonean state, by the end of the second century BCE, in a position functionally comparable to the smaller Hellenistic successor kingdoms and city-states examined throughout Chapters 27 and 28, even before any Hasmonean ruler formally adopted the title of king.

That final step came under Hyrcanus's son Aristobulus I, who ruled only briefly from 104 to 103 BCE and who, according to Josephus, was the first Hasmonean ruler to adopt the diadem and the title basileus, or king, in explicit imitation of surrounding Hellenistic monarchies; some modern scholarship, however, leaves the precise beginning of formal Hasmonean kingship uncertain between Aristobulus I and his successor Alexander Jannaeus, since the ancient evidence does not permit full certainty on this point. His successor and brother, Alexander Jannaeus, who ruled from 103 to 76 BCE, continued this royal style while pursuing the extensive territorial expansion examined in the following section, combining Hellenistic-style kingship, the high priesthood, and continued military conquest in a single ruling figure whose reign represents, in many respects, the fullest development of the institutional pattern first established at the assembly of 140 BCE.

Historical Vocabulary
High Priesthood and Ethnarchy

The term ethnarch, from Greek roots meaning "ruler of a people," describes a Hellenistic-era title granted to a local leader recognized as head of a specific ethnic or national community, typically as an alternative to full territorial kingship, often used where an imperial power wished to recognize a subordinate leader's authority without granting the more prestigious and potentially threatening title of king. Simon's title in the 140 BCE assembly reflects this pattern: full sovereignty remained formally contested and constrained by continuing, if weakened, Seleucid claims, and the title ethnarch acknowledged Simon's real authority over the Jewish community without yet claiming the independent royal status that Aristobulus I would later adopt.

The combination of the high priesthood with this kind of civil and military ethnarchic authority, and eventually with formal kingship, represented the Hasmonean dynasty's most distinctive and, to some contemporaries, most troubling institutional innovation. Because the high priesthood traditionally required Zadokite descent, a qualification not clearly established for the Hasmonean family, the dynasty's combination of priestly and political-military authority remained a source of ongoing religious controversy throughout the following century, discussed further in Section 29.12 and directly relevant to the emergence of some of the distinct Jewish groups examined there.

Section 29.11

Expansion, Conquest, and the Problem of Hasmonean Rule

The century following Simon's ethnarchy saw the Hasmonean state expand from a small autonomous territory centered on Jerusalem and the Judean hill country into a substantially larger regional kingdom, achieved primarily through military conquest under John Hyrcanus, Aristobulus I, and Alexander Jannaeus. This expansion transformed the movement's original character in ways worth examining carefully, since a state that had begun as a defensive response to religious persecution increasingly took on the character of an expansionist regional power employing methods that raised serious questions, both among contemporaries and among later historians, about the relationship between the dynasty's founding religious legitimacy and its later territorial ambitions.

John Hyrcanus's most significant conquest was Idumea, the region south of Judea inhabited by descendants of the biblical Edomites, whom Hyrcanus's forces defeated and, according to Josephus, compelled to accept circumcision and Jewish law as a condition of remaining in their territory under Hasmonean rule. This episode, described by Josephus using the Greek term for "forced conversion" or, in some translations, simply "compelled to live according to Jewish customs," represents one of the clearest instances in ancient Jewish history of large-scale, coercive incorporation of a non-Jewish population into Jewish religious and communal life, a practice with no clear precedent in earlier Judean history and one that modern historians debate in terms of both its precise legal mechanism and its longer-term social consequences, including the eventual integration of Idumean elites, most famously the family of Herod examined in Chapter 31, into later Judean political life.

Hyrcanus also destroyed the Samaritan temple on Mount Gerizim, the rival sanctuary of the Samaritan community, whose distinct religious tradition and long-standing tension with Jerusalem-centered Judaism are discussed further in later chapters on Second Temple Jewish sectarian diversity. This destruction, generally dated to the final decades of the second century BCE on the basis of both literary and archaeological evidence, deepened a religious and communal division between Jews and Samaritans that would persist for centuries and that appears repeatedly in later Second Temple and early Christian-era sources.

Alexander Jannaeus pursued the most extensive territorial expansion of any Hasmonean ruler, campaigning along the Mediterranean coastal cities, in Transjordan, and in the Galilee, extending Hasmonean control over a territory considerably larger than Judea's traditional core and, at its height, encompassing much of the land historically associated with ancient Israel and Judah, discussed in Chapter 18. This expansion, however, came at a substantial cost. Jannaeus's military campaigns were frequently brutal, and his reign, examined further from the perspective of internal Jewish religious politics in the next section, was marked by serious domestic conflict, including a civil war in which Jannaeus's Jewish opponents reportedly invited Seleucid intervention against their own king, and a subsequent, harshly reported episode of mass execution of defeated opponents. These episodes illustrate a broader pattern worth emphasizing to students: the same dynasty whose founding narrative celebrated resistance to foreign religious persecution had, within two or three generations, become capable of harsh domestic repression against fellow Jews and coercive incorporation of non-Jewish populations, a transformation this chapter treats as historically significant rather than as a betrayal to be moralized about, and one that helps explain the growing internal Jewish opposition examined in Section 29.12.

Section 29.12

Jewish Political and Religious Diversity under the Hasmoneans

The Hasmonean century did not produce a single, unified Jewish religious or political community, despite the dynasty's own claims to represent legitimate continuity with the original revolt's religious commitments. Instead, this period saw the emergence, or at least the first clear historical visibility, of several distinct Jewish groups whose differing approaches to religious law, Temple authority, and the legitimacy of Hasmonean rule would matter enormously for later Jewish history, examined further in Chapter 31 and in later chapters treating rabbinic Judaism's formation after the Temple's destruction.

The Pharisees, first clearly attested as a distinct group during the Hasmonean period, are described by Josephus and later rabbinic sources as upholding, alongside the written Torah, a body of ancestral tradition concerning the law's application to daily practice; Josephus speaks of their adherence to such "ancestral traditions," though the ancient evidence does not establish that the Pharisees themselves defined their own authority specifically in terms of an exclusively oral mode of transmission, a characterization that owes a good deal to later rabbinic retrospection. The Pharisees appear, from this same outside evidence, to have drawn support from a relatively broad social base beyond the priestly aristocracy. Josephus records serious conflict between Pharisaic groups and Hasmonean rulers, most dramatically during Alexander Jannaeus's reign, when Pharisaic opposition to Jannaeus's combination of kingship and high priesthood, and to his conduct as both military and religious leader, contributed to the civil conflict mentioned in the previous section; Josephus reports Jannaeus's brutal suppression of Pharisaic and allied opposition, including mass crucifixions, an episode that, whatever its precise numerical accuracy, indicates the severity of internal Jewish political division by this point in Hasmonean rule.

The Sadducees present an even harder evidentiary problem, since they left no surviving writings of their own; nearly everything historians know about Sadducean belief and practice comes from outsiders or opponents, chiefly Josephus, the New Testament, and later rabbinic sources, all of which had their own reasons to characterize the group in particular ways. According to this outside evidence, the Sadducees were generally associated with priestly and aristocratic circles and with the Hasmonean court itself for much of this period, were reported to reject certain Pharisaic traditions not explicitly grounded in the written Torah, and were said to hold distinctive positions on specific religious questions, including beliefs about resurrection and divine providence. Because the evidence is entirely second-hand, students should treat these characterizations as what Josephus and later sources report about the Sadducees rather than as a securely reconstructed statement of Sadducean doctrine in the group's own terms. The relationship between the Sadducees and Hasmonean rulers was generally reported as closer than the often-strained relationship between the Hasmoneans and the Pharisees, though this alignment shifted over the course of the dynasty's history and should not be treated as fixed or permanent.

A third group, the Essenes, described by several ancient sources including Josephus, Philo of Alexandria, and the Roman writer Pliny the Elder, practiced a more separatist form of communal religious life, and many modern scholars associate at least some Essene communities with the settlement at Qumran near the Dead Sea and with the Dead Sea Scrolls discovered there in the twentieth century, a connection this textbook treats as probable but not certain, since the precise relationship between the Qumran community, the scrolls, and the Essenes described in ancient literary sources remains a genuinely debated question in modern scholarship. Some scholars associate the scrolls' distinctive criticism of a "Wicked Priest," a figure some interpreters identify with a specific Hasmonean high priest, as further evidence of serious religious opposition to Hasmonean control of the high priesthood from at least some sectors of Jewish society.

These divisions, taken together, illustrate a central theme of this chapter's final section and, indeed, of the chapter as a whole: the Hasmonean state, for all its real achievements in restoring Temple worship, securing political independence, and expanding Jewish-controlled territory, did not resolve the underlying tensions over religious authority and political legitimacy that had contributed to the original crisis of the 170s and 160s BCE. Instead, Hasmonean rule generated new and serious divisions of its own, rooted in disagreement over whether the dynasty's combination of priestly and political-military power was legitimate, over how Jewish law should properly be interpreted and by what authority, and over the moral and religious costs of the dynasty's later territorial expansion and domestic repression. These unresolved tensions would continue to shape Jewish political and religious life into the following century, examined in Chapter 31, when internal Hasmonean succession conflict would provide the opening through which Roman power entered Judean affairs directly, bringing to a close the century of independence that the Maccabean revolt had achieved.

Primary Source Window

1 Maccabees 1:11–15 on Jerusalem's Civic Reformers, 2nd century BCE

Creator: Unknown Judean author, writing in Hebrew (surviving only in Greek translation) · Date: composed in the late second century BCE, describing events of the 170s–130s BCE · Type: Historical narrative, strongly favorable to the Hasmonean dynasty

Context: 1 Maccabees narrates the revolt and the establishment of Hasmonean rule from a perspective sympathetic to the Hasmonean family, likely composed under one of the later Hasmonean rulers to legitimate the dynasty's authority. The specific passage excerpted here, 1 Maccabees 1:11–15, describes unnamed Jerusalem reformers who built a gymnasium and adopted Greek customs; the text does not name Jason. The fuller, named narrative of Jason's purchase of the high priesthood and his civic reform program comes instead from the independent account in 2 Maccabees 4, discussed throughout Section 29.4 of this chapter. Reading both sources together allows historians to connect 1 Maccabees's unnamed "lawless men" with the civic program 2 Maccabees attributes specifically to Jason, while recognizing that this connection is an inference rather than something either text states outright.

Paraphrase (1 Maccabees 1:11–15): The text describes how, in those days, certain lawless men came forth from Israel and persuaded others, saying "let us make a covenant with the surrounding nations." Some of the people eagerly went to the king, who authorized them to follow the ordinances of the nations; they built a gymnasium in Jerusalem according to Gentile custom, disguised the marks of their circumcision, abandoned the holy covenant, and joined themselves to the surrounding nations.

Analysis: This passage illustrates the author's strongly critical framing of the civic reform program, treating cultural Hellenization among some Jerusalem elites as a serious departure from covenantal faithfulness. Read alongside Section 29.2's broader discussion, however, students should recognize that this passage describes the actions of a specific, unnamed faction pursuing specific civic changes, not a general Jewish embrace of Greek culture, and that the author's sharply negative characterization reflects the text's overall purpose of legitimating the Hasmonean dynasty's later rule as a restoration of proper covenantal fidelity. The passage should therefore be read as a valuable but partisan historical source, not as a neutral description of Hellenistic Jewish society as a whole, and its unnamed reformers should not be silently equated with Jason without acknowledging that the identification rests on reading 1 Maccabees alongside 2 Maccabees's differently framed account.

Multiple Perspectives

Living through the Crisis and Revolt

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns historians have identified from the ancient sources discussed throughout this chapter. They are not quotations from any single named historical actor.

From the perspective of a Jerusalem priestly official aligned with Jason's reforms: Adopting Greek civic institutions and Jerusalem's status as a recognized Hellenistic polis offers practical advantages in dealings with the Seleucid court and neighboring Greek cities, without necessarily requiring abandonment of Temple sacrifice or ancestral religious observance, whatever critics may later claim.
From the perspective of a rural Judean supporter of Mattathias's resistance: The demand to violate the Sabbath, abandon circumcision, and sacrifice to a foreign god on the Temple's own altar represents an intolerable assault on the covenant itself, one that must be resisted even at the cost of one's life or livelihood, regardless of the political risks of armed defiance against the crown.
From the perspective of a Seleucid provincial administrator under Antiochus IV: Jerusalem's repeated internal unrest, competing claimants to the high priesthood, and apparent instability during a period of military and fiscal pressure on the kingdom demand firm royal intervention to restore order and secure reliable revenue and loyalty from this province.
From the perspective of a Pharisaic critic under Alexander Jannaeus's later reign: A dynasty that began by defending the Torah against foreign persecution has, within a few generations, itself combined kingship with the high priesthood in a manner inconsistent with proper priestly lineage, waged harsh wars against fellow Jews, and ruled in ways that betray rather than fulfill the revolt's original religious commitments.
Continuity and Change Over Time

The High Priesthood Transformed

What Changed

  • The high priesthood moved from hereditary Zadokite succession to competitive royal appointment under Jason and Menelaus, and then to Hasmonean hereditary control lacking clear Zadokite descent
  • Judean political authority moved from a high priest representing the community to a Seleucid crown, to a dynastic ethnarch and eventually king combining religious, military, and civil authority
  • Judea's territory expanded from the core hill country around Jerusalem to a substantially larger kingdom including Idumea, Samaria, and Galilee
  • Armed Jewish resistance moved from local guerrilla activity under Judas to a professionalized standing army employing foreign mercenaries under later Hasmonean rulers

What Stayed the Same

  • The Jerusalem Temple remained the central site of legitimate Jewish sacrificial worship throughout this entire period and beyond
  • Jewish communities continued to combine varying degrees of Hellenistic cultural participation with continued religious observance throughout the century
  • Disputes over religious authority and legitimate interpretation of the law persisted from the pre-revolt elite competition through the Pharisee-Sadducee-Essene divisions of the later Hasmonean period
  • Outside imperial powers — first Ptolemaic, then Seleucid, and eventually Roman, examined in Chapter 31 — continued to shape Judean political outcomes throughout the period

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both the dramatic institutional changes and the underlying continuities in Judean religious and political life helps students avoid treating the Maccabean revolt as either a complete rupture with earlier Judean history or a simple restoration of an unchanging traditional order.

Cause and Effect

From Crisis to State

Long-term causes

The structural vulnerability of a high priesthood appointed by distant Hellenistic kings, combined with the Seleucid Empire's chronic fiscal pressure following the Treaty of Apamea, created conditions in which elite competition for office could escalate into a wider crisis.

Short-term causes

Jason's and Menelaus's competing bids for the high priesthood, Antiochus IV's Roman-forced withdrawal from Egypt, renewed Jerusalem unrest, and the imposition of unprecedented religious restrictions in 167 BCE combined to trigger armed resistance under Mattathias and Judas.

Effects

Religious restoration in 164 BCE gradually expanded, through continued warfare and the exploitation of Seleucid dynastic conflict, into formal tribute exemption, capture of the Akra, and the establishment of hereditary Hasmonean rule combining priesthood, military command, and eventually kingship.

This textbook's response

This textbook treats the revolt's outcome as the product of interacting causes — internal Judean factional competition, Seleucid imperial weakness, and specific contingent events — rather than as the inevitable unfolding of either religious destiny or nationalist historical necessity.

Historical complexity: The same dynasty whose founding narrative rested on defense of Jewish law against foreign persecution became, within three generations, an expansionist regional monarchy whose methods and internal conflicts generated the religious and political divisions examined in Section 29.12, a complexity this textbook treats as essential to understanding the period rather than as a contradiction to be resolved.

Everyday Life and Social History

Ordinary Life amid Crisis and State-Building

Behind the narrative of high priests, kings, and armies examined in this chapter lay the experience of ordinary Judean farmers, townspeople, and villagers whose lives were directly affected by decades of warfare, shifting taxation, and religious upheaval. Rural communities in the Judean hill country, from which Mattathias's resistance drew much of its early support, faced the practical burdens of sheltering fighters, providing supplies, and risking Seleucid reprisal, while urban residents of Jerusalem experienced repeated changes in civic and religious authority, periods of military occupation, and, at various points, direct violence associated with factional conflict among rival elite claimants.

Women's experience during this period is only sparsely documented in the surviving sources, which focus overwhelmingly on male priestly, military, and political figures, but scattered evidence, including passages in 2 Maccabees describing mothers and families affected by the persecution, indicates that religious observance, family continuity, and the transmission of Jewish practice across generations remained central concerns for ordinary households throughout the crisis. The incorporation of Idumean and other non-Jewish populations under later Hasmonean expansion, discussed in Section 29.11, likewise had significant, if unevenly documented, consequences for family life, religious practice, and social status among the populations affected, consequences that later chapters on Herodian Judea, itself descended from incorporated Idumean elites, will examine further.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced the transformation of Judea from a small, generally quiet Seleucid province into an independent Jewish kingdom over the course of roughly seventy years, beginning with the political and religious crisis of the 170s and 160s BCE and culminating in the mature Hasmonean state of the later second and early first centuries BCE. Section 29.1 established Judea's transition from Ptolemaic to Seleucid rule after the Battle of Panium (198 BCE in current scholarship; traditionally dated 200 BCE), and the negotiated charter through which Antiochus III initially preserved substantial local autonomy under the traditional Zadokite high priesthood.

Section 29.2 cautioned against reading the crisis that followed as a simple conflict between Hellenism and Judaism, establishing instead that Hellenistic cultural participation was already widespread and variable among Jews of this period, coexisting with continued Temple worship and legal observance in numerous combinations. Section 29.3 explained why control of the high priesthood carried such enormous stakes, combining hereditary religious legitimacy, political representation, and control of substantial Temple wealth in a single office subject to royal appointment.

Section 29.4 examined the elite competition that destabilized this office in the 170s BCE, as Jason and then Menelaus displaced traditional Zadokite claimants through competitive payments to the Seleucid crown, while Section 29.5 traced how this internal factional conflict intersected with Antiochus IV's own military and fiscal pressures, particularly following his Roman-forced withdrawal from Egypt, to produce direct Seleucid military intervention in Jerusalem. Section 29.6 examined the unprecedented religious restrictions imposed in 167 BCE and the genuine historical debate, developed further in this chapter's Historical Debate feature, over whether these restrictions originated primarily in local Judean factional politics, in Antiochus IV's political and fiscal calculations, or in a broader royal ideological program.

Sections 29.7 and 29.8 traced the armed resistance that followed, from Mattathias's initial defiance at Modiin through Judas Maccabeus's military campaigns to the recapture and rededication of the Temple in 164 BCE, while carefully distinguishing the earliest sources' largely military and political framing of this event from the later rabbinic tradition of the miraculous oil associated with the modern festival of Hanukkah. Section 29.9 showed how the movement's goals expanded gradually, over nearly two more decades under Jonathan and Simon, from religious restoration to effective political independence, achieved substantially through the exploitation of Seleucid dynastic civil war rather than through military victory alone, culminating in the assembly of 140 BCE that confirmed hereditary Hasmonean leadership.

Section 29.10 examined the resulting Hasmonean state's distinctive combination of high priesthood, military command, and eventually Hellenistic-style kingship in a single hereditary line, an institutional innovation without clear precedent in earlier Judean history. Section 29.11 traced the substantial territorial expansion achieved under John Hyrcanus, Aristobulus I, and Alexander Jannaeus, including the coercive incorporation of Idumea and the destruction of the Samaritan temple, developments that transformed the movement's original defensive character into something closer to an expansionist regional monarchy.

Section 29.12 concluded the chapter by examining the Pharisees, Sadducees, and Essenes as distinct Jewish groups whose differing approaches to law, Temple authority, and the legitimacy of Hasmonean rule became clearly visible during this period, generating serious internal conflict, including violent confrontation under Alexander Jannaeus, that reflected unresolved tensions over religious and political legitimacy inherited from the original crisis of the 170s and 160s BCE.

Taken as a whole, this chapter has argued against two common oversimplifications: that the Maccabean revolt arose from a straightforward clash between Judaism and Hellenism, and that the revolt's success in 164 BCE marked the completion, rather than merely the beginning, of the movement's transformation of Judean society. The actual history, as this chapter has shown, involved a much longer and more complicated interaction of internal elite competition, Seleucid imperial pressure, contingent military and diplomatic developments, and the gradual, often contested construction of new political and religious institutions whose consequences would continue to shape Jewish history well beyond the Hasmonean dynasty's eventual fall to Roman power, examined in Chapter 31.

These developments also illustrate this textbook's recurring insistence, stated in Chapter 1 and repeated throughout Volume II, that religious history must be understood as historically dynamic rather than essentialist: Second Temple Judaism in the second and first centuries BCE was not a single, fixed tradition but a field of genuine internal diversity and contestation, out of which distinct movements would emerge whose later development, examined in subsequent volumes, would shape Jewish religious life for centuries to come.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Hellenization
The spread of Greek language, institutions, and cultural forms throughout the territories of Alexander's successor kingdoms; a variable and often coexisting, rather than replacing, influence on Jewish religious practice, discussed in Section 29.2.
High priesthood
The hereditary office combining religious leadership of the Jerusalem Temple with political representation of the Judean community, whose disruption in the 170s BCE helped precipitate the crisis examined in Section 29.4.
Gymnasium
A Greek civic institution for physical and educational training, whose establishment in Jerusalem under Jason symbolized a program of Greek-style civic reorganization, discussed in Section 29.2 and 29.4.
Ethnarch
A Hellenistic-era title designating recognized leadership of an ethnic community, short of full kingship, granted to Simon in 140 BCE, discussed in Section 29.9 and 29.10.
Coele-Syria
The Hellenistic term for the southern Levant, including Judea, contested between the Ptolemaic and Seleucid kingdoms until the Battle of Panium, dated to 198 BCE in current scholarship (traditionally 200 BCE), discussed in Section 29.1.
Akra
A fortified Seleucid stronghold in Jerusalem whose precise location relative to the Temple remains disputed, garrisoned by Seleucid and allied forces from 167 BCE until its capture by Simon in 141 BCE, discussed in Sections 29.5 and 29.9.
Hasidim
A group of Jews described in 1 Maccabees as "devoted to the law" who joined Mattathias's early resistance, discussed in Section 29.7.
Apocalyptic literature
A genre of ancient Jewish and Christian writing, exemplified by the book of Daniel, that interprets historical crisis through symbolic visions of divine judgment and ultimate vindication, discussed in Section 29.6.
Hasmonean
The family name, derived from an ancestor called Hasmon, applied by modern historians to the dynasty descended from Mattathias and his sons, discussed throughout Sections 29.9 through 29.12.
Diadem
The royal headband symbolizing Hellenistic kingship; according to Josephus, first adopted among Hasmonean rulers by Aristobulus I, though some modern scholarship leaves it uncertain whether Aristobulus or Alexander Jannaeus first claimed the royal title, discussed in Section 29.10.
Pharisees
A Jewish group first clearly attested during the Hasmonean period, described by Josephus and later rabbinic sources as upholding ancestral traditions alongside the written Torah, though the ancient evidence does not establish that Pharisees themselves defined their authority in terms of an exclusively oral mode of transmission; often in conflict with Hasmonean rulers, discussed in Section 29.12.
Sadducees
A Jewish group reported by outsiders, chiefly Josephus and later sources, to be associated with priestly and aristocratic circles and to reject certain traditions not grounded in the written Torah; since the Sadducees left no surviving writings of their own, these characterizations should be read as what outside sources report rather than securely reconstructed Sadducean doctrine, discussed in Section 29.12.
Essenes
A separatist Jewish group described by ancient sources and possibly associated with the Dead Sea Scrolls and the Qumran community, discussed in Section 29.12.
Halakhah
The body of Jewish legal interpretation and practice, subject to differing approaches among Pharisees, Sadducees, and other groups during this period, discussed in Section 29.12.
Idumea
The region south of Judea, inhabited by descendants of the ancient Edomites, conquered and incorporated, including coerced adoption of Jewish law, under John Hyrcanus, discussed in Section 29.11.
Jewish-Samaritan religious divergence
The long historical process, rather than a single identifiable schism, by which Jewish and Samaritan religious traditions grew apart; the process was deepened, though not caused outright, by John Hyrcanus's destruction of the Samaritan temple on Mount Gerizim, discussed in Section 29.11.
Client kingship
A form of rule in which a local ruler retains internal authority while formally subordinate to a larger imperial power; relevant background for understanding the qualified independence achieved by Simon and later Hasmonean rulers.
Tax farming
A system in which the right to collect taxes was purchased from the crown in exchange for a fixed payment, practiced by Jewish elite families such as the Tobiads under Ptolemaic rule, discussed in Section 29.1.
Zadokite lineage
The traditional hereditary priestly line, tracing legitimacy to the priest Zadok, from which the pre-crisis high priesthood was drawn and from which the Hasmonean family could not clearly claim descent, discussed in Sections 29.3 and 29.10.
Second Temple Judaism
The broad term for Jewish religious life centered on the Jerusalem Temple from its postexilic rebuilding until its destruction in 70 CE, of which the Hasmonean period forms a central and formative chapter.
1 Maccabees
A historical narrative sympathetic to the Hasmonean dynasty, composed in the late second century BCE, one of the two principal narrative sources for this chapter, discussed throughout.
2 Maccabees
An independent narrative account of the crisis and revolt, differing in emphasis and literary framing from 1 Maccabees, discussed throughout this chapter.
Hanukkah
The eight-day Jewish festival commemorating the Temple's rededication in 164 BCE, whose earliest attested meaning differed from the later rabbinic oil-miracle tradition, discussed in Section 29.8.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has traced one of the best-documented and most consequential transformations in the history of the Hellenistic Near East: the passage of a small Seleucid province through religious crisis, armed revolt, and gradual state formation into an independent Jewish kingdom governed by a single dynastic family. The habits of mind this chapter has emphasized — resisting the temptation to reduce complex crises to a single clash of civilizations, distinguishing the earliest evidence from later commemorative tradition, and tracing how a movement's goals and character can change substantially over the course of decades — are not specific to Jewish history alone. They apply equally to the study of nationalist movements, religious reform movements, and revolutionary states examined throughout the later volumes of this textbook.

The Hasmonean state's achievements were real: the restoration of Temple worship, the establishment of a century of substantial Jewish political self-governance in a region otherwise dominated by larger imperial powers, and the territorial and institutional foundations upon which later Judean society, including the Herodian and Roman-era developments examined in Chapter 31, would build. Its costs and contradictions were equally real: the coercive incorporation of conquered populations, the violent suppression of internal Jewish opposition, and the unresolved tension between the dynasty's priestly claims and its increasingly Hellenistic style of kingship, a tension that would continue to generate conflict until Roman intervention brought the dynasty's independent rule to an end.

Students who understand this chapter's central argument — that the Maccabean revolt and Hasmonean state arose from a specific, contingent interaction of internal Jewish political competition, Seleucid imperial pressure, and the gradual, often improvised construction of new institutions, rather than from an inevitable clash between Judaism and Hellenism — are well prepared to approach later chapters treating religious and political transformation with the same careful, evidence-based skepticism toward simple, triumphant, or purely oppositional narratives.

Chapter 30 turns from this focused Judean case study back outward to the wider geopolitics of the eastern Mediterranean and Near East in this same broad period, examining Parthia's imperial development, Armenia's strategic position, Nabataean commerce, and the emergence of a durable Roman-Iranian frontier. Chapter 31 will return directly to Judea, examining how the internal succession conflicts of the later Hasmonean dynasty opened the door to direct Roman intervention, Herodian rule, and the Jewish-Roman wars that would ultimately bring about the Second Temple's destruction — a story that depends, at every stage, on the institutions, divisions, and unresolved tensions this chapter has examined in detail.
