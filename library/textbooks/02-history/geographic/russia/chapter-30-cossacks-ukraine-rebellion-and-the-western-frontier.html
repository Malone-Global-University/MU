Cossacks, Ukraine, Rebellion, and the Western Frontier

A History of Russia: From the Eurasian World to the Russian Federation

Chapter Opening

In January 1654, at the Cossack garrison town of Pereiaslav on the Left Bank of the Dnipro River, the hetman of the Zaporozhian Host, Bohdan Khmelnytsky, met with Muscovite envoys to settle the terms on which his war-weary Cossack polity would place itself under the protection of Tsar Alexei Mikhailovich. What exactly the two sides believed they had agreed to that winter has been argued over by lawyers, chroniclers, and historians for more than three and a half centuries.

By the winter of 1653–1654, Bohdan Khmelnytsky had been at war, on and off, for nearly six years. He had led the Zaporozhian Host and a broad coalition of Cossacks, Orthodox clergy, townspeople, and peasants of the Ukrainian palatinates of the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth in an uprising that began as a rebellion over Cossack status and grievances against magnate power and grew into something far larger: a war that shattered Commonwealth authority across much of the Dnipro basin, produced a new Cossack polity governed by its own officers and institutions, and drew in the Crimean Khanate, the Ottoman Empire, Sweden, and, eventually, Muscovy as interested or participating powers. By early 1654, Khmelnytsky's coalition was exhausted, his Crimean Tatar allies had proven unreliable, and the Commonwealth remained a dangerous adversary. He needed a stronger patron. Tsar Alexei Mikhailovich, ruling a Muscovite state that Chapters 26 and 27 have shown was still rebuilding its institutions and its finances after the Time of Troubles, saw an opportunity to extend Muscovite influence over Orthodox lands that had once belonged, in some sense, to the medieval world of Rus' examined in Part II of this textbook, and to weaken a longstanding rival.

What happened at Pereiaslav, and in the subsequent negotiations that produced the so-called March Articles of 1654, has never had a single settled meaning. Cossack officers who swore an oath of allegiance to the tsar retained, on paper, extensive rights: their own courts, their own elected hetman, their own army, their own diplomatic correspondence with some foreign powers, and the collection of their own revenues. Muscovite officials, for their part, generally regarded the arrangement as an act of the tsar extending his sovereignty and protection over new subjects, in a language of allegiance not fundamentally different from that used elsewhere in Muscovite political culture. These were not necessarily contradictory positions in 1654; they became contradictory as Muscovite power in the Hetmanate grew over the following decades, and as later generations of chroniclers, jurists, and national historians read back into the agreement meanings that served their own political purposes. This chapter's task is to explain what can be established about 1654 and the surrounding decades, and to show why the search for a single, timeless verdict on Pereiaslav — "reunification," "annexation," "personal union," "protectorate," "vassalage" — is itself a historical problem rather than a historical fact waiting to be uncovered.

This chapter examines Muscovy's western and southern frontier during a period when that frontier was not a settled line on a map but a zone of overlapping and competing authorities. The Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth, one of Europe's largest states by territory, second in extent only to Muscovy itself, governed the Ukrainian palatinates through a noble political system that gave enormous power to magnate families while nominally protecting the liberties of the Cossack estate. The Crimean Khanate, a Muslim state allied for much of this period with the Ottoman Empire, raided, negotiated, and allied with all its neighbors as circumstances demanded, a role this textbook has traced since Chapter 24's account of Crimean Tatar raiding and will continue to trace through Chapter 34's treatment of Catherine II's annexation of Crimea. Orthodox clergy, some sympathetic to Muscovite Orthodoxy and some wary of it, positioned themselves within these rivalries. And Cossack communities themselves — on the Dnipro, at the Zaporozhian Sich below the river's rapids, and far to the east on the Don and the Volga — were neither simple instruments of any state nor a single unified nation, but distinct, self-governing frontier societies whose relationship with neighboring powers combined military service, negotiated autonomy, and, when their interests were threatened, open rebellion.

The chapter therefore moves along two related tracks. The first follows the Khmelnytsky uprising of 1648, the Pereiaslav settlement of 1654, the resulting Muscovite-Commonwealth war of 1654–1667, the internal politics of the Cossack Hetmanate that Khmelnytsky's uprising created, and the territorial partition of the Dnipro basin that the war eventually produced. The second track moves southeast to the Don and Volga, where a very different Cossack rebellion, led by Stepan (Stenka) Razin in 1670–1671, drew together Cossacks, fugitive peasants, townspeople, and non-Russian Volga peoples in a rising against Muscovite authority itself. Read together, these two episodes show that the same frontier military culture that Muscovy relied upon for defense and expansion — as Chapter 28 has already shown for Siberia — could also become a source of profound instability, and that "Cossack" named not one political community but several, with different relationships to the states around them.

Finally, this chapter asks students to hold two things in mind simultaneously: the concrete, contested details of what seventeenth-century actors negotiated, fought over, and built, and the much later political uses to which those events have been put by imperial Russian, Soviet, Ukrainian, Polish, and other historians and political movements. Distinguishing these two layers — the seventeenth-century reality and its later reinterpretation — is one of the central historical thinking skills this chapter develops, and it prepares students for comparable distinctions in Part VIII's treatment of nationality politics and Part IX's treatment of the Russian Revolution's own regional and national dimensions.

By the chapter's end, students should be able to explain why Cossack communities combined self-government with state service, trace the causes and course of the 1648 uprising, evaluate competing interpretations of the Pereiaslav settlement, explain how the resulting war produced a territorial partition of the Dnipro basin, and compare the Khmelnytsky uprising with Stenka Razin's rebellion as two distinct expressions of frontier instability in seventeenth-century Muscovy's borderlands.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Describe the social and military organization of Cossack communities on the Dnipro, the Don, and the Volga, and explain the recurring tension between autonomy and service to neighboring states.
  2. Explain the political and religious structure of the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth as it governed the Ukrainian palatinates before 1648.
  3. Trace the causes, course, and consequences of the Khmelnytsky uprising of 1648, including its military successes, its diplomatic alliances, and the violence it produced against civilian populations.
  4. Evaluate competing interpretations of the Pereiaslav Agreement of 1654 without reducing the settlement to a single label.
  5. Analyze the Muscovite-Commonwealth war of 1654–1667 as an international conflict shaped by Cossack, Muscovite, Commonwealth, Swedish, and Crimean interests.
  6. Explain the internal political institutions of the Cossack Hetmanate, including the hetman, the General Military Council, and the officer elite (starshyna).
  7. Assess the causes and consequences of political instability in the Hetmanate after Khmelnytsky's death, known as the Ruin.
  8. Explain the territorial partition of the Dnipro basin produced by the Truce of Andrusovo in 1667 and the Eternal Peace of 1686.
  9. Compare the Khmelnytsky uprising with Stenka Razin's rebellion of 1670–1671 as distinct expressions of frontier rebellion with different causes, participants, and outcomes.
  10. Critique later national historiographical interpretations of Pereiaslav, the Hetmanate, and Cossack rebellion, distinguishing seventeenth-century arrangements from later political narratives.
  11. Identify the historical experience of Jewish, Polish, Orthodox, and other populations affected by the violence of 1648–1649 without minimizing or exaggerating its scale.
  12. Connect the frontier instability examined in this chapter to Muscovy's broader seventeenth-century state-building project examined in Chapters 26–29 and to its eighteenth-century consequences examined in Part VI.

Historical Context

Chapters 26 through 29 have traced Muscovy's reconstruction after the Time of Troubles: the restoration of central institutions under the first Romanovs, the legal consolidation of serfdom in the Sobornoe Ulozhenie of 1649, the expansion of Russian settlement across Siberia, and the religious upheaval produced by Patriarch Nikon's reforms and the resulting Old Believer schism. This chapter turns to a different kind of frontier — not the sparsely populated forests of Siberia examined in Chapter 28, but the densely settled, politically contested borderlands of the western steppe and the Dnipro basin, where Muscovy's ambitions collided with those of the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth, the Crimean Khanate, and the Ottoman Empire, and where Cossack communities pursued interests of their own that did not automatically align with any of the larger states around them.

The term "Cossack" (from a Turkic word associated with free or masterless warriors) had by the sixteenth century come to describe several distinct frontier populations: the Zaporozhian Cossacks, based below the Dnipro rapids in what is now central Ukraine, who combined river-borne raiding, fishing, and hunting with periodic military service to the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth; the "registered" Cossacks, a formally enrolled body of Cossacks paid and recognized by the Commonwealth government in exchange for military service and, crucially, exempted from many of the obligations imposed on ordinary peasants; and, far to the east, the Don Cossacks, a separate community that had developed along the Don River largely outside direct Commonwealth or Muscovite control. From the later sixteenth century onward the Don Host maintained a longstanding service and client relationship with Muscovy — receiving pay, provisions, and diplomatic recognition in exchange for military service and frontier defense — while preserving extensive internal self-government through its own assembly, the krug; this relationship became substantially more formal only in 1671, after the suppression of Razin's rebellion examined in Section 30.8, when the Host swore a direct oath of allegiance to the tsar. These communities shared a broadly similar social pattern — a warrior population governing itself through elected officers and communal assemblies, deeply attached to a self-image of freedom and equality among the Cossack estate — but they were not a single nation and did not act in political concert.

By the 1640s, the Ukrainian palatinates of the Commonwealth — Kyiv, Bratslav, and Chernihiv, together with neighboring territories — had become a zone of acute social and religious tension. Commonwealth magnates, often absentee landlords governing enormous estates through hired stewards, had extended serfdom and labor obligations deep into the Dnipro basin in the decades before 1648, a process examined further in Section 30.2. Religious tension between the Orthodox population and the Catholic and Uniate (Greek Catholic) churches, the latter created by the Union of Brest in 1596 to bring part of the Orthodox hierarchy into communion with Rome while retaining Eastern liturgical practice, added a further layer of grievance, particularly as Commonwealth policy in this period generally favored Catholic and Uniate institutions over Orthodox ones. Registered Cossacks resented the Commonwealth's periodic reduction of the register — the official list of paid, recognized Cossacks — which threw unregistered Cossacks back into the legal status of ordinary peasants or townspeople, a demotion that struck at both their economic position and their sense of status and honor.

It was within this combustible mixture of social, religious, and status grievances that Bohdan Khmelnytsky, an officer of the registered Cossack host with personal grievances against a local magnate official, launched the rebellion examined in Section 30.3. Students should understand from the outset that the uprising's transformation from a localized Cossack grievance into a movement encompassing much of the Ukrainian population, and eventually into a fundamental renegotiation of political authority across the Dnipro basin, requires explanation rather than assumption; this chapter traces that transformation section by section, while consistently insisting that the peoples of the Ukrainian lands, the Commonwealth, Muscovy, the Crimean Khanate, and the Cossack Hetmanate itself each pursued distinct and often conflicting interests throughout.

Section 30.1

Cossack Communities and Frontier Society

Before examining the uprising of 1648, students need a clear understanding of what a Cossack community actually was in the seventeenth century, since the term is easily and mistakenly folded into modern national categories that did not yet exist in the form we know them. A Cossack was, at base, a member of a self-governing frontier military community, typically organized around fortified encampments or settlements, that combined warfare, raiding, fishing, hunting, and, increasingly, agriculture with a strong communal ethic of personal freedom and elected leadership. Cossack communities recruited from a wide range of backgrounds — runaway peasants, townspeople fleeing debt or serfdom, minor nobles, and long-settled frontier families — and this open, fluid recruitment pattern is itself historically significant: it meant that Cossack identity was as much a chosen status and way of life as an inherited ethnicity, though over generations family lines within the Cossack estate did become hereditary in many respects.

By the mid-seventeenth century, several distinct Cossack populations existed across the wide arc of steppe frontier this textbook has followed since Chapter 28's treatment of Siberian Cossacks. The Zaporozhian Host, based at the Sich, a fortified encampment complex below the Dnipro rapids (the name Zaporozhian derives from the Cossacks' position "beyond the rapids"), formed the core military and political community around which the 1648 uprising crystallized. Zaporozhian Cossacks governed themselves through a General Military Council (a mass assembly of the Host) that elected the hetman, or supreme commander, along with subordinate officers, and enforced a code of internal discipline and shared plunder that bound the community together even as individual Cossacks moved in and out of formal military service. A second, related population — "registered" Cossacks — existed within the Commonwealth's formal military establishment: these were Cossacks placed on an official register, paid a modest salary, granted certain legal exemptions from the obligations imposed on Commonwealth peasants, and organized into a smaller, more disciplined force intended to serve Commonwealth military needs, particularly against Crimean Tatar raiding and, at times, against Ottoman forces. Far to the east, the Don Cossacks had developed along broadly similar lines but under different political conditions, evolving in nominal Muscovite service from the later sixteenth century while retaining, through their own assembly (the krug), substantial control over internal affairs, including, notably, the right to offer refuge to fugitives without automatic extradition to Muscovite authorities — a right captured in the Cossack saying "there is no extradition from the Don," which Muscovy only gradually eroded over the following century, as Section 30.8 and later chapters will show.

The central analytical theme of this section, and one that recurs throughout the chapter, is the tension between autonomy and service. Cossack communities prized their internal self-government, their exemption from serfdom and many forms of taxation, their right to bear arms, and their communal assemblies as core markers of Cossack identity and honor. At the same time, Cossack military power was valuable to neighboring states — the Commonwealth, Muscovy, and at times the Ottoman Empire — precisely because Cossacks could fight effectively on the steppe frontier, and states therefore had strong incentives to draw Cossack manpower into formal service, whether through the Commonwealth's register, Muscovite pay and provisions to the Don Host, or, in the following century, more thoroughgoing incorporation examined in Part VI. This bargain — autonomy traded for service, service traded for recognition and support — was inherently unstable, because states typically sought to expand their control over Cossack communities over time, while Cossacks resisted any erosion of the liberties that defined their way of life. When states reduced the register, delayed pay, imposed unwelcome officers, or otherwise appeared to encroach on Cossack self-government, the result was frequently rebellion, a pattern this chapter traces in both the Khmelnytsky uprising of 1648 and Razin's rebellion of 1670–1671.

It is worth noting explicitly, in light of this textbook's consistent caution against projecting modern national identity backward, that Cossack communities in this period did not think of themselves in terms of modern Ukrainian, Russian, or Polish nationality. Zaporozhian and registered Cossacks were overwhelmingly Orthodox in religion and spoke a variety of East Slavic dialects ancestral to modern Ukrainian, and they frequently described themselves and their cause using the term "Ruthenian" (Rus'kyi), a term connecting them, in their own understanding, to the medieval inheritance of Rus' examined in Part II — but "Ruthenian" identity in this period was primarily religious and cultural rather than a claim to a modern nation-state, and it coexisted with strong loyalty to the Cossack estate specifically, to particular regiments, and to particular leaders, rather than to a unified national program in the modern sense.

Section 30.2

The Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth and Ukraine

The Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth, formally established by the Union of Lublin in 1569, was by the mid-seventeenth century one of Europe's largest states by territory, second in extent only to Muscovy itself, and was governed through a political system markedly different from Muscovite autocracy examined in Chapters 22 and 26. Sovereignty rested formally with an elective king, chosen by the Commonwealth's szlachta, an unusually large noble class (in some estimates comprising a higher proportion of the population than in most other contemporary European states) that jealously guarded its political privileges through a national parliament, the Sejm, and provincial assemblies, the sejmiks. Royal power in the Commonwealth was consequently far more constrained by noble consent than tsarist power in Muscovy, and a small number of extraordinarily wealthy magnate families — controlling vast estates, private armies, and extensive political patronage networks — exercised enormous practical authority, particularly in the Commonwealth's eastern and southeastern territories, including the Ukrainian palatinates of Kyiv and Bratslav, which had been transferred from Lithuanian to direct Polish administration at Lublin in 1569. Chernihiv followed a different and later path: recovered from Muscovy after the 1618 Truce of Deulino, examined in Chapter 25, it was organized as a separate Chernihiv palatinate only in 1635, and its Cossack and peasant population experienced Commonwealth rule for a considerably shorter period than that of Kyiv and Bratslav before 1648.

Within this system, Commonwealth policy in the Ukrainian palatinates in the decades before 1648 combined active colonization, economic development, and increasing social and religious tension. Magnate families such as the Wiśniowiecki, Koniecpolski, and others extended large-scale grain agriculture into the fertile Dnipro basin, recruiting settlers with initial tax exemptions before gradually imposing labor obligations that, over a generation or two, could harden into forms of serfdom resembling, though not identical to, the institution examined in Chapter 27's account of the Sobornoe Ulozhenie. Jewish communities, invited by many magnates to manage estates, collect taxes, run mills, and operate taverns under leasehold arrangements known as arenda, became economically significant intermediaries between magnate landlords and the peasant and Cossack population — a role that provided many Jewish families with a livelihood but also positioned them, in the eyes of an aggrieved peasant and Cossack population, as visible agents of an exploitative economic system, a perception with catastrophic consequences examined in Section 30.3.

Religious policy compounded these social tensions. The Union of Brest in 1596 had created the Uniate, or Greek Catholic, Church, bringing a substantial portion of the Orthodox hierarchy of the Commonwealth's eastern territories into communion with Rome while permitting the continuation of Eastern liturgical rites, clerical marriage, and other Orthodox practices. Commonwealth authorities generally favored Uniate and Catholic institutions in matters of legal status, landholding, and access to civic office, while Orthodox institutions faced increasing legal and social disadvantage, despite continuing to command the loyalty of the great majority of the Ukrainian peasant and Cossack population and much of the urban population as well. Orthodox brotherhoods in cities such as Kyiv and Lviv worked to sustain Orthodox schools, printing, and church life under these disadvantaged conditions, and the Kyiv Mohyla Academy, founded in 1632, became an important center of Orthodox learning that would later supply the Hetmanate, and eventually Muscovy itself, with educated clergy and administrators, a connection examined further in Chapter 32's treatment of Petrine cultural change.

The register — the Commonwealth's official list of paid, legally recognized Cossacks — sat at the center of the political relationship between the Commonwealth and its Cossack subjects. Commonwealth authorities valued registered Cossacks as a disciplined military force useful against Crimean Tatar raiding and, at times, against Ottoman power, but they also feared an overly large or autonomous Cossack host as a potential threat to noble authority and social order, particularly given the register's practical effect of drawing peasants out of serfdom into a privileged military estate. The register was consequently expanded in times of military need and reduced in times of peace, a pattern that left thousands of Cossacks who had served in earlier campaigns facing sudden demotion back into peasant or townsman status — precisely the grievance that would place Bohdan Khmelnytsky, a registered officer with a personal grievance against a local magnate official, at the head of a much larger rebellion in 1648.

Section 30.3

Bohdan Khmelnytsky and the 1648 Uprising

The immediate origin of the 1648 uprising lay in a personal dispute: Bohdan Khmelnytsky, a registered Cossack officer of some standing near Chyhyryn, accused a local Commonwealth official, Daniel Czapliński, of seizing his estate, abusing his family, and, according to the traditional account, causing the death of his young son, while Commonwealth courts failed to provide redress. Personal grievance alone, however, cannot explain why Khmelnytsky's subsequent uprising grew, within months, into a movement encompassing the great majority of the Ukrainian Cossack, peasant, and, in many towns, burgher population. That wider mobilization drew directly on the accumulated grievances examined in Section 30.2: reduced Cossack registers that had displaced thousands of men from privileged status, expanding magnate control over land and labor, and religious discrimination against the Orthodox population under a Commonwealth political system that offered peasants and townspeople almost no institutional means of redress.

Khmelnytsky's military strategy from the outset depended on an alliance with the Crimean Khanate, whose light cavalry supplemented Cossack infantry and artillery tactics and whose Tatar raiding capacity threatened Commonwealth forces' lines of supply and retreat. This alliance produced a string of major Cossack victories in 1648: at Zhovti Vody and Korsun in the spring, where Commonwealth forces were destroyed or captured, and at Pyliavtsi in the autumn, where a large Commonwealth army collapsed in a rout that opened much of the Ukrainian palatinates, and briefly threatened territory well to the west, to Cossack control. These victories transformed what had begun as a Cossack mutiny into a full-scale political and social uprising: as news of Commonwealth military collapse spread, peasants across the Ukrainian palatinates rose against magnate estates, Orthodox townspeople expelled or attacked Catholic and Uniate institutions, and thousands of ordinary people who were not formally Cossacks joined Khmelnytsky's forces or acted independently against local lords and administrators.

This chapter cannot responsibly pass over what accompanied this uprising: a wave of extreme violence directed at Jewish communities across the Ukrainian palatinates, together with violence against Polish nobles, Catholic and Uniate clergy, and Commonwealth officials, particularly severe in 1648 and 1649. Jewish communities, positioned by the arenda system examined in Section 30.2 as visible economic intermediaries for magnate landlords, became targets of massacre, expulsion, and destruction of property on a scale that later Jewish historical memory would treat as one of the great catastrophes of early modern Jewish history in Eastern Europe, a memory examined further in this textbook's historiography appendix. Modern demographic estimates are substantially lower than many early accounts and remain disputed among specialists; one influential reconstruction estimates overall Jewish population losses at roughly twenty thousand out of a prewar Ukrainian Jewish population of approximately forty thousand, while explicitly cautioning that direct killings cannot be separated precisely from deaths caused by hunger, disease, and flight. Such figures should not be presented with false precision, and some estimates from earlier scholarship ran considerably higher than this demographic reconstruction supports; what remains well documented in contemporary Jewish, Polish, and Ukrainian sources, regardless of the precise death toll, is the qualitative evidence of massacre, destruction, and displacement across numerous specific communities. This violence should not be explained away as an inevitable product of timeless ethnic or religious hatred, nor should it be minimized as an unfortunate but marginal side effect of an otherwise straightforward war of liberation; it requires explanation in its own terms, connected to the specific economic and social position Jewish communities occupied within the Commonwealth's exploitative estate system, to wartime breakdown of order, and to religious animosities inflamed by the broader conflict, while remaining a documented atrocity whose victims deserve accurate historical acknowledgment rather than either exaggeration or silence.

Politically, the uprising's rapid military success forced both Khmelnytsky and the Commonwealth toward negotiation. The Treaty of Zboriv in 1649 granted a substantially expanded Cossack register (traditionally given as forty thousand, a dramatic increase over prewar levels) and effective Cossack self-government within three palatinates, without formally separating the Ukrainian lands from Commonwealth sovereignty. This compromise satisfied neither the Commonwealth's magnate political class, which regarded the settlement as an intolerable concession, nor the broader peasant population that had risen against magnate authority and had no interest in restored magnate landholding, nor Khmelnytsky himself, who continued to seek a stronger, more secure political position. Renewed warfare followed, culminating in a serious Cossack defeat at Berestechko in 1651 and the harsher Treaty of Bila Tserkva, which sharply reduced the register and Cossack self-government once again. It was this renewed weakness, combined with the unreliability of Crimean Tatar support (the Tatars had already withdrawn support at a crucial moment during the Berestechko campaign, prioritizing their own interests over Khmelnytsky's), that pushed Khmelnytsky, by 1653, to seek a more dependable outside patron — a search that led to Pereiaslav, examined in the following section.

Section 30.4

The Pereiaslav Agreement

By late 1653, Khmelnytsky's position had grown precarious enough that he turned formally to Muscovy, a power he and his advisers had approached with varying degrees of seriousness since early in the uprising, but which Tsar Alexei Mikhailovich's government had previously hesitated to embrace fully, wary of provoking a costly war with the Commonwealth while Muscovy's own post-Troubles institutions, examined in Chapters 26 and 27, remained under reconstruction. By 1653, however, the calculation in Moscow had shifted: a Zemsky Sobor (the consultative assembly examined in Chapter 26) endorsed accepting the Zaporozhian Host under the tsar's protection, and Muscovite envoys traveled to Pereiaslav to formalize the arrangement in January 1654.

What occurred at Pereiaslav should be described carefully rather than summarized in a single word. A council of Cossack officers and, according to the traditional account, a wider assembly of Cossacks and townspeople, gathered to hear the tsar's terms and swore an oath of allegiance to Alexei Mikhailovich. Subsequent negotiations in Moscow in March 1654 produced a set of articles — commonly called the March Articles — specifying the terms of the relationship in more detail: the Hetmanate would retain its own elected hetman, its own courts and legal customs, its own army with a specified register, the right to conduct diplomatic correspondence with most foreign powers (though not, notably, with the Commonwealth or the Ottoman Empire without the tsar's knowledge), and the collection of its own revenues, subject to reporting requirements to Moscow. In exchange, the Hetmanate swore allegiance to the tsar, accepted a Muscovite garrison in Kyiv, and undertook to support Muscovite military efforts against the Commonwealth.

The documentary record surrounding these events is itself a central historical problem. No single, uncontested master text titled a "Treaty of Pereiaslav" survives in the way a modern reader might expect of a formal international treaty; instead, historians work from the oath texts, the March Articles as negotiated and as they survive in variant Muscovite and Cossack versions, subsequent confirmatory charters issued by later tsars to later hetmans (each of which restated, and sometimes subtly altered, the terms), and the surrounding diplomatic correspondence. This documentary complexity has allowed, and continues to allow, historians operating from different legal and historiographical traditions to characterize the 1654 settlement in strikingly different terms, a problem this chapter examines directly in the Historical Debate feature below.

Historical Vocabulary
Pereiaslav

Pereiaslav refers both to the Left-Bank Dnipro town where the January 1654 council took place and, by extension, to the broader settlement — the oath sworn there together with the subsequently negotiated March Articles — establishing a relationship between the Zaporozhian Host and the Muscovite tsar. Historians use "the Pereiaslav Agreement" or "the Pereiaslav settlement" as a convenient shorthand, but students should understand that this shorthand compresses a process involving a council, an oath, and subsequent written articles rather than naming a single treaty document comparable to a modern bilateral treaty.

The term matters historically because later political and national traditions have assigned Pereiaslav sharply different meanings — ranging from voluntary reunification of a divided Rus' people, to a defensive military alliance, to a protectorate arrangement, to the first step in an eventual Muscovite annexation — and because the surviving documentary evidence, examined in this chapter's Primary Source Window, does not by itself resolve which of these later characterizations best fits the actual expectations of the participants in January and March 1654.

What can be said with reasonable confidence is this: Khmelnytsky and the Cossack officer elite sought Muscovite military support and a stronger, more durable patron than the unreliable Crimean alliance had proven to be, while seeking to preserve the internal self-government the Hetmanate had won through years of warfare; Muscovite officials, for their part, understood the arrangement within a political vocabulary of the tsar extending sovereignty and protection over new subjects who had sworn allegiance, a vocabulary not fundamentally different from that used to describe Muscovite authority over other subject populations, including, in more coercive form, the Siberian peoples examined in Chapter 28. These were not necessarily incompatible understandings in January 1654, when both sides had immediate and overlapping interests in a durable alliance against the Commonwealth. They became incompatible over subsequent decades, as Muscovite administrators steadily sought to expand direct control over Hetmanate affairs — appointing Muscovite governors (voevody) alongside Cossack officials, restricting the Hetmanate's independent foreign correspondence, and, eventually, in the eighteenth century covered in Part VI, dismantling most of the Hetmanate's distinct institutions altogether — while generations of Cossack officers and, later, national historians increasingly emphasized the autonomy the 1654 settlement had originally promised.

Section 30.5

War Between Muscovy and the Commonwealth

The Pereiaslav settlement immediately committed Muscovy to war with the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth, a conflict that lasted, with interruptions, from 1654 to 1667 and reshaped the political balance of eastern Europe. Muscovite forces, benefiting from the military reforms of "new formation" regiments trained along Western European lines that Chapter 26 has already introduced, achieved substantial early successes, capturing Smolensk in 1654 (recovering territory lost during the Time of Troubles, examined in Chapter 25) and advancing deep into Lithuanian and Belarusian territory, including the capture of Vilnius in 1655.

The Commonwealth's crisis deepened dramatically in 1655 when Sweden, exploiting Commonwealth weakness, launched a massive invasion from the north and west in what Polish historical tradition calls the "Deluge" (Potop), briefly threatening the Commonwealth's very survival as an independent state. This Swedish intervention transformed the conflict's international character: Muscovy, wary of Swedish gains that might threaten its own interests in the Baltic region (a concern that would recur far more prominently in Chapter 31's account of Peter the Great's Great Northern War), concluded a temporary truce with the Commonwealth in 1656 and turned briefly to war against Sweden instead, a conflict that produced few lasting Muscovite gains; an armistice was reached at Valiesar in 1658, and the final Peace of Cardis, restoring the prewar territorial settlement, followed in 1661. Khmelnytsky, watching Muscovy negotiate independently with the Commonwealth without full Cossack participation, grew increasingly wary of Muscovite intentions in the final months of his life — an early sign of the tensions that would deepen dramatically after his death.

Fighting between Muscovy and the Commonwealth resumed after the temporary truce and continued, with shifting fortunes on both sides, through the 1660s, compounded by the internal Hetmanate instability examined in Section 30.6, as rival Cossack factions allied at different moments with Muscovy, the Commonwealth, or the Crimean Khanate, each pursuing what they judged to be the Hetmanate's or their own faction's best interest amid a rapidly shifting military and political landscape. By the mid-1660s, both Muscovy and the Commonwealth faced exhaustion: Muscovy's finances, strained by prolonged warfare on multiple fronts, had already produced serious currency debasement and urban unrest in the early 1660s, while the Commonwealth, having survived the Swedish Deluge, remained politically fragmented and militarily depleted. These mutual exhaustions produced the negotiated territorial settlement examined in Section 30.7.

This prolonged conflict should be understood as more than a bilateral contest between two great powers. Cossack aims — securing and expanding Hetmanate autonomy, protecting Orthodox institutions, preserving the register and Cossack social privileges — shaped the war's course as much as Muscovite or Commonwealth strategic calculations, and Cossack military and political actors, including Khmelnytsky and his eventual successors, repeatedly acted with a degree of independent agency that neither Moscow nor Warsaw fully controlled. The war also drew in the Crimean Khanate as a fluctuating participant, allying at different points with the Commonwealth, with rival Cossack factions, and occasionally threatening Muscovite forces directly, a reminder that the steppe-frontier dynamics examined in Chapter 4's treatment of ancient nomadic-sedentary relations, and in Chapter 24's account of Crimean Tatar raiding, remained a live and consequential factor in seventeenth-century international politics.

Section 30.6

The Cossack Hetmanate

The Cossack Hetmanate that Khmelnytsky's uprising created was a genuine polity with its own institutions, not merely a Muscovite or Commonwealth administrative subdivision, and it deserves examination on its own institutional terms. At its head stood the hetman, elected in principle by the General Military Council — a mass assembly of the Cossack Host that in practice was often dominated by the senior officer corps rather than functioning as a fully open popular vote — and vested with supreme military command, broad executive authority, and significant control over land grants and appointments within Hetmanate territory. Beneath the hetman, the Hetmanate's territory was organized into regiments (polky), each combining military and civil administrative functions under a colonel (polkovnyk), a structure that gave the Hetmanate a distinctive blend of military and territorial government unlike either Commonwealth noble self-government or Muscovite centralized administration.

The Cossack officer elite, known collectively as the starshyna, formed the Hetmanate's governing class. Drawn originally from Cossack military ranks — colonels, captains, and other officers who had risen through service in the uprising — the starshyna increasingly consolidated its position as a landholding elite over the following decades, acquiring estates (in some cases former magnate lands seized during the uprising) and gradually assuming many of the social and economic characteristics of a noble class, even as it continued to derive its formal authority from Cossack military rank and communal election rather than hereditary noble title in the Commonwealth or Muscovite sense. This evolution — from an egalitarian-minded military brotherhood toward a stratified landholding elite — is itself a significant historical development, illustrating how frontier military communities, once they gained territorial control and stable resources, tended to develop internal hierarchies resembling the very noble societies their founding uprisings had often rebelled against.

Historical Vocabulary
Hetman and Starshyna

Hetman designated the elected supreme military and political leader of the Cossack Hetmanate, a title also used more loosely in the Commonwealth for senior military commanders generally. Within the Hetmanate specifically, the hetman commanded the Cossack army, oversaw diplomatic correspondence permitted under the Hetmanate's evolving relationship with Muscovy, controlled significant land-grant authority, and presided over, though did not always fully dominate, the General Military Council.

Starshyna (literally, "the elders" or "senior ones") designated the Cossack officer elite — colonels, captains, and other ranking officers — who governed the Hetmanate's regiments and increasingly functioned as a landholding governing class over the decades following 1648. The term matters historically because it names the social group whose internal rivalries, shifting foreign alignments, and consolidation of landed power shaped the Hetmanate's fate through the Ruin examined below and its eventual transformation and dismantlement in the following century, examined further in Part VI.

Khmelnytsky's death in 1657 opened a prolonged period of internal Hetmanate instability that historians and the Cossack chronicle tradition itself came to call the Ruin (Ruina). His immediate successors — including his son Yurii Khmelnytsky, and subsequent hetmans such as Ivan Vyhovsky, Pavlo Teteria, and others — struggled to maintain unified authority amid rival factional ambitions among the starshyna, disagreement over whether to maintain, deepen, or abandon the Muscovite alliance, and the continuing military and diplomatic pressure of the Commonwealth, the Crimean Khanate, and, increasingly, the Ottoman Empire, each of which some Cossack factions at different moments sought as an alternative patron. Ivan Vyhovsky, for example, negotiated the Treaty of Hadiach with the Commonwealth in 1658, which would have restructured the Commonwealth into a three-part union including a Ruthenian component with substantial autonomy — an arrangement never fully implemented and rejected by much of the Cossack rank and file, who distrusted a return to Commonwealth sovereignty regardless of its terms. The Hetmanate split repeatedly during these decades between Left-Bank and Right-Bank factions recognizing different hetmans and different foreign patrons, a division that anticipated, and helped produce, the formal territorial partition examined in Section 30.7.

Students should resist treating this instability as evidence that Cossack self-government was inherently doomed to failure, or as a morally simple story of Cossack disunity inviting outside domination. The Hetmanate's fragmentation reflected genuine and understandable disagreements among Cossack leaders about which alignment — Muscovite, Commonwealth, Ottoman, or some combination — best protected Cossack autonomy, Orthodox religious life, and social order against the magnate landholding system the uprising had originally targeted, disagreements made more difficult to resolve by the simple fact that Muscovy, the Commonwealth, the Crimean Khanate, and the Ottoman Empire were all simultaneously competing for influence over Hetmanate territory and were each willing to support rival Cossack factions when doing so served their own interests.

Section 30.7

The Partition of Ukrainian Lands

The Truce of Andrusovo, concluded between Muscovy and the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth in 1667 after more than a decade of exhausting warfare, formally divided the Dnipro basin between the two powers. Muscovy retained the Left Bank of the Dnipro (the territory east of the river), together with the city of Smolensk and other territories recovered earlier in the war, while the Commonwealth retained the Right Bank (west of the river), with the significant exception of Kyiv, which, though situated on the Right Bank, was placed under temporary Muscovite control pending a final resolution — an arrangement that in practice became permanent, as Muscovy declined to return the city when the truce's temporary provisions technically expired.

This partition should be understood as the product of prolonged interstate warfare and Cossack factional division, not as the drawing of a border across an already unified Russian-Ukrainian political space, since no such unified space existed in 1667; it instead formalized, in treaty form, a division that had already emerged in practice through the Ruin's rival Left-Bank and Right-Bank hetmanates and their differing foreign alignments. The consequences for different populations varied considerably. The Left-Bank Hetmanate, under Muscovite suzerainty, retained substantial internal institutions — its own hetman, courts, and administration — for several more decades, though, as Section 30.6 has already indicated, Muscovite officials steadily worked to narrow that autonomy over time, a process that would accelerate sharply in the following century. The Right Bank was assigned to the Commonwealth on paper, but effective control there remained highly contested for years afterward rather than settling neatly along the treaty line. The Right-Bank hetman Petro Doroshenko refused to accept subordination to either Commonwealth or Muscovite authority, continued to govern a separate Right-Bank Hetmanate, and by 1669 had placed his regime under the protection of the Ottoman Empire, drawing Ottoman and Crimean forces directly into the Dnipro basin's politics and producing years of further fighting among Cossack, Commonwealth, Crimean, Ottoman, and Muscovite forces before Commonwealth authority was gradually reasserted and Cossack institutions on the Right Bank were suppressed. The Andrusovo settlement itself further illustrates how untidy the supposed partition actually was: rather than assigning the Zaporozhian Sich cleanly to either power, the truce placed it under the nominal joint protection of both Muscovy and the Commonwealth, a provision that satisfied neither the Zaporozhian Cossacks themselves nor either signatory state in practice.

The "Eternal Peace" of 1686 between Muscovy and the Commonwealth confirmed and extended the essential terms of Andrusovo, formalizing Muscovite possession of Kyiv (in exchange for a Muscovite payment to the Commonwealth) and Left-Bank Ukraine, while also committing Muscovy to a broader anti-Ottoman alliance that drew Muscovy more fully into the international diplomatic and military system of Christian Europe's ongoing conflict with the Ottoman Empire — a commitment whose military consequences, including renewed campaigns against Crimea, are examined further in Chapter 31's account of the background to Peter the Great's reign. For the purposes of this chapter, the Eternal Peace should be understood chiefly as the durable confirmation of the territorial and political division the Andrusovo truce had already established, rather than as a fresh settlement requiring extended separate treatment.

Section 30.8

Stenka Razin's Rebellion

While the Hetmanate's internal politics unfolded on the Dnipro, a very different kind of Cossack rebellion erupted far to the southeast, on the Don and Volga rivers, in 1670–1671, led by the Don Cossack Stepan Timofeyevich Razin, known to history as Stenka Razin. This rebellion, examined here to ensure that students do not equate Cossack history solely with the Dnipro and Ukraine, drew on a distinct set of frontier grievances and a different regional population, though it shared with the Khmelnytsky uprising the underlying pattern of frontier military communities turning against a state whose expanding authority threatened their autonomy and attracted large numbers of non-Cossack participants suffering under Muscovite social and fiscal policy.

The Don Cossack community, examined in Section 30.1, had by the 1660s absorbed a growing population of fugitives fleeing the expanding serfdom that Chapter 27's account of the Sobornoe Ulozhenie had made increasingly rigorous, since Muscovite law by mid-century bound peasants ever more permanently to their landlords and pursued fugitives with growing determination. The Don's traditional practice of offering refuge without extradition placed it in increasing tension with a Muscovite government eager to recover fugitive peasants and to extend fiscal and administrative control over Don Cossack territory and its lucrative access to Caspian and Volga trade routes. Razin himself first rose to prominence leading a major Cossack raiding expedition into Persian Caspian territory beginning in 1667, with further raids in 1668 and a notable naval victory over a Persian fleet in 1669; these campaigns brought him plunder, prestige, and a substantial personal following among Don Cossacks eager for wealth and status outside the constraints of formal Muscovite service.

By 1670, Razin turned this following against Muscovite authority itself, launching a rebellion that spread rapidly along the Volga, capturing towns including Tsaritsyn and Astrakhan, and drawing in an unusually broad coalition: Don and Volga Cossacks, fugitive and rebellious peasants fleeing serfdom, dissatisfied townspeople, and, notably, non-Russian Volga peoples — including Mordvins, Chuvash, and Tatars, among others — who had their own grievances against Muscovite taxation, land encroachment, and administrative authority in the Volga region. Razin's movement drew on a diffuse appeal combining opposition to boyar and landlord oppression, hostility to specific unpopular officials, and, in some accounts, calls for the restoration of a "true" tsar freed from corrupt boyar influence — a pattern of rhetoric that appeared in other Russian popular uprisings both before and after Razin and that historians should read as a means of legitimating rebellion within a political culture that still generally accepted the tsar's authority in principle while attacking specific officials and social arrangements as illegitimate departures from proper rule.

Muscovite forces, after initial setbacks, mobilized substantial regular military resources against the rebellion, defeating Razin's main forces near Simbirsk in October 1670. Razin himself fled to the Don, where, in 1671, rival, more conservative Don Cossack leaders — wary of the destruction Muscovite military reprisal might bring upon the whole Don community, and themselves invested in maintaining a working relationship with Moscow — captured Razin and delivered him to Muscovite authorities, who executed him in Moscow in June 1671. The rebellion's suppression was followed by harsh reprisals across the affected region and by a decisive change in the Don Host's formal status: later in 1671, the Great Don Host swore a direct oath of allegiance to the tsar for the first time, converting a relationship that had long rested on service, pay, and informal recognition, as this chapter's Historical Context has already indicated, into a more explicit subordination that Muscovite, and subsequently Russian imperial, authorities would continue to tighten in subsequent decades, a process that would continue, with further episodes of Cossack rebellion, into the following century.

The comparison between Khmelnytsky's uprising and Razin's rebellion rewards careful attention. Both drew on frontier military communities whose autonomy felt threatened by an expanding state; both attracted broad coalitions of participants beyond the Cossack estate itself, including peasants and townspeople with their own grievances; and both ultimately required negotiation with, or subordination to, a larger patron state. But the two movements differed substantially in aim and outcome: Khmelnytsky's uprising produced a durable, if contested and ultimately partitioned, Cossack polity with its own lasting institutions, while Razin's rebellion, lacking any comparable program of territorial state-building and facing a more unified and militarily prepared Muscovite response, was suppressed relatively quickly and left no comparable lasting Cossack polity, instead accelerating the long-term erosion of Don Cossack autonomy that would culminate in fuller administrative incorporation the following century.

Section 30.9

Frontier Expansion and Competing Historical Interpretations

This section confronts directly the later political uses to which the events of this chapter have been put, a task this textbook regards as essential rather than optional, since the historical record of 1648–1671 has been interpreted, and reinterpreted, by successive imperial Russian, Soviet, Ukrainian, Polish, and other historiographical traditions in ways that reflect the political needs of the interpreters at least as much as the documentary evidence from the seventeenth century itself.

Imperial Russian historiography from the eighteenth century onward generally characterized the events examined in this chapter as the "reunification" of a Russian people artificially divided since the Mongol era and the rise of Lithuania and Poland examined in Chapter 18, treating Pereiaslav as the restoration of a natural political unity rooted in the shared inheritance of Kyivan Rus' examined in Part II. Soviet historiography, particularly from the mid-twentieth century onward, generally continued this framework while adding a class-based emphasis on the uprising as a progressive struggle of Ukrainian and Russian working people against Polish feudal oppression, and officially commemorated the 1954 tercentenary of Pereiaslav as a celebration of enduring Russian-Ukrainian friendship. Ukrainian national historiography, by contrast, particularly from the nineteenth century onward and especially in independent Ukraine after 1991, has more often emphasized the Hetmanate as an autonomous, even proto-independent, Ukrainian state whose subsequent absorption into the Russian Empire, examined further in Part VI, represented a loss of self-government imposed by an increasingly centralizing imperial power rather than a natural reunification. Polish historiography has generally treated the uprising within the framework of the broader crisis and eventual decline of the Commonwealth, emphasizing the loss of Ukrainian territory as part of a wider seventeenth-century pattern of Commonwealth weakness examined further in the partitions discussed in Chapter 34.

None of these traditions should simply be adopted wholesale by this textbook, and none should be dismissed as pure propaganda without engaging its evidentiary basis. Each reflects genuine historical evidence selectively emphasized: it is true that Khmelnytsky and Muscovite officials both used a shared vocabulary of Orthodox solidarity and, at times, of a common Rus' inheritance; it is also true that the Hetmanate possessed real, distinct institutions that Muscovy progressively narrowed over subsequent decades; it is also true that Commonwealth noble governance had produced genuine grievances whose scale and character this chapter has documented; and it is also true that the eventual outcome — increasing Muscovite, and later Russian imperial, control over Left-Bank Ukraine — was not a foregone conclusion in 1654 but the product of decades of subsequent political struggle, negotiation, and, at times, coercion. Students should therefore learn to ask, of any characterization of Pereiaslav or the Hetmanate's fate, which specific piece of evidence supports it, and to recognize when a label — reunification, annexation, protectorate, alliance, vassalage, colonization — is doing more political work than the surviving seventeenth-century evidence can responsibly support.

This caution extends to the chapter's broader theme of frontier expansion. Muscovy's acquisition of Left-Bank Ukraine and Kyiv through the events examined in this chapter should not be described as either simple, benevolent reunification or simple, straightforward foreign conquest; it was the outcome of a Cossack-led uprising against Commonwealth rule, a negotiated (and variously interpreted) alliance between the resulting Cossack polity and Muscovy, a prolonged interstate war shaped by multiple competing powers, and a subsequent, gradual process of Muscovite administrative encroachment on Hetmanate autonomy that would only be completed in the following century. Explaining this process in its full complexity serves students far better than adopting any single later national narrative as settled historical fact.

Primary Source Window

The Pereiaslav Documentary Complex, 1654

Creators: Bohdan Khmelnytsky and the Zaporozhian officer corps; the Muscovite embassy led by boyar Vasilii Buturlin at Pereiaslav; Cossack envoys and Muscovite chancery officials negotiating in Moscow · Dates: January 1654 (Pereiaslav council and oath) and March 1654 (negotiated articles in Moscow) · Type: Several distinct, only partially surviving documents — an oath record, a set of Cossack petitions, and Muscovite written responses to those petitions — rather than a single treaty

Context: What is loosely called "the Treaty of Pereiaslav" is not one document. It is a documentary complex assembled from a January 1654 oath taken before Buturlin's embassy at Pereiaslav, a set of petitions the Cossack officers subsequently carried to Moscow requesting specific guarantees, and Muscovite written answers to those petitions issued in March 1654 — the "March Articles." Some of the original instruments have not survived at all; historians instead work from drafts, later copies, translations, and the confirmatory charters that later tsars issued to successive hetmans, which did not always restate identical terms. This incompleteness is not an obstacle to be regretted but part of the evidence itself, since it shows that no single, fixed text ever definitively settled the relationship.

Brief paraphrase of the substance (not a verbatim translated quotation):

At Pereiaslav, Khmelnytsky and the assembled Cossacks swore an oath of allegiance to Tsar Alexei Mikhailovich. According to the Cossack side's own later account, they expected Buturlin, as the tsar's representative, to swear a reciprocal oath guaranteeing that the tsar would uphold their liberties — a normal expectation within the Commonwealth's own political culture, where a king's coronation oath bound the monarch to his subjects. Buturlin refused, explaining that an autocratic tsar did not swear oaths to his subjects; the Cossacks ultimately took their oath without receiving the reciprocal guarantee they had sought. The subsequent March petitions and Muscovite answers addressed, in writing, the Hetmanate's register, courts, revenues, and limited rights of foreign correspondence, though even these written answers survive in variant and incomplete form.

Analysis: The Buturlin episode is one of the most revealing moments in the surviving record, because it exposes a genuine clash between two political cultures rather than a simple misunderstanding: Cossack officers, shaped by Commonwealth constitutional practice, expected a reciprocal, quasi-contractual oath; Muscovite envoys, operating within a political culture in which the tsar's authority was not conditioned on obligations owed to subjects, considered such a reciprocal oath inappropriate to offer. Historian Serhii Plokhy has identified this exchange as evidence that the two sides approached the settlement with different assumptions about what kind of relationship was being created — a divergence that helps explain why the settlement proved so durably open to conflicting interpretation. The documentary incompleteness surrounding the March Articles compounds this problem: because no single authoritative text fixed the agreement's terms once and for all, later tsars' confirmatory charters, later Cossack petitions, and later historians all had room to read the relationship differently.

Questions for students:

  1. Why does the Pereiaslav documentary complex consist of several distinct instruments rather than one treaty, and why does that matter for historical interpretation?
  2. What does Buturlin's refusal to swear a reciprocal oath suggest about differing Cossack and Muscovite assumptions concerning the nature of the relationship being created?
  3. Why does the incomplete survival of the original March Articles strengthen, rather than merely complicate, the case for treating Pereiaslav's meaning as historically contested?
  4. What kind of additional evidence would help historians determine whether later Muscovite expansion of control represented a violation of the original settlement or a development within its ambiguous terms?
Historical Debate

What Did Pereiaslav Create?

The question: How should historians characterize the political relationship established between the Zaporozhian Host and the Muscovite tsar at Pereiaslav in 1654: a voluntary reunification, a military alliance, a protectorate, an act of vassalage, or something not fully captured by any single modern term?

The "reunification" interpretation: Rooted in imperial Russian and Soviet historiography, this view emphasizes the shared Orthodox faith, the common inheritance of Kyivan Rus', and the language of allegiance in the sources themselves to argue that Pereiaslav restored an underlying political and cultural unity that Mongol conquest and Lithuanian-Polish rule had interrupted, examined in Chapters 17 and 18.
The "military alliance / protectorate" interpretation: This view, common in much Ukrainian and Western scholarship, emphasizes the extensive self-government the March Articles reserved to the Hetmanate — its own hetman, courts, army, and diplomatic latitude — and argues that Khmelnytsky sought a protecting patron against the Commonwealth and Crimea rather than the surrender of Cossack sovereignty, with subsequent Muscovite centralization representing a later and separate historical process rather than the fulfillment of the original 1654 bargain.
The "progressive subordination" interpretation: A third view treats the precise character of the 1654 settlement as less important than the documented long-term pattern: regardless of the settlement's original terms, Muscovite and later Russian imperial authorities steadily narrowed Hetmanate autonomy over the following century, a process this textbook traces further in Part VI, suggesting that whatever balance of power existed in 1654 shifted decisively toward Muscovite control well before the Hetmanate's formal abolition.

Evidence: Historians weigh the surviving oath and article texts and their variant versions, subsequent confirmatory charters and how their terms changed over time, Muscovite administrative correspondence and actions in the Hetmanate, and the broader pattern of Muscovite policy toward other subject and allied populations in this period.

Points of agreement: Most historians agree that the March Articles provided for substantial Hetmanate self-government in 1654, and that Muscovite control over Hetmanate affairs expanded considerably over the following decades and century.

Points of disagreement: Historians disagree over whether this later expansion of control represented a violation of an original bargain of alliance, the natural evolution of an inherently unequal relationship between a great power and a smaller polity under its protection, or simply an open question the 1654 settlement never definitively resolved.

Student task: Using evidence from the Primary Source Window and Section 30.4, construct a short argument explaining which interpretation the surviving 1654 documentary evidence best supports, while acknowledging what the evidence cannot settle.

Multiple Perspectives

Six Views of the Frontier

A registered Cossack officer: For an officer such as Khmelnytsky himself, status rested on military service, personal honor, and the register's formal recognition; reduction of the register or personal mistreatment by Commonwealth officials threatened both livelihood and dignity, and alliance with Muscovy in 1654 represented a calculated wager for a more secure patron after the unreliable Crimean alliance.
A Commonwealth magnate official: For a magnate administrator or noble official, the Cossack register and expanding peasant labor obligations represented normal, legitimate instruments of estate management and military organization; the 1648 uprising appeared as an intolerable and dangerous rebellion against established noble authority and social order.
An Orthodox peasant of the Dnipro basin: For a peasant family subject to expanding magnate labor obligations and religious discrimination favoring Catholic and Uniate institutions, the uprising offered the possibility of relief from both economic exploitation and religious disadvantage, and many joined Khmelnytsky's forces or rose independently against local lords once Commonwealth military authority collapsed in 1648.
A Jewish leaseholder or estate manager: For a Jewish family managing a magnate estate, mill, or tavern under an arenda leasehold, the uprising's outbreak brought sudden, often lethal danger, as economic resentment against the exploitative estate system in which Jewish leaseholders played a visible intermediary role turned into catastrophic communal violence, examined directly in Section 30.3.
A Muscovite chancery official: For an official in Moscow, the Zaporozhian Host's 1654 oath of allegiance fit within a familiar political vocabulary of subjects submitting to the tsar's protection and sovereignty, and subsequent efforts to place Muscovite governors and garrisons in Hetmanate territory appeared as the ordinary extension of legitimate authority over newly acquired subjects rather than a violation of any specific bargain.
A Don Cossack fugitive from serfdom: For a peasant who had fled a Muscovite landlord's estate to join the Don Cossack community, the Don's traditional practice of refusing extradition represented an essential and jealously guarded liberty; Muscovite efforts to recover fugitives and extend fiscal control over the Don threatened that liberty directly, a grievance that fed into Razin's rebellion examined in Section 30.8.

These perspectives illustrate why the frontier examined in this chapter resists any single narrative: liberation for one population could mean catastrophe for another, and alliance for one set of actors could appear as subordination to another.

Cause and Effect

The 1648 Uprising

Long-Term Conditions

Expansion of magnate estates and serfdom into the Ukrainian palatinates; religious discrimination against the Orthodox population favoring Catholic and Uniate institutions since the Union of Brest (1596).

Intermediate Developments

Periodic reduction of the Cossack register, displacing thousands from privileged status; the Jewish arenda leasehold system positioning Jewish communities as visible economic intermediaries for magnate landlords.

Short-Term Triggers

Khmelnytsky's personal dispute with Commonwealth official Daniel Czapliński over his estate and family; failure of Commonwealth courts to provide redress; Khmelnytsky's alliance with the Crimean Khanate.

Effects

Major Cossack military victories in 1648; catastrophic violence against Jewish, Polish, and clerical populations; creation of the Cossack Hetmanate; eventual Pereiaslav settlement and prolonged Muscovite-Commonwealth war.

Historical complexity: No single cause—not personal grievance alone, not social oppression alone, not religious discrimination alone—fully explains the uprising's scale; each factor reinforced the others, and Cossack military success transformed a narrower grievance into a mass mobilization.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Cossack Autonomy, 1648–1671

What Stayed the Same

  • The underlying Cossack tension between valued self-government and dependence on a larger patron state persisted throughout the period, on both the Dnipro and the Don.
  • Orthodox religious identity remained a consistent unifying element across the Hetmanate's political fluctuations.
  • The starshyna's exercise of military-territorial authority through the regimental system persisted despite repeated changes of hetman.

What Changed

  • The Hetmanate moved from Commonwealth sovereignty, to a negotiated relationship with Muscovy, to formal territorial partition between two great powers by 1667.
  • The starshyna evolved from a military officer corps toward a landholding governing elite over the decades following 1648.
  • Muscovite control over Hetmanate affairs expanded steadily, narrowing the scope of the self-government the 1654 settlement had initially provided.

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both continuity and change equips students to see Cossack autonomy as a historical process under continuous negotiation and pressure, rather than either a fixed liberty destroyed at a single moment or an illusion that never existed.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Registered Cossack Household

For most Cossacks, life did not center on councils and treaties but on the practical rhythms of a frontier military household: farming a modest landholding, maintaining horses and weapons for campaign service, fishing and hunting along the Dnipro or Don, and participating in the seasonal cycle of military mobilization and return. A registered Cossack family occupied an intermediate social position — exempt from many peasant obligations, entitled to bear arms and participate in Cossack assemblies, but far from wealthy, and vulnerable to the loss of registered status whenever the Commonwealth or, later, the Hetmanate reduced the register in response to fiscal or political pressure.

Women in Cossack communities managed households, agricultural production, and family economic life during the frequent and often prolonged absence of men on campaign, and Cossack settlements, unlike the wholly male military encampment of the Zaporozhian Sich itself, generally included family households resembling those of neighboring peasant and townsman communities, adapted to the particular rhythms and risks of frontier military life. The uprising and subsequent decades of warfare disrupted these households severely: military mobilization, population displacement, and the violence documented in Section 30.3 touched ordinary Cossack, peasant, Jewish, and townsman families throughout the Dnipro basin, a reminder that the political and military narrative traced in this chapter's numbered sections rested upon, and profoundly disrupted, the everyday lives of the wider population.

Regional Context

The Don and Volga Frontier

The Don and Volga frontier examined in Section 30.8 developed under conditions distinct from the Dnipro basin. Far from major European power centers and closer to the steppe world of the Crimean Khanate and the Volga's Tatar, Mordvin, and Chuvash populations, the Don Cossack Host had emerged from the sixteenth century onward as a Muscovite ally rather than, as the Zaporozhian Host had been, a subject population of a rival power. This difference in origin shaped a different trajectory: rather than rebelling to escape Commonwealth rule and negotiating a new external patron, as Khmelnytsky's Cossacks did, Don Cossacks under Razin rebelled against the very state, Muscovy, to which they had long been nominally attached, driven by resentment at its growing fiscal and administrative encroachment upon the Don's traditional liberties.

This regional context matters for understanding why the two rebellions examined in this chapter produced such different outcomes. The Zaporozhian uprising occurred within a Commonwealth already weakened by internal noble factionalism and vulnerable to Cossack, Swedish, and Muscovite pressure simultaneously, conditions that allowed a new Cossack polity to establish itself, however precariously. Razin's rebellion, by contrast, confronted a Muscovite state that, whatever its internal fiscal and social strains examined in Chapter 27, possessed sufficient military organization and political cohesion to suppress the rising within roughly a year, a difference in state capacity that goes far in explaining why one rebellion produced a lasting, if contested, polity and the other did not.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined Muscovy's western and southern frontier during a period of intense political and military upheaval, from the outbreak of Bohdan Khmelnytsky's Cossack uprising against the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth in 1648 through the suppression of Stenka Razin's rebellion on the Don and Volga in 1671. Throughout, the chapter has insisted that this frontier was not simply territory over which Moscow steadily extended control, but a politically plural borderland containing Cossack communities, the Commonwealth, the Crimean Khanate, the Ottoman world, Orthodox institutions, and diverse peasant, urban, and religious minority populations whose interests and alliances shifted rapidly.

The chapter began by defining Cossack communities on their own terms: self-governing frontier military societies combining autonomy with periodic state service, existing in several distinct forms — Zaporozhian, registered, and Don — that should not be collapsed into a single modern national category. It then examined the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth's noble political system, magnate landholding, and religious policy as the specific institutional context that produced the grievances feeding into 1648.

The chapter traced Khmelnytsky's uprising from its origins in personal grievance through its transformation into a mass movement, giving unflinching attention to the catastrophic violence against Jewish, Polish, and clerical populations that accompanied its spread, while explaining that violence in its specific historical context rather than through timeless stereotype. It then examined the Pereiaslav settlement of 1654 as this chapter's central and most historiographically contested episode, insisting that the surviving documentary evidence supports multiple legitimate interpretations rather than a single settled verdict.

The chapter followed the resulting Muscovite-Commonwealth war of 1654–1667 as an international conflict drawing in Sweden and the Crimean Khanate, examined the Cossack Hetmanate's own internal institutions and the instability of the Ruin following Khmelnytsky's death, and traced the territorial partition of the Dnipro basin at Andrusovo in 1667 and its confirmation at the Eternal Peace of 1686.

Turning southeast, the chapter examined Stenka Razin's rebellion on the Don and Volga as a distinct episode of frontier instability, drawing together Cossacks, fugitive peasants, and non-Russian Volga peoples against Muscovite fiscal and administrative encroachment, and compared its causes, course, and outcome with those of the Khmelnytsky uprising.

Finally, the chapter confronted directly the later national historiographical traditions — imperial Russian, Soviet, Ukrainian, and Polish — that have assigned sharply different meanings to Pereiaslav and the Hetmanate's fate, insisting that responsible historical analysis distinguishes the seventeenth-century evidence from later political uses of that evidence.

These lessons—careful attention to contested documentary evidence, recognition of multiple affected populations, and skepticism toward single-label national narratives—prepare students for Chapter 31's examination of Peter the Great and the Great Northern War, in which the frontier commitments and international alignments examined in this chapter would shape Muscovy's, and then Russia's, emerging role as a European power.
