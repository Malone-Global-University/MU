Part VI — Peter the Great and the Eighteenth-Century Empire
36

Russia and the Napoleonic Wars

A History of Russia: From the Eurasian World to the Russian Federation

Chapter Opening

In September 1812, the army of Napoleon Bonaparte — one of the largest multinational armies yet assembled in Europe — stood inside Moscow, one of the Russian Empire's two greatest cities. Napoleon had defeated successive major continental coalitions and built an extraordinary record of battlefield success, though not an unbroken one: his own army had been checked at Aspern-Essling in 1809, a reminder that Napoleonic invincibility was already, by 1812, more legend than established fact. He now occupied Russia's ancient religious and cultural capital. By the ordinary logic of eighteenth- and early nineteenth-century warfare, an enemy who had taken your capital, or one of your capitals, had won, or was close to winning: the losing side sued for peace, and a settlement followed. Alexander I did not sue for peace. Within weeks, Napoleon's army had begun a retreat from which it would never recover, and within two years Alexander himself would enter Paris at the head of a coalition army.

This chapter asks how that reversal happened, and it asks a prior question first: why were Russia and France fighting a war of this scale at all? The answer is not obvious. Five years before Napoleon's army crossed the Niemen River into Russian territory, Alexander I and Napoleon had met on a raft in the middle of that same river, at Tilsit, and constructed what both rulers described, at least for public purposes, as a partnership between the two greatest powers of continental Europe. Alexander had, by 1807, fought two costly and unsuccessful coalition campaigns against Napoleon. At Tilsit he did not continue that struggle; he joined, at least nominally, Napoleon's system. Five years later that partnership had collapsed into the largest invasion Europe had seen. Explaining that collapse, rather than treating a Russo-French war as an inevitability written into the two states' natures, is one of this chapter's central purposes.

The chapter's subject, then, is not simply "Napoleon invades Russia." It is the fuller and more difficult question of how Russia's relationship with revolutionary and then Napoleonic France shifted repeatedly, across roughly two decades, among ideological hostility, coalition warfare, diplomatic accommodation, economic rivalry, and finally open invasion and counter-invasion. Understanding this sequence requires attention to European coalition diplomacy, to the specific bargain and the specific failures of Tilsit, to the economic strains of Napoleon's Continental System on the Russian Empire's trade, and to the decisions, mistaken and otherwise, made by rulers, generals, and ordinary soldiers and civilians on both sides in 1812 itself.

The chapter also asks students to resist a familiar but misleading shorthand: that "the Russian winter defeated Napoleon." Winter mattered, and this chapter will not minimize it. But by the time the first serious frosts arrived, Napoleon's Grande Armée had already been devastated by heat, disease, hunger, desertion, and the sheer logistical impossibility of supplying several hundred thousand men and horses across the vast distances of the western Russian Empire. Treating a complex catastrophe of logistics, strategy, disease, and geography as a story about weather flatters both sides: it excuses Napoleon's own operational failures, and it substitutes an accident of climate for the deliberate strategic choices, and the enormous sacrifices of Russian soldiers, militiamen, and civilians, that actually shaped the campaign's outcome.

The human costs examined in this chapter were severe on every side. Hundreds of thousands of soldiers of many nationalities died in the campaign of 1812 alone, most from disease, starvation, and exposure rather than combat; Moscow itself burned during the French occupation, under disputed circumstances examined in Section 36.7; and the civilian population of the invasion corridor experienced requisitioning, displacement, and violence regardless of which army currently controlled their district. This chapter treats these costs directly rather than folding them into a triumphant national narrative, while also explaining why the campaign became, and remains, one of the most powerful sources of Russian patriotic memory, a theme examined critically in Section 36.9.

Finally, this chapter situates Russia within the wider European and global convulsion of the Napoleonic era. Russian armies fought in Italy, Switzerland, Germany, Poland, and eventually France itself; Russian diplomacy helped shape the post-Napoleonic settlement at the Congress of Vienna; and the experience of campaigning across Europe exposed a generation of Russian officers to political institutions and ideas markedly different from the autocratic and serf-based order of the Russian Empire at home — an exposure whose political consequences the next chapter, on the Decembrists, will examine in detail. By the chapter's end, students should be able to explain why the Franco-Russian relationship changed so many times between the 1790s and 1815, evaluate competing explanations for Napoleon's defeat in Russia, and distinguish the historical campaign of 1812 from its later mythologized memory as the "Patriotic War."

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Trace the shifting alignments of Russian foreign policy toward revolutionary and Napoleonic France from Paul I through the Congress of Vienna.
  2. Explain Russia's participation in the Second and Third Coalitions and evaluate the consequences of the defeat at Austerlitz.
  3. Analyze the Tilsit settlement as an attempted geopolitical bargain rather than simply a humiliation.
  4. Explain the economic and strategic strains the Continental System placed on Russia and connect them causally to the breakdown of Franco-Russian relations.
  5. Describe the composition, strategy, and logistical challenges of Napoleon's Grande Armée and the Russian defensive strategy of 1812.
  6. Evaluate the Battle of Borodino and the occupation of Moscow, distinguishing tactical outcomes from strategic consequences.
  7. Assess multiple interacting causes of Napoleon's defeat, including logistics, disease, Russian strategy, and weather, without reducing the explanation to any single factor.
  8. Trace the transformation of Russia's role from defensive campaign in 1812 to coalition offensive across Central Europe in 1813–1814.
  9. Explain Russia's position and influence at the Congress of Vienna and the post-Napoleonic European settlement.
  10. Distinguish the historical campaign of 1812 from its later commemoration as the "Patriotic War" in Russian national memory.
  11. Identify the social and institutional continuities that connected the empire of Catherine II and Paul I to the empire that fought Napoleon.
  12. Connect the political and cultural consequences of the Napoleonic Wars to the emergence of new political ideas among Russian officers, examined further in Chapter 37.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Was a major war between Russia and Napoleonic France inevitable, or did it result from a specific, contingent breakdown of diplomacy?
  2. Why did the Tilsit settlement, initially presented as a partnership, ultimately fail to hold?
  3. How did Russia's participation in the Continental System create economic and political strain, and how did that strain contribute to war?
  4. Why was Napoleon unable to convert battlefield success, including the occupation of Moscow, into a negotiated peace?
  5. What combination of causes explains Napoleon's catastrophic losses in the campaign of 1812?
  6. How did the war affect soldiers, militiamen, and civilians across the social order of the Russian Empire?
  7. How did Russia's role change between 1812 and 1814, from repelling invasion to reshaping the political map of Europe?
  8. How has the memory of 1812 differed from the documented historical campaign, and why does that difference matter?

Historical Context

Chapters 34 and 35 examined Catherine the Great's empire: an autocratic state resting on an increasingly stratified estate society, expanding steadily along the Black Sea coast and into partitioned Poland, engaged with Enlightenment political language while governing through serfdom and noble privilege. That empire's relationship with France had already been transformed once before this chapter's narrative begins, when the French Revolution of 1789 turned France, in the eyes of Catherine and most other European monarchs, from a familiar rival great power into an ideological threat to monarchical order itself. Catherine's final years and the brief reign of her son Paul I, examined in Section 36.1, unfolded against the background of a European system reorganizing itself around the question of how to respond to revolutionary France, and later to Napoleon Bonaparte, the general who emerged from the Revolution's wars to seize power in France and, by 1804, to crown himself emperor.

Russia's earlier wars against Napoleonic France, examined in Sections 36.1 through 36.3, took place as part of a series of European coalitions — shifting alliances of Austria, Britain, Prussia, Russia, and smaller states organized, with varying degrees of unity and success, against French power on the continent. These coalition wars were not primarily fought on Russian soil; Russian armies campaigned in Italy, Switzerland, Germany, and Poland, reflecting Russia's established position, since Peter the Great's reign examined in Chapters 31 and 32, as a major European military power with interests and ambitions extending well beyond its own borders. Readers should recall from Chapter 33 that eighteenth-century Russian foreign policy after Peter had already been characterized by shifting alliances and rapid reversals depending on the strategic calculations of individual rulers and their advisors; the instability examined in this chapter's early sections continues, rather than breaks from, that established pattern.

By the time Alexander I became emperor in 1801, following the assassination of his father Paul I, Napoleon had consolidated power in France and begun reorganizing much of continental Europe under French domination or influence. The central diplomatic and military problem facing Alexander, and facing this chapter, is how a power of Napoleon's military effectiveness could be resisted, accommodated, or managed by a Russian Empire that was itself a major military power but one operating at a considerable geographic remove from the core French sphere. The sections that follow trace Russia's shifting answers to that problem: renewed coalition warfare, culminating in defeat at Austerlitz; an attempted partnership at Tilsit; the collapse of that partnership under the strain of the Continental System and competing strategic interests, particularly over Poland; and finally full-scale invasion, defeat of that invasion, and Russian participation in the military and diplomatic reconstruction of Europe after Napoleon's fall.

Historical Vocabulary
Continental System

Continental System refers to Napoleon's policy, formally proclaimed in the 1806 Berlin Decree and extended in subsequent decrees, of excluding British trade from continental Europe in an attempt to damage the British economy severely enough to force a negotiated peace on French terms. Because Britain's navy made direct invasion impractical after Trafalgar, Napoleon substituted economic warfare for military conquest of Britain itself.

The system mattered for Russia because Alexander I's agreement at Tilsit to participate placed heavy strain on Russian trade, particularly grain, timber, and naval-stores exports long directed toward British markets, examined in Section 36.5. Weakening Russian enforcement of the system after roughly 1810 became one of the central, though not sole, causes of the breakdown of Franco-Russian relations that led to the invasion of 1812.

Historical Vocabulary
Patriotic War of 1812

Patriotic War of 1812 (Отечественная война 1812 года) is the conventional Russian historical and cultural term for the 1812 campaign, emphasizing national unity, sacrifice, and the defense of the homeland against a foreign invader. The term reflects a powerful and enduring commemorative tradition rather than a neutral technical label, and it should not be treated as interchangeable with a complete account of the war's diplomatic origins, military conduct, or social costs.

Students should distinguish the documented military and diplomatic history examined in Sections 36.6–36.9 from the later literary, artistic, and political commemoration examined in Section 36.9, while recognizing that this commemorative tradition is itself an important and legitimate subject of historical study, treated further in this textbook's Historical Memory appendix.

Section 36.1

Paul I and European War

Russia's involvement in the wars against revolutionary and Napoleonic France did not begin with Alexander I, and it did not begin as a settled, permanent commitment. It began under Paul I, who succeeded Catherine the Great in 1796, and it began as one part of a wider European mobilization against the French Revolution's armies, which by the mid-1790s had already defeated several of the coalitions organized against them and had begun exporting revolutionary institutions into occupied and allied territories in Italy and the Low Countries. Paul joined the Second Coalition, an alliance including Britain, Austria, the Ottoman Empire, and smaller states, formed in 1798 in response to French expansion, including Napoleon's invasion of Egypt and French advances in Italy and Switzerland.

Russia's most visible contribution to this coalition effort was the campaign led by Field Marshal Alexander Suvorov, already a celebrated commander from earlier wars against the Ottoman Empire and against the Polish uprising of 1794. Suvorov's Italian campaign of 1799 drove French forces from much of northern Italy in a rapid and successful offensive, and his subsequent, far more difficult campaign across the Swiss Alps, undertaken amid deteriorating coalition coordination with Austria, demonstrated both the Russian army's capacity for difficult mountain operations and the severe strain that poor allied cooperation could place on even a successful field commander. Suvorov's campaigns matter for this chapter less as a narrative of individual battles than as evidence that Russia entered the Napoleonic era already established as a major, mobile European military power capable of projecting force far beyond its own frontiers — a capacity that would matter again, in the opposite direction, when Russian armies advanced into Central Europe in 1813 and 1814.

Coalition warfare, however, proved deeply frustrating for Paul. Coordination among Austria, Britain, and Russia broke down repeatedly over strategy, territorial ambitions, and mutual suspicion; Paul grew convinced that Austria in particular had abandoned Suvorov's forces to unnecessary risk in Switzerland, and that Britain was pursuing its own commercial and colonial interests rather than the coalition's shared cause. By 1800, Paul had reversed course dramatically, withdrawing Russia from the Second Coalition, moving toward diplomatic accommodation with Napoleon's France, and adopting an increasingly hostile posture toward Britain, including participation in an armed neutrality intended to resist British naval interference with neutral shipping. This reversal, completed only months before Paul's assassination by a group of court and military conspirators in March 1801, illustrates a theme this chapter will return to repeatedly: Russian alignment in the Napoleonic wars was not fixed by ideology or permanent enmity but reflected specific, changeable calculations of interest, alliance reliability, and personal judgment by the reigning emperor and his advisors.

Section 36.2

Alexander I

Alexander I came to the throne in 1801 amid circumstances his government never openly acknowledged: Alexander had accepted a conspiracy intended to force Paul's abdication on the understanding that his father's life would be spared; Paul was instead killed during the coup, leaving the extent of Alexander's own responsibility for the fatal outcome a lasting historical controversy. This origin shadowed his reign's early years and must be read alongside this textbook's coverage of Alexander's domestic reform ambitions, examined more fully in later chapters. For this chapter's purposes, Alexander matters chiefly as a foreign-policy actor confronting the central strategic problem of his generation: how should a major continental power, with substantial military resources but no direct land border with France's Rhineland and Italian core, respond to Napoleon's continued consolidation of power and expansion of French influence across Central Europe?

Alexander's early reign combined genuine, if inconsistently applied, interest in Enlightenment-influenced governmental reform with an evolving and often contradictory foreign policy outlook. He did not initially seek war with France; Russian diplomacy in 1801–1804 explored various arrangements, including renewed relations with Britain and cautious dealings with Napoleon's government. Napoleon's self-coronation as Emperor of the French in 1804, together with continued French expansion in Italy and Germany and rising tension over the fate of smaller German and Italian states, pushed Alexander back toward coalition diplomacy. By 1805 Russia had joined Austria and Britain in the Third Coalition, believing that combined Austrian and Russian military strength, backed by British subsidies and naval power, could check further French expansion and restore a European balance of power more favorable to the traditional monarchies.

Alexander should not be understood simply as an idealistic counterpart to a cynical Napoleon, a contrast sometimes drawn in older, more romanticized accounts of the period. Alexander pursued Russian dynastic security, Russian influence in Poland and the Balkans, and his own evolving, genuinely held but also politically convenient vision of a legitimate European order resting on monarchical cooperation — a vision that would find fuller expression after 1814 in the diplomacy examined in Section 36.9. His decisions between 1805 and 1812, including both his willingness to fight Napoleon and his later willingness to negotiate with him at Tilsit, reflect calculated judgments about Russian interest and capability rather than a single fixed ideological commitment either for or against Napoleonic France.

Section 36.3

Austerlitz and Coalition Warfare

The Third Coalition's war against France in 1805 tested whether combined Austrian and Russian military power, reinforced by British financial subsidies, could check Napoleon's continental dominance. The campaign did not go as the coalition powers hoped. Napoleon's forces defeated an Austrian army at Ulm in October 1805 before Russian forces under Kutuzov could join it, and Napoleon then advanced into Austrian territory, occupying Vienna. Russian and Austrian forces, with Alexander I and Emperor Francis personally present at allied headquarters and deeply involved in the campaign's decision-making, met Napoleon's army at Austerlitz, in Moravia, on December 2, 1805. Kutuzov formally held overall command of the combined force, though the allied operational plan owed much to the Austrian chief of staff, Franz von Weyrother, and to the pressure the two monarchs' presence placed on more cautious commanders to seek battle.

The battle became one of Napoleon's most celebrated tactical victories. Napoleon deliberately weakened his right flank to draw the coalition armies into an attack on ground of his choosing, then used the resulting gap in the coalition line to launch a decisive counterattack against the allied center, splitting and defeating the combined Austrian and Russian forces with heavy losses. The defeat forced Austria to withdraw from the war and accept a punitive peace, while Russian forces retreated toward their own territory. Austerlitz demonstrated, in unusually vivid form, the operational superiority Napoleon's reorganized French army, and his own generalship, held over the more traditionally organized armies of Austria and Russia at this stage of the wars.

Defeat at Austerlitz did not end Russian involvement in the broader struggle. Russia renewed coalition warfare in 1806–1807 alongside Prussia, following Prussia's own entry into the war and subsequent rapid defeat at Jena and Auerstedt. Russian forces then fought two further costly campaigns against Napoleon at Eylau, in February 1807, an exceptionally bloody and tactically indecisive battle fought in severe winter conditions, and at Friedland in June 1807, where Napoleon achieved a decisive victory over Russian forces under Bennigsen. These repeated defeats, rather than producing outright Russian collapse or unconditional surrender, instead produced exhaustion on both sides and created the conditions for the negotiated settlement examined in the next section. The persistence of warfare despite repeated defeat illustrates a pattern this chapter traces throughout: military defeat alone did not automatically produce a stable peace between Russia and France, because neither side's underlying strategic problem — how to secure lasting security and influence on a continent where the other possessed formidable power — was resolved by any single battlefield outcome.

Section 36.4

Tilsit

Following the defeat at Friedland, Alexander I and Napoleon met at Tilsit, on the Niemen River, in July 1807, and negotiated a settlement on a raft anchored in the river — a staged, symbolically significant venue emphasizing the two emperors' claimed equality of status. The resulting Treaty of Tilsit should be understood not merely as Russian humiliation, though it was received with considerable domestic unease among segments of the Russian nobility, but as an attempted geopolitical bargain negotiated after costly campaigns culminating in Russia's decisive defeat at Friedland, with neither ruler eager to prolong the war, and each seeking, at least for a time, to divide European influence between them rather than continue an exhausting conflict.

The settlement's terms reflected this attempted division. Napoleon secured Russian recognition of extensive French predominance across much of continental Europe, including the creation of the Duchy of Warsaw from formerly Prussian Polish territory, a new political entity whose existence immediately created a point of long-term friction, since Russian officials worried, correctly as later events showed, that the duchy might become a base for renewed Polish independence movements bordering directly on Russian imperial territory. In exchange, Alexander received a degree of French acquiescence to Russian ambitions elsewhere, including against Ottoman territory and in relation to Sweden, and both rulers professed hope that a working Franco-Russian partnership might stabilize the continent under joint great-power management. Crucially, Alexander also agreed that Russia would join Napoleon's Continental System, the economic blockade against British trade examined in the next section — a commitment that would prove, over the following five years, to be the settlement's most consequential and ultimately unsustainable provision.

Primary Source Window

The Tilsit Settlement, 1807

Creator: Negotiated directly between Alexander I and Napoleon · Date: July 1807 · Type: Public diplomatic treaty, with separate secret articles

Context: The publicly announced Franco-Russian treaty established peace and amity between the two empires and required Russian recognition of French territorial reorganization in Central Europe, including the newly created Duchy of Warsaw. A separate, secret set of arrangements, not published at the time, laid out a conditional anti-British policy: Alexander was first to offer Russian mediation between France and Britain on specified terms; only if Britain rejected mediation would Russia join France in economic measures against British trade and help pressure Denmark, Sweden, and Portugal to close their ports as well.

Paraphrase of the settlement's substance (not a verbatim quotation):

The public treaty bound the two emperors to peace and to mutual recognition of each other's continental influence and established the Duchy of Warsaw under the king of Saxony. The secret arrangements committed Alexander to attempt mediation between France and Britain first, and only upon its failure to join Napoleon in compelling an end to British trade with the continent, while the two courts professed continued cooperation on matters of European order.

Analysis: Contemporary observers on both sides recognized Tilsit as an unstable bargain rather than a natural alliance between compatible partners. Napoleon needed Russian cooperation, or at least neutrality, to complete his economic war against Britain and to secure his eastern flank; Alexander needed a respite from costly and unsuccessful coalition warfare and hoped to gain freedom of action elsewhere. Neither ruler fully trusted the other, and the settlement's most burdensome provision for Russia, participation in the Continental System, created strains that the treaty's own text could not resolve.

Questions for students:

  1. What did each ruler gain, and what did each ruler give up, at Tilsit?
  2. Why might a settlement negotiated symbolically as an agreement between equals still function, in practice, as an unequal bargain?
  3. What provision of the treaty proved most difficult for Russia to sustain, and why?
  4. How does Tilsit illustrate the difference between a peace treaty and a durable political partnership?
Section 36.5

The Continental System

Napoleon could not defeat Britain militarily after the destruction of the combined French and Spanish fleets at Trafalgar in 1805 ended any realistic prospect of a cross-Channel invasion. He turned instead to economic warfare, attempting to exclude British goods from continental European markets through the Continental System, formally proclaimed in the Berlin Decree of 1806 and extended through subsequent decrees, with the goal of damaging British trade and finance severely enough to force a negotiated peace on French terms. Russian participation, agreed at Tilsit, was essential to the system's effectiveness, since Russia controlled major Baltic ports through which British trade had long flowed and possessed close commercial ties to Britain, particularly in the export of grain, timber, hemp, and other naval stores on which British shipping and shipbuilding substantially depended.

Compliance proved economically painful for Russia. British trade had been a major source of revenue for Russian landowners and merchants and a major source of customs income for the Russian state; excluding it disrupted established commercial networks without providing Russia any comparable alternative market of similar scale. Russian elites, particularly noble landowners dependent on grain and timber exports, experienced genuine financial strain, and the value of Russian currency depreciated over the years following Tilsit. Enforcement grew progressively weaker as Russian officials tolerated, and at times actively facilitated, continued trade with Britain under neutral flags and through licensed exceptions, provoking repeated French complaints and demands for stricter compliance.

Economic strain was not the only source of deteriorating relations. The Duchy of Warsaw remained a persistent point of friction, with Russian officials suspicious that Napoleon might expand it, restore a fully independent Poland, or use it as a staging ground against Russian territory; Napoleon, for his part, grew increasingly frustrated with what he regarded as Russian bad faith in enforcing the blockade and with Alexander's continued pursuit of Russian interests against the Ottoman Empire and in the Baltic in ways Napoleon had not fully endorsed. Dynastic friction added a further complication when a proposed marriage alliance between Napoleon and a Russian grand duchess failed to materialize, and Napoleon instead married into the Austrian imperial family, a development some historians read as contributing to a cooling of personal relations between the two emperors, though it should not be treated as a primary cause of the war that followed.

Cause and Effect

The Collapse of the Tilsit Settlement

Long-Term Conditions

A generation of shifting, unstable Franco-Russian relations since the 1790s; the absence of a natural, lasting alignment of interest between the two powers; Russia's established position as a major independent European power unwilling to accept permanent subordination.

Intermediate Developments

The economic burden of the Continental System on Russian trade and elite finances; weakening Russian enforcement of the blockade; continued friction over the Duchy of Warsaw and Poland; competing Russian and French ambitions in the Balkans and Baltic.

Short-Term Triggers

Russia's effective abandonment of strict blockade enforcement by 1810–1811; Napoleon's continued military buildup in Central Europe and the Duchy of Warsaw; mutual accusations of bad faith and failed efforts at renewed diplomatic accommodation.

Effects

Napoleon's decision to assemble the Grande Armée and invade Russian imperial territory in June 1812, examined in Section 36.6.

Historical complexity: No single grievance—not economics alone, not Poland alone, not personal distrust alone—fully explains the collapse of Tilsit; each factor reinforced the others across five years of deteriorating relations.

Section 36.6

Napoleon's Invasion of Russia

On June 24, 1812, Napoleon's Grande Armée began crossing the Niemen River into Russian imperial territory. The invading force, whose initial strength crossing or approaching the Niemen is commonly estimated by modern scholarship at roughly 440,000 to 455,000 men, while total figures for all forces committed to the campaign — including later reinforcements and supporting formations brought in over subsequent months — run substantially higher and vary considerably depending on counting method, was not a purely French army but a genuinely multinational force, including substantial contingents from Napoleon's German, Italian, and Polish allied and subject states, reflecting the extent of French continental hegemony achieved by 1812. Napoleon's strategic aim was to force a decisive battle early in the campaign, destroy the main Russian field armies, and compel Alexander to accept renewed and stricter compliance with French continental policy, much as earlier campaigns against Austria and Prussia had been resolved through rapid, decisive military victory.

Russian commanders faced an acute strategic dilemma. The Russian armies under Barclay de Tolly and Bagration, considerably smaller than the invading force and initially separated from one another, could not safely risk a decisive early battle against Napoleon's concentrated strength without courting the kind of catastrophic defeat that had befallen Austria and Prussia in earlier campaigns. Barclay de Tolly, serving as both minister of war and a field commander, adopted a strategy of controlled retreat, withdrawing Russian forces eastward while attempting to avoid decisive engagement and to unite the separated Russian armies. This strategy proved militarily sound but politically controversial: it surrendered substantial Russian territory, including the important trading city of Smolensk after a costly battle in August 1812, and provoked criticism from officers, nobles, and public opinion demanding a more aggressive defense of Russian soil. The political pressure eventually contributed to Alexander's decision to appoint Kutuzov as overall commander in chief in late August, though Kutuzov initially continued much of Barclay's underlying strategic logic.

The invading army's deterioration began well before any serious autumn or winter weather arrived, a point of considerable importance for evaluating the causes of Napoleon's eventual defeat, examined further in this chapter's Historical Debate feature. Summer heat, inadequate water supplies for men and especially for the enormous number of horses the campaign required, disease including dysentery and typhus, and the sheer difficulty of supplying several hundred thousand men across the vast distances and poor road networks of the western Russian Empire produced substantial losses to desertion, illness, and death well before the army reached Moscow. Historians estimate that Napoleon's force had already been reduced by a very substantial proportion, in some estimates by roughly half, before the Battle of Borodino was fought in September, a reduction owing chiefly to logistical breakdown and disease rather than combat losses.

Section 36.7

Borodino and Moscow

By early September 1812, sustained Russian retreat without a major battle had become politically and militarily untenable, and Kutuzov, under pressure from Alexander and Russian public opinion, chose to offer battle at Borodino, roughly 120 kilometers west of Moscow, on September 7. The battle was among the bloodiest single-day engagements of the entire Napoleonic era: modern estimates of combined casualties, though they vary considerably among sources and should be treated as approximate, commonly range from roughly 70,000 to over 80,000 killed, wounded, and missing on both sides combined, out of forces numbering somewhat over 250,000 total combatants.

The battle's outcome resists simple characterization as either a French or a Russian victory. Napoleon's forces held the battlefield at the day's end and inflicted somewhat heavier losses proportionally, allowing French sources at the time and since to describe the battle as a victory in the narrow tactical sense. But Napoleon achieved neither of his real strategic objectives: he did not destroy the Russian army, which withdrew from the field in reasonably good order under Kutuzov's continued command, and he did not thereby compel Alexander to negotiate. Bagration was mortally wounded during the battle, and both armies suffered losses so severe that neither could immediately press a decisive follow-up action. Kutuzov, judging that the Russian army needed to preserve itself rather than risk destruction defending Moscow directly, made the controversial decision to abandon the city without further battle, allowing Napoleon's forces to enter Moscow on September 14.

Napoleon's occupation of Moscow produced neither the peace settlement nor the military triumph he had anticipated. Much of the city burned during the following days, in fires whose precise origin remains debated among historians: French accounts at the time blamed Russian arson, ordered or encouraged by the Moscow governor Fyodor Rostopchin as a deliberate scorched-earth measure to deny the French usable shelter and supplies, while some later scholarship has suggested a more complicated combination of deliberate destruction, accidental fires spreading through a largely wooden city under wartime chaos, and looting-related fires; students should recognize this as a genuinely disputed historical question rather than a settled fact. Whatever their precise cause, the fires destroyed much of Moscow's housing stock and supplies, worsening the already severe logistical position of Napoleon's army. Napoleon remained in the ruined city for over a month, repeatedly attempting to open negotiations with Alexander, who refused to respond. The occupation of one of the empire's greatest cities, a result that in most earlier Napoleonic campaigns had reliably produced negotiated capitulation, here produced nothing of the kind — a central paradox this chapter's opening posed and that this section now resolves: Moscow was not the Russian imperial administrative capital, which remained at St. Petersburg, and its loss, however symbolically and materially devastating, did not by itself threaten the survival of the Russian state or compel its ruler to submit.

Multiple Perspectives

Borodino and Moscow, 1812

A Russian regimental officer at Borodino: For an officer in the Russian line, the battle meant standing under sustained artillery bombardment and repeated infantry and cavalry assaults against fortified positions, in a battle whose staggering losses were, at the time, widely understood among participants as a demonstration of Russian resolve rather than as a clear victory or defeat, and whose long retreat past Moscow afterward was received with anger and confusion by officers convinced the army should have defended the ancient capital directly.
A Moscow resident during the evacuation and occupation: For an ordinary Moscow resident, the weeks before the French entry brought evacuation of much of the population and removable property under Governor Rostopchin's direction, followed, for those who remained, by fire, looting, and an unfamiliar occupying army living off requisitioned and looted supplies in a devastated city, with little certainty about how long the occupation would last or what would follow.
A French soldier of the Grande Armée entering Moscow: For a soldier who had marched hundreds of kilometers on inadequate supply, entry into Moscow initially promised shelter, plunder, and the expectation of an imminent peace settlement; the following weeks of waiting in a burned, half-emptied city, with no Russian negotiator arriving and supplies dwindling, instead produced growing anxiety about the approaching Russian winter and the army's increasingly precarious position deep inside hostile territory.

These perspectives illustrate why the campaign's turning point cannot be reduced to a single narrative of triumph or disaster: the same weeks in Moscow represented, simultaneously, a Russian strategic sacrifice, a civilian catastrophe, and a French army's slow realization that occupying a city had not won the war.

Section 36.8

Retreat and Russian Counteroffensive

Napoleon ordered the retreat from Moscow on October 19, 1812, having waited over a month for a negotiated settlement that never came and facing a deteriorating supply and shelter situation in the ruined city as autumn advanced. What followed was one of the most catastrophic military retreats in modern European history, though the causes of that catastrophe were considerably more varied than popular memory, emphasizing the Russian winter alone, generally acknowledges. The retreating army, already severely weakened by the losses of the advance and the occupation, faced an interlocking set of disasters: an attempt to retreat along a more southerly route with unexploited supplies met Russian forces at Maloyaroslavets, where French troops held the town tactically but Kutuzov's forces blocked the road farther south, helping induce Napoleon to abandon the southern route and turn the army back toward its already-devastated original road through Smolensk; horses, essential for cavalry, artillery, and supply transport, died in enormous numbers from exhaustion, starvation, and eventually cold, crippling the army's mobility and its ability to move remaining supplies and artillery; disease, especially typhus, continued to kill soldiers at a rate that in many weeks exceeded combat losses; and Cossack cavalry and Russian partisan detachments harassed the retreating columns continuously, cutting off stragglers and foraging parties.

The crossing of the Berezina River in late November 1812 became the retreat's most infamous episode, as French military engineers constructed makeshift bridges under fire to allow the remaining organized units to escape encirclement by converging Russian forces, at a cost of thousands of additional deaths among stragglers, camp followers, and units unable to cross before the bridges were destroyed. By the time surviving remnants of the Grande Armée finally recrossed the Niemen in December 1812, the invading force had suffered losses that essentially destroyed it as an effective fighting force, though precise casualty figures for the full campaign remain debated among historians and should be treated as estimates reflecting the difficulty of tracking losses across an army of multiple nationalities over six months of continuous crisis.

The campaign's outcome transformed Russia's position from defender to leading member of a renewed anti-Napoleonic coalition. Rather than simply expelling Napoleon's forces from Russian territory and halting, Alexander I chose to pursue the war into Central Europe, reasoning that a Napoleon left in control of Germany and Poland would remain a permanent threat to Russian security. Russian forces, in alliance with Prussia and eventually Austria and other powers in what became known as the War of the Sixth Coalition, campaigned through Germany in 1813, achieving a decisive coalition victory at the Battle of Leipzig in October 1813, and advanced into France itself in early 1814, culminating in the coalition occupation of Paris in March 1814 and Napoleon's first abdication. This transition marks a crucial turning point often underemphasized in accounts that treat 1812 as the war's climax: a defensive campaign to expel an invader became, within a year, a coalition offensive that helped determine the political future of the entire European continent.

Section 36.9

Vienna, Victory, and the Memory of 1812

Napoleon's defeat, his brief return to power during the Hundred Days of 1815 and final defeat at Waterloo, and the broader collapse of French continental hegemony opened the question of how postwar Europe would be politically reorganized. The Congress of Vienna, convened in 1814–1815 and bringing together representatives of the major European powers, undertook this reorganization, and Russia, having fielded one of the coalition's largest armies and played a central role in the campaigns of 1812–1814, occupied a position of substantial influence at the negotiations. Alexander I personally attended much of the Congress and pursued an active diplomatic role, seeking, among other goals, a settlement in Poland that would place a reconstituted Polish kingdom under Russian dynastic control, an outcome achieved, with modification, in the Congress's final settlement, which created a Congress Kingdom of Poland in personal union with the Russian crown.

Russia's enhanced European standing after 1815 rested on more than military success alone. Alexander promoted a vision of post-Napoleonic international order resting on monarchical solidarity and periodic great-power consultation to manage crises and suppress revolutionary threats, an approach formalized in the Holy Alliance, a declaration of Christian monarchical cooperation that Alexander proposed and that Austria and Prussia joined, and in the broader Congress system of periodic great-power meetings that shaped European diplomacy for much of the following decade. This diplomatic architecture, examined further in later chapters treating Russian foreign policy under Nicholas I, represented Alexander's attempt to translate the experience of coalition victory into a durable framework for continental stability, one that generally favored existing monarchical governments against revolutionary or nationalist movements.

The war's political consequences within Russia itself require careful and limited treatment here, since their fuller development belongs to the following chapter. What can be said in this chapter is that the campaigns of 1812–1814 exposed a substantial generation of Russian officers, including many from noble families who would later play prominent roles in Russian political life, to direct experience of Western and Central European society, institutions, and political ideas markedly different from the autocratic, serf-based order they had left behind. Officers who marched through Central and Western European societies with administrative, legal, and political arrangements markedly different from Russia's own, and into a France that had, whatever its recent turmoil, experienced two decades of revolutionary and constitutional experimentation, returned to an empire that had defeated Napoleon but had changed remarkably little in its own domestic political structure. This gap between international triumph and continued domestic autocracy created a current of dissatisfaction among some officers that would, in the following years, feed into the organized political movements examined in Chapter 37; this chapter does no more than identify that current's origin.

The historical memory of 1812 within Russia developed along a different, though related, track. Within a generation, the campaign had become established in Russian culture as the "Patriotic War" (Отечественная война), a term emphasizing national unity, sacrifice, and the successful defense of the homeland against a foreign invader, and this framing shaped subsequent commemoration, monuments, and literature, most famously Leo Tolstoy's novel War and Peace, published decades after the events it depicts and reflecting Tolstoy's own later philosophical concerns about history and human agency rather than serving as a contemporaneous historical record. Students should understand this later literary and commemorative tradition as important evidence about nineteenth-century Russian national identity and memory in its own right, examined further in this textbook's later treatment of Russian culture and in the Historical Memory appendix, but should not treat it as a substitute for the documented military and diplomatic history this chapter has presented.

Russia and the World

The Congress of Vienna

Russia's role at Vienna illustrates a recurring pattern in this textbook: Russian power was consistently exercised in active engagement with, rather than isolation from, the wider European state system. Alexander's Polish ambitions provoked friction with Austria, Prussia, and Britain, requiring difficult negotiation before a compromise settlement was reached; Russian diplomats worked alongside Austrian, British, French, and Prussian counterparts to construct a settlement intended to prevent renewed continental war for a generation, an aim that, despite significant strains, succeeded in avoiding another general European war until the Crimean War examined in Chapter 41. Russia's post-1815 international position, resting on demonstrated military capability, extensive territory, and active diplomatic engagement, marked a high point of Russian influence in European affairs that later chapters will show eroding gradually over the following decades.

Historical Debate

Why Did Napoleon Fail in Russia?

The question: Historians broadly agree that Napoleon's Russian campaign ended in catastrophic failure, but they weigh the relative importance of its causes differently.

The logistical-primacy interpretation: Many historians emphasize that the Grande Armée's losses to heat, disease, inadequate water and forage, and the sheer difficulty of supplying several hundred thousand men and horses across enormous distances had already reduced the army by roughly half before Borodino was even fought. On this reading, the campaign was strategically doomed by problems of scale and logistics that Napoleon's planning failed to solve, regardless of what happened afterward.
The Russian-strategy interpretation: Other historians place greater weight on the deliberate Russian strategy of controlled retreat, refusal to risk premature decisive battle, the abandonment of Moscow rather than its costly defense, and sustained partisan and Cossack harassment of the retreating army, arguing that Russian commanders made a series of difficult but sound strategic choices that denied Napoleon the decisive early victory his entire campaign plan required.
The weather-and-attrition interpretation: A further body of scholarship, while not treating winter as a sufficient explanation on its own, argues that the early and unusually severe onset of cold during the retreat compounded an already desperate logistical crisis, accelerating horse deaths, frostbite, and starvation to a degree that transformed a difficult retreat into a near-total catastrophe.

Evidence: Historians draw on surviving French army records and memoirs, Russian military reports, and modern demographic and logistical reconstructions of army size and losses at each stage of the campaign.

Points of agreement: Nearly all historians agree that no single factor is sufficient by itself, and that popular explanations crediting Russian winter alone seriously oversimplify a campaign whose worst losses, in relative terms, had already occurred before the coldest weather arrived.

Points of disagreement: Historians differ on how to weigh the relative contribution of Napoleon's own strategic and logistical planning failures against the effectiveness of Russian strategy, and on how much independent causal weight to assign to weather as opposed to disease and supply failure.

Student task: Using evidence from Sections 36.6 through 36.8, construct an argument that ranks at least three contributing causes of Napoleon's defeat, explaining how they interacted rather than treating any single cause as sufficient alone.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Russian Foreign Policy, 1796–1815

What Stayed the Same

  • Russia consistently acted as a major, independent European power pursuing its own dynastic and strategic interests rather than as a permanently fixed member of any single alliance bloc.
  • Russian rulers throughout this period, as in the eighteenth century examined in Chapters 33 and 34, showed a willingness to reverse alliances rapidly when circumstances changed.
  • Poland remained a persistent and unresolved point of Russian strategic concern across the entire period, from the partitions examined in Chapter 34 through the Duchy of Warsaw and the Congress Kingdom.

What Changed

  • Russia moved from junior coalition partner, suffering major defeats at Austerlitz and Friedland, to leading continental power directing the pursuit of a defeated Napoleon into France itself.
  • The scale of Russian military mobilization and the geographic reach of Russian campaigning expanded dramatically, from expeditionary forces in Italy and Switzerland to a war fought partly on Russian soil and then across the length of Central Europe.
  • Russia's diplomatic standing after 1815 reached a height not seen since Peter the Great's victory in the Great Northern War, examined in Chapter 31, giving Alexander I substantial influence over the postwar European settlement.

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both continuity in Russia's independent great-power behavior and genuine change in its relative standing equips students to see 1812–1815 as a turning point in Russia's European role rather than an isolated military episode.

Everyday Life and Social History

War, Militia, and the Home Front

The war of 1812 mobilized Russian society well beyond the regular army. Facing invasion, the government called for the formation of a militia, or opolchenie, drawing on landowners to organize and partly equip peasant recruits from their estates, a mobilization that touched rural communities across a wide swath of the empire's western and central provinces and that operated through the serf-based social order examined in Chapters 34 and 35: militia mobilization worked through the existing estate and serf system, and service did not itself alter the legal status of those who served, even as the war's primary purpose remained the defense of the empire against invasion and Napoleonic domination rather than the defense of serfdom as such.

Civilians in the path of the campaign experienced requisitioning, destruction, and displacement regardless of which army currently controlled their district; peasant communities in the invasion corridor lost livestock, grain stores, and housing to both armies' foraging needs, and some peasant groups organized informal, localized resistance to foraging parties from the invading force, a phenomenon later Russian commemoration would celebrate, sometimes with considerable exaggeration, as evidence of a spontaneous popular uprising against the invader. Nobility experienced the war differently: those who could afford to evacuate movable property, as many Moscow residents did before the French occupation, generally suffered less direct material loss than peasant communities with fewer resources for flight. Noble officers faced substantial combat risk of their own, but the overwhelmingly lower-estate rank and file of both armies, together with militia recruits and civilians along the invasion corridor, bore the enormous human and material costs of the campaign in numbers far exceeding those borne by the officer corps.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined Russia's shifting relationship with revolutionary and Napoleonic France between the 1790s and 1815, treating that relationship as a sequence of contingent political choices rather than an inevitable march toward war. It began with Paul I's participation in the Second Coalition, including Suvorov's celebrated Italian and Swiss campaigns, and his subsequent, rapid reversal toward accommodation with France, establishing early the theme of unstable Russian alignment that recurs throughout the chapter.

Alexander I's early reign brought renewed coalition warfare and the decisive French victory at Austerlitz in 1805, followed by further costly campaigns at Eylau and Friedland in 1806–1807. Rather than producing Russian capitulation, these defeats led to the negotiated settlement at Tilsit in 1807, examined as an attempted geopolitical partnership between exhausted rivals rather than simple humiliation.

The chapter traced the Continental System's economic burden on Russian trade and elite finances, alongside continuing friction over the Duchy of Warsaw and competing strategic interests, as the interacting causes that gradually destroyed the Tilsit settlement over roughly five years. This causal analysis resists any single-factor explanation for the outbreak of war in 1812.

Napoleon's invasion, launched in June 1812 with a massive multinational Grande Armée, is examined as a campaign whose catastrophic outcome resulted from interacting logistical, strategic, and environmental factors rather than from the Russian winter alone. Russian commanders' strategy of controlled retreat, the costly Battle of Borodino, and the ultimately futile occupation of Moscow together demonstrate why capturing a great city did not, in this campaign, compel Russian surrender.

The catastrophic French retreat from Moscow, examined alongside the Battle of the Berezina and the campaign's staggering losses, marked the effective destruction of the Grande Armée as a fighting force. The chapter then traced Russia's transformation from defender to leading offensive coalition power, culminating in the Battle of Leipzig and the coalition occupation of Paris in 1814.

At the Congress of Vienna, Russia, and Alexander I personally, exercised substantial influence over the reconstruction of the European political order, securing a reorganized Polish settlement and promoting a vision of monarchical cooperation embodied in the Holy Alliance and the broader Congress system.

The chapter closed by distinguishing the documented military and diplomatic history of 1812–1815 from its later cultural memory as the "Patriotic War," a memory shaped significantly by later nineteenth-century literature and commemoration, and by identifying, without developing in detail, the beginnings of political dissatisfaction among Russian officers exposed to European institutions during the campaigns — a development Chapter 37 will examine as the origin of organized constitutional and revolutionary opposition within the Russian officer corps.

These lessons — attention to contingency over inevitability, multi-causal historical explanation, and the careful distinction between historical event and national memory — prepare students directly for Chapter 37's treatment of the Decembrists and for the wider nineteenth-century political history examined in Part VII.

Mapping the Grande Armée's Losses, 1812

This project asks you to investigate the changing size of Napoleon's invasion force at successive stages of the 1812 campaign, evaluating what the pattern of losses reveals about the relative importance of logistics, disease, combat, and weather.

  1. Gather estimates. Research scholarly estimates of Grande Armée strength at the Niemen crossing, at Smolensk, at Borodino, at Moscow, and at the final recrossing of the Niemen in December 1812.
  2. Compare sources. Note where estimates differ among historians and consider why precise figures remain difficult to establish.
  3. Construct a timeline or chart. Organize the estimates chronologically to visualize where the steepest losses occurred.
  4. Analyze causation. Identify which known causes of loss (disease, desertion, combat, cold, starvation) were most significant at each stage.
  5. Write your findings. In a 3–5 page essay, explain what this pattern of losses demonstrates about the causes of Napoleon's defeat, and evaluate whether "the Russian winter" is an adequate explanation.

Library resources to use: The MGU History Library's Napoleonic era collection, academic databases on military history and historical demography, and English-language editions of French and Russian campaign memoirs.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has carried students through nearly two decades of shifting Russian relations with revolutionary and Napoleonic France, from Paul I's early coalition warfare through Alexander I's defeat, partnership, betrayal, invasion, and ultimate triumph. The central lesson is one of contingency: at no point between 1798 and 1815 was the eventual outcome — Russian victory and a leading Russian role in reconstructing European order — foreordained. Coalition warfare failed repeatedly before it succeeded; a negotiated partnership at Tilsit seemed, for a time, a genuine alternative to continued war; and Napoleon's invasion, though catastrophic in its outcome, was not doomed from its first day by weather alone but unraveled through an interacting chain of logistical, strategic, and environmental failures.

The war's consequences extended well beyond the battlefield. It reshaped Russia's international standing, placing Alexander I at the center of the diplomatic reconstruction of Europe at Vienna. It also, more quietly, began reshaping the political outlook of a generation of Russian officers whose experience of European institutions abroad would not easily be reconciled with the autocratic and serf-based order they returned to at home — a tension the next chapter will trace directly into organized political opposition and the Decembrist uprising of 1825.

As the textbook proceeds to Chapter 37's examination of the Decembrists and the political legacy of 1812, students should carry forward this chapter's central habits of historical thinking: resistance to single-cause explanations, attention to the gap between contemporaries' experience of events and later national memory of those events, and recognition that even a war ending in decisive victory can leave behind unresolved tensions that shape the following generation's political life.
