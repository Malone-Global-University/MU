The Crimean War and the Crisis of the Imperial System

A History of Russia: From the Eurasian World to the Russian Federation

Chapter Opening

This chapter treats the Crimean War less as a story of battles than as a test: a test of the state that Nicholas I built, of the army that was its proudest instrument, and of the social order on which both depended.

On the night of 8–9 September 1855 (New Style), the defenders of Sevastopol crossed their harbor on a floating bridge. Behind them, on the southern side of the bay, lay a town that had been under bombardment for nearly a year. Earlier that day, French troops had seized the Malakhov Kurgan, the hill that dominated the Russian defenses, and the commanders had concluded that the position could no longer be held. Magazines were blown up, buildings burned, and what remained of the garrison, along with wounded men who could be moved, withdrew to the northern shore. The allied armies entered a ruin. Contemporaries on every side understood that something extraordinary had occurred: a siege of roughly eleven months, fought for a single naval base, had consumed enormous quantities of men, money, and attention in Russia, the Ottoman Empire, France, Britain, and Piedmont-Sardinia.

The war is remembered under the name of a peninsula, but it was neither confined to Crimea nor reducible to it. Fighting and strategic pressure extended from the lower Danube to eastern Anatolia and the Caucasus, from the Baltic and the White Sea to the Pacific coast of Kamchatka. It arose from a long-running international problem that contemporaries called the Eastern Question, the problem of how the European powers would manage the weakening, and the possible collapse, of Ottoman authority in southeastern Europe and the Black Sea region. Section 41.1 and Section 41.2 explain that background, because a war that began as a dispute among Russia, the Ottoman Empire, and the Christian churches of Jerusalem cannot be understood without it.

The chapter's central argument is about the Russian state. Under Nicholas I, as Chapter 38 showed, the empire had acquired an impressive reputation: a very large army, a sophisticated bureaucracy of codification and surveillance, a confident ideology that joined autocracy, Orthodoxy, and nationality, and a record of having helped to defeat Napoleon (Chapter 36) and to suppress revolution in Hungary in 1849. The Crimean War tested that reputation under conditions the system had not been designed for: a coalition war against opponents with steam-powered fleets, rifled weapons, and sea access to the theater of operations, fought at the edge of Russia's transport network. Russian soldiers and sailors fought with great endurance, and the defense of Sevastopol deserves to be understood as such. Yet the war also revealed that the ability to field large numbers of men was not the same as the ability to move, supply, equip, and command them.

Who experienced the war? Conscripted soldiers, serving nominal terms of twenty-five years that often meant long separation from home, bore its heaviest physical burden. Sailors of the Black Sea Fleet, whose ships were sunk to block the harbor, fought on land as infantry and gunners. Civilians in Sevastopol endured bombardment and evacuation. Women, including those of the new Sisters of Mercy, worked in overcrowded hospitals. Serfs, in provinces affected by the militia levy of 1855, drew their own conclusions about what service to the tsar might earn them. Crimean Tatar villagers, Ottoman soldiers, Balkan Christians, Georgian and Armenian communities along the Caucasian front, and British volunteer soldiers and French conscripts and volunteers all stood in different relationships to the same conflict. The chapter attempts to keep these experiences in view alongside the decisions of ministers and generals.

The evidence for the war is unusually rich and unusually varied. It includes diplomatic correspondence, treaties, military dispatches, memoirs, the reports and letters of the surgeon Nikolai Pirogov, the literary sketches of Lev Tolstoy, who served as an artillery officer at Sevastopol, the dispatches of war correspondents such as William Howard Russell of The Times, and the first systematic war photography. These sources do not agree with one another, and each carries the purposes of its author. A recurring task in this chapter is to ask who is describing the war, for whom, and with what interest in how it is remembered, a question that matters again in the Historical Memory discussion of Sevastopol in later Russian and Soviet commemoration.

The chapter also resists two simplifications. The first is the familiar formula that Russia lost because it was backward and had serfdom. Serfdom and the institutional structure it sustained mattered, but the war was lost by a particular empire against a particular coalition under particular strategic conditions, and Section 41.6 and the Historical Debate feature show how much explanatory work the word "backward" can obscure. The second simplification is the opposite one: that the war was a minor episode that changed little. Within a year of the Treaty of Paris, leading figures within the government itself were speaking openly of the need for fundamental change, and Chapter 42 follows the reforms that resulted.

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to explain how the Eastern Question produced a war that none of the principal governments entirely intended, why a Russo-Ottoman dispute became a European coalition war, what the siege of Sevastopol showed about both Russian resilience and Russian institutional limits, and how defeat converted a long-standing argument among officials, landowners, and intellectuals into a problem of state effectiveness and great-power standing.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain the Eastern Question as a multi-power problem involving Ottoman weakness, the Straits, the Balkans, and the Black Sea, rather than a bilateral Russo-Ottoman rivalry.
  2. Trace the development of the Russo-Ottoman relationship from the Treaty of Küçük Kaynarca to the 1840s, including the role of claims to protect Orthodox Christians.
  3. Analyze the origins of the war as a multicausal escalation in which the Holy Places dispute was a catalyst but not a sufficient explanation.
  4. Evaluate why Britain and France entered the war and why Austria's hostile neutrality undermined Nicholas I's diplomatic assumptions.
  5. Describe the course of the siege of Sevastopol and identify what it reveals about both Russian endurance and Russian systemic limitations.
  6. Assess the military, logistical, fiscal, and administrative weaknesses of the Russian state, connecting them to one another and to the social order of serfdom.
  7. Interpret the experiences of soldiers, sailors, medical personnel, civilians, serfs, and frontier populations during the war.
  8. Distinguish what Russia lost and what it did not lose under the Treaty of Paris of 1856.
  9. Explain why defeat generated pressure for reform without treating the Great Reforms themselves, which belong to Chapter 42, as inevitable.
  10. Evaluate competing historians' arguments about whether the war exposed Russian "backwardness" and how much it caused later reform.
  11. Connect the war to the broader European system, the Ottoman reform era, and the technology and medicine of mid-nineteenth-century warfare.
  12. Distinguish the historical events of 1853–1856 from later commemoration of Sevastopol and the Crimean War.

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did the weakening of Ottoman power create dangers and opportunities for every major European state, and not only for Russia?
  2. What assumptions about Britain, France, and Austria led Nicholas I to expect that he could press the Ottomans without provoking a coalition?
  3. Why is the Holy Places dispute better understood as a trigger than as a cause of the war?
  4. What did the siege of Sevastopol demonstrate about the strengths of the Russian army and navy, and what did it demonstrate about their limits?
  5. How are transport, arms, finance, administration, medicine, and recruitment connected to one another in the explanation of Russian defeat?
  6. How did the war look from the perspective of conscripts, nurses, serfs, Crimean Tatars, and Allied soldiers?
  7. What did the Treaty of Paris take from Russia, and what did it leave intact?
  8. How should historians weigh the Crimean War against earlier debates and projects when explaining the emergence of reform in the late 1850s?

Historical Context

The Crimean War must be read against the system described in Chapters 38 through 40. By the early 1850s, Nicholas I had governed for more than a quarter-century. His state rested on personal autocracy exercised through the His Majesty's Own Chancery, including the Third Section; on a bureaucracy that was large, formalistic, and thinly staffed in the provinces; on an army recruited by levy from the taxpaying population; and on a rural society in which the majority of peasants were either privately owned serfs, numbering in the low twenty millions, or state peasants subject to the treasury and its officials. Official Nationality, examined in Section 38.2, supplied the ideology. The revolutions of 1848 and Russia's role in suppressing the Hungarian rising of 1849 reinforced the tsar's self-image as the guardian of conservative order in Europe, and the period from 1848 to 1855 saw tightened censorship and suspicion of educated opinion, a period later called the "dismal seven years."

Russia's external position looked strong. The country had emerged from the Napoleonic Wars as the largest land power in Europe (Chapter 36), had expanded its Black Sea coast and annexed Crimea in the reign of Catherine II (Section 34.8), had defeated the Ottomans repeatedly, and had extended its position in the Caucasus (Chapter 39). The Black Sea ports, especially Odesa, had become channels for the grain exports on which southern landowners depended, and those exports passed through the Straits, the Bosphorus and the Dardanelles, which the Ottoman government controlled. A naval base at Sevastopol, founded in the 1780s, sheltered a Black Sea Fleet. Russia's strategic interest in the Straits was therefore commercial and military as well as symbolic.

The Ottoman Empire, by contrast, was widely described in European diplomacy as a failing state. Historians now treat that description with caution. The Ottoman government had embarked on the Tanzimat, a series of administrative, military, legal, and fiscal reforms inaugurated in 1839, and its armies in the 1850s were more capable than Russian planners assumed. Ottoman weakness was real in some respects, particularly its finances and its loss of control over some provinces and over the Balkan Christian populations who increasingly looked to outside powers. But "decline" was both a description and a political argument, and each European government used it for its own purposes. Britain and France had commercial and strategic interests in the Mediterranean and the Middle East, and Austria, whose Danubian and Balkan frontiers adjoined Ottoman territory, feared both Ottoman collapse and Russian advance.

Students should also understand the calendar and the terminology used in this chapter. Russia used the Julian calendar, which in the nineteenth century ran twelve days behind the Gregorian calendar used in Western Europe. Dates in this chapter are given in New Style (Gregorian) unless marked O.S. (Old Style), and the Old Style equivalent is supplied for major events whose Russian commemoration uses it. For example, the Battle of Sinope took place on 30 November 1853 N.S. (18 November O.S.). Place names follow conventional English forms; "Sevastopol" is used for the city in the period discussed, and "Kyiv," "Odesa," and other Ukrainian places follow current English standard forms unless a historical spelling is under discussion. The designation "Crimean War" is the English convention. In Russian usage the war is also called the Eastern War (Vostochnaya voina), and the Ottoman and other historiographies use different names, a reminder that the label itself prioritizes one theater.

The war also fell at a moment of transition in the technologies of war and communication. Steam propulsion was transforming navies, the rifled musket was beginning to replace the smoothbore, railways and the electric telegraph were changing the logistics of campaigns and the speed of news, anesthesia had recently been introduced into surgery, and a mass press could publish dispatches from the front within days. The Russian state was not ignorant of these developments, and its officers and engineers were well informed about them. The question the war posed was whether an empire built to a different template could adapt quickly enough, and at what social and political cost.

Section 41.1

The Eastern Question

The phrase "Eastern Question" was used by nineteenth-century European diplomats and later by historians to name a cluster of related problems arising from the declining territorial control of the Ottoman Empire. The empire still ruled, in the 1850s, much of the Balkan Peninsula, the Black Sea's southern and western coasts, Anatolia, the Arab provinces, and the approaches to the Caucasus. The questions were these: What would happen to Ottoman territories if the central government weakened further? Which power, or which combination of powers, would control the Straits connecting the Black Sea to the Mediterranean? How would the empire's large Christian populations, including Orthodox Greeks, Slavs, Romanians, and Armenians, relate to Ottoman authority and to outside states that claimed to speak for them? And how could any of these questions be answered without upsetting the balance of power in Europe that the settlement of 1815 had been designed to preserve?

The crucial point for students is that the Eastern Question was a multi-power problem. For Russia, the Black Sea coast and the Straits were strategic and commercial concerns: southern grain exports passed through the Bosphorus, and a hostile fleet in the Black Sea would threaten Odesa, Sevastopol, and the Caucasian coast. For Austria, the Danube and the Balkans were a security frontier, and the Habsburg monarchy contained its own large Slavic and Orthodox populations whose national aspirations might be encouraged by Russian influence. For Britain, the principal concerns were the security of the routes to India and the eastern Mediterranean, the balance of power in Europe, and the commercial position of British traders in Ottoman markets. For France, the Eastern Question intersected with a long tradition of French protection of Catholic interests in the Levant and, under Napoleon III, with a determination to restore French prestige. For the Ottomans themselves, it was a question of survival and reform, and Ottoman statesmen sought to play the powers off against one another.

Several earlier episodes had shaped the diplomatic environment. The Greek war of independence (1821–1829) had shown that Christian revolts within the Ottoman Empire could draw in the great powers; Russia, Britain, and France intervened jointly, and the Russo-Ottoman war that followed ended with the Treaty of Adrianople in 1829. The crises involving Muhammad Ali, the Ottoman governor of Egypt, whose forces defeated the sultan's armies in the 1830s, led to the Russo-Ottoman Treaty of Unkiar Skelessi of 1833, widely interpreted in Britain and France as giving Russia a privileged position at the Straits. The powers reacted by pressing for a collective arrangement, and the London Straits Convention of 1841 established that the Straits would be closed to foreign warships in peacetime. For Russia, the convention was double-edged: it protected the Black Sea from Western fleets, but it also replaced a bilateral arrangement with a regime that Britain and France had helped to write and could claim to enforce.

It is also important to avoid reading the Ottoman Empire's later reputation back into the 1850s. The phrase "sick man of Europe," associated with Nicholas I's remarks to the British ambassador in 1853, captured a widely held diplomatic assumption. But the Ottoman state was undergoing reforms, its provincial armies were being retrained, and its leaders did not regard themselves as awaiting partition. The Eastern Question was therefore partly a problem of real Ottoman weakness and partly a problem of European projection: powers acted on predictions of Ottoman collapse that, in the event, did not come true for another sixty years.

For this chapter, the lesson is that the Crimean War did not emerge from a simple bilateral feud between Russia and the Ottoman Empire. It emerged from a diplomatic system in which each power feared that another would gain disproportionate advantage from any change, and in which a local crisis could therefore escalate far beyond its origins.

Historical Vocabulary
Eastern Question

Definition: The diplomatic and strategic problem, as seen from Europe, of how to manage the weakening of Ottoman territorial control and the competing interests of the powers in southeastern Europe, the Black Sea, the Straits, and the eastern Mediterranean.

Historical context: The term came into common use in the late eighteenth and nineteenth centuries and applies especially to the period from the Russo-Ottoman wars of Catherine II to the First World War.

Why it matters: The expression can mislead because it frames the Ottoman Empire as an object of European decision. Students should treat it as a European diplomatic category, while remembering that Ottoman governments, Balkan communities, and Arab and Anatolian societies had their own agency and their own histories.

Section 41.2

Russia and the Ottoman Empire

The Russian relationship with the Ottoman Empire had been shaped by more than a century of war and expansion. Chapter 34 traced the wars of Catherine II, which brought Russia to the northern shore of the Black Sea and ended in the annexation of the Crimean Khanate in 1783 (Section 34.8). The Treaty of Küçük Kaynarca of 1774 had given Russia access to the Black Sea and the right to maintain a church in Constantinople, and its language concerning Russia's right to make representations on behalf of that church and its congregations was ambiguous. Later Russian governments interpreted these provisions expansively, as a basis for protecting Orthodox Christians throughout the empire; the Ottoman government and other European powers generally read them narrowly. The disagreement over what the treaty actually conveyed became one of the sources of the crisis of 1853.

Subsequent treaties moved the frontier and the balance of influence. The Treaty of Jassy (1792) and the Treaty of Bucharest (1812), which transferred Bessarabia to Russia, extended the Russian position toward the Danube. The Treaty of Adrianople (1829) gave Russia control of the mouths of the Danube and confirmed Russian influence in the Danubian Principalities of Moldavia and Wallachia, where the Organic Regulations of the 1830s, introduced under Russian occupation and the administration of Count Pavel Kiselyov, created a more regular governmental framework dominated by the local boyar elites. Kiselyov, notably, was the same official who later reorganized the administration of state peasants in Russia proper (Chapter 38), a reminder that imperial and domestic administration were connected.

Nicholas I's approach to the Ottoman Empire combined strategic prudence with ideological confidence. In the 1820s and 1830s, Russian policy had often favored a weak but intact Ottoman state over a collapse that might be exploited by rivals. By the early 1850s, the tsar's assessment appears to have shifted. In conversations with the British ambassador, Sir George Hamilton Seymour, in January and February 1853, he described the Ottoman Empire as a sick body whose collapse might be imminent and proposed that Russia and Britain agree in advance on how to handle it. The British government declined to discuss the partition of Ottoman territory. Historians differ on how far the tsar actually intended to dismember the empire and how far he sought a secure Russian predominance within a surviving Ottoman state; his statements were not always consistent, and what Seymour recorded was selectively interpreted by British politicians.

His confidence rested on several assumptions. The suppression of the Hungarian revolution in 1849 had, in his view, placed Austria in Russia's debt. Britain, divided between factions led by the cautious Lord Aberdeen, who headed the government from late 1852, and the more assertive Lord Palmerston, seemed unlikely to fight for the Ottoman Empire. France was ruled by Napoleon III, whom Nicholas regarded as a parvenu without legitimacy, and the Anglo-French rivalry of earlier decades suggested that the two would not cooperate. Each assumption contained an element of truth, and each proved wrong at a critical moment.

The ideological dimension also mattered. Official Nationality (Section 38.2) presented Orthodoxy as one of the pillars of the Russian state, and Orthodox solidarity with Ottoman Christians had resonance among the clergy, the Slavophile circles discussed in Section 40.4, and elements of the public. Yet students should avoid two oversimplifications. The war was not simply a religious crusade; Russian policy was also driven by strategic calculations about the Straits and the Black Sea, by commercial interests, and by the tsar's sense of Russia's rank. Nor was it simply cynical imperial aggression using religion as a pretext; the Russian government and many of its subjects sincerely held that Russia had obligations toward Orthodox Christians. Religion, strategy, prestige, and commerce operated together, and different actors weighted them differently.

The perspective of Ottoman Orthodox Christians was itself diverse. Many Balkan Christians welcomed the prospect of Russian support, and in Epirus and Thessaly in 1854 some Greek communities rose against Ottoman rule in hope of Russian help; the risings were suppressed. Others, including leaders in Serbia and the Romanian principalities, were wary of exchanging Ottoman suzerainty for Russian domination. Russian claims to protect them were thus received, within the Ottoman Empire, in different ways.

Section 41.3

Origins of the Crimean War

The immediate crisis began with a dispute over rights in the Christian Holy Places of Palestine, then Ottoman territory. Roman Catholic and Orthodox clergy, backed respectively by France and Russia, competed for privileges at sites in Jerusalem and Bethlehem, including control of keys, the right to repair a church roof, and the placement of a silver star in the Church of the Nativity. These questions were symbolically charged, involved long-standing treaty claims by both sides, and became political when Napoleon III, seeking Catholic support at home and a diplomatic success abroad, pressed the Ottomans in 1850–1852 to confirm French privileges. An Ottoman decision in 1852 that favored the Latin clergy angered the Russian government, which regarded it as a challenge to Orthodox rights.

The Holy Places dispute was not, however, a sufficient explanation of the war. It was a catalyst that exposed larger conflicts over prestige and influence. In February 1853 Nicholas I sent Prince Alexander Menshikov, an admiral and statesman with limited diplomatic experience and a reputation for abrasiveness, to Constantinople. Menshikov's mission had two parts: to secure the resolution of the Holy Places question on terms favorable to the Orthodox, and to obtain a formal agreement by which the Ottomans would guarantee the privileges of the Orthodox Church and its adherents in the empire, in effect confirming a Russian role as their protector. The second demand, which the Ottomans interpreted as a surrender of sovereignty over millions of subjects, was the heart of the crisis.

The Ottoman government, with encouragement from the British ambassador Stratford Canning, conceded much on the Holy Places but rejected the demand for a binding Russian-guaranteed agreement. Menshikov left Constantinople in May 1853. Nicholas responded by ordering the occupation of the Danubian Principalities, which Russian troops entered in July 1853, framing the move as a pledge to be held until the Ottomans accepted Russian terms. The occupation, however, struck the other powers as a violation of Ottoman territory and a step toward coercion, and it moved the crisis from negotiation to confrontation.

Diplomatic attempts to settle the dispute continued through the summer and autumn. Austria, with the other powers, drafted a compromise text, the Vienna Note, which Russia accepted. The Ottomans proposed amendments that Russia rejected, in part because Nesselrode's own interpretation of the unamended text, which implied a Russian right of intervention, surfaced and embarrassed the powers that had hoped the text was neutral. The episode illustrates how ambiguity in diplomatic language could harden into confrontation. In October 1853 the Ottoman government, supported by public opinion in Constantinople, demanded Russian evacuation of the Principalities and, when this was not met, declared war. Ottoman forces crossed the Danube, and fighting began on the Danubian and Caucasian fronts.

On 30 November 1853 N.S. (18 November O.S.), a Russian squadron under Admiral Pavel Nakhimov destroyed an Ottoman naval squadron at anchor in the harbor of Sinope on the Anatolian coast. The victory was tactically decisive and, in a conventional naval sense, legitimate, since the two countries were at war. But it was reported in British and French newspapers as a massacre of an anchored fleet, and the loss of life among the Ottoman crews, generally reckoned in the thousands, was described in language that inflamed public opinion. Sinope thus produced a strategic effect opposite to its military one: it strengthened the case in London and Paris for acting against Russia.

A multicausal analysis is therefore necessary. Long-term conditions included Ottoman weakness and European fear of Russian advantage at the Straits. Intermediate developments included Napoleon III's pursuit of prestige and Nicholas I's belief in Western disunity. The Holy Places dispute triggered the crisis, Menshikov's mission and the occupation of the Principalities escalated it, and Ottoman resistance, the failed Vienna Note, and Sinope turned it into war. Popular accounts that reduce the war to religion, or to Russian aggression alone, or to British and French manipulation, leave out parts of this sequence. Misjudgment, by Nicholas about British resolve, by the British and French governments about Russian intentions, and by all parties about escalation, also played a role.

Section 41.4

Britain and France Enter the War

Perhaps the most important diplomatic question about the war is why a Russo-Ottoman conflict became a European coalition war. The answer lies in the interests, domestic politics, and perceptions of the powers that intervened, and in the failure of Russian diplomacy to anticipate them.

British concerns were strategic and political. Successive governments had defined it as a vital interest that the Straits and Constantinople should not fall under the control of a single great power, in particular Russia, and the occupation of the Principalities and the destruction of the Ottoman squadron at Sinope appeared to confirm fears of Russian advance. British public opinion, stirred by the press and by long-standing hostility to Russian autocracy, which was reinforced by events such as the suppression of Hungary and Poland, strongly favored resistance. The coalition government of Lord Aberdeen, initially reluctant, was pushed by Palmerston and by public pressure toward confrontation. British leaders also had interests in the Baltic, where they feared Russian naval power, and in the Mediterranean and the routes to India.

France under Napoleon III had distinct motives. The emperor sought to break the diplomatic isolation imposed on France by the settlement of 1815, to secure the legitimacy of his regime through military prestige, and to cooperate with Britain in a way that would end the post-1815 pattern of anti-French coalitions. The Anglo-French alliance of 1854 was itself a significant diplomatic novelty. Both governments sent fleets into the Black Sea in January 1854, presented Russia with an ultimatum to withdraw from the Principalities in the spring, and declared war in late March 1854.

Austria's position was particularly important for the course of the war. Nicholas expected Austrian support or at least benevolent neutrality, given the Russian role in 1849. Instead, the Habsburg government, led by Foreign Minister Count Karl Buol, regarded the Russian occupation of the Principalities as a threat to Austria's own interests on the lower Danube and feared Russian influence among its Slavic and Orthodox subjects. In 1854 Austria demanded Russian withdrawal, concluded an agreement with the Ottomans allowing Austrian occupation of the Principalities after Russian forces left, and mobilized troops in Galicia and elsewhere along the Russian frontier without formally entering the war. This hostile neutrality forced Russia to keep large forces in the west, and in December 1855 an Austrian ultimatum was a major factor in persuading the Russian government to accept preliminary peace terms. Prussia remained neutral, so that Russia faced a coalition in the south and sea theaters and a potentially hostile Austria to the west.

The Ottoman Empire, finally, was a belligerent in its own right. Ottoman armies under Omar Pasha defended the Danube line and relieved Silistra in 1854 as Russian forces withdrew, and fought a separate campaign in the Caucasus. Piedmont-Sardinia joined the Allies in January 1855, hoping that participation would earn the goodwill of Britain and France for the cause of Italian unification, and sent a contingent to Crimea. The coalition was thus broader than the phrase "Britain and France" suggests.

The war's early phase on the Danube showed that Russia's position was weaker than the tsar had imagined. Allied forces landed at Varna on the Black Sea coast in the summer of 1854 and were ravaged by cholera; but Russian forces, under Austrian pressure and operating against a stubborn Ottoman defense, abandoned the Principalities in the summer of 1854, removing the original casus belli. The war continued because the Allied governments now sought to weaken Russian power in the Black Sea, and the most visible way to do so was to take Sevastopol. In August 1854 the Allied governments set out their conditions in the Four Points, which included replacing the Russian protectorate over the Principalities with a collective guarantee, free navigation of the Danube, revision of the Straits Convention in the interest of the European balance, and an end to Russia's claim to protect Orthodox subjects of the sultan. Russia's failure to accept these terms led to the Allied decision to invade Crimea.

Section 41.5

The Siege of Sevastopol

In September 1854 an Allied expeditionary force of British, French, and Ottoman troops landed north of Sevastopol, near Evpatoria, on the western coast of Crimea. The Allied commanders, Lord Raglan for Britain and Marshal Saint-Arnaud, soon succeeded by General François Canrobert, for France, intended to seize the naval base quickly, as a bargaining counter and as the means to end Russian naval power in the Black Sea. Sevastopol was well protected on the sea side by coastal batteries, but its land defenses, to the south and east, were weak. The Russian commander in Crimea, Prince Menshikov, concentrated his forces to meet the Allies on the Alma River. On 20 September 1854 N.S. (8 September O.S.), Allied infantry, whose rifled muskets gave them superiority at range, stormed the heights and forced the Russians to retreat. Menshikov withdrew his field army into the interior to protect communications with the rest of Russia, leaving the city to be defended by a garrison of sailors, marines, and what land troops could be gathered.

The defense of the city was organized by Admiral Vladimir Kornilov, Admiral Pavel Nakhimov, and the engineer Eduard Totleben. Several Black Sea Fleet ships of the line, which could not compete with Allied steam vessels and whose crews were needed ashore, were scuttled across the harbor entrance to block it. Their guns and their sailors went to the bastions. Totleben, who had experience in fortification, directed the construction of earthworks that improved quickly in the weeks when the Allies hesitated to assault, and that turned the unfinished defenses into a formidable system, with the Malakhov Kurgan as the key position on the eastern side. The Allied armies, rather than storming a weakly defended town, were drawn into a siege, bringing up heavy guns and establishing a base at Balaklava, the small harbor to the south.

The first Allied bombardment began in mid-October 1854 and Kornilov was mortally wounded on its first day. Fighting around the siege lines followed a pattern that persisted through the winter. On 25 October 1854 N.S. (13 October O.S.), a Russian attack on the Allied base at Balaklava was repulsed, in an engagement remembered in Britain for the disastrous charge of the Light Brigade. On 5 November 1854 N.S. (24 October O.S.), the Russians attacked the Allied right flank at Inkerman in heavy fog. Despite numerical advantages and bold local fighting, Russian columns attacked piecemeal and were defeated, with heavy casualties on both sides; the engagement showed the limits of Russian tactical command, as well as the stubbornness of the defenders. The winter of 1854–1855 imposed severe hardships on both armies. The British, whose supply system collapsed, suffered particularly from disease and exposure, and Russian forces suffered because supplies, reinforcements, and the wounded all had to move over long, poor roads.

Nicholas I died on 2 March 1855 N.S. (18 February O.S.), after a brief illness following the winter. His death occurred as the siege continued and as news of reversals, including a failed Russian attack on Evpatoria in February, reached St. Petersburg. Rumors of suicide circulated, but the evidence points to illness, and historians generally treat them as rumor rather than established fact. Menshikov was replaced as commander by Prince Mikhail Gorchakov, whose own conduct of the campaign has also been criticized. Alexander II, the new emperor, announced that the war would continue.

The Allied armies bombarded the city repeatedly in 1855, in the spring, in June, and in August and early September. Admiral Istomin was killed in March; Nakhimov was fatally wounded in the summer. The Allied assaults of June failed, but each bombardment destroyed Russian defenses and killed defenders faster than they could be replaced. A Russian attempt on 16 August 1855 N.S. (4 August O.S.) to relieve the city by attacking Allied positions on the Chernaya River failed. On 8 September 1855 N.S. (27 August O.S.), after a heavy bombardment, French forces seized the Malakhov Kurgan, which the Russians were unable to retake. The Russian command ordered the evacuation of the southern side, and the garrison crossed the bay to the north.

Sevastopol demonstrates Russian resilience and Russian systemic limitations at the same time. The garrison, composed of conscript infantry, sailors, and artillerymen led by a capable, if sometimes divided, group of officers, defended the city against superior firepower for almost a year, and it did so while the Allies suffered their own hardships. Yet the siege also showed that Russian reinforcement depended on slow overland transport, that the field army could neither relieve the city nor prevent the Allies from sustaining the siege by sea, that the Black Sea Fleet could not contest naval control, and that casualties, many of them from disease and many among the wounded who could not be evacuated efficiently, were enormous. The human cost of the siege is difficult to state precisely; estimates of Russian losses vary, and total deaths on all sides during the war, mostly from disease, are commonly estimated in the hundreds of thousands. Students should treat any precise figure with caution and ask what sources and definitions produced it.

The fall of the southern side did not end the war immediately. Allied forces captured the fortress of Kinburn at the mouth of the Dnieper in October 1855, using armored floating batteries, a notable technological development. In the Caucasus, Russian forces under General Nikolai Muravyov took the Ottoman fortress of Kars in November 1855 after a long siege, which gave Russia a bargaining counter in the negotiations that followed. The Russian army in Crimea remained intact in the north, and the war's last months were a period of exhaustion on both sides rather than of Russian collapse.

Historical Vocabulary
Recruit Levy (rekrutchina)

Definition: The system of military recruitment, introduced under Peter I and used through the mid-nineteenth century, by which the government periodically demanded a quota of men from the taxpaying population, mainly serfs and townspeople. Nobles were exempt, and the selection of recruits within a village or estate was made by the landlord or by the community.

Historical context: Under Nicholas I, soldiers served terms of twenty-five years, which in practice often meant long separation from home. Reforms of 1834 allowed long-serving soldiers to be placed on indefinite leave while remaining liable to recall, creating a reserve of trained men. The system produced a professional army and a distinct soldier caste, but its reserve was limited in size and inflexible, and it proved too small for a prolonged coalition war.

Why it matters: The levy connected the army to serfdom. Every recruit was taken from a household and community, and the system provided manpower at the expense of economic productivity and social stability. It cannot be compared directly with the conscription systems of Western states without attention to these social conditions.

Section 41.6

Military and Logistical Weaknesses

Russia in 1853 possessed one of the largest armies in Europe, numbering on paper a million men or more, and a population several times that of Britain or France. Why, then, did it struggle to defeat or even to hold off two industrial maritime powers, an Ottoman army, and a smaller Sardinian contingent? The answer requires unpacking the word "backwardness" into identifiable and connected problems. This section treats the problems under six headings and then asks how they reinforced one another.

Recruitment, Service, and Reserves

The recruit levy and the nominal twenty-five-year term produced a long-serving army of considerable discipline and individual toughness. Nicholas I's reforms of 1834 had introduced indefinite leave for long-serving soldiers, who remained liable to recall, so Russia did possess a trained reserve of perhaps a couple of hundred thousand men, and most of them were mobilized during the war. The weakness was that this pool was too small and too inflexible for a major, prolonged coalition war. It was rapidly absorbed, and the government then had to rely on additional recruit levies and, in 1855, a militia (opolchenie) of poorly trained men. A large part of the army was also tied down in places other than the war zone: in Poland and the western provinces, where Austria's attitude and the possibility of Polish unrest required forces to be kept in place; in the Caucasus, where the long war discussed in Chapter 39 continued; and along the Baltic coast and Finland, where Allied fleets threatened. The Russian field army in Crimea therefore represented only a portion of the empire's military strength, and numerical superiority on paper did not translate into numerical superiority in the theater.

Weapons and Naval Technology

The Allied armies carried rifled muskets, notably the British Enfield and French Minié-type weapons, which had longer effective range and greater accuracy than the smoothbore muskets that equipped most Russian infantry. Only a portion of Russian infantry had rifled arms. The difference, evident at the Alma and at Inkerman, meant that Russian columns advancing in close formation suffered heavily before they could close with their opponents. Naval technology mattered as well. Russia's Black Sea Fleet consisted largely of sailing ships of the line, which proved unable to oppose Allied steam-powered warships. At Sinope the Russian fleet had defeated a weaker Ottoman force, but the arrival of Allied steam warships removed the possibility of Russian control of the sea. Russian industry could produce guns and ships, but it did so in limited quantities, using older technologies and facing shortages of high-quality iron, steam engines, and skilled workers. These were not unique to Russia; they were characteristic of an economy that, in the 1850s, was beginning to industrialize (Section 38.7) but remained overwhelmingly agrarian.

Transport and Supply

The most decisive disadvantage was transportation. Russia in 1853 had very few railways: a short line from St. Petersburg to Tsarskoe Selo, the St. Petersburg–Moscow line completed in 1851, and some lines in the western provinces. No railway linked Moscow, or any major base of supply, to the south. Reinforcements, ammunition, and food had to be hauled in carts over earthen roads that became impassable in the spring thaw and autumn rains, and across the steppe to Crimea, where the Perekop isthmus formed a bottleneck. It could take weeks or months for supplies to travel from central Russia to Sevastopol, and the animals that drew the carts consumed much of what they carried. The Allies, by contrast, had sea transport. Even though their logistical performance was often poor, as the British experience in the winter of 1854–1855 showed, they could land supplies directly at Balaklava and Kamiesch, and in 1855 they built a railway from Balaklava to the siege lines and laid a submarine telegraph cable to link the army with London and Paris. The Russian command, with communications measured in days or weeks, could not coordinate operations in real time. The history of the war's operations is, in important respects, a history of lines of communication.

Command, Administration, and Corruption

The Russian command structure was hampered by rigid hierarchies and by dependence on the personal confidence of the emperor. Menshikov, who held the offices of naval minister and commander in Crimea, and whose performance in both diplomacy and command was criticized, owed his position to court favor rather than to demonstrated competence. Orders from St. Petersburg could conflict with local judgments, and initiative was discouraged. The supply departments of the army (the commissariat) were notorious for inefficiency and, according to many contemporary and later accounts, for embezzlement; contractors and officials profited from deliveries of substandard food, clothing, and equipment. The extent of corruption is difficult to measure, since the evidence comes largely from critics, memoirists, and later reformers with reasons to emphasize it, but the consistency of the reports across independent witnesses suggests that it was a real and serious problem. Corruption, like the other weaknesses, was linked to the structure of the administration: officials were poorly paid, supervised mainly by superiors with similar incentives, and subject to few outside checks, a pattern that Chapter 38 described in the context of the Nicholas I bureaucracy.

Finance

The war strained state finances. The Russian treasury relied on a limited tax base, on state monopolies and customs revenues, and on borrowing, and its paper currency was backed by limited reserves. Wartime expenditure increased rapidly while the blockade reduced foreign trade and customs receipts, and the government resorted to increased issues of paper money and to domestic and foreign borrowing on difficult terms. The financial strain, which became severe by 1855, meant that the state could not indefinitely sustain the army at its wartime level, and it supported the argument within the government that peace was necessary.

Medicine and Sanitation

Military medicine was a further area of weakness. In all armies of the period, disease killed more soldiers than combat did. But the Russian medical service, under-resourced and short of trained personnel, struggled to deal with the numbers of wounded and sick, and conditions in hospitals were frequently appalling. The surgeon Nikolai Pirogov's reports, discussed in the Primary Source Window and in Section 41.7, document the shortages of dressings and staff, the overcrowding, the long journey to hospitals in Simferopol and beyond, and the spread of infection. Medicine was also an area of innovation, and Pirogov's use of anesthesia and of the sorting of casualties by severity of need reflected advances that the war accelerated.

How the Weaknesses Reinforced One Another

These problems formed a system. Poor transport meant that reinforcements and supplies arrived late, which magnified the effects of inadequate commissariat arrangements and corruption. A long-service system with a reserve too small for a major war was difficult to expand quickly, and the levy placed the burden on peasant households in ways that undermined agricultural production and heightened discontent. A limited industrial base and constrained finances reduced the capacity to re-equip the army with new weapons. Weak administration and the discouragement of initiative reduced the capacity to correct problems as they appeared. Underlying many of these were social structures: serfdom tied the majority of the population to landlords and village communities, limited mobility and the development of a free labor market, reduced the incentives for investment in transport and industry, and shaped the army's recruitment. Serfdom was therefore part of the explanation, but as part of a broader institutional system, not as a single cause.

Two further cautions are needed. First, Britain and France should not be treated as models of efficiency. The British army's administrative breakdown in the winter of 1854–1855, the collapse of its supply and medical systems, and the outrage that followed contributed to the fall of the Aberdeen government and to later British military reform. French forces suffered very high losses to disease. The Allies succeeded in part because they had sea access, a concentrated theater near their bases, and the ability to sustain the siege despite their own difficulties. Second, the Russian army was not incompetent: its defense of Sevastopol, its capture of Kars, and its stubborn fighting at several points show that the weaknesses were institutional and structural, rather than evidence of lack of courage or skill among soldiers and many officers. The war was lost, therefore, not because Russia was simply "backward," but because a particular configuration of political, social, economic, and technological conditions limited its ability to mobilize its large resources in the face of a particular coalition in a particular theater.

Section 41.7

Society and the Experience of War

The war's burden fell unevenly on Russian society. The most heavily affected were the conscripts, drawn from the serf and state peasantry and from the lower strata of the towns, and the sailors of the Black Sea Fleet. Their experience is difficult to recover because few left written accounts. Officers' memoirs, official reports, and the literary sketches of Tolstoy provide indirect evidence, as do the occasional letters and later recollections of soldiers. What emerges is a picture of men who endured shelling, cold, hunger, and disease with a stoicism that observers, Russian and foreign, commented on. Tolstoy's Sevastopol Sketches, published in the journal Sovremennik in 1855 and 1856, portrayed both the courage of ordinary defenders and the vanity, fear, and ambition of officers, and the censorship of the time modified some passages; the work was widely read and shaped later images of the siege, but it is literature informed by observation rather than a neutral record.

Sevastopol's civilian population, which before the siege included sailors' families, craftsmen, dockyard workers, and officials, experienced bombardment, flight, and loss of homes. Women and children were evacuated or took shelter in the northern part of the city, and some women provided food, water, and care to soldiers on the bastions. The best-known example, "Dasha of Sevastopol" (Daria Mikhailova), is a figure whom later commemoration celebrated, and the extent to which her story has been embellished is a reminder to distinguish documentary evidence from patriotic memory.

The war also produced an unprecedented medical and nursing effort. Nikolai Pirogov, a leading surgeon and anatomist, traveled to Sevastopol in late 1854 and worked in the city's hospitals. He organized the sorting of wounded men by the severity of injury, advocated the use of anesthesia in field surgery, and is credited with using plaster casts to immobilize fractures in the field. He also wrote critically about the administrative disorder, shortages, and unsanitary conditions he found. Grand Duchess Elena Pavlovna founded the Holy Cross Community of Sisters of Mercy in late 1854, and groups of women volunteers traveled to Crimea to nurse the wounded. Their work was among the first organized participation of women from different social strata in a public effort of this kind in Russia, and it contributed to wider debates about women's education and public roles in the following decade. British nursing efforts associated with Florence Nightingale at Scutari, and the work of Mary Seacole, belong to the same wider pattern of wartime medical change, but the Russian experience should be understood on its own terms.

Beyond the front, the war had domestic social consequences. The recruit levies and requisitions for transport and supply affected peasant households across central and southern Russia, and the strain of providing carts and draft animals was felt especially along the roads to Crimea. In 1855 the government called up a militia in a number of provinces. In some regions, rumors circulated among serfs that those who joined the militia or the Cossack forces would be freed, a belief with precedents in Russian history, and in the Kyiv region in 1855 these rumors contributed to a peasant movement in which villagers sought to enroll in what they understood as a Cossack levy. The authorities suppressed it. The episode shows both the readiness of some peasants to link service to the state with freedom and the government's fear of the social consequences of mobilization.

Educated society reacted in several ways. Many Slavophile and conservative writers supported the war as a defense of Orthodox and Slavic interests, and public patriotism was strong, particularly after the Allied landing. At the same time, the censorship regime that had tightened after 1848 limited the discussion of the conduct of the war, and many officials and landowners were privately critical of the government's management. As news of defeats and of administrative failures reached the capitals, criticism grew, and by 1855 many writers and officials were speaking more openly of the weaknesses of the state. The Russian press, constrained as it was, was far less able than the British press to publicize the conditions of the army, a contrast illustrated by the effect of William Howard Russell's reports in The Times and the photographs of Roger Fenton on British opinion.

Frontier populations were also affected. In Crimea, Crimean Tatar villagers, who had lived under Russian rule since 1783, were drawn into the conflict by the movement of armies, requisitions, and in some cases contact with the Allies. Russian authorities suspected disloyalty, and the war's aftermath contributed to Tatar emigration to the Ottoman Empire in the following years, a process that continued into the 1860s and whose scale has been estimated in various ways. Students should treat the causes of the emigration as multiple, including land policies, religious and cultural pressures, and the wartime climate of suspicion, rather than attributing it to one factor. The Allies, for their part, relied on local labor and purchased supplies, and relations between occupiers and local people varied.

Section 41.8

The Treaty of Paris

Peace negotiations were possible after the fall of Sevastopol, the death of Nicholas I, and the pressure of Austria. In December 1855 Austria presented Russia with an ultimatum based on terms resembling the Four Points, with the threat that Vienna would join the Allies if they were rejected. After deliberation within the Russian government, in which Alexander II and his advisers weighed the exhaustion of the treasury, the military situation, and the danger of an Austrian entry, the Russian government accepted preliminary terms in January 1856. A congress of the powers opened in Paris in late February 1856, with representatives of Britain, France, Austria, Russia, Piedmont-Sardinia, and the Ottoman Empire, and Prussia admitted later. The Treaty of Paris was signed on 30 March 1856 N.S. (18 March O.S.). Russia was represented by Count Alexei Orlov and Baron Philipp Brunnov.

The principal provisions affected Russia's position in the Black Sea and on the Danube. The Black Sea was neutralized: it was to be closed to warships of all nations, with exceptions for a small number of light vessels, and Russia and the Ottoman Empire agreed not to maintain naval arsenals or large fleets on its coasts. This was the most humiliating provision for Russia, as it removed the Black Sea Fleet and the naval base of Sevastopol from their strategic function. Russia ceded a strip of southern Bessarabia at the mouth of the Danube to Moldavia, depriving it of direct access to the river, and returned Kars to the Ottomans in exchange for the Allied restoration of Sevastopol and other captured places. The Danubian Principalities, which had been under a Russian protectorate, were placed under the collective guarantee of the powers while remaining under Ottoman suzerainty, an arrangement that in the following years allowed Moldavia and Wallachia to move toward unification. Freedom of navigation on the Danube was affirmed. The Ottoman Empire was admitted to the European concert, and the powers agreed to respect its independence and territorial integrity; Britain, France, and Austria later signed a separate guarantee. The Russian claim to a special protective role over Ottoman Orthodox Christians was set aside. The sultan communicated his reform decree in favor of his Christian subjects to the powers, but Article IX explicitly denied them any right to intervene, either collectively or separately, in his relations with his subjects or in Ottoman internal administration. A separate declaration of the congress on maritime law abolished privateering and established rules on neutral goods and blockades.

Students should also understand what Russia did not lose. It lost no major territory in Europe or the Caucasus beyond the small Bessarabian strip, paid no indemnity, retained its armies, and continued to be a great power in the European system. Its territorial integrity was not at issue. The settlement was a serious strategic setback because it neutralized the Black Sea, ended the claim to a protectorate, and showed the limits of Russian influence, but it did not dismember or destroy the empire. The diplomatic humiliation was real, and it was felt strongly in Russian elite opinion, but it was also temporary: Alexander Gorchakov, who became foreign minister in 1856, adopted a policy of restraint in Europe while rebuilding Russia's strength, which he described, in a circular of 1856, as a recollection of resources rather than a sulking withdrawal. In 1870–1871, taking advantage of the Franco-Prussian War, Russia repudiated the Black Sea clauses, and the powers accepted the change at a conference in London.

The settlement also illustrates how the war reshaped the international system. The collective guarantee of the Ottoman Empire replaced the bilateral arrangements of earlier decades; the Concert of Europe, which had been unable to prevent the crisis, was reconstituted in a different form; and the Holy Alliance system of conservative cooperation among Russia, Austria, and Prussia, which Nicholas had upheld, was effectively ended by Austria's conduct. Russian foreign policy in the following decades, discussed in later chapters, cannot be understood without this realignment.

Section 41.9

Defeat and the Demand for Reform

Alexander II inherited both the war and its lessons. Born in 1818, he had been prepared for rule by tutors including the poet Vasily Zhukovsky, had served in various state bodies, and had been closely associated with his father's regime. He was not a reformer by temperament or training, and the sources do not support the view that defeat converted him at once into a liberal. But within months of his accession, the argument that the war had exposed the need for change was widely made, inside and outside government. The task of this section is to explain how defeat transformed an existing discussion into a pressing demand, and then to stop before the reforms themselves, which are the subject of Chapter 42.

Discussion of the problems of serfdom did not begin with the Crimean War. Nicholas I himself had established secret committees to consider the peasant question; Count Kiselyov's reorganization of the state peasantry in the late 1830s and early 1840s had shown that the government could restructure peasant administration; the inventory regulations in the western provinces in the late 1840s had tried to define obligations; and educated Russians had debated emancipation for decades (Chapters 38 and 40). Landowners were divided, many opposing change and others recognizing that the system's economic efficiency was in doubt. What the war changed was the context: it provided evidence, visible to the court and officials, that the existing system was failing in the most important test a great power could face, and it removed the confidence of the previous generation that Russia's strength was secure.

Several connected problems stood out. The first was serfdom and the rural order. The war's levies, requisitions, and rumors of freedom exposed the dependence of the army on the serf population and the instability that mobilization could produce. Critics argued that a society in which most peasants were bound to landlords or to the state could neither generate the economic development needed to sustain a modern army nor command the loyalty of its soldiers. The second was military recruitment and organization. The long-service system produced a reserve structure too small and inflexible for the demands of a major coalition war, and the army's structure and equipment needed revision. The third was transport and communication. The absence of railways in the south had been a decisive disadvantage, and in the following years the government encouraged railway construction, a development taken up in Chapter 44. The fourth was finance, because the war had strained the treasury and shown the need for fiscal reform and for stable credit. The fifth was local administration and the courts, whose weaknesses and corruption had been revealed by the war's demands. The sixth was education and the supply of trained officials, officers, and technical specialists. The seventh was the censorship regime, which had prevented the open discussion of failings, and whose relaxation after 1855 was part of the new climate.

These problems were interconnected. Reform of the army required changes to the recruitment system, which depended on the status of peasants; the status of peasants required decisions about land, landowners' rights, and compensation, which depended on finance and local administration; administration required educated personnel and a functioning legal system. A reform in one area would therefore necessarily affect others, which helps explain why the reform program of Alexander's reign, examined in Chapter 42, was so extensive.

The immediate signals were visible in 1856. In March of that year, in a speech to the Moscow nobility, Alexander II reportedly said that it was better to begin to abolish serfdom from above than to wait until it began to abolish itself from below. The wording is known through reports of the speech and is paraphrased here rather than quoted. In the same year, the coronation manifesto granted an amnesty to the Decembrists and others, the strictest controls on foreign travel and the press were eased, and discussion of the peasant question was permitted to become more open. In January 1857 a Secret Committee on the peasant question was established, the first step in the process traced in Chapter 42. Publications such as Alexander Herzen's Kolokol, issued from London, reached Russian readers, and the intellectual currents discussed in Chapter 40 acquired an urgency they had previously lacked.

It would be a mistake to conclude that the war made reform inevitable. Many in the nobility and in the bureaucracy opposed fundamental change, and the course of the reform program was shaped by conflicts, compromises, and choices that were not determined in advance. Nor would it be accurate to say that the war was the sole cause of the Great Reforms, since the economic limits of serf agriculture, long-standing concerns among officials, intellectual criticism, and fear of peasant unrest all contributed. The war's importance lay in making the costs of inaction visible and in shifting the balance of argument within the elite. The Historical Debate feature examines how historians weigh these causes.

Creator: Nikolai Ivanovich Pirogov (1810–1881), surgeon and anatomist · Date: late 1854–1855 · Type: Private letters and official reports, later published in collections under titles rendered in English as Sevastopol Letters

Context: Pirogov, a leading Russian surgeon, traveled to the Crimean theater in late 1854 and worked in the military hospitals of Sevastopol and Simferopol. He wrote to colleagues, to patrons such as Grand Duchess Elena Pavlovna, and to officials about what he saw. His letters and reports were circulated in his lifetime in part and published more fully later. The exact wording of individual passages varies among editions, so what follows is a paraphrase of the general content of his criticisms, not a quotation.

Paraphrase of the general sense (not a verbatim quotation):

Pirogov described hospitals overwhelmed by wounded men, many lying on straw with inadequate dressings and bedding, and he reported that wounded soldiers were transported long distances over rough roads, which worsened their condition and spread infection. He argued that the sorting of casualties by the urgency of their injuries, and the use of anesthesia and immobilizing casts, improved outcomes, but he also concluded that disorganization, divided responsibility, and the inattention of administrators did as much harm as the shortage of surgical skill.

Analysis: The source is valuable because it comes from an expert observer who recorded specific shortcomings and who combined clinical authority with willingness to criticize the administration. It provides evidence for the connection between medicine, transport, and administration discussed in Section 41.6. It also demonstrates innovation, since Pirogov was not simply a critic but a practitioner who introduced methods that were later widely adopted. Its limitations are equally important. Pirogov was a reformer with an agenda, writing to audiences whose support he needed; he focused on the hospitals he knew, so his observations cannot be generalized mechanically to every medical facility; and the accounts were later edited and published in a political climate in which criticism of the old regime was encouraged. The perspectives of ordinary patients, orderlies, and local civilians are largely absent.

Historical Debate

Did the Crimean War Expose Russian Backwardness?

The question: Was Russia's defeat evidence of a deep structural inferiority to the industrializing Western powers, rooted in serfdom and a pre-industrial economy, or was it the result of particular strategic and diplomatic circumstances, such that "backwardness" is an overstatement?

Interpretation A: Structural inferiority. Many Soviet and Western historians, including the Soviet historian Evgeny Tarle in his study of the war, have emphasized that the war revealed the limits of a serf-based economy and army. They point to the absence of railways, the limited industrial base, the reliance on smoothbore weapons and sailing ships, the weakness of finance, and the inability of the administration to correct its failures. On this view, the defeat was a symptom of social and economic structures that required fundamental reform, and the Great Reforms were a response to demonstrated inadequacy.
Interpretation B: Particular circumstances. Other scholars, drawing on diplomatic and military history, stress contingency and circumstance. Work on the war's origins, such as David Goldfrank's study, emphasizes that the crisis was not inevitable and that the participants' domestic concerns shaped it, while naval and strategic studies, such as Andrew Lambert's on British grand strategy, stress that Russia was defeated by a coalition with naval control, a concentrated theater near its bases, and the added pressure of Austria. They note that Russia held Kars and its army remained intact, that the Allies suffered serious breakdowns of their own, and that the settlement left Russia a great power. On this view, labeling the result as the verdict of backwardness reads later reform rhetoric back into the war.
Interpretation C: Catalyst for existing debates. A third approach, reflected in much work on the reform era, holds that the war's importance was less in proving a diagnosis than in changing the political conditions under which an existing diagnosis could be acted on. Reform arguments predated the war, but defeat altered their persuasiveness within the elite.

Evidence: Historians use diplomatic archives, military dispatches, statistics on railways, production, and finance, memoirs and letters, official reports on the army, and comparative evidence about the Allied armies' performance.

Points of agreement: Scholars broadly agree that Russia's transport network, supply system, and weapons were disadvantages; that Russian soldiers and sailors fought with great endurance; that the defeat damaged the confidence of the elite; and that it contributed to the climate in which reform became possible.

Points of disagreement: Historians differ about how far the Russian economy and army were generally inferior as opposed to disadvantaged in this particular war, about how much weight to give diplomacy and coalition politics, and about how much the war itself, as opposed to longer-term pressures, caused the Great Reforms.

Why it matters: The answer affects how one explains the Great Reforms, how one reads Russian history between 1815 and 1917, and whether one treats "backwardness" as an explanation or as a label that needs explanation.

Student task: Choose two features of the Russian war effort discussed in Section 41.6 and decide, using evidence from this chapter, whether each supports Interpretation A, B, or C, and why.

Multiple Perspectives

Experiencing the War

A conscripted soldier or sailor: Taken from a village by the recruit levy, often years or decades earlier, the Russian soldier at Sevastopol had little say in the war's aims. His experience was shaped by the discipline of the army, the demands of the siege, the shortage of food and medical care, and the possibility of wounds, disease, and death. Evidence about his views is indirect, coming mostly from officers, writers, and administrators, and it should be read with this limitation in mind.
A nurse or medical worker: For women of the Sisters of Mercy and for surgeons such as Pirogov, the war was experienced through the hospital, where they dealt with overcrowding, infection, and shortages. For many of the women, the work was a new form of public service, and it introduced them to hierarchies and administrative practices with which they sometimes clashed.
A serf-owning landowner and a serf household: Landowners provided recruits, supplies, and in some provinces militia contingents, and many supported the war on patriotic grounds. Serf families bore the loss of men and the burden of requisitions, and some believed that service might bring freedom. The same mobilization thus produced loyalty, anxiety, and, in places, unrest.
A Crimean Tatar villager or an Allied soldier: For Crimean Tatar villagers, the war brought armies, requisitions, and suspicion from Russian authorities, with long-term consequences for emigration. For British and French soldiers, the war meant disease, uneven supply, and exposure to the press and public attention at home. Their experience, like that of Russian soldiers, illustrates that war's hardships were not confined to one side.

These perspectives are reconstructed from historical evidence, not invented first-person accounts. They show why a single narrative, whether of imperial diplomacy or of national heroism, cannot capture the war.

Cause and Effect

From the Eastern Question to Reform Pressure

Long-Term Conditions

Ottoman weakness and reform; the Straits and Black Sea trade; Russian claims to protect Orthodox Christians; the post-1815 European system; an army and state shaped by serfdom, long-term conscription, limited rail transport, and a restricted industrial base.

Intermediate Developments

Napoleon III's pursuit of prestige; Nicholas I's confidence in Austrian gratitude and British division; the Holy Places dispute; Menshikov's mission; the Russian occupation of the Principalities; Ottoman resistance and the failed Vienna Note.

Short-Term Triggers

Ottoman declaration of war in October 1853; Sinope in November 1853; the Anglo-French fleets' entry into the Black Sea; the Anglo-French declaration of war in March 1854; Austria's pressure on the Principalities.

Event or Transformation

The Allied landing in Crimea and the siege of Sevastopol, in which Russian endurance met Allied sea power, rifled weapons, and a Russian logistical system that could not sustain the field army or relieve the city.

Immediate Consequences

The fall of Sevastopol's southern side; the death of Nicholas I; the Treaty of Paris and the neutralization of the Black Sea; Russian diplomatic isolation; fiscal strain.

Long-Term Consequences

Reorientation of Russian foreign policy; collective European guarantees for the Ottoman Empire; the shift of elite opinion toward reform, leading to the process examined in Chapter 42.

Historical complexity: No single factor is sufficient. Without Ottoman resistance, Western intervention, Austrian pressure, and Russian logistical limits, the war would not have taken the course it did, and without earlier debates among officials and intellectuals, defeat would not have translated into reform in the way it did.

Continuity and Change Over Time

The Post-1815 Military Reputation and the Reality of 1853–1856

What Changed?

  • Russia's reputation as the preeminent land power of Europe, built on the victory over Napoleon (Chapter 36) and reinforced in 1849, was damaged, and the Black Sea clauses ended its naval presence there.
  • Confidence in the permanence of conservative alliance with Austria collapsed after Austria's hostile neutrality.
  • The assumption within government that the existing social order could support great-power competition came under challenge.

What Remained?

  • The autocracy, the serf-based rural order, and the army's recruitment system persisted until reforms were enacted, and some of their features outlasted the reforms.
  • Russia remained a very large state with extensive military and demographic resources, and a recognized great power.
  • Russian interest in the Balkans, the Straits, and the Black Sea continued, though in a different diplomatic context.

Pace and experience of change: The change in elite opinion was rapid, occurring within months of defeat, while institutional change was gradual and contested. Peasant soldiers and their families experienced the war's burdens most directly, while court and bureaucratic circles experienced the change as a loss of confidence. The pattern matters because it shows that a single event can accelerate change without producing it alone, and that a change of ruler did not by itself transform society.

Everyday Life and Social History

Siege and Field Hospital

For defenders and civilians in Sevastopol, daily life during the siege was organized around the rhythms of bombardment. Soldiers and sailors worked in shifts on the bastions, repairing earthworks at night, and rested in dugouts and shelters. Food was monotonous and sometimes scarce, water had to be fetched under fire, and cold and damp were persistent hardships in winter. Civilians, many of them the families of sailors, sheltered in cellars or fled to the northern side. Some women carried food and water to the bastions, washed clothing, and tended the wounded, as later accounts and some contemporary reports indicate, though the number and role of such women are difficult to quantify.

Medical care was the central point at which the siege's suffering became visible. Wounded men were carried to dressing stations near the front, where surgeons performed amputations and other operations, often in crowded rooms. Those who survived were moved, frequently in carts over poor roads, to larger hospitals further from the front, where overcrowding, a shortage of linen and dressings, and the spread of typhus, dysentery, and other diseases claimed many lives. Pirogov's methods of triage and the use of anesthesia and casts improved some outcomes, and the Sisters of Mercy introduced a more systematic approach to nursing care, but conditions remained harsh. As in all the armies, deaths from disease exceeded deaths from combat. Evidence for this experience comes largely from physicians, officers, and writers, so the voices of the soldiers and nurses themselves are underrepresented, and generalizations should be made cautiously.

Regional Context

The Wider Theaters: Danube, Caucasus, Baltic, and Pacific

Seen from outside Moscow and St. Petersburg, the war was not one conflict but several. On the Danube, Ottoman and Russian armies fought over the Principalities, whose inhabitants, including Romanian boyars, peasants, and townspeople, experienced occupation, requisition, and the shift from Russian to Austrian presence, and for whom the collective guarantee of 1856 opened new political possibilities. In the Caucasus, Russian and Ottoman forces fought in eastern Anatolia and the borderlands, the Russian garrisons withdrew from some Black Sea coastal forts in the face of Circassian resistance and Allied naval power, and the capture of Kars in 1855 was a notable Russian success. The long Caucasian war (Chapter 39) continued, and the broader implications of the war for the region's peoples, Georgians, Armenians, Azerbaijanis, Circassians, and others, were considerable.

In the Baltic, Allied fleets captured the fortress of Bomarsund on the Åland Islands in 1854 and bombarded Sveaborg off Helsinki in 1855, and the threat to the capital forced the Russian government to maintain substantial forces in the north and in Finland. Finland, a grand duchy within the empire, experienced the war through coastal attacks and the movement of troops, and its autonomy and loyalty were tested. In the North and Far East, Allied ships bombarded the Solovetsky Monastery in the White Sea in 1854, and an Anglo-French squadron attacked Petropavlovsk on Kamchatka, where its defenders repulsed the attack; the Russian government subsequently reorganized its Pacific position, an episode connected to the expansion toward the Amur and the Pacific discussed in Section 39.6. These theaters are a reminder that the empire's strategic situation was global in scope and that its peripheral regions were part of its military history.

Russia and the World

A Modern European War

The Crimean War linked Russia to wider developments in technology, communication, and international law. Steam navies, rifled weapons, armored floating batteries, railways, submarine and land telegraph cables, and the use of photography and the mass press made it a conflict in which industrial and communications capacities mattered more than in earlier wars. The Allied powers learned from their failures, as British reforms in army administration and medical care show, and Russia drew its own conclusions about arms, transport, and recruitment. Medicine and nursing, through the work of Pirogov, Nightingale, and others, gave the war lasting importance in the history of medical care.

The war also shaped the international order. The Treaty of Paris incorporated the Ottoman Empire into the European system, produced a collective approach to the Principalities and the Straits, and established new rules of maritime law. The conservative cooperation of the three eastern powers was broken, and the diplomatic realignments that followed contributed to the changes in the European balance of power in the 1860s. Russia was not simply a pupil imitating Western practice. It was a participant in a shared system of rivalry and exchange, and its experience in this war helped shape the direction of the reforms of the next decade.

Section 41.10

Chapter Review and Historical Thinking Checkpoint

This chapter has used the Crimean War as an instrument for testing the state that Chapters 38 through 40 described. The Eastern Question created a situation in which every power feared the others' gains, and Nicholas I's assumptions about Western division, Austrian gratitude, and Ottoman weakness proved wrong. The Holy Places dispute triggered a crisis whose deeper causes lay in the Straits, Black Sea trade, prestige, and rival interpretations of earlier treaties. The entry of Britain and France, and Austria's hostile neutrality, turned a Russo-Ottoman war into a coalition war that Russia could not win on the terms it had expected. The siege of Sevastopol revealed Russian endurance and the limits of a system whose recruitment, transport, supply, finance, and administration could not sustain its large forces against opponents with sea access and more modern equipment. The Treaty of Paris imposed a significant strategic reverse without destroying Russia's great-power standing, and defeat made the argument for reform more urgent inside the elite.

The central lessons concern causation, evidence, and interpretation. In causation, no single explanation suffices: diplomacy, geography, technology, institutions, and social structure operated together. In evidence, the war is known through sources whose authors had strong purposes, from diplomats to surgeons to novelists, and the historian must weigh them accordingly. In interpretation, scholars disagree on how far the war revealed structural backwardness and how far it caused the Great Reforms, and students should be able to explain the evidence behind each view. Chapter Summary

This chapter examined the Crimean War as a test of the imperial system of Nicholas I. It argued that Russia entered the war as one of Europe's great military powers but that the conflict exposed a gap between its ambitions and the capacity of its institutions to realize them, and that defeat helped convince important parts of the state that preserving imperial power required reform.

The chapter began with the Eastern Question, the problem of managing Ottoman weakness and the competing interests of the powers in the Balkans, the Black Sea, and the Straits. It stressed that the problem involved Russia, Austria, Britain, France, and the Ottoman Empire itself, and that Ottoman "decline" was both a description and a political argument.

It traced the Russo-Ottoman relationship from the Treaty of Küçük Kaynarca through the treaties of the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries and the Straits arrangements of 1833 and 1841, noting the ambiguity of Russian claims to protect Orthodox Christians and the combination of religious, strategic, commercial, and prestige motives behind Russian policy.

The origins of the war were treated as multicausal. The Holy Places dispute was a catalyst, Menshikov's mission and the occupation of the Principalities escalated it, and Ottoman resistance, the failure of the Vienna Note, and Sinope made war likely. Nicholas I's misjudgments, and those of other leaders, were part of the explanation.

The entry of Britain and France resulted from strategic concerns, public opinion, and Napoleon III's search for prestige, while Austria's hostile neutrality forced Russia to withdraw from the Principalities and to keep large forces on its western frontier. Sardinia joined, and the Ottoman Empire fought on its own account.

The siege of Sevastopol was the central military episode. It demonstrated the endurance of Russian soldiers and sailors and the skill of leaders such as Kornilov, Nakhimov, and Totleben, while also showing the limits of a field army that could not relieve the city and of a fleet that could not contest control of the sea. Other theaters, in the Caucasus, the Baltic, the White Sea, and the Pacific, show the global scale of the war.

The analytical core of the chapter linked recruitment, weapons, transport, command, finance, and medicine into a system of weaknesses connected to the social order of serfdom, while cautioning against treating serfdom as a single cause or the Allies as models of efficiency. The experience of soldiers, sailors, nurses, civilians, serfs, and Crimean Tatars showed the war's uneven burdens and the new forms of public participation it produced.

The Treaty of Paris neutralized the Black Sea, ended the Russian protectorate over the Principalities and the claim to a special role among Ottoman Orthodox Christians, and reorganized the European diplomatic order, but it left Russia a great power with its territory largely intact. The settlement was a significant but not catastrophic reverse.

Defeat intensified an existing discussion among officials, landowners, and intellectuals, turning a debate about serfdom, recruitment, transport, finance, administration, and education into a question of state effectiveness. The chapter showed that the Great Reforms were not inevitable and that the war was a catalyst, not the only cause.

The historiographical debate examined whether the war exposed structural backwardness or reflected particular strategic circumstances, and the chapter concluded that both structural limits and contingent factors mattered. These issues lead directly to Chapter 42 on Alexander II's reforms and to later chapters on industrialization, nationalities, and the late imperial state.

Chapter Reflection

The Crimean War matters because it turned a long-running argument about the Russian state into a practical test. The empire that emerged from it was still a great power, but it was one whose leaders could no longer assume that its military reputation, its social order, and its institutions were secure. The war changed the climate in which decisions were made: it made the costs of inaction visible, relaxed some of the constraints on discussion, and strengthened the position of those within the elite who argued for change.

What continued was as important as what changed. The autocracy remained, serfdom persisted until the reforms of Chapter 42, and the empire's strategic interests in the Black Sea and the Balkans endured in altered form. The war also left a lasting body of memory, in literature, medicine, and commemoration, and later Russian and Soviet memory of Sevastopol, including in debates connected to the Black Sea region in the twenty-first century (Chapter 70), draws on images of the siege that differ from the more complicated record examined here.

The chapter reveals several themes that recur in the textbook: the interaction of empire and frontier, the relationship between military performance and social structure, the importance of evidence and its limits, and the way a single event can accelerate change without making it inevitable. It also leaves questions unresolved, notably how far the war explains the timing and character of reform, and how the experiences of peoples on the empire's periphery should be integrated into accounts centered on the capitals. Chapter 42 turns to how Alexander II and his officials answered the demand for reform, and to the limits and consequences of their answers.
