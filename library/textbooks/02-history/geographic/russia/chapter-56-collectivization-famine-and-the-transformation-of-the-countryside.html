Collectivization, Famine, and the Transformation of the Countryside

A History of Russia: From the Eurasian World to the Russian Federation

Chapter Opening

On the morning of 2 March 1930, readers of Pravda found Stalin’s article blaming local officials for coercing peasants into collective farms. Weeks earlier, those same officials had been urged to move faster. The article captures a central feature of this chapter: policy made in Moscow was enforced in villages by pressure, improvisation, and violence, and responsibility was shifted downward when the results alarmed the leadership.

This chapter studies how the Soviet state transformed agriculture and rural society between 1927 and the later 1930s. It examines grain procurement, collectivization, dekulakization, deportation, resistance, new farm institutions, and the famines of 1932–1933 in Ukraine, Kazakhstan, the Russian republic, and other regions.

The subject matters because the countryside held most of the Soviet population and supplied the grain, labor, and exports on which industrialization (Chapter 55) depended. Collectivization also ended the independent peasant household, closed or transformed village institutions, and created a farm system that lasted until 1991. The famines killed millions of people and shaped Ukrainian, Kazakh, and Russian historical memory.

Peasants were not one group. Landless laborers, middle peasants, wealthier households, Cossacks, Kazakh herders, and women experienced the changes differently, and historical actors included party activists, local officials, security police, and republic leaders as well as Stalin. Rural people resisted, adapted, informed on neighbors, joined the new farms, fled, or perished, and the chapter tries to explain these choices in context.

The evidence includes party and OGPU documents, decrees, statistics, petitions, memoirs, foreign reports, and demographic studies. Each has limits: official reports exaggerated success and concealed failure, police classifications are not independent counts, and demographic totals depend on method, territory, and years. Historians also disagree about responsibility and intent, and the chapter separates what is well established from what is contested.

The chapter follows Chapters 53 and 54, which treat the NEP and Stalin’s rise, and Chapter 55 on industrialization. It leads to Chapter 57 on terror and the Gulag and Chapter 58 on nationality policy. It also connects Soviet history to Eurasian history: the Kazakh steppe, the Ukrainian grain lands, and Siberia’s special settlements all became parts of one state project. Nothing in the account assumes that the outcome was inevitable.

Historical Context

The Revolution of 1917 and the peasant land seizures left the countryside dominated by small household farms organized in village communes. War Communism (Chapter 52) had meant forced requisitions of grain, which provoked rebellion and, in 1921–1922, famine. The NEP (Chapter 53) replaced requisitions with a tax in kind and restored a legal market in grain, and by the late 1920s production had recovered to roughly prewar levels. Marketed output, however, was lower than before 1914, since peasants consumed more of what they grew.

Industrialization, as discussed in Chapter 55, required food for cities and grain for export, and leaders worried that prices and peasant incentives could not supply these. The party was also divided (Chapter 54): Bukharin and the Right defended the NEP bargain, while Stalin and his majority viewed the peasantry as a political and economic obstacle. The Marxist-Leninist category of “class” shaped how officials read the countryside, as did memories of the civil war.

The USSR was a multinational state, and the regions involved differed. Ukraine and the Kuban were major grain regions with commercial farming and a strong peasant tradition; the Volga and Central Black Earth region were also grain-producing; Kazakhstan’s economy was mostly pastoral nomadism; and Siberia and the Urals became destinations for deported peasants. Peasants were also not uniform: landless laborers, middle peasants, and better-off households, as well as Cossacks and herders, had different interests and different relations with the state.

Evidence for the period comes from party and police archives, opened mainly after 1991, as well as from statistics, petitions, memoirs, and foreign reports. Soviet publications at the time claimed success, so sources must be read critically, as Chapter 1 explains.

Section 56.1

The Grain Procurement Crisis of 1927–1928

The Soviet countryside of the mid-1920s was dominated by some 25 million small peasant households, most of which farmed land redistributed after 1917 and organized in village communes. After the NEP replaced forced requisitions with a tax in kind in 1921, agriculture recovered. Peasants sold grain on a partly free market to state agencies, cooperatives, and private traders, but sold less than before 1914 because they consumed more themselves and because the large estates that had once supplied much of the marketed grain had disappeared.

The state’s purchasing in the winter of 1927–1928 fell about two million tons behind the previous year’s pace by January 1928. Historians point to several interacting causes. State purchase prices for grain were set low relative to prices for other crops and for manufactured goods, which were scarce (the “goods famine” of 1926–1927); peasants could therefore feed grain to livestock, sell other products, or hold it back. The war scare of 1927 led many households to hoard. The party also feared that the shortfall would endanger grain supplies for industrial cities and the army, and for export earnings that paid for imported machinery.

The leadership interpreted the shortage chiefly as political resistance by kulaks, rather than as the result of its own price policy. This interpretation was not inevitable: Bukharin, Rykov, and Tomsky argued that higher grain prices and more consumer goods would restore deliveries. The December 1927 Party Congress endorsed greater attention to collective farming, but it still envisioned a gradual and voluntary process.

Section 56.2

Emergency Procurement and the Assault on the Peasant Market

In January 1928 Stalin traveled to Siberia, where he criticized local officials for failing to take grain from the kulaks and urged them to apply Article 107 of the RSFSR Criminal Code, which provided penalties for speculation. Local authorities closed private markets, searched for grain, and confiscated stocks, and a share of what they took was distributed to poor peasants to encourage informing. Procurement improved in the spring of 1928.

These extraordinary measures were officially temporary and partly criticized. But they alienated middle peasants, who feared they would be treated as kulaks, and many reduced sowings. In early 1929 officials in the Urals and Siberia developed a variant, the Ural-Siberian method, in which village assemblies were pressed to vote to deliver specified quantities of grain under threat of fines and confiscation, with penalties for those who did not comply. The method was endorsed by Stalin and spread widely.

By early 1929 the cities faced bread rationing. Bukharin and the Right condemned the new policy as a return to War Communism, and in April 1929 they were defeated at a Central Committee plenum (Chapter 54). The crucial development was that, once emergency methods had become routine, the market relationship between peasants and the state was effectively abandoned, even before a decision for collectivization.

Section 56.3

The Great Break and the Decision for Collectivization

Before 1929 official plans for collective farming were modest. The first Five-Year Plan, approved in 1929, projected collectivization of roughly 20 percent of peasant households by 1933 (summaries also cite about 17.5 percent of sown area, depending on the version of the plan and the measure used), and many economists favored voluntary cooperatives of various kinds. Agricultural cooperative theorists such as Aleksandr Chayanov and, politically, Bukharin represented a different path, and Chayanov was arrested in 1930.

In 1929 the pace changed. Pravda published Stalin’s article “The Year of the Great Break” on 7 November 1929, claiming that peasants were joining collective farms in large numbers and that the middle peasants were turning to them. The November 1929 Central Committee plenum then called for rapid collectivization and decided to send some 25,000 urban workers to the countryside. On 27 December 1929 Stalin announced a policy of “liquidating the kulaks as a class.”

Historians disagree about the reasons. Some stress procurement: rapid collectivization promised that the state could take grain directly from farms it controlled. Others emphasize ideology and the party’s view of small private farming as a source of capitalism, and the perceived danger of a rural class that could resist. The decision was therefore a political choice: other methods, such as higher prices or slower cooperation, were available and were advocated within the party, and it is debated whether industrialization required collectivization at all.

Section 56.4

Mass Collectivization and the Rural Offensive of 1929–1930

The 5 January 1930 Central Committee resolution set the timetable. The North Caucasus and the Middle and Lower Volga were to be largely collectivized by autumn 1930 or spring 1931; Ukraine and the other main grain regions by autumn 1931 or spring 1932; and the remaining regions within the five-year plan. Local officials, competing to show success, frequently ran ahead of these dates and used quotas of their own.

The campaign relied on party plenipotentiaries, local soviet and party officials, and workers sent from the cities (the “twenty-five thousanders,” in practice more than 27,000). They held village meetings, offered promises of tractors and credits, and threatened those who refused with dekulakization, loss of voting rights, taxes, and arrest. Households were required to pool land, draft animals, and tools, and in some places cows, poultry, and household goods. Many peasants slaughtered livestock rather than hand it over.

By early March 1930 official data claimed that about 58 percent of peasant households had been collectivized. The figure reflected compulsion and a race to report numbers and is not evidence of peasants’ consent. A model statute for the artel issued in March 1930 stated that members could keep a cow and small livestock, a concession to the resistance described in §56.7.

Section 56.5

Dekulakization: Class Categories, Confiscation, and Repression

“Kulak” had referred before 1917 to a relatively well-off peasant who hired labor or lent at interest. In Soviet practice by 1930 it was a political category with no stable economic definition. After Stalin’s December 1929 announcement, a 30 January 1930 resolution divided kulak households into three categories: counter-revolutionary “activists,” to be arrested and sent to camps or shot (a plan of about 60,000 individuals, not households); a larger group, about 150,000 households, to be deported; and a third group to be resettled within their districts. A secret instruction of 4 February 1930 set an average of roughly 3 to 5 percent of households per district for exile or resettlement (Davies and Wheatcroft); it was an average, not an identical quota in every region.

Security police troikas handled the first category; the others were handled by local committees and OGPU. Quotas and local rivalries meant that the label was applied well beyond a small wealthy minority. Middle peasants, priests’ families, former soldiers of the White armies, and critics of collectivization were labeled kulaks or “subkulaks.” Property was confiscated, and neighbors, local activists, and officials often took part in seizing and dividing it.

In 1930–1931 about 380,000 families, some 1.8 million people, were deported (§56.6), according to figures from police records used by Viktor Zemskov and Nicolas Werth. Many more households were dekulakized locally or fled. For villagers, dekulakization was both an act of state terror and an opportunity for local conflict, and it also warned the remaining peasants of the consequences of resistance.

Historical Vocabulary
Kulak / Dekulakization

Definition: a prosperous peasant, and the campaign to eliminate such households “as a class.” Context: 1929–1933 USSR. Why it matters: the category was elastic and political, not a stable economic one.

Section 56.6

Deportation, Special Settlements, and Dispossession

In 1930–1931 about 381,000 households, some 1,803,000 people, were exiled as kulaks, according to figures from police records presented by Davies and Wheatcroft. About 241,000 of those households were sent outside their home regions, while the others were moved to other locations within the same region or territory, sometimes over great distances. The deportations were carried out in waves, mainly from February to May 1930 and again in 1931. Families were given short notice, allowed to bring limited belongings, and taken by rail in freight cars under guard to the Northern Krai, the Urals, western and eastern Siberia, and Kazakhstan.

The deportees became “special settlers” under the OGPU, which ran the settlements through commandants. Arrival sites were often unprepared, and families lived in barracks, dugouts, or tents, with scarce food and little medical care. Children made up a large share of the settlers and suffered the highest mortality from cold, hunger, and disease. Adults were put to work in timber cutting, mining, construction, and agriculture, including at new industrial sites such as Magnitogorsk (Chapter 55). Many escaped or tried to; others sent petitions asking to be returned.

Nicolas Werth reports about 151,600 recorded deaths among special settlers in 1933, the worst year, and the true toll was probably higher because registration was incomplete. Special settlers were not in the camps of the Gulag but were administered by the same security police and performed forced labor; the system of camps is treated in Chapter 57.

Section 56.7

Peasant Resistance and Rural Rebellion

Peasants resisted collectivization in many ways. At the household level, they slaughtered livestock, hid grain and tools, wrote petitions, and left for towns and building sites. At the village level, they disrupted meetings, attacked or threatened activists and officials, and gathered to demand the return of church bells and property. According to the OGPU, there were 13,754 mass disturbances in 1930, as analyzed by Lynne Viola. That figure is a police classification and not an independent count of uprisings, and the peak came in March, at the height of the campaign.

Women played a leading role in protests, known as bab’i bunty (“women’s riots”). Peasant women defended the family’s cow and household goods, and men reasoned that women would be punished less severely. Religion was central in many disturbances: closing churches and removing bells provoked anger, and rumors that collectivization heralded the Antichrist or the end of the world circulated in many regions, as documented by Viola and others.

Resistance varied by region. It was widespread in Ukraine, the North Caucasus, the Volga and Central Black Earth regions, and in parts of Central Asia, where it overlapped with armed revolts, and in Kazakhstan (§56.16). The authorities responded with arrests, deportations, and armed force, and by blaming local “excesses.” Resistance made the campaign costly and contributed to the retreat of March 1930, but it did not alter the long-term aim.

Section 56.8

Stalin’s Retreat of 1930 and the Renewed Collectivization Drive

On 2 March 1930 Pravda published Stalin’s article “Dizzy with Success.” It condemned the use of force, said that collectivization must be voluntary, and blamed local officials for “distortions.” A Central Committee resolution on distortions of the party line in the collective-farm movement, dated 14 March 1930 and published in Pravda on 15 March, repeated the criticism. Peasants understood the article as permission to leave, and collective-farm membership fell from about 58 percent of households in early March to about a quarter by summer 1930.

The retreat was tactical. It preserved Stalin’s authority by shifting responsibility to subordinates, calmed the countryside before the spring sowing, and left in place the dekulakization, the confiscation of property, and the procurement system. A good harvest in 1930 (the official figure was 83.5 million tons, which overstated the actual crop) encouraged the leadership to resume pressure, and the 16th Party Congress in the summer reaffirmed the goal of collectivization.

From late 1930 officials used taxation, procurement, and denial of access to supplies and credit to push households back into collective farms. By the end of 1931 about half of peasant households were collectivized; by 1932 about 60 percent; by 1935 more than 80 percent; and by 1937 over 90 percent (approximate figures from Soviet statistics). The sequence shows that coercion was repeatedly adjusted, but not abandoned.

Section 56.9

Collective Farms, State Farms, and the Organization of Agriculture

The standard form of the collective farm (kolkhoz) was the artel. Land had been nationalized in 1917, so a kolkhoz held land in permanent use but did not own it, and the state controlled what it sowed and delivered. Members pooled most of their land, draft animals, and equipment, and worked in brigades under a chairman who in practice was approved by party and state officials. A collective farm was legally a cooperative, but in practice an instrument of state procurement.

The farm had to meet compulsory deliveries of grain and other products to the state at low fixed prices, set aside seed and other funds, and only then divide the remainder among members according to workdays (trudodni), each representing a quantity of work and its difficulty. In poor years the remainder was small, and members often received little. The 1935 model charter, adopted at the congress of collective-farm shock workers, formally permitted each household a plot (generally up to about a half hectare, larger in some regions) and a few animals. Those plots became essential to rural food supply.

A state farm (sovkhoz) was a state enterprise whose workers were paid wages and whose land and equipment belonged to the state. State farms were a small proportion of agriculture, often large grain or livestock operations. Collective-farm members had fewer rights than wage workers; most lacked internal passports, which were introduced in 1932–1933 for urban residents (with exceptions) and issued widely to collective farmers only after the 1974 regulations, in 1976–1981, which limited movement.

Historical Vocabulary
Kolkhoz and Sovkhoz

Definition: a collective farm nominally owned by its members, and a state-owned farm with wage workers. Why it matters: “cooperative” did not mean independent: the state set deliveries and appointed leaders.

Section 56.10

Machine-Tractor Stations and Agricultural Mechanization

Machine-Tractor Stations (MTS) were state enterprises that owned tractors and combines and provided plowing, sowing, and harvesting to collective farms in exchange for payment in grain. The first station was established in 1928; the network grew quickly after 1929, reaching 2,916 stations in 1933 and 5,617 in 1937 (a Soviet statistical collection, counts as of 1 June). Their purpose was both economic and political: mechanization was supposed to demonstrate the advantages of large farms, and ownership of machinery by the state gave it leverage over the collectives.

Mechanization was limited at first. New plants such as the Stalingrad Tractor Plant (Chapter 55) produced tractors, but breakdowns were frequent, spare parts and fuel were scarce, and trained drivers and mechanics were in short supply. Hastily trained operators damaged equipment, and in the early 1930s the loss of horses outpaced the arrival of tractors, so overall draft power declined. Some women, such as the tractor driver Pasha Angelina, were promoted as symbols of mechanization.

In January 1933 the party created political departments (politotdely) in the MTS to supervise collective-farm affairs, procurement, and local officials. The stations therefore served as a means for the party to monitor the village, which was especially important in the period of the famine.

Section 56.11

Village Life, Women, Families, and the Destruction of Rural Institutions

Collectivization changed village institutions. The old village assembly and commune lost authority, replaced by the kolkhoz board, party cell, and village soviet. Churches were closed or turned into granaries and clubs, bells were removed, and many priests were deprived of rights or deported. Local leaders, wealthier households, and sometimes whole families vanished through dekulakization, and the remaining households learned to be cautious about what they said.

Women took on much of the field work as men were deported or left for industry and building sites. They worked in brigades, often at heavy tasks, and also cared for children and the household plot. Payments in workdays went mostly to the person recorded in the farm’s books, usually a man. Families split, children were orphaned, and old customs of marriage and religion changed unevenly. Historians such as Lynne Viola and Sheila Fitzpatrick describe how peasants used everyday strategies, such as petitions, foot-dragging, theft, and informal trade, to cope with the new system.

The household plot and a small number of animals became the core of survival. After 1932 collective-farm markets were legalized, and the sale of plot produce provided cash. The experience differed by region, harvest, farm leadership, and household composition; evidence from memoirs, petitions, and police reports comes mainly from sources produced by the state or recorded long afterward.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Kolkhoz Household, 1930s

A typical household combined work in the collective-farm brigade with labor on a small plot. Women rose early to milk the family cow and tend the plot before field work; children helped with animals and sometimes with brigade labor. Payment in workdays often yielded little grain, so the household depended on the plot’s potatoes, vegetables, and animal products and on selling surplus at the market.

Experiences differed by region, farm, and year; households in Ukraine or Kazakhstan in 1933 faced starvation that those in other regions did not. Sources include petitions, police reports, and memoirs, which reflect the interests of their authors.

Section 56.12

Agricultural Disruption, Livestock Losses, and Falling Productivity

Collectivization disrupted production in several ways. Livestock fell steeply as peasants slaughtered animals, and as farms lacked fodder and shelter; draft animals declined by about half from 1928 to 1932, while tractors were too few to replace them. Fields were sown late and unevenly, weeding and harvesting were poorly organized, and many experienced farmers had been deported or had fled. Collective-farm members had little incentive to work for the collective rather than for their household plots.

The official statistics for harvests and procurement show a striking pattern. According to the official series reproduced in Alec Nove’s economic history (other reconstructions give slightly different totals), grain output was 73.3 million tons in 1928 and 68.4 million in 1933, while state procurements rose from about 10.8 million tons in 1928 to 22.6 million in 1933, with a decline in 1932 to about 18.5 million. Official harvest figures overstated the actual crop because they used estimates of the standing crop (“biological yield”), so actual harvests in the early 1930s were lower. As the state took a larger share of a stagnant or smaller crop, less food remained in the villages.

This relationship helps explain how the reorganization of agriculture contributed to the famines of 1932–1933 (§56.14). Disruption did not by itself determine the outcome, but it created conditions in which procurement policy had catastrophic effects.

Section 56.13

Grain Quotas, Procurement Enforcement, and the Escalation of Crisis

The harvest of 1932 was disappointing, but procurement targets stayed high. Quotas in several regions were lowered during the year, yet remained far above what many districts could deliver, and grain exports, though lower than in 1930–1931 (about 4.8 and 5.2 million tons in 1930 and 1931 and about 1.8 and 1.7 million tons in 1932 and 1933, rounded calendar-year totals from Soviet trade statistics and later historical series), continued. Local officials who reported shortages could be accused of “opportunism” or of sheltering saboteurs, which pushed many to meet quotas at any cost.

The 7 August 1932 law (see the Primary Source Window) made the taking of grain from collective fields a capital offense in principle, and thousands of peasants were convicted under it. Stalin’s letters to Lazar Kaganovich in the summer of 1932 show that he regarded the situation in Ukraine as dangerous; in a letter dated 11 August 1932 in the HREC excerpt (10 August in a Russian documentary edition) he warned that the party might “lose” Ukraine if its leadership did not act. His concern included the republic’s leaders, the Ukrainian party’s reliability, and the possibility of peasant revolt.

In the autumn and winter of 1932, farms and villages that missed targets were blacklisted: denied manufactured goods, shut out of trade, and subject to special inspections. Authorities imposed penalties in kind, such as meat fines, and brigades searched for hidden grain and seized other foodstuffs. These measures differed between regions and over time, and enforcement was shaped by local officials as well as by directives from Moscow.

Primary Source Window

Source: Decree of the Central Executive Committee and Council of People’s Commissars of the USSR, 7 August 1932, “On the Protection of the Property of State Enterprises, Collective Farms and Cooperatives and the Strengthening of Public (Socialist) Property” (paraphrase checked against the published Russian text and the English translation hosted by the Holodomor Research and Education Consortium). Context: Moscow, during the procurement crisis; Stalin’s correspondence shows he pressed for harsher measures against theft of grain.

The decree declared collective-farm and cooperative property to be public property, equivalent to state property. Its principal penalty for theft was shooting with confiscation of all property; where mitigating circumstances applied, the penalty was imprisonment of not less than ten years with confiscation. It barred amnesty for those convicted under it.

Analysis: The decree shows how the state defined peasants’ taking of grain as an offense against the state. It does not show how often courts applied the harshest penalty, how local officials interpreted “theft,” or how peasants understood the law. Popular usage later called it the “law of five ears of grain,” but that nickname should be checked against the documents on cases. Prosecution records, court statistics, and peasant testimony are needed for the other half of the story.

  1. What does the decree say, and which penalties did it set?
  2. Who issued it, and for what purpose?
  3. What can historians learn about state priorities from it?
  4. What additional evidence would show how it was applied?
Section 56.14

The Soviet Famines of 1932–1933: Origins, Mechanisms, and Chronology

The famine took different forms in different places. In Kazakhstan it began in 1930–1931 with the collapse of herds; in 1932–1933 it struck the Volga regions, the North Caucasus, Ukraine, and other grain regions. Mortality was highest in the spring and summer of 1933, when stocks were exhausted and the new harvest had not yet come. A chronology therefore distinguishes the early Kazakh catastrophe, the harvest and procurement crisis of autumn 1932, the intensification of punitive measures in winter 1932–1933, and the peak of starvation in spring–summer 1933.

The causal question is how several factors combined. Collectivization had destroyed draft animals, disrupted sowing and harvesting, and weakened farm management; officials overestimated the 1932 harvest and set procurement targets accordingly; grain exports continued; and enforcement, blacklisting, and from January 1933 restrictions on leaving affected regions cut peasants off from food. Weather, plant disease, and harvest difficulties also played a part, and R. W. Davies and Stephen Wheatcroft in The Years of Hunger emphasize the interaction of agricultural conditions and policy. Their account does not remove responsibility from the leadership: the scale of the catastrophe followed from decisions about quotas, exports, enforcement, and relief.

Scholars differ on how much weight each factor deserves, and the question of intent is treated separately in §56.20. What the evidence does show is that food was available in some places and not others, that the state was able to move grain and to choose between exports, urban rations, and relief, and that these choices determined who survived.

Section 56.15

The Holodomor: Famine in Soviet Ukraine

Soviet Ukraine was one of the country’s main grain regions, with a population of about 30 million, most of them rural. Resistance to collectivization had been strong there in 1930, and republic officials, including party leader Stanislav Kosior, reported in 1932 that the harvest could not meet the quotas. Quotas were reduced during the year but remained high, and delivery lagged. In the same period the policy of Ukrainization (promotion of Ukrainian language and cadres, Chapter 53) was under criticism, and Moscow suspected the Ukrainian party and cultural leadership of disloyalty.

In November and December 1932 decrees blacklisted districts and collective farms that failed to deliver, ended their access to manufactured goods, and authorized the seizure of grain and, in practice, other foodstuffs. In the Kuban, a heavily Ukrainian-speaking region of the North Caucasus, villages were blacklisted and some entire communities deported; a December 1932 decree also ended Ukrainization policies there. In January 1933 Pavel Postyshev was sent to Ukraine as Moscow’s representative, and a purge of Ukrainian party officials followed. On 22 January 1933 Stalin and Molotov directed that peasants be prevented from leaving Ukraine and the North Caucasus. An OGPU report of 13 March 1933 recorded 219,460 people apprehended, of whom 186,588 were returned to their places of residence; these were apprehensions, not criminal arrests.

Mortality rose steeply in the winter of 1932–1933 and peaked in the late spring and summer of 1933, with villages reporting mass hunger, abandoned children, and cannibalism. Deaths were concentrated in rural areas, including central and eastern regions of the republic. Moscow provided some seed and food aid in spring 1933, mostly to ensure the sowing. The Ptoukha Institute and other demographic studies estimate about 3.9 million excess deaths and 0.6 million lost births in Soviet Ukraine in 1932–1934. The evidence includes party and police archives, local reports, census and registration data, and survivor testimony collected later, each with limitations. Ukrainian sources and Western historians have debated whether the political measures aimed at Ukraine were part of the cause of the famine’s severity or a response to resistance, which is the subject of §56.20.

Section 56.16

The Kazakh Famine: Forced Settlement and Pastoral Collapse

Kazakh society before collectivization was based on mobile pastoralism. Herds of sheep, goats, horses, camels, and cattle were the main source of food, clothing, transport, and wealth, and families moved seasonally between winter and summer pastures within clan-based networks. Livestock was also a form of savings and exchange, so its loss meant the loss of both subsistence and security.

From 1928, under the republic’s party secretary Filipp Goloshchekin, who called for a “Little October” in the steppe, authorities confiscated the herds of wealthy herders (bais) and then pressed for rapid collectivization and settlement of nomads. At the same time, Kazakhstan was assigned heavy meat and grain quotas. Herds fell from tens of millions of animals in 1929 to a few million by 1933, with the specific losses depending on the species and period. Rebellions broke out in 1929–1931 and were suppressed.

The famine that followed lasted from 1930 to 1933 and varied regionally; Niccolò Pianciola’s work, for example, distinguishes the Aral Sea region and other areas where food sources and relief differed. Sarah Cameron reports that more than one million people fled Kazakhstan during the catastrophe, to neighboring Soviet republics, western Siberia, and China, and many died on the way. Refugees, the dead, and the permanently displaced are separate categories, and the totals do not simply add up. Sarah Cameron estimates more than 1.5 million deaths, about a quarter of the republic’s population and over a third of ethnic Kazakhs; these denominators differ and should not be interchanged. The Kazakh share of the republic’s population fell sharply, so that Kazakhs became a minority in their own republic. Historians treat the Kazakh famine as a distinct catastrophe rooted in pastoral collapse and colonial-style modernization, and not simply as a variant of the Ukrainian case.

Regional Context

Kazakhstan and the Aral Sea Region

The famine in Kazakhstan was not uniform. Niccolò Pianciola’s work contrasts northern Kazakh areas with southern areas such as Karakalpakia near the Aral Sea. Proximity to railways, the intensity of livestock procurement, and the availability of alternative food such as fish and irrigated crops affected mortality and flight in different ways. Seen from Almaty and the steppe, collectivization was a campaign against pastoral nomadism, and the famine was part of a longer history of Russian and Soviet rule in Central Asia (Chapters 39 and 53).

Historical Vocabulary
Sedentarization

Definition: the forced settlement of nomadic herders. Context: Kazakhstan, 1928–1933, combined with livestock requisition and collectivization. Why it matters: for pastoralists, livestock was both food and wealth, so settlement without means of subsistence was catastrophic, unlike the grain-procurement crisis in farming regions.

Section 56.17

Famine Beyond Ukraine and Kazakhstan

Famine also struck the North Caucasus (including the Kuban, the Don, and Stavropol), the Lower and Middle Volga, the Central Black Earth region, the southern Urals, and parts of western Siberia. These were major grain regions, and many had been sites of strong resistance to collectivization. The Kuban and the Don had Cossack populations and large Ukrainian-speaking communities, which complicates simple divisions of the famine by republic or nationality.

Mortality was lower in Russia’s non-grain regions, in Belarus, and in the large cities, which were protected by rationing. A demographic reconstruction by Levchuk, Wolowyna, Rudnytskyi, Kovbasiuk, and Kulyk (2020) estimates about 3.32 million excess deaths in 1932–1934 across twenty Russian regions, compared with about 3.94 million in Soviet Ukraine over the same period. The Russian total excludes the Kazakh ASSR, which was administratively part of the RSFSR until 1936, so it is not an estimate for the whole RSFSR of the time, nor for the Volga or North Caucasus alone. Mortality varied greatly among Russian regions and was highest in the grain-producing regions named above. The regional patterns show that famine followed grain procurement and the collapse of farm production, and not a single national boundary, even though policies and political conditions in each region differed.

The Volga region included Russian, Ukrainian, Tatar, Mordvin, and German communities, among them the Volga German Autonomous Republic, and the lower Volga was a major scene of grain requisition and famine. The Central Black Earth region, one of the empire’s old grain areas, suffered when the 1932 harvest failed and procurement continued. Cities were protected by rationing and by the passport system introduced in 1932, which allowed authorities to remove “unregistered” people, so urban and rural death rates diverged. Famine was therefore not simply a Ukrainian phenomenon, and it was not a single event, but a set of regional catastrophes connected by the state’s grain policy.

Section 56.18

State Responses: Relief, Restrictions, Denial, and Information Control

The leadership received detailed reports of mass hunger from local officials, the OGPU, and republic leaders, including reports of cannibalism and of people dying in villages. It responded unevenly. Moscow granted seed loans and some food aid in 1932–1933, directed disproportionately to sowing and to selected regions, and maintained grain exports, though at lower levels than before. At the same time, it blamed peasants for hiding grain and local officials for failing to find it. In a reply of 6 May 1933 to the writer Mikhail Sholokhov, who had reported abuses and hunger in the Don region, Stalin acknowledged some abuses but accused the grain growers of a silent war against Soviet power.

Domestic reporting on the famine was censored, and the authorities denied that there was a famine. Foreign correspondents’ access to affected regions was restricted. Gareth Jones, a Welsh journalist, published reports based on his travels in Ukraine in March 1933; Malcolm Muggeridge also reported the famine. Other correspondents, notably Walter Duranty of the New York Times, minimized it. Soviet authorities rejected offers of international aid. Knowing what officials believed privately and what they said publicly is essential: the documents show awareness, while public statements show concealment.

Relief was selective. In 1932–1933 the government issued seed loans and food loans, but allocations favored the sowing campaign and the restoration of working capacity over feeding the starving, and assistance went mainly to people able to work and to seed for the sowing, so that relief was uneven across affected regions. Cities and favored groups such as industrial workers and the army received rations, while the rural population relied on household plots, markets, and relatives. Stores run by Torgsin, which accepted foreign currency, gold, and valuables in exchange for food, offered some a way to survive, which shows how desperate households were. The pattern of aid and denial means that historians evaluate responsibility on the basis of what the leadership knew, what resources it controlled, and what it chose to do with them.

Soviet Union and the World

Reporting and Denial

Foreign journalists, diplomats, and relief organizations learned of the famine by different routes. Gareth Jones’s March 1933 reports and Malcolm Muggeridge’s dispatches contrasted with Walter Duranty’s dismissals, and Western governments, concerned with trade and recognition (the US recognized the USSR in November 1933), were cautious. The Soviet government denied famine and rejected aid. International reporting shows how the state controlled information and how perceptions abroad shaped later debates about the famine.

Section 56.19

Mortality, Population Loss, and the Human Consequences

Reconstructing famine mortality requires comparing the censuses of 1926 and 1939 with registration of births and deaths, which was incomplete in the famine years, and with regional data. Researchers estimate the population that would have existed without the famine and subtract the one observed, adjusting for migration. Studies differ in the territory covered, years included, and the baseline assumed, so their results measure different things and should not be read as one range. Excess mortality means deaths above those expected from prior trends; the birth deficit means children who were not born; and refugee accounting asks how many people left and how many died elsewhere.

The Ptoukha Institute and Harvard’s MAPA project report about 8.7 million excess deaths in the USSR in 1932–1934, with about 3.94 million in Soviet Ukraine plus about 0.6 million lost births, and about 3.32 million in twenty Russian regions, excluding the Kazakh ASSR (Levchuk, Wolowyna, and colleagues, 2020). Davies and Wheatcroft’s The Years of Hunger estimates about 5.5 to 6.5 million excess deaths, using different methods and boundaries. Sarah Cameron estimates over 1.5 million deaths in Soviet Kazakhstan, about a quarter of its population and more than a third of ethnic Kazakhs. The comparative table in this section lists these estimates; the differences stem from method, territory, and period, and not from a single disagreement about whether mass death occurred.

The 1937 census recorded about 162 million people, compared with about 147 million in 1926, but well below official expectations. The government suppressed the results and arrested census officials, as Catherine Merridale and others have shown, because they exposed the demographic cost of famine and repression. The 1939 census published a total of about 170.6 million, but the demographer Andrei Volkov argued that the published figure was inflated and reconstructed a lower total of about 167.6 million, a view supported by Mark Tolts. The evidence of mass mortality thus comes from sources that the state tried to conceal, which makes its reconstruction more difficult but not impossible.

Section 56.20

Historical Debates: Responsibility, Intent, and Genocide

The scholarly debate concerns four separate questions. Did the regime’s policies cause mass starvation? Virtually all historians say yes. Did leaders know that people were starving? Local reports, OGPU summaries, and letters such as Sholokhov’s to Stalin show that they did by early 1933. Did they knowingly persist in policies that worsened the famine, for example by continuing exports and imposing the January 1933 movement restrictions? The documents support this for the period of mass hunger. Did they intend to destroy a national group? That question is contested.

Robert Conquest’s The Harvest of Sorrow (1986) argued that Stalin deliberately used famine against Ukrainian peasants and national identity. Andrea Graziosi, Timothy Snyder in Bloodlands (2010), and Anne Applebaum in Red Famine (2017) argue, with different emphases, that measures taken in Ukraine and the Kuban in late 1932 and 1933, including blacklisting, seizure of food, border closures, and the attack on Ukrainization and the Ukrainian party, targeted Ukrainians in ways that fit a genocide interpretation. Raphael Lemkin, who coined the word genocide, characterized in his 1953 text Soviet Genocide in the Ukraine a broader campaign against the Ukrainian nation and its institutions, not famine mortality in isolation. R. W. Davies and Stephen Wheatcroft in The Years of Hunger argue that the famine resulted from a combination of harvest difficulties, ruthless priorities, and poor information, and that Stalin’s aim was to extract grain and suppress resistance, not to kill a nation, although he bore heavy responsibility. Some historians locate the intent in the class war against the peasantry.

The Kazakh case adds a further dimension. Niccolò Pianciola’s research documents livestock requisition, the destruction of the pastoral economy, and regional differences in the catastrophe, and Sarah Cameron’s The Hungry Steppe (2018) shows how forced sedentarization, requisitions, and neglect devastated Kazakh society. Scholars of Kazakhstan debate how best to characterize these policies, as colonial violence, a modernizing campaign, or, in some arguments, genocide, and the works cited here differ in their emphases. The legal concept requires intent to destroy a protected national, ethnic, racial, or religious group as such, and the 1948 Genocide Convention does not cover social or political groups, so the legal and historical questions differ. The European Parliament and many national legislatures have recognized the Holodomor as genocide, Ukraine by a law of 28 November 2006; the Russian State Duma, in a resolution of 2 April 2008, condemned the Soviet regime’s disregard for human lives and described the famine as a tragedy of many peoples of the USSR, but rejected the genocide classification. Such declarations are political and legal acts and cannot settle a historical question, while the lack of agreement on the legal term does not diminish the documented mass deaths.

Section 56.21

The New Soviet Countryside: Consolidation and Long-Term Consequences

By the later 1930s the collective-farm system was stable but not productive. The 1935 model charter fixed the artel as the standard form, defined household plots, and confirmed households’ right to sell plot produce at the collective-farm markets legalized in 1932; a decree of 27 May 1939 (on measures to protect collective-farm land from squandering) restricted plots exceeding the permitted size and set minimum annual workday requirements, which varied by region. Chairmen, party secretaries, and MTS political departments supervised farms, and compulsory deliveries were set by the state, so farm leaders were accountable upward more than to members. Payment in workdays remained low, and many households relied on their plots.

Agricultural output recovered slowly. Official grain figures rose in the mid-1930s (and in 1937 were reported as exceptional), but livestock herds took until the 1950s to recover to pre-collectivization levels, and productivity remained low. Peasants’ mobility was restricted by the internal passport system, which most collective farmers were initially excluded from receiving (with exceptions) and which were issued widely to them only after the 1974 regulations (1976–1981), yet millions left the countryside for towns and industry during the 1930s, one reason the urban population doubled between the 1926 and 1939 censuses (Chapter 55). Village social life changed: the commune, church, and independent households gave way to collective-farm institutions and household plots.

The famine was officially denied in the Soviet Union for decades. Public discussion began in the Gorbachev era, and memory differs between Ukraine, where the Holodomor is a central national memory, Kazakhstan, and Russia. These later uses of the past are examined in later chapters (Chapters 65 and 66); here the key point is that the collectivized countryside shaped Soviet agriculture until 1991 and beyond.

Continuity and Change Over Time

The Village Before and After Collectivization

Changed: land use and ownership, village authority, the church and commune, mobility, and the relation of households to the state. Continued: household farming on small plots, women’s labor, local cooperation, and peasant poverty. Change was rapid in 1929–1933 and slower in later years. Those who had lost most were deportees and the famine regions; for households that survived on plots, continuities were stronger.

Section 56.22

Chapter Review and Historical Thinking Checkpoint

Chapter 56 has answered its central question in stages. The collectivization drive grew out of the procurement crisis of 1927–1928, but the choice of rapid compulsory collectivization was a political decision and not an unavoidable consequence of industrialization. Evidence for this includes the modest original plan, the alternatives advocated within the party, and the leadership’s reading of the crisis as kulak resistance.

Peasants were not a single group, and their responses depended on property, region, gender, religion, and local officials. Collectivization, dekulakization, and famine were connected but distinct: collectivization created the new farming system, dekulakization removed households and spread fear, and famine resulted from the collapse of production, high procurement, exports, punitive enforcement, restrictions on movement, and inadequate relief. Ukraine, Kazakhstan, the Russian SFSR, and other regions experienced the famine differently, and statistics from different studies measure different things.

The chapter also illustrates historical method. Students should ask what each source can show: a decree shows the state’s priorities but not its application; a police report shows what the OGPU recorded, not independent events; a demographic study depends on its baseline and territory; a recognition by a legislature is a political act. The debate over responsibility separates causation, knowledge, persistence, and intent, and requires evidence for each. The chapter connects forward to the terror and the Gulag (Chapter 57) and to nationality policy (Chapter 58), and the central question it leaves is what the evidence reveals about the choices and responsibilities of those who directed the transformation.

Chapter Summary

This chapter asked how the Soviet leadership’s attempt to control and transform the countryside produced a new agricultural system, widespread resistance, and catastrophic famine. It began with the grain procurement crisis of 1927–1928, when purchases fell about two million tons behind the previous year’s pace because of price policy, scarce goods, and peasant responses. The leadership read the shortfall as kulak resistance and turned to emergency measures.

Emergency procurement ended the NEP market relationship in practice, and in 1929 the leadership decided on rapid mass collectivization. The decision was political: the original plan was modest, alternatives were proposed within the party, and it is debated whether industrialization required collectivization at all.

The campaign used plenipotentiaries, urban workers, quotas, threats, and the label “kulak.” About 1.8 million people were deported in 1930–1931 and placed in special settlements, where children suffered heavily. Peasants resisted, with women prominent, and in March 1930 Stalin retreated tactically, before pressure resumed.

The new system consisted of collective and state farms, compulsory deliveries, workday payments, household plots, and Machine-Tractor Stations with political oversight. Village institutions, religion, and family life were transformed, and livestock herds collapsed, so draft power and productivity fell even as state procurement rose.

These conditions, together with exports, punitive enforcement, blacklisting, restrictions on movement, and limited relief, produced famine in 1932–1933. Weather and harvest difficulties contributed, but the scale of the catastrophe followed from state decisions. Leaders received reports of mass hunger and persisted, while denying the famine publicly.

The famines varied by region. Kazakhstan, where the pastoral economy was destroyed, had the highest proportional losses; Ukraine, with about 3.9 million excess deaths in the Ptoukha study, suffered measures that included blacklisting and border restrictions; and twenty Russian regions (excluding the Kazakh ASSR), with about 3.3 million in one reconstruction, suffered severe losses in grain regions such as the Volga and North Caucasus.

Mortality estimates depend on method, territory, and years, and they distinguish excess deaths from lost births. The suppressed 1937 census and the disputed 1939 census show how evidence was concealed or altered, and the surviving data still support responsible ranges.

Historians agree that policy caused mass starvation and that leaders knew of it. They disagree about intent and the classification of the Ukrainian famine as genocide, and the legal concept differs from historical causation. The collectivized countryside lasted until 1991, and the chapter prepares for Chapter 57 and Chapter 58 without treating later outcomes as inevitable.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a state remade the countryside where most of its people lived. Collectivization ended the independent peasant household, removed village institutions, and created a system of state procurement that was a defining feature of Soviet life. It did so by coercion, and it was accompanied by deportations, famine, and the loss of millions of lives.

Some institutions continued: the household plot, women’s agricultural labor, local networks of kinship and exchange, and the collective farm itself, which endured until 1991. The human consequences were uneven, and the experience of Ukraine, Kazakhstan, Russia’s grain regions, and the special settlements differed. For many families, the losses lasted generations.

The chapter connects to the larger textbook in several ways. It shows how industrialization (Chapter 55) depended on the countryside, how coercive institutions grew (Chapter 57), and how the USSR’s multinational structure shaped policy and memory (Chapter 58). It also shows the Eurasian scale of Soviet rule, from the Kazakh steppe to the Ukrainian grain lands and Siberian settlements.

Historical memory remains contested. In Ukraine the Holodomor is central to national remembrance; in Kazakhstan the catastrophe has been recovered more recently; in Russia, officials and scholars have emphasized the tragedy of many peoples. Historians continue to debate intent, classification, and the exact scale of mortality. These questions should be argued from evidence, and not from the assumption that later outcomes were inevitable.
