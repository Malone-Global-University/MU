Part I — The Lands and Peoples Before Rus'
6

Khazars, Bulgars, Vikings, and the Eurasian Trade Networks

A History of Russia: From the Eurasian World to the Russian Federation

Chapter Opening

In 921–922, an envoy of the Abbasid caliph in Baghdad traveled north along the Volga to the newly Islamized kingdom of the Volga Bulgars, and there encountered a group of northern merchants he called the Rus, whose funeral customs, dress, hygiene, and commercial habits he described in unusually close, if plainly outsider, detail. His account survives as one of the most valuable, and most carefully debated, windows historians possess onto the world this chapter examines.

Across the tenth-century Eurasian world, a single silver coin minted in Samarkand or Baghdad might travel thousands of kilometers before it came to rest: carried north along the Volga to a Bulgar market, exchanged there for furs trapped by a Finnic hunter in the northern forests, passed to a Scandinavian trader who carried it onward toward the Baltic, and eventually buried, centuries later to be recovered by archaeologists, in a hoard on the island of Gotland or in a farmstead near Stockholm. Tens of thousands of such Islamic silver dirhams, struck under Abbasid, Samanid, and other Muslim dynasties, have been excavated from hoards scattered across Scandinavia, the Baltic coast, the islands of the Baltic Sea, and the river valleys of what are now Russia, Ukraine, and Belarus. No single merchant carried a coin the entire distance from Central Asia to Scandinavia; no single state controlled the routes along which it traveled. The coin's journey was instead the product of an interlocking, multiethnic, and only loosely coordinated system of exchange that connected Scandinavia and the Baltic world to the Volga, the Caspian Sea, the Islamic caliphates, Byzantium, and the Black Sea, all flowing through the river valleys, forests, and steppe margins of Eastern Europe.

This chapter examines that system directly, as the essential background against which the political consolidation of Rus', examined beginning in Chapter 7, took place. Chapters 1 through 5 have already established the geography of forest, steppe, and river that shaped this region; the archaeological and later steppe societies, from the Bronze Age cultures of Chapter 3 through the Scythians and Sarmatians of Chapter 4; and the diverse Slavic, Finnic, Baltic, and Turkic-speaking populations who inhabited Eastern Europe on the eve of Rus', discussed in Chapter 5. None of those peoples lived in isolation from wider Eurasian developments. By the eighth and ninth centuries, Eastern Europe had become an intermediary zone linking several of the era's major civilizations: the Byzantine Empire, centered on Constantinople; the Abbasid Caliphate and its successor and rival Islamic states, centered on Baghdad and, later, other cities of Central Asia and Persia; and the expanding, restless maritime and riverine world of Scandinavia, whose seafarers, traders, and raiders were, in these same centuries, reaching Ireland, Francia, England, Iceland, and the Mediterranean as well as the rivers of Eastern Europe.

At the same time, Eastern Europe was not merely a corridor through which outside powers passed. Two substantial states of local and regional origin, the Khazar Khaganate on the lower Volga and northern Caucasus steppe, and Volga Bulgaria on the middle Volga, organized, taxed, and profited from long-distance trade passing through their territories, built fortified towns, absorbed and governed diverse populations, and engaged as diplomatic and military actors in their own right with Byzantium, the caliphates, and their steppe neighbors. Understanding these states on their own historical terms, rather than treating them as a passive backdrop to later Rus' history, is one of this chapter's central purposes.

This chapter also introduces, with appropriate caution, Scandinavian-associated traders and warriors variously identified in the sources as Rus' and as Varangians, categories that overlap but are not simply synonymous, as Section 6.5 will explain more fully. Their arrival did not create a network from nothing; it inserted a new and highly mobile set of participants into commercial and political systems that Slavic, Finnic, Baltic, Khazar, and Bulgar populations had already been building and adapting for generations. This chapter deliberately stops short of the political formation of the Rus' state itself, a subject reserved for Chapter 7, because the two developments, commercial network and political state, though closely related, are not the same thing and should not be collapsed into a single, tidy narrative in which "the Vikings came and founded Russia."

The chapter's evidence is unusually varied for this early period. Alongside the archaeological evidence already familiar from Chapters 3 through 5, above all the tens of thousands of Islamic silver coins recovered in hoards across Northern and Eastern Europe, this chapter draws on outside written testimony from Islamic geographers and travelers, Byzantine administrative and diplomatic writing, and the retrospective account offered generations later by the Rus' chronicle tradition itself, examined more fully in Chapter 7. Each source type carries its own limitations, and this chapter models the same critical caution toward outside written testimony that Chapter 4 modeled for Herodotus's account of the Scythians.

By the chapter's end, students should be able to explain how Scandinavian, Slavic, Finnic, Baltic, Khazar, Bulgar, and steppe populations became joined together within overlapping commercial systems moving along the Volga and Dnieper river corridors; describe the political structures of the Khazar Khaganate and Volga Bulgaria; evaluate the evidentiary strengths and limitations of sources such as Ibn Fadlan's travel account; and explain, in general terms, how control over trade, tribute, and fortified river nodes could generate new and more durable forms of political authority, without assuming that the emergence of a Rus' state was the network's automatic or inevitable outcome.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how Eastern Europe's river systems connected Scandinavia and the Baltic world to the Volga, the Caspian, Byzantium, and the Islamic world before the political consolidation of Rus'.
  2. Describe the political structure, dual rulership, multiethnic population, and diplomatic position of the Khazar Khaganate.
  3. Evaluate the evidence for, and the historiographical caution surrounding, the Khazar elite's adoption of Judaism.
  4. Trace the emergence of Volga Bulgaria as a distinct Turkic-speaking commercial and political society on the Middle Volga, including its adoption of Islam in the early 920s.
  5. Distinguish the broader phenomenon of Scandinavian expansion across the Baltic and North Atlantic from the more specific term Varangian as applied to Scandinavian activity in Eastern Europe.
  6. Trace the geography of the Volga trade route and the Dnieper route to Byzantium, including their portages, dangers, and market centers.
  7. Analyze the commodities that moved through these networks, including furs, slaves, silver, and other goods, and the systems of extraction, tribute, and exchange that produced them.
  8. Assess how control over trade routes, tribute collection, and fortified nodes could generate increasingly durable forms of political authority.
  9. Critically evaluate Ibn Fadlan's account of the Rus and other outside written sources as evidence, distinguishing direct observation from secondhand report and from later retrospective chronicle tradition.
  10. Explain why historians reject an older model in which Scandinavian traders single-handedly created political order in an otherwise undeveloped Eastern Europe.
  11. Connect the commercial and political systems examined in this chapter to Chapter 7's analysis of the formation of Rus'.
  12. Distinguish trade from tribute, and migration from state formation, as related but analytically separate historical processes.

Historical Context

Chapter 2 described the great river systems of the East European Plain, the Volga, the Dnieper, the Don, and the Dvina, as long-distance highways connecting forest, forest-steppe, and steppe zones and, ultimately, distant seas: the Baltic, the Caspian, and the Black Sea. Chapter 5 traced the settlement of this landscape by Eastern Slavic tribal groupings, Finnic and Uralic peoples of the northern forests, Baltic-speaking populations along the eastern Baltic coast, and Turkic-speaking steppe peoples, including the remnants of Avar power, the fragments of Old Great Bulgaria after its seventh-century dissolution, and the Khazars, who by the later seventh century had established dominance over much of the Pontic-Caspian steppe.

The Khazar Khaganate's own rise is best understood against the collapse of the Western Turkic Khaganate in the mid-seventh century, a much larger steppe empire whose fragmentation opened the way for Khazar dominance across the Pontic-Caspian region; Khazar expansion then contributed to the displacement and dispersal of the Bulgar tribal confederation discussed in Chapter 5, while the earlier westward withdrawal of Avar power was part of the same broader steppe transformation rather than the immediate cause of Khazar state formation.

By the eighth century, several further long-term developments converged to draw this region into a wider Eurasian commercial system on an unprecedented scale. First, the rapid seventh- and eighth-century expansion of the Islamic caliphates created an enormous zone of relatively unified commercial law, currency, and demand stretching from Spain to Central Asia, with a persistent appetite for northern products, above all furs, together with slaves, wax, and other goods difficult to obtain within the Islamic world's own territory. Second, the Abbasid Caliphate, established in 750 with its capital eventually at Baghdad, together with mint centers in Central Asia such as Samarkand and, later, other regional Islamic states, produced enormous quantities of silver dirhams, a standardized coinage that circulated far beyond the caliphate's political borders and became, in effect, an international medium of exchange across much of northern Eurasia. Third, Scandinavian societies, whose maritime technology, seafaring skill, and social organization were simultaneously driving expansion westward into the British Isles, Ireland, Frankish territory, and the North Atlantic, also turned eastward across the Baltic Sea, entering the Gulf of Finland and the river systems beyond it in growing numbers from at least the eighth century onward.

These developments did not create the region's commercial life from nothing. Chapter 5 has already shown that Slavic, Finnic, Baltic, and Turkic populations engaged in trade, tribute exchange, and cross-cultural contact well before the eighth century. What changed, over the eighth and ninth centuries, was the scale, the distance, and the degree of integration: local and regional exchange networks became linked, through the Volga and Dnieper corridors, to the vastly larger commercial systems of the Islamic world and Byzantium, with Scandinavian participants providing additional long-distance maritime and riverine mobility, and with the Khazar Khaganate and, somewhat later, Volga Bulgaria providing the political and fiscal infrastructure, tribute systems, fortified towns, and diplomatic relationships, through which this trade could be organized, taxed, and protected.

Students should approach this chapter's evidence with the same critical habits developed in earlier chapters. Much of what is known about the Khazars comes from outside sources, Byzantine, Islamic, and later Rus' chronicle material, supplemented by a small and much-debated corpus of internal Khazar documents and by archaeology that remains, in several respects, still developing. Volga Bulgar history depends heavily on Islamic geographical writing and on archaeological excavation of Bulgar sites on the Middle Volga. Evidence for Scandinavian activity in Eastern Europe combines archaeological finds, including burial evidence, trade goods, and fortified trading sites, with outside written testimony from Byzantine, Islamic, and Western European sources, and, for later developments, the retrospective narrative of the Rus' Primary Chronicle, compiled in the early twelfth century and examined fully in Chapter 7. That chronicle's account of ninth-century events was written more than two centuries after the fact and reflects the political and religious concerns of its own time as much as, or more than, reliable memory of the ninth century; this chapter treats it accordingly, as a source requiring careful, retrospective handling rather than a straightforward transcript of contemporary events.

Section 6.1

Eurasian Commerce Before Rus'

Before examining any individual state or people, this chapter first establishes the network itself. Chapter 2 introduced the rivers of the East European Plain as historical infrastructure rather than mere geographic backdrop, and Chapter 5 traced the diverse Slavic, Finnic, Baltic, and Turkic populations who inhabited this landscape. By the eighth century, this landscape functioned as an intermediary zone linking several distinct worlds: the Baltic Sea and, beyond it, Scandinavia and the emerging trading towns of the North and Baltic Sea coasts; the Volga and Caspian world, opening onto the Islamic caliphates of the Middle East and Central Asia; and the Black Sea and Dnieper system, opening onto Byzantium and the wider Mediterranean.

None of these worlds was isolated from the others, and Eastern Europe's rivers, rather than its comparatively difficult overland routes, provided the connective infrastructure that made large-scale, long-distance exchange practical. Boats could carry bulky goods, furs, honey, wax, and enslaved captives, far greater distances at far lower cost than pack animals or wagons over the region's often poor and seasonally impassable roads, provided that traders were willing and able to negotiate portages, the short overland stretches where boats and cargo had to be dragged or carried between navigable river segments or between separate river systems.

This network should not be imagined as a single, continuous highway running in one direction. It was instead a set of overlapping, locally organized, and only loosely coordinated segments, controlled at different points by different populations, cultures, and emerging polities, and linked together as much by successive exchanges among intermediaries as by any individual trader's continuous journey from one end to the other. A fur trapped by a Finnic hunter in the northern forests might change hands at a river market to a Slavic or Scandinavian trader, be carried south and exchanged again at a Volga Bulgar market town for silver or manufactured goods, and from there enter the wider commercial world of the Islamic caliphates through further intermediaries. Understanding this network as a chain of exchanges, rather than a single trade route in the modern sense, is essential to interpreting the archaeological and textual evidence examined throughout this chapter.

The states and peoples examined in the sections that follow, the Khazars, the Volga Bulgars, and the Scandinavian Varangians, did not create this network from nothing. They inserted themselves into, extended, taxed, and reorganized a landscape of exchange that Slavic, Finnic, and Baltic populations, together with steppe intermediaries, had already been shaping for generations. The chapter's remaining sections examine, in turn, the major political actors who organized and profited from this system, the specific geography of its two principal river corridors, the commodities that moved through it, and, finally, the ways in which control over commerce could generate new and increasingly durable forms of political authority, a development that sets the stage directly for Chapter 7's examination of the formation of Rus'.

Section 6.2

The Khazar Khaganate

The Khazar Khaganate emerged amid the mid-seventh-century fragmentation of the Western Turkic Khaganate, described in Chapter 5. As Khazar power expanded across the western Eurasian steppe, it contributed to the displacement and dispersal of Bulgar groups associated with the confederation traditionally linked to Khan Kubrat. By the later seventh century, the Khazars, a Turkic-speaking steppe people, had established political dominance across a vast territory encompassing the Pontic-Caspian steppe, the northern Caucasus, and, at its height, portions of the forest-steppe and forest zone to the north, including tributary relationships with some Eastern Slavic tribal groupings discussed in Chapter 5.

Khazar political organization is reported, in a range of outside sources, to have followed a dual pattern in which a khagan held a largely sacral, ceremonially elevated position associated with religious legitimacy and dynastic prestige, while practical political, military, and administrative authority rested with a separate official, often rendered in sources as the bek or beg. This arrangement should not be treated as a simple, unchanging constitutional structure documented with full confidence for the entire span of Khazar history; the surviving evidence for it comes chiefly from outside observers and from later, sometimes fragmentary, testimony, and its precise workings, and the degree to which it changed across the Khaganate's several centuries of existence, remain subjects of ongoing scholarly investigation.

The Khazar state governed an unusually diverse population, including Turkic-speaking groups, Iranian-speaking populations, Slavic tributaries, and communities of merchants and settlers connected to Byzantine, Islamic, and Jewish commercial and religious networks. Its capital, at various points associated with the cities of Itil, near the mouth of the Volga on the Caspian Sea, and earlier Samandar in the northern Caucasus, functioned as a major node in the commercial system examined throughout this chapter, drawing revenue from tolls, tribute, and the taxation of goods passing along the Volga and toward the Caspian.

The Khazar Khaganate's diplomatic position between Byzantium and the Islamic caliphates shaped much of its history. Khazar forces fought a long series of wars against Umayyad and, later, Abbasid armies contesting control of the Caucasus passes during the seventh and eighth centuries, at times serving as an effective barrier limiting the northward expansion of Islamic power into the steppe. Byzantine emperors, for their part, cultivated diplomatic and, on occasion, marital ties with the Khazar elite, viewing the Khaganate as a useful counterweight against other steppe powers and, at times, against the Caliphate itself. This intermediary diplomatic position, situated between two of the era's major imperial civilizations while also directly controlling access to the Volga trade route examined in Section 6.6, gave the Khazar state disproportionate strategic and commercial importance relative to its population.

Chapter 5 introduced the Primary Chronicle's later account describing certain Eastern Slavic tribal groupings as having paid tribute to the Khazars before eventually shifting allegiance to emerging Rus' authority. That account should be read with the same retrospective caution this chapter has already urged: the Primary Chronicle was compiled in Kyiv in the early twelfth century, more than two centuries after the events it describes, within a Rus' political and religious context invested in presenting the eventual displacement of Khazar authority as a meaningful historical turning point. The chronicle's specific claims about which tribes paid tribute, in what form, and for how long, cannot be independently verified in every detail, even though the broader pattern of Khazar tributary influence over parts of the southern forest-steppe in the eighth and ninth centuries is consistent with other evidence and generally accepted by historians. Students should distinguish, in other words, between the general historical plausibility of Khazar tribute-taking, which is well supported, and the chronicle's specific narrative details, which reflect a later and interested retelling.

Historical Vocabulary
Khaganate

Khaganate refers to a steppe state ruled, or nominally ruled, by a khagan, a title of Inner Asian and Turkic-Mongolic origin roughly comparable in prestige to "emperor" or "great khan," historically associated with confederations of nomadic or semi-nomadic peoples exercising authority, often through tribute and alliance, over subordinate tribes and settled populations rather than through the kind of continuous territorial administration typical of settled agrarian empires.

The term matters historically because it identifies the Khazar state's origins within a wider Eurasian steppe political tradition shared with earlier and later khaganates, including the Avar and, far to the east, the Gökturk khaganates, rather than treating Khazar political organization as a uniquely local invention. Recognizing the khaganate as a recognizable category of steppe state also helps students place Khazar political history within the recurring pattern of steppe-sedentary interaction introduced in Chapter 4 and continued throughout this textbook.

Historical Debate

The Khazar Conversion to Judaism

The question: Ancient and medieval sources report that the Khazar khagan and at least part of the ruling elite adopted Judaism at some point probably between the later eighth and ninth centuries, with a Jewish ruling court clearly attested by the early tenth century, though the sources disagree substantially on the exact date, the process, and above all the social extent of the conversion among the wider population. How should historians treat this evidence?

Interpretation A — a genuine elite conversion, cautiously affirmed: Several independent lines of evidence, including a small corpus of Hebrew-language correspondence associated with a later Khazar ruler, references in Islamic geographical writing, and later Byzantine and Rus' testimony, converge on the general claim that some portion of the Khazar ruling elite adopted Judaism. Historians holding this view generally treat the core claim of an elite conversion as reasonably well supported, while still debating its precise date, causes, and extent.
Interpretation B — a more limited or later development: Other historians caution that key sources, including the Hebrew correspondence, are difficult to date and authenticate with certainty, that outside observers had incentives to exaggerate or misreport the religious practices of a distant and diplomatically significant state, and that the conversion, if it occurred, may have been more limited in scope, timing, or political significance than older historiography sometimes assumed.

Evidence: Historians weigh the internal consistency and dating of the Hebrew correspondence, references in contemporary and near-contemporary Islamic and Byzantine sources, the absence of certain kinds of confirming archaeological evidence (such as securely identified Jewish religious architecture at Khazar sites), and the general plausibility of a steppe elite adopting a minority monotheistic tradition as a way of maintaining diplomatic independence from both Christian Byzantium and the Islamic caliphates.

Points of agreement: Most historians agree that some connection existed between the Khazar elite and Judaism, and that this connection, whatever its precise extent, was historically unusual and diplomatically significant.

Points of disagreement: Historians differ substantially over the date, extent within the population, and political motivation of the conversion, and over how much confidence the surviving evidence deserves.

Why it matters: The Khazar case illustrates how difficult it can be to establish even the basic religious history of a state known mostly through outside and retrospective sources, and cautions against treating popular or later nationalist claims about Khazar religious history, some of which have been used for unrelated modern political arguments, as more certain than the evidence supports.

Student task: Identify what kind of evidence would most strengthen or weaken each interpretation, and explain why a single Hebrew-language letter, by itself, cannot settle the question of how widely Judaism was practiced among the wider Khazar population.

Section 6.3

Volga Bulgaria

Chapter 5 traced the mid-seventh-century fragmentation of Old Great Bulgaria, the steppe confederation associated with Khan Kubrat, following his death and the subsequent dispersal of his sons and their followers under pressure from the rising Khazar Khaganate. While one branch of this dispersal moved southwest into the Balkans, eventually forming the Bulgarian polity examined in later chapters of this textbook, another branch migrated northward and eastward, eventually settling along the Middle Volga and Kama river confluence, a region of fertile land and strategic commercial position at the northern reach of the Volga trade corridor examined in Section 6.6.

By the ninth century, this Middle Volga Bulgar population had developed into a distinct and increasingly organized polity, generally known to historians as Volga Bulgaria, ruled by its own royal line and governing a population that included Bulgar Turkic speakers together with neighboring Finnic and Uralic populations, some incorporated as tributaries and others as trading partners. Volga Bulgaria should not be understood as a simple northern branch or colony of the Balkan Bulgarian state; the two Bulgar successor societies developed along substantially different trajectories, in different environments, among different neighboring populations, and, as this section will show, eventually adopted different religious traditions.

Volga Bulgaria's commercial importance rested on its position at the point where the northern forest zone, rich in furs and other forest products gathered chiefly by neighboring Finnic populations, met the long-distance Volga trade route leading south toward the Khazar sphere and the Caspian. Bulgar towns, most notably the capital city conventionally called Bulgar, functioned as major market centers where northern furs, honey, wax, and other forest goods were exchanged for silver, textiles, and manufactured goods arriving from the Khazar lands, the Caspian world, and, ultimately, the Islamic caliphates.

The most decisive and best-documented event in early Volga Bulgar history is the ruler's adoption of Islam, conventionally dated to 922 and closely associated with the arrival of an official Abbasid embassy from Baghdad, of which the writer Ibn Fadlan, examined in this chapter's Primary Source Window, was a member. According to Ibn Fadlan's own account, the Bulgar ruler had already adopted Islam and sought the embassy's assistance chiefly in the form of financial and, some scholars argue, informal military support against Khazar pressure, rather than religious instruction as such; the embassy's role therefore appears to have been diplomatic and confirmatory rather than the sole cause of an otherwise sudden religious transformation. Islam's subsequent spread among the wider Bulgar population over the following decades and centuries should be understood as a gradual process of consolidation rather than a single completed event in 922 itself.

Volga Bulgaria's adoption of Islam carried significant long-term consequences. It aligned the Bulgar state culturally and commercially with the wider Islamic world at precisely the period when Islamic silver and manufactured goods were flowing most heavily along the Volga route, reinforced Bulgar independence from Khazar political and religious influence, and established a durable Islamic religious and cultural presence on the Middle Volga. That regional Islamic tradition persisted, in transformed form, through the later political upheavals this textbook examines, including the Mongol conquest of the Rus' principalities in Chapter 16, the Middle Volga's own incorporation into the Mongol-successor khanates, and the eventual conquest of Kazan under Ivan IV examined in Chapter 23, remaining a defining feature of the modern Volga-Ural region and its Tatar and Bashkir populations.

Section 6.4

Scandinavian Expansion into Eastern Europe

Scandinavian expansion into Eastern Europe formed part of a much larger pattern of Scandinavian maritime and riverine activity across the North Atlantic, the British Isles, Frankish territory, and the Mediterranean during the eighth through eleventh centuries, an era conventionally, if imprecisely, labeled the Viking Age in general historical usage. Advances in shipbuilding, including shallow-draft vessels capable of both open-sea travel and river navigation, together with social and political conditions within Scandinavian societies that rewarded successful raiding, trading, and the acquisition of wealth and prestige abroad, drove Scandinavian populations outward across an unusually wide geographic range in these centuries.

Eastward expansion across the Baltic Sea followed a somewhat different pattern from the raiding voyages more familiar from Scandinavian activity in Western Europe. While raiding certainly occurred, the eastern routes into the Gulf of Finland and the river systems beyond it were oriented at least as strongly toward trade, tribute collection, and, in time, settlement and intermarriage with local Slavic, Finnic, and Baltic populations, as toward plunder. Archaeological evidence from sites such as Ladoga, known in Old Norse sources as Aldeigjuborg and today identified with the settlement of Staraya Ladoga near Lake Ladoga, documents a mixed population from at least the eighth century onward, including material culture associated with Scandinavian, Finnic, and Slavic communities living and, evidently, trading in close proximity, sometimes within the same settlement.

The specific motivations drawing Scandinavians eastward combined several factors examined earlier in this chapter: the demand for northern furs and other forest products within the Islamic world, reachable via the Volga route examined in Section 6.6; the opportunity to acquire Islamic silver, which could then be carried back to Scandinavia, converted into ornament, hoarded, or used to underwrite prestige and political standing at home; and the more general Scandinavian pattern of seeking wealth, status, and opportunity through long-distance travel, trade, and, where advantageous, mercenary military service, discussed further in Section 6.5.

It would be a serious historical distortion, however, to treat Scandinavian arrival as though it introduced trade, political organization, or settled life into an otherwise empty or undeveloped Eastern Europe. Chapter 5 has already demonstrated that Slavic, Finnic, and Baltic populations occupied, cultivated, and traded across this landscape well before the eighth century, and this chapter's Section 6.2 and 6.3 have shown that the Khazar Khaganate and, somewhat later, Volga Bulgaria had already established substantial political and commercial infrastructure along the very routes Scandinavian traders now began to use. Scandinavian expansion into Eastern Europe is therefore best understood not as the founding of a new commercial world but as the insertion of an additional highly mobile population into an already functioning, already sophisticated Eurasian network, a network that Scandinavian participation would help extend, intensify, and, in time, reorganize politically, a development examined further in Section 6.9 and, in full, in Chapter 7.

Section 6.5

The Varangians

The term Varangian, appearing in Old East Slavic, Byzantine Greek, and, in related forms, Islamic and Old Norse sources, requires careful definition. It is narrower and more historically specific than the modern popular label "Viking," which today is often applied loosely to any Scandinavian of the relevant centuries, whether raider, trader, settler, or farmer, and which was not itself the primary term contemporaries used for Scandinavian activity in Eastern Europe. "Varangian" instead denoted, with some variation across sources and periods, Scandinavian traders, warriors, and, especially from the tenth century onward, mercenaries serving Rus' princes or, further south, the Byzantine emperor, who eventually maintained an elite mercenary formation, the Varangian Guard, substantially recruited from Scandinavian and, later, other northern European volunteers.

Distinct from, though closely related to, the term Varangian is the term Rus itself, whose origin and precise early meaning remain genuinely debated among historians, a debate this chapter introduces without resolving, since its fuller treatment belongs to Chapter 7's examination of the Normanist controversy. What can be said at this stage, consistent with the outside evidence examined in this chapter, is that outside observers, including Ibn Fadlan, used some form of the term Rus in the early tenth century to describe a specific group of Scandinavian-associated traders active on the Volga, distinguishing them from other populations they encountered, while the term's later political meaning, as the name of an emerging state and dynasty examined in Chapter 7, developed through a more complex and contested process than a simple, direct transfer of an ethnic label onto a political entity.

Archaeological and textual evidence together suggest that Scandinavian activity in Eastern Europe during this period should be understood as operating through mixed, multiethnic networks rather than through exclusively Scandinavian communities acting in isolation. Scandinavian traders and warriors intermarried with local Slavic and Finnic populations, adopted elements of local material culture, and, in turn, introduced Scandinavian objects, burial customs, and, in some contexts, political titles and practices into the communities where they settled. Fortified trading sites such as Ladoga, and, somewhat later, other emerging centers examined more fully in Chapter 7, functioned as points of genuine cultural mixture rather than enclaves of a single, unified Scandinavian population transplanted wholesale into Eastern Europe.

This section deliberately stops short of narrating the political formation of a Rus' state, the arrival or invitation of specific named rulers such as Rurik, or the consolidation of authority at Novgorod and Kyiv, all of which belong properly to Chapter 7. What matters for this chapter's purposes is that, by the ninth century, Scandinavian-associated traders and warriors, operating through mixed networks alongside Slavic, Finnic, and Baltic populations, had become active and increasingly important participants in the commercial systems examined throughout this chapter, laying part of the groundwork, though not by themselves determining the outcome, for the political developments Chapter 7 examines in detail.

Historical Vocabulary
Varangian

Varangian (Old East Slavic variag, Byzantine Greek Varangos) refers, in the historical sources of this period, chiefly to Scandinavian traders, warriors, and mercenaries active in Eastern Europe and, via Rus'-controlled routes, in Byzantine imperial service. It is a narrower and more source-specific term than the modern popular label "Viking," and its precise scope varied somewhat across different sources and periods.

The term matters historically because it identifies a specific, source-attested category of Scandinavian activity in this region, distinct from the broader and more loosely used modern term "Viking," and because it will remain relevant in Chapter 7's discussion of the so-called Varangian Question and the Normanist controversy over the origins of Rus' political authority. Students should avoid assuming that "Varangian" and "Rus" were simply synonymous terms for the same, single, ethnically uniform group.

Section 6.6

The Volga Trade Route

The Volga trade route linked the Baltic and Lake Ladoga region, in the north, to the Caspian Sea and, through it, the wider Islamic commercial world, in the south. Traders moving southward from the Baltic entered the river system via Lake Ladoga and the Volkhov River, crossed a network of interconnected lakes and smaller rivers, and, through a series of portages, the overland stretches where boats and cargo had to be dragged or carried between navigable segments, reached the upper Volga. From there, the river itself provided continuous navigable passage southward past the emerging territory of Volga Bulgaria, examined in Section 6.3, and onward through Khazar-controlled territory toward the Caspian Sea.

This route was neither swift nor safe. Portages required substantial labor, exposed traders and their cargo to potential raiding, and depended on cooperative or at least tolerant relations with the populations, Finnic, Slavic, Bulgar, and Khazar, who controlled the land through which portages passed. Seasonal conditions, including the timing of ice melt and river flow, shaped when journeys could practically be undertaken, and travel along the full length of the route, from the Baltic to the Caspian, likely occurred in stages, with different traders and intermediaries handling different segments, rather than as a single continuous voyage undertaken by one party from start to finish.

Volga Bulgaria, examined in Section 6.3, functioned as a critical intermediary node on this route, a market center where northern furs and other forest goods, gathered chiefly through the labor of Finnic populations, were exchanged for southern goods, above all Islamic silver dirhams, arriving via Khazar territory and the Caspian trade with Central Asia and the wider Abbasid commercial world. Ibn Fadlan's 921–922 account, examined in this chapter's Primary Source Window, describes this Bulgar market world directly, including his description of a group of Rus merchants he encountered conducting trade, likely in furs and enslaved captives, at a Volga riverside market.

The enormous quantities of Islamic silver dirhams recovered by archaeologists in hoards across the Baltic region, Scandinavia, and the river valleys of Eastern Europe provide the single strongest body of material evidence for the Volga route's scale and intensity during the ninth and tenth centuries. Coin hoards allow historians to trace, with considerable precision, the changing mint origins, dates, and quantities of dirhams reaching different parts of Northern and Eastern Europe over time, offering a kind of quantitative economic evidence rarely available for other aspects of this early period. A decline in the flow of fresh Islamic silver reaching the Baltic during the later tenth and eleventh centuries, associated with fiscal and political changes within the Islamic world's own silver-producing regions, in turn had significant consequences for the commercial systems examined in this chapter, consequences this textbook will trace further in Chapter 7's account of the Dnieper route's growing relative importance.

Section 6.7

The Dnieper Route to Byzantium

A second major corridor, conventionally known from later Rus' chronicle tradition as the route "from the Varangians to the Greeks," connected the Baltic and northern river systems, via a network including the Western Dvina and Lovat rivers, to the Dnieper River, and, following the Dnieper south to its mouth on the Black Sea, onward by sea to Constantinople and the wider Byzantine world. Like the Volga route examined in Section 6.6, this corridor required multiple portages linking separate river systems, and its southern stretches, where the Dnieper passed through a series of dangerous rapids near the great bend of the river, posed particular hazards from both the river's own currents and from steppe populations, including Pechenegs and other steppe confederations introduced in Chapter 5, capable of ambushing vessels and portage parties at these vulnerable points.

The Byzantine emperor Constantine VII Porphyrogenitus, in his administrative treatise De Administrando Imperio, compiled at his court around 948–952, provides a detailed Byzantine description of this route as it functioned in his own mid-tenth-century era, including its rapids, the labor of portaging boats around them, and the political relationships between Rus' authorities and neighboring steppe populations along the route. This source is valuable precisely because it offers a near-contemporary, outside Byzantine perspective on Dnieper-route conditions in the mid-tenth century, but students should recognize that it describes conditions from that specific period and administrative viewpoint, and should not be uncritically projected backward to describe every earlier period this chapter examines.

Access to Constantinople carried enormous economic and political significance for populations controlling the Dnieper route's northern approaches. Constantinople was one of the largest and wealthiest urban and commercial centers accessible from Eastern Europe, offering silk, wine, fine metalwork, and other luxury goods in exchange for northern furs, wax, honey, and, as this chapter's next section examines, enslaved captives. Byzantine sources record a Rus' raid on Constantinople itself, conventionally dated to 860, well before the political developments examined in Chapter 7, demonstrating that Rus'-identified groups were already capable of operating militarily in the Black Sea–Constantinople sphere at a relatively early date, seeking not only trade but also, on at least this occasion, plunder and, ultimately, a more secure ongoing commercial and diplomatic relationship with the Byzantine state. The 860 raid establishes that such groups could reach Constantinople by this period; it does not, by itself, prove that they already followed the specific, fully developed Dnieper itinerary that Constantine VII would describe a century later.

This chapter presents the Dnieper route's ninth-century activity as an important precondition for, rather than a full narrative of, the later Rus'-Byzantine relationship. The subsequent political and military history of Rus'-Byzantine relations, including the treaties, campaigns, and diplomatic exchanges associated with rulers such as Oleg, Igor, Olga, and Sviatoslav, is examined in full in Chapter 8; this chapter's task is only to establish the route's geography, its risks, and its Black Sea terminus as part of the wider commercial system within which Rus' political authority would eventually take shape.

Section 6.8

Silver, Slaves, Furs, and Long-Distance Commerce

Understanding what actually moved through the Volga and Dnieper networks, examined in Sections 6.6 and 6.7, requires attention to specific commodities, the labor and coercion that produced them, and the intermediaries who moved them along the route, rather than a simple list of trade goods treated in the abstract.

Furs, above all from martens, squirrels, beavers, and other forest animals, formed one of the network's most important northern exports, gathered chiefly through the hunting and trapping labor of Finnic and other forest populations examined in Chapter 5, often, historians believe, under tribute obligations owed to Slavic, Scandinavian, Khazar, or Bulgar authorities rather than through direct market sale alone. Wax and honey, gathered from wild and, increasingly, managed beehives across the forest and forest-steppe zones, provided further valuable northern exports, wax being especially prized for candles and other uses across the Byzantine and Islamic worlds.

Enslaved captives constituted another major, and historically significant, category of commerce moving along both the Volga and Dnieper routes. Ibn Fadlan's account, examined in this chapter's Primary Source Window, explicitly describes the Rus merchants he encountered as engaged in the slave trade, and other outside sources corroborate the significance of slave trading, often involving captives taken in raiding or warfare among neighboring Slavic, Finnic, Baltic, and steppe populations, along these routes toward both the Islamic world via the Volga and, to a lesser but still significant extent, the Byzantine world via the Dnieper. The English word "slave" itself derives, through a chain of medieval linguistic borrowing, from "Slav," a linguistic fact that reflects, without by itself proving every specific historical claim about who enslaved whom, the historical prominence of Slavic-speaking populations among those captured and sold into slavery along these Eurasian networks during this period. Students should understand slave trading in this context as an integral, rather than incidental, part of the commercial system examined throughout this chapter, involving coercion and violence that fell disproportionately on populations with the least political and military power to resist raiding and capture.

Silver, chiefly in the form of Islamic dirhams examined in Section 6.6, moved in the opposite direction, northward from the Volga Bulgar and Khazar markets toward the Baltic and Scandinavia, alongside smaller quantities of manufactured goods including glass beads, fine textiles, and metalwork. Silver served multiple functions: as a medium of exchange facilitating further trade, as a store of wealth that could be hoarded, melted down, or fashioned into ornament, and, for successful traders and warriors returning to Scandinavia, as a tangible marker of prestige and standing within their home communities.

Students should resist the temptation to imagine a single, tidy commodity chain running continuously from Scandinavia to Baghdad or from the northern forests to Constantinople. Archaeological and textual evidence instead suggests a system of repeated, localized exchanges among multiple intermediaries, Finnic trappers, Slavic cultivators and traders, Scandinavian long-distance carriers, Khazar and Bulgar market authorities, and, at the network's southern termini, Islamic and Byzantine merchants and officials, each capturing a portion of the value at each stage of exchange. This layered, intermediary-dependent structure helps explain both the network's remarkable geographic reach and its vulnerability to disruption at any of its many points of transfer.

Section 6.9

Trade Networks and the Origins of Political Authority

This section brings together the chapter's preceding analysis to address a central analytical question: how could control over trade routes, tribute collection, and fortified river nodes generate increasingly durable forms of political authority among the diverse populations examined in this chapter? The answer developed here is deliberately cautious. Commerce helped create conditions favorable to new and more durable political authority; it did not mechanically or inevitably produce any single political outcome, including the formation of a Rus' state, a subject this chapter leaves for Chapter 7 to examine in full.

Several interlocking mechanisms connected commercial activity to political power across the network examined in this chapter. First, control over strategic points along the Volga and Dnieper routes, portages, river confluences, and market towns, allowed whoever held such points to levy tolls, tribute, or protection payments on traders passing through, converting geographic position into a reliable revenue stream. The Khazar Khaganate's control over the lower Volga and the approaches to the Caspian, examined in Section 6.2, and Volga Bulgaria's position at the critical Middle Volga junction between northern forest goods and southern Islamic markets, examined in Section 6.3, both illustrate this mechanism directly.

Second, the wealth generated through such tolls, tribute, and market taxation could be converted into military and political resources: hiring or rewarding armed retainers, including, in some contexts, Scandinavian mercenaries and traders examined in Section 6.5, constructing and garrisoning fortified towns, and underwriting diplomatic relationships with powerful neighbors such as Byzantium and the Islamic caliphates. Political authority built on this foundation differed in character from authority based purely on control over agricultural land and its cultivators, since it depended on the continued flow of long-distance trade and could, in principle, be disrupted by changes in trade routes, in the political stability of trading partners, or in the availability of key commodities such as Islamic silver, discussed in Section 6.6.

Third, the fortified trading settlements examined throughout this chapter, including Ladoga and the Bulgar capital, together with similar emerging centers along the Dnieper route that Chapter 7 will examine directly, functioned not merely as economic nodes but as sites where diverse populations, Slavic, Finnic, Baltic, Scandinavian, and steppe, came into sustained, repeated contact under conditions that rewarded some degree of shared rules, mutual accommodation, and organized leadership capable of arbitrating disputes, protecting trade, and coordinating defense. Such conditions, historians argue, could favor the emergence of leaders and, eventually, more institutionalized political authority capable of drawing on wealth, prestige, and military capacity derived from commerce, even though the specific form such authority would take, and which population or dynasty would ultimately establish it, remained historically open and contingent rather than predetermined by the commercial system itself.

This section's argument should not be mistaken for a claim that trade alone, or any single factor examined in this chapter, explains the subsequent political formation of Rus'. Rather, the commercial, tributary, and multiethnic conditions examined throughout this chapter created a landscape of opportunity, wealth, contact, and competition within which new political authority could plausibly emerge, without guaranteeing that it would take the specific form, timing, or location that Chapter 7 will go on to describe. Readers should carry this cautious, conditions-not-causes framing directly into that chapter's examination of the formation of Rus'.

Primary Source Window

Ibn Fadlan, Travel Account (921–922)

Creator: Ibn Fadlan, secretary to an Abbasid embassy from Baghdad · Date: 921–922 · Type: Travel and diplomatic-mission narrative

Context: Ibn Fadlan accompanied an Abbasid diplomatic mission sent from Baghdad to the ruler of Volga Bulgaria, examined in Section 6.3. His surviving account describes the journey north, the Bulgar ruler's court, and, in a widely studied passage, a group of merchants he identifies with a term rendered in English as "Rus," whom he encountered trading on the Volga, most likely near a Bulgar market settlement.

Brief paraphrase of the account (not a verbatim translated quotation):

Ibn Fadlan describes the Rus traders he observed as physically striking, heavily tattooed, and armed, arriving by boat with goods including furs and enslaved captives for sale. He describes their encampments, their religious practices involving wooden idols to whom they made offerings in connection with trade, and, in a separate and much-discussed passage, an elaborate funeral ceremony for a deceased Rus leader involving cremation aboard a ship, animal sacrifice, and the death of an enslaved woman whom Ibn Fadlan reports as having volunteered to accompany her master in death, although the coercive circumstances of her enslaved and profoundly unequal status make the meaning of that apparent consent difficult for historians to assess.

Analysis: Ibn Fadlan's account is invaluable as a rare, roughly contemporary outside description of Rus-identified traders on the Volga, offering ethnographic detail unavailable from any other tenth-century source. It must nonetheless be read critically. He was a cultured, urban, Muslim court secretary describing people whose customs he clearly found strange, sometimes admirable in specific respects such as physical vigor, and elsewhere distasteful, particularly regarding hygiene and certain funeral practices; his account reflects his own cultural vantage point and rhetorical purposes as much as neutral ethnographic observation. Furthermore, his "Rus" cannot automatically be equated with the later political entity of Rus' examined in Chapter 7; he describes a specific group of traders encountered at a specific place and time, not a fully formed state or a single, unified ethnic nation.

Questions for students:

  1. What does Ibn Fadlan's account suggest about the economic activities of the Rus traders he observed, and how does this connect to Section 6.8's discussion of commodities?
  2. Why might a Muslim court official's description of the Rus combine admiration and distaste, and how should historians read such mixed reactions?
  3. What are the risks of treating Ibn Fadlan's description of "the Rus" as evidence for the ethnic or political identity of the later Rus' state examined in Chapter 7?
  4. What kind of additional evidence would help historians evaluate how representative Ibn Fadlan's specific observations were of Rus commercial activity more broadly?
Multiple Perspectives

Along the River Routes

A Finnic hunter-trapper of the northern forests: For a hunter supplying furs that would eventually travel the Volga route, participation in the wider commercial system likely arrived chiefly in the form of tribute obligations owed to Slavic, Scandinavian, or Bulgar intermediaries, converting forest labor into goods that entered a trade network whose distant southern markets, and the silver they generated, the trapper himself would likely never see directly.
A Scandinavian trader-warrior at Ladoga: For a Scandinavian participant in the eastern trade, the river routes offered a path to silver, prestige, and opportunity unavailable at home, achieved through a combination of trading, tribute collection, and, where advantageous, mercenary service or raiding, within networks that required cooperation and accommodation with Slavic, Finnic, Khazar, and Bulgar populations as much as displays of force.
A Volga Bulgar market official: For an official managing trade at a Bulgar river town, the arrival of Rus merchants, Finnic furs, and southern silver represented an opportunity to tax and regulate exchange, reinforcing the ruler's revenue and diplomatic standing, but also required careful management of relations with Khazar authorities to the south and forest populations to the north.

These perspectives illustrate why this chapter treats the network as a system of interdependent, unequal, and sometimes coercive relationships rather than a simple story of adventurous merchants moving freely across an open frontier.

Cause and Effect

From Commerce to Political Authority

Long-Term Conditions

The river geography of the East European Plain, examined in Chapter 2, and the preexisting Slavic, Finnic, Baltic, and Turkic settlement and tribute patterns examined in Chapter 5.

Intermediate Developments

The rise of the Khazar Khaganate and, later, Volga Bulgaria as states capable of taxing and protecting trade; the seventh- and eighth-century expansion of the Islamic caliphates and their demand for northern goods; growing Scandinavian maritime mobility.

Short-Term Triggers

The intensifying flow of Islamic silver dirhams along the Volga from the ninth century; increasing Scandinavian settlement at nodes such as Ladoga; growing multiethnic contact and competition at fortified river towns.

Effects

Wealth, prestige, and military resources increasingly concentrated at strategic river nodes; conditions favorable to new, more durable, commercially based political authority, examined further in Chapter 7's account of the formation of Rus'.

Historical complexity: Commerce created conditions favorable to new political authority; it did not by itself determine which population, dynasty, or state would emerge, or guarantee that any single state would emerge at all.

Continuity and Change Over Time

The Pre-Rus' Networks

What Stayed the Same

  • Rivers remained the essential infrastructure of long-distance exchange throughout the period examined in this chapter and well beyond it.
  • Tribute-taking by dominant political authorities over subordinate populations, already visible in Chapter 5's account of Avar and early Khazar tribute, continued as a basic mechanism of resource extraction.
  • Multiethnic contact and intermediary-based exchange, rather than direct end-to-end trading by any single group, remained the network's basic operating structure.

What Changed

  • The scale and distance of exchange expanded enormously from the eighth century onward, linking Scandinavia directly to the Islamic world and Byzantium through Eastern European intermediaries.
  • Scandinavian participants became an increasingly significant new element within networks previously dominated by Slavic, Finnic, Khazar, and Bulgar actors.
  • Political authority at key nodes grew more concentrated and more clearly linked to control over trade and tribute, setting the stage for the political consolidation examined in Chapter 7.

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both continuity and change helps students see the formation of Rus' as an outgrowth of long-developing regional systems rather than a sudden, externally imposed event.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Portage Market Town

For most people who lived along the Volga and Dnieper corridors, direct participation in long-distance trade was probably occasional rather than constant, woven into the seasonal rhythms of agriculture, hunting, and tribute payment examined in Chapter 5. A small riverside settlement near a portage might see its population multiply briefly during the trading season, as traders, boat crews, and porters arrived to move goods overland between navigable stretches, generating temporary demand for food, shelter, labor, and protection, before returning to a quieter, smaller community for much of the year.

Archaeological evidence from sites such as Ladoga suggests dense, mixed occupation, with evidence of craft production, including metalworking and boat repair, alongside imported goods such as glass beads and, occasionally, higher-status objects that moved along the trade network examined in this chapter. Life in such a settlement likely combined ordinary agricultural and forest-based subsistence with periodic, intense involvement in trade, tribute collection, and, at times, the risks of raiding or seizure that accompanied a commercial world in which enslaved captives, examined in Section 6.8, were themselves a traded commodity.

Regional Context

The Forest Zone and the Networks

The commercial systems examined in this chapter depended fundamentally on the northern forest zone examined in Chapter 2, home to the Finnic and other populations discussed in Chapter 5, whose hunting, trapping, and beekeeping labor produced the furs, wax, and honey that gave the entire network much of its economic value. Yet the forest zone itself rarely appears as a political actor in the outside sources this chapter has examined, Islamic geographical writing, Byzantine diplomatic treatises, and later Rus' chronicle tradition, all of which were composed from the vantage point of southern, commercially and politically dominant centers.

This asymmetry in the surviving evidence should caution students against assuming that the forest populations who supplied the network's most valuable exports were passive or powerless; archaeological evidence, together with the tribute relationships discussed throughout this chapter, suggests active, if unequal, participation and negotiation rather than simple, uncontested extraction. The forest zone's central economic role, despite its marginal position in most surviving written sources, exemplifies a pattern this textbook will revisit in later chapters examining Siberian expansion and the fur trade in Chapters 24 and 28.

Russia and the World

Islamic Silver and the Eurasian Economy

The Islamic silver dirham, examined throughout this chapter, illustrates how developments far outside Eastern Europe directly shaped the region's history. Ninth-century flows reaching the Baltic were dominated by Abbasid coinage, while from around 900 onward Samanid dirhams minted in Central Asia became especially important; in both phases, the Islamic world's own silver mining and minting capacity greatly intensified a commercial network that Slavic, Finnic, Khazar, and Bulgar populations had already been building. When that minting capacity later declined, owing to factors internal to the Islamic world's own political and economic history, the flow of fresh silver into the Baltic diminished correspondingly, a change with significant consequences for the commercial systems examined here and their political successors.

This connection should be read as a two-way relationship rather than a one-directional flow of Islamic influence outward. Northern furs, and enslaved captives, moved southward into Islamic and Byzantine markets in exchange for that silver, meaning that the economic life of Baghdad and Constantinople was itself, to a modest but real degree, sustained by goods extracted from the forests and river valleys of Eastern Europe. The later, more fully documented relationships between Rus', and eventually Russian, polities and the wider world, examined throughout this textbook, had genuine antecedents in this early, already global pattern of exchange.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has served as the capstone of Part I, drawing together the geography, archaeology, ancient steppe history, and ethnolinguistic landscape examined in Chapters 2 through 5 into an account of the commercial and political systems that linked Eastern Europe to Scandinavia, Byzantium, and the Islamic world on the eve of the formation of Rus'.

The chapter began by establishing Eastern Europe's rivers as the infrastructure of a layered, intermediary-dependent Eurasian commercial network rather than a single continuous trade route, before examining, in turn, the two major regional states that organized and profited from this system: the Khazar Khaganate, a multiethnic steppe polity whose dual rulership, diplomatic position between Byzantium and the Islamic caliphates, and reported elite adoption of Judaism all required careful, source-critical treatment, and Volga Bulgaria, a Middle Volga commercial society whose 922 adoption of Islam, associated with Ibn Fadlan's embassy, marked a major and durable religious and cultural turning point on the Volga.

The chapter then examined Scandinavian expansion into Eastern Europe as part of a wider pattern of Scandinavian maritime activity, carefully distinguishing the specific historical term Varangian from the broader modern label "Viking," and emphasizing that Scandinavian participants entered and extended, rather than created, already sophisticated commercial systems built up by Slavic, Finnic, Baltic, Khazar, and Bulgar populations.

Two major trade corridors received sustained attention: the Volga route linking the Baltic to the Caspian and the Islamic world through the intermediary market world of Volga Bulgaria, and the Dnieper route linking the Baltic to Byzantium through the hazardous rapids and steppe-adjacent territory of the Dnieper's southern reaches. The chapter examined the specific commodities moving through these networks, furs, wax, honey, silver, and enslaved captives, and the systems of tribute, labor, and coercion, including systematic slave trading, that produced and moved them.

The chapter's analytical climax examined how control over trade routes, tribute, and fortified river nodes could generate increasingly durable political authority, while explicitly cautioning that commerce created conditions favorable to new political authority rather than mechanically or inevitably producing any single specific political outcome, including the formation of Rus' itself.

Throughout, the chapter modeled careful, source-critical historical reasoning, distinguishing archaeological evidence, such as Islamic silver hoards, from outside written testimony, such as Ibn Fadlan's account and Constantine VII's Byzantine treatise, and from later retrospective chronicle tradition, whose specific claims about earlier centuries require particular caution.

These lessons, careful use of multiple evidence types, attention to multiethnic and intermediary-based commercial systems, and a cautious, conditions-not-causes approach to political change, prepare students directly for Chapter 7's examination of the formation of Rus', which will take up directly the political consolidation this chapter has deliberately left open.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Khaganate
A steppe state ruled, or nominally ruled, by a khagan, associated with confederations exercising tribute-based authority over subordinate populations.
Khagan
A sacral or ceremonially elevated ruling title associated with Khazar and other Inner Asian steppe political traditions.
Bek
An executive official reported to have held practical political and military authority within the Khazar dual-rulership system.
Tribute
Goods or payments owed by a subordinate population to a dominant political authority, distinct from voluntary market trade.
Dirham
A standardized silver coin minted across the Islamic world, widely circulated as a medium of exchange along the Volga and Baltic networks.
Portage
An overland stretch where boats and cargo were carried or dragged between navigable river segments.
Emporium
A market or trading center serving as a hub of exchange between different populations or commercial zones.
Varangian
A historically specific term for Scandinavian traders, warriors, and mercenaries active in Eastern Europe and Byzantine service.
Rus (as term)
A name applied by outside observers, including Ibn Fadlan, to certain Scandinavian-associated traders, whose later political meaning is examined in Chapter 7.
Ibn Fadlan
A tenth-century Abbasid secretary whose 921–922 travel account is the chapter's central Primary Source.
Volga route
The trade corridor linking the Baltic to the Caspian and Islamic world via Volga Bulgaria and Khazar territory.
Dnieper route
The trade corridor linking the Baltic to Byzantium via the Dnieper River and Black Sea, known in chronicle tradition as the route "from the Varangians to the Greeks."
Ladoga (Staraya Ladoga)
An early mixed Scandinavian-Finnic-Slavic trading settlement near Lake Ladoga.
Silver hoard
An archaeologically recovered cache of buried coins, chiefly Islamic dirhams, used to trace the scale and chronology of long-distance trade.
Fur trade
The exchange of forest-trapped furs, gathered chiefly by Finnic populations, as a major northern export within this chapter's networks.
Slave trade
The systematic trade in enslaved captives moving along the Volga and Dnieper routes toward Islamic and Byzantine markets.
Tributary polity
A political community obligated to pay tribute to a dominant outside authority while typically retaining internal self-governance.
Islamic geography
The tradition of geographical and travel writing within the Islamic world, including Ibn Fadlan's account, describing distant peoples and regions.
Byzantine diplomacy
Byzantium's characteristic use of treaties, titles, marriage alliances, and administrative description, exemplified by Constantine VII's treatise, to manage relations with neighboring peoples.
Political economy
The relationship between economic activity, such as trade and tribute, and the development of political power and institutions.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has served as the capstone of Part I, showing how the geography, archaeology, and diverse peoples examined in the preceding five chapters became joined together, by the eighth and ninth centuries, within a far-reaching Eurasian system of river commerce, tribute, migration, and diplomacy linking Scandinavia and the Baltic to the Volga, the Caspian, the Black Sea, Byzantium, and the Islamic world. The Khazar Khaganate and Volga Bulgaria emerge from this chapter not as peripheral curiosities but as substantial states in their own right, whose political structures, religious histories, and commercial power shaped the environment within which later Rus' history would unfold.

The chapter's central methodological lesson, that commerce created conditions favorable to new political authority without determining any single specific outcome, prepares students directly for Chapter 7's examination of how political authority specifically identified with Rus' actually took shape amid the networks this chapter has described. Readers should carry forward this chapter's insistence that Scandinavian participants entered and reshaped, rather than created, an already sophisticated Eastern European commercial world.

As the textbook proceeds to Part II and Chapter 7's direct treatment of the formation of Rus', students should retain this chapter's critical habits: distinguishing trade from tribute, migration from state formation, archaeological evidence from later chronicle narrative, and ethnicity from political function, habits that will prove essential for evaluating the contested evidence, including the Normanist controversy, that Chapter 7 examines in detail.
