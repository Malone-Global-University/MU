Part X — Stalinism and the Second World War
58

Culture, Nationalities, and Soviet Foreign Policy in the 1930s

A History of Russia: From the Eurasian World to the Russian Federation

Chapter Opening

In 1932 Stalin is reported by witnesses to have described Soviet writers as "engineers of human souls," a phrase Andrei Zhdanov repeated at the Writers' Congress of 1934. Whatever its exact wording on the original occasion, it captures the ambition of the decade: the state sought to build not only factories and collective farms but also a new kind of citizen.

On 15 May 1935 the first Moscow Metro system opened to the public. It had thirteen stations over approximately 11.2 kilometers, comprising the main Sokolniki–Park Kultury route and a branch toward Smolenskaya, and its stations were lit by chandeliers and faced with marble from quarries across the Soviet Union. Tens of thousands of Muscovites rode the trains on the first days, many of them for the sheer pleasure of descending into what the press called a "palace of the people." The line had been built by thousands of workers, including many women and many peasant migrants new to the city, and it served the government's purposes handsomely: it was a functioning piece of urban infrastructure, a showcase of Soviet engineering, and a work of propaganda that told citizens what the future was supposed to look like. The Metro is a useful emblem for this chapter because it combined genuine achievement with political stagecraft, and because those two qualities were rarely separable in the Soviet 1930s.

Within a few months of the Metro's opening, the Soviet Union was also doing other things. In January 1936 the party newspaper Pravda condemned the opera of Dmitri Shostakovich, one of the most gifted composers of the century, under a headline that translates as "Muddle Instead of Music." In June 1936 the government forbade most abortions after a nationwide public "discussion" that was far from free. In September and October 1937 the NKVD loaded roughly 172,000 Soviet Koreans onto freight trains in the Far East and sent them to Kazakhstan and Uzbekistan, on the grounds that they might serve Japanese intelligence. In 1938 Russian became a compulsory subject in the schools of the non-Russian republics. And on the night of 23 August 1939, in the Kremlin, the foreign ministers of the Soviet Union and Nazi Germany signed a nonaggression treaty and a secret protocol that divided Eastern Europe into spheres of influence, only four years after the Soviet Union had joined the League of Nations and had signed a mutual-assistance treaty directed against precisely that German threat.

This chapter treats these developments together because they belonged to a single political system. The same leadership, the same party apparatus, and often the same officials presided over cultural policy, the governance of dozens of nationalities, and the conduct of diplomacy. The chapter is organized in three parts. The first (§§58.1–58.8) examines how the state tried to supervise literature, film, music, architecture, schooling, science, religion, and family life, and how writers, teachers, believers, and ordinary households responded. The second (§§58.9–58.15) examines the Soviet Union as a multinational federation and the shift away from the 1920s policy of korenizatsiya, with attention to Ukraine, Belarus, Kazakhstan, Central Asia, the Caucasus, the Volga region, and the Far East. The third (§§58.16–58.25) follows Soviet diplomacy from the search for collective security against Germany and Japan to the agreement of August 1939.

Students should keep in mind that the Soviet Union was not Russia. It was a federal state of eleven union republics by the end of 1936, with an ethnic Russian majority that was real but not overwhelming (roughly three-fifths of the population in the census of January 1939). The cultural policies discussed here were therefore experienced differently in Moscow, Kyiv, Minsk, Tbilisi, Baku, Tashkent, Alma-Ata, and Birobidzhan. Similarly, the foreign policy discussed here was made in Moscow, but its consequences fell heavily on Poland, Czechoslovakia, Spain, China, Finland, the Baltic states, and, in the end, on Soviet citizens themselves. The chapter therefore moves between the center and the periphery, and between the Soviet state and the societies that it affected.

The evidence for these subjects is unusually varied and unusually uneven. Published literature, films, and newspapers survive in abundance but were produced under censorship. Party and government decrees survive, and since 1991 many internal Soviet documents have become available to historians, including the NKVD reports on the Korean deportation and the Politburo decisions on Spain. Diplomatic archives in Britain, France, Germany, Poland, Czechoslovakia, and Russia allow the negotiations of 1938–1939 to be reconstructed from several sides, though the Russian archival record on the decision for the 1939 pact remains incomplete in important respects. Memoirs and diaries, such as those of foreign diplomats, Soviet cultural figures, and ordinary citizens, supply perspective but have to be read critically. This chapter therefore pays continuing attention to what each kind of source can and cannot establish.

The central argument is that the Soviet 1930s were a period of contradictions that the leadership did not regard as contradictions. The state promoted mass literacy, scientific research, and cultural participation while narrowing the range of permitted expression and imprisoning or killing many of those who produced it. It celebrated the "friendship of peoples" and the cultural forms of the national republics while centralizing power and repressing national cultural elites. It denounced fascism and sought alliances against it, while at the same time its internal violence, its suspicion of foreign communists, and the distrust of Western governments left it poorly placed to build those alliances. Understanding the chapter's events as products of choices made under pressure, rather than as the inevitable working-out of an ideology or the expression of one man's will, is the task of the sections that follow. The chapter ends at the threshold of the Second World War; the invasion of Poland, the Winter War, and the annexations of 1939–1940 belong to Chapter 59.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how the 1932 reorganization of artistic organizations and the 1934 Writers' Congress changed the institutional framework of Soviet cultural life.
  2. Distinguish the official doctrine of socialist realism from the actual creative practices, achievements, and evasions of Soviet writers, filmmakers, composers, and artists.
  3. Analyze the Soviet school system, mass literacy, and youth organizations as both genuine educational advancement and instruments of political socialization.
  4. Evaluate the relationship between scientific research and political oversight in the 1930s, including the early rise of Trofim Lysenko, without projecting the controversies of 1948 backward.
  5. Describe Soviet religious policy and the continuing religious life of Orthodox Christians, Muslims, Jews, Buddhists, Protestants, and other communities.
  6. Assess the family-law changes of 1936 and the distance between proclaimed gender equality and women's lived experience.
  7. Trace the evolution of Soviet nationality policy from korenizatsiya to the late-1930s settlement, noting variation among republics and regions.
  8. Compare the experiences of Ukrainians, Belarusians, Kazakhs, Central Asian peoples, Caucasian peoples, Volga Tatars and Germans, Jews, Koreans, and other communities under Soviet nationality policy.
  9. Explain the security rationale offered for the 1930s deportations, evaluate the evidence for it, and distinguish these removals from the NKVD national operations discussed in Chapter 57.
  10. Trace Soviet diplomacy from 1931 to August 1939, identifying the aims of collective security, the Franco-Soviet and Czechoslovak treaties, the Popular Front, intervention in Spain, and the East Asian crises.
  11. Interpret the Munich crisis, the Anglo-French-Soviet negotiations, and the Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact as the outcome of decisions made by several governments rather than a single inevitable path.
  12. Distinguish the documentary record of the 1939 treaty and secret protocol from later political and historiographical interpretations of them.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did the Soviet leadership centralize cultural institutions in 1932, and how did unified unions change the careers and work of artists and writers?
  2. How did genuine achievements in education, science, cinema, music, and literature coexist with censorship, denunciation, arrest, and execution?
  3. How did religious believers, women, teachers, and families adapt to, resist, or make use of the policies that were directed at them?
  4. What was korenizatsiya intended to accomplish, and why did the state move away from important parts of it in the 1930s?
  5. Why did the reversal affect Ukraine, Belarus, Kazakhstan, the Caucasus, and Central Asia in different ways and at different times?
  6. What reasons did the Soviet government give for deporting entire nationalities from border regions, and what does the documentary record show about the evidence behind those reasons?
  7. Why did the Soviet Union pursue collective security between 1934 and 1938, and why did that policy fail to produce an effective anti-German alliance?
  8. How should historians weigh Soviet security calculations, Western policy, Polish and Romanian attitudes, and ideology in explaining the pact of August 1939?

Historical Context

Chapters 55 through 57 described the transformation of the Soviet economy and countryside and the construction of a system of political terror. By 1933, the First Five-Year Plan had been declared fulfilled ahead of schedule, collectivization had been carried through at enormous human cost, and the famine of 1932–1933 had devastated Ukraine, Kazakhstan, the Volga region, the North Caucasus, and other areas (Chapter 56). The state had built a large industrial sector, relocated millions of people to new industrial cities (Chapter 55), and turned the Communist Party into an instrument of unified direction under a General Secretary whose personal authority was by then unrivaled (Chapter 54 and Chapter 57). The arrest and killing of party members, officials, officers, and ordinary citizens in 1937–1938 (Chapter 57) overlapped with nearly all of the developments discussed in this chapter.

The cultural landscape of the early 1930s grew out of the revolutionary decade. After 1917 the Bolsheviks had inherited a country in which a majority of adults could not read and in which religion, local custom, and imperial hierarchies structured daily life. In the 1920s they had tolerated, and sometimes sponsored, a remarkable diversity of artistic experiment, from Constructivist design and the montage cinema of Sergei Eisenstein to the literary fellow-travelers who wrote in a range of styles, even as they built a party-directed system of censorship (Chapter 53). During the "Cultural Revolution" of 1928–1931, militant organizations of proletarian writers, artists, and scholars, encouraged by party leaders, attacked "bourgeois specialists" and the older intelligentsia. The campaign created a climate of fear and class-based accusation that the leadership later judged to be disorderly and counterproductive.

The nationality question had been central since 1917. The Bolsheviks had condemned the Russian Empire's policies of Russification and had constructed, in 1922, a union of formally equal republics (Chapter 53). During the 1920s they pursued korenizatsiya, usually translated "indigenization" or "rooting": the promotion of local languages, schools, publishing, and cadres in the national republics and regions. This was a policy of what the historian Terry Martin has called "affirmative action" for nationalities, intended to defuse nationalist resentment by giving it institutional form within a socialist framework. It was also a policy with built-in tensions, since the party remained centralized and the leadership was always suspicious of "local nationalism" as well as of "Great Russian chauvinism." The late 1920s and 1930s brought those tensions to a head.

The international environment changed sharply as well. The Soviet Union had been diplomatically isolated for much of the 1920s, and it had cooperated militarily and economically with Weimar Germany under the Treaty of Rapallo (1922) and the Treaty of Berlin (1926). That relationship ended with Hitler's appointment as German Chancellor on 30 January 1933. Japanese forces had meanwhile seized Manchuria in 1931–1932, placing a hostile and expanding power on the Soviet Union's long Far Eastern frontier. The Great Depression was weakening the democracies, and the international communist movement, the Comintern, was still following a line, adopted in 1928, that treated social democrats as rivals or even as "social fascists." The Soviet leadership therefore confronted, from the early 1930s, the possibility of a two-front threat from Germany in the west and Japan in the east, at a time when its army was being reequipped and its relations with the Western democracies were poor.

One practical note on dates: the Soviet Union adopted the Gregorian calendar in 1918, so every date in this chapter is New Style, and no Old Style conversions are needed. Another concerns terminology. This chapter uses "Soviet Union" or "USSR" for the state as a whole, "Russian SFSR" for the largest union republic, and "Russian" for the language, the ethnic group, and the culture. Where sources of the 1930s speak of "the center," they usually mean the party leadership and central government in Moscow.

Section 58.1

From Revolutionary Experimentation to Stalinist Cultural Consolidation

The cultural life of the 1920s had been contested rather than uniform. The party's own leaders disagreed about how far to direct the arts. Leon Trotsky and Nikolai Bukharin, in different ways, argued in the early 1920s that the party should guide but not dictate in literature and that the existing intelligentsia should be won over rather than crushed. A range of groups competed for influence: the Proletkult movement, the avant-garde futurists and Constructivists, the "fellow travelers" (poputchiki), non-party writers who accepted the Revolution without joining the party, and, increasingly after 1925, the Russian Association of Proletarian Writers (RAPP), which claimed that literature must be written by and for the proletariat under party guidance. State censorship, exercised through Glavlit, the Main Administration for Literary Affairs and Publishing, had existed since 1922, but it coexisted with a plural publishing market, particularly during the New Economic Policy (Chapter 53).

The First Five-Year Plan changed this balance. Between 1928 and 1931 the party encouraged a campaign, usually called the Cultural Revolution, to create a new "proletarian" intelligentsia and to remove or discipline older specialists. The Shakhty trial of 1928, in which engineers from a Donbas mining district were accused of sabotage, set a pattern of public suspicion toward technical and cultural experts. RAPP's leaders, among them Leopold Averbakh, used the climate to demand that writers adopt "dialectical-materialist" methods and to attack independent authors. Universities and academies were purged, workers were pushed into higher education through accelerated programs, and some writers who had been prominent in the 1920s fell silent or were driven abroad. Evgeny Zamyatin, author of the dystopian novel We, was allowed to leave the country in 1931 after appealing to Stalin.

By 1932 the leadership judged that the Cultural Revolution had produced sectarian squabbling among writers and divisions among specialists that complicated rather than helped its aims. On 23 April 1932 a resolution of the Central Committee, "On the Restructuring of Literary-Artistic Organizations," dissolved RAPP and similar groups in other arts. It called for a single union of Soviet writers, with parallel unions for composers, architects, and artists, to be organized under party supervision. The resolution is often read as a liberalization because it ended RAPP's dominance, and many writers initially welcomed it for that reason. In practice it replaced a contest among organized factions with a unified, centrally supervised structure. Membership in the new unions came with material privileges, including access to publishing, housing, holiday homes, and stipends, and exclusion from them carried material and political penalties.

The change in 1932 should be understood in several contexts. Institutionally, it was part of a general move in these years toward consolidated, hierarchical bodies under party control. Politically, it reflected a leadership that wanted professionals to serve state goals and was increasingly impatient with revolutionary radicalism of the late 1920s. Culturally, it accompanied a reversion to more conventional forms and a renewed respect for pre-revolutionary classics, a shift that showed in school curricula, theater repertoires, and the publishing of Pushkin and Tolstoy in mass editions. Historians who emphasize the totalitarian dimension of this restructuring and those who emphasize its relationship to modernizing and mobilizational aims both draw on this evidence, and both agree that by the mid-1930s the plurality of the 1920s had gone. What remained, as the following sections show, was a cultural system in which the state defined the boundaries of the permissible, rewarded conformity, and punished transgression, but in which writers, performers, and audiences still made choices.

Section 58.2

Socialist Realism and the Soviet Writers' Union

The phrase "socialist realism" came into use in 1932, in the wake of the April resolution. It appeared in the press, was endorsed by Stalin in conversations with writers at Maxim Gorky's Moscow residence in October 1932, and was written into the statute of the Writers' Union at the First All-Union Congress of Soviet Writers, which met in Moscow from 17 August to 1 September 1934. Gorky, who had returned to the Soviet Union from Italy in the early 1930s and who was the most celebrated living Russian writer, opened and chaired the proceedings. The leading party speech was delivered by Andrei Zhdanov, a Central Committee secretary. Nikolai Bukharin, shortly before his own political destruction, gave a report on poetry that defended a broad conception of literary quality, and Karl Radek attacked modernist writers, including James Joyce, as symptoms of bourgeois decay. Foreign guests, among them André Malraux, Louis Aragon, and delegates from other countries, gave the Congress an international aura.

The doctrine itself was set out in general terms. Socialist realism demanded the "truthful, historically concrete" portrayal of reality "in its revolutionary development," and it combined this requirement with the task of educating readers in the spirit of socialism. The formula left considerable room for interpretation, and that ambiguity was both a source of flexibility and a mechanism of control. Because it did not define precisely what was acceptable, it allowed party officials and critics to judge each work after the fact, and writers had to anticipate their judgments. In practice the doctrine encouraged certain conventions: positive heroes who embodied the qualities of the new society, narratives of the growth of political consciousness, optimism about the construction of socialism, and an accessible style. Typical subjects included industrial construction, collectivized agriculture, the Civil War, and the transformation of individuals into "new people."

Two features of the Writers' Union deserve emphasis. First, it was an institution as well as a doctrine. Its secretariat assigned housing, paid advances, organized trips, and supervised publishing houses and journals. This system of patronage turned writing into a protected profession for those who stayed inside it, and made writers dependent on the state for their livelihoods. Second, censorship operated at several levels at once: Glavlit reviewed texts for political and military secrets, editors and publishing houses exercised preliminary control, and the Union and the party's cultural apparatus supervised content and reputation. Self-censorship was probably the most important form. Under these conditions, many works could not be published at all, such as Mikhail Bulgakov's novel The Master and Margarita, written between 1928 and 1940, and not published until the 1960s, or Andrei Platonov's The Foundation Pit, a bleak satire of collectivization-era utopianism that was written in 1930 and remained unpublished in the USSR until the late 1980s.

It is a mistake to treat socialist realism as identical with a single artistic style or to assume that all work produced in the 1930s was crude propaganda. Writers navigated the requirements in very different ways. Some embraced them sincerely, believing in the socialist project; others adapted their craft to produce work that met the formula while retaining literary interest, and some, like Anna Akhmatova, wrote privately and kept their most important work unpublished. Children's literature, historical fiction, translation, and scholarly editing offered refuges for talented writers. Meanwhile the state's pressure was real and often lethal. Osip Mandelstam, arrested in 1934 after reciting a poem satirizing Stalin, died in a transit camp in December 1938. Isaac Babel, Boris Pilnyak, and many other writers were arrested in the Great Terror (Chapter 57); Babel was shot in 1940. The distinction between prescribed ideology and creative practice, which the planning of this chapter urges, must therefore be held together with the fact that deviation from the unwritten rules could be fatal.

Historical Vocabulary
Socialist Realism

Definition: The officially endorsed method of Soviet literature and the arts, formulated in 1932–1934, which required the truthful, historically concrete depiction of reality "in its revolutionary development" and linked artistic work to the ideological education of workers.

Historical context: The term was institutionalized by the Writers' Union statute of 1934 and extended to music, painting, architecture, and film. It replaced the factional competition of the 1920s with a single official aesthetic framework applied through unions, publishers, and censorship.

Why it matters: Students should not mistake socialist realism for "realism" in the ordinary literary sense, nor for a simple list of prohibitions. It was both a demand for optimistic, party-minded content and an elastic standard that officials interpreted case by case. Its meaning shifted over time and across the arts, and its boundaries were enforced through patronage, criticism, and, in the late 1930s, arrest.

Primary Source Window

Statute of the Union of Soviet Writers (adopted at the First Writers' Congress, 1934)

Creator: Organizing committee and the Congress under party supervision · Date: August–September 1934 · Type: Institutional charter and definition of an official method

Context: The statute was adopted at the end of the Congress and defined both the Writers' Union as an organization and socialist realism as the "basic method" of Soviet literature and criticism. It was published in the Soviet press and in the stenographic record of the Congress. The text is available in Russian and in translated excerpts in several source collections.

Paraphrase (not a verbatim translation):

The statute declares socialist realism the basic method of Soviet artistic literature and criticism. It says that the method requires writers to represent reality truthfully and in concrete historical terms as it develops in a revolutionary direction, and that this truthful representation must be combined with the aim of ideologically remolding and educating working people in the spirit of socialism. The statute also asserts that the method gives creative writers the opportunity to display creative initiative and to choose among a variety of forms, styles, and genres.

Analysis: The document is useful because it shows the regime's own formulation of its aims, including its claim to combine truthfulness with ideological purpose and its promise of stylistic variety. Its limitations are significant. It tells historians what the state said it wanted, not what writers actually wrote or what readers read, and it says nothing of the practical means by which judgments of "truthfulness" were made. Its phrases mix descriptive and prescriptive claims, so the same sentence can be read as an invitation to realism or as a license to require propaganda. Missing perspectives include those of writers who were excluded from the Union, those whose works were never published, and ordinary readers. Corroboration comes from Congress speeches, editors' correspondence, writers' diaries and memoirs, and archival records of Glavlit and the Union.

Questions for students:

  1. What does the statute say socialist realism requires of a writer?
  2. Who created the document, and for what possible purposes: guidance, control, representation to foreign audiences, or all three?
  3. What can historians learn from the document about the party's cultural aims, and what can they not learn about actual literary production?
  4. What additional evidence, such as memoirs, archival censorship files, or actual texts, would help evaluate how the formula worked?
Section 58.3

Literature, Cinema, Theater, and Music under Stalinism

The works of the 1930s ranged from official epics to private masterpieces. Among published fiction, Mikhail Sholokhov's Quiet Don, a novel of the Don Cossacks in war and revolution that appeared in installments between 1928 and 1940, won wide acclaim for its portrayal of moral and political ambiguity, and his Virgin Soil Upturned (1932) dealt with collectivization. Nikolai Ostrovsky's How the Steel Was Tempered (1932–1934), a semi-autobiographical novel of a wounded young Communist, became one of the best-known positive-hero narratives and was read by millions. Alexei Tolstoy turned to historical fiction with Peter the First, whose subject suited the decade's renewed interest in Russian state-builders. Ilia Ilf and Evgeny Petrov's comic novels The Twelve Chairs (1928) and The Golden Calf (1931) survived as satire because they poked fun at petty swindlers rather than at the system. Children's authors such as Samuil Marshak and Kornei Chukovsky found a large and relatively sheltered readership, and translators produced Russian versions of world literature.

Poets and prose writers whose sensibilities did not fit were pushed into silence, private composition, or camp and prison. Anna Akhmatova composed her cycle Requiem between 1935 and 1940 in response to the arrest of her son and the experience of waiting in prison queues; the work circulated only among a few trusted friends, who memorized it, and was not published in the USSR until 1987. Boris Pasternak withdrew into translation. Mandelstam's fate was noted above. The decade thus produced a literature with two parts: the published, officially approved canon, and a hidden body of works that were later brought to light through manuscript circulation (samizdat, Chapter 64) and archival discovery.

Cinema was the art form that the leadership valued most highly for mass instruction. The industry was centralized under the Main Directorate of the Film and Photographic Industry, led by Boris Shumyatsky until his arrest in 1938. The transition to sound film encouraged accessible narrative styles. Chapaev (1934), directed by the "Vasilyev brothers" (Georgi and Sergei, unrelated by blood), dramatized the career of a Civil War commander and was reportedly seen by tens of millions; Stalin was said to have viewed it many times. Grigori Aleksandrov's musical comedies, including The Jolly Fellows (1934), Circus (1936), and Volga-Volga (1938), gave Soviet audiences popular songs and star performers such as Lyubov Orlova. Not all filmmakers fared well. Sergei Eisenstein's Bezhin Meadow, a film about collectivization, was halted in 1937 and effectively destroyed; the director returned to favor with Alexander Nevsky (1938), a patriotic medieval epic with a score by Sergei Prokofiev whose anti-German imagery would be removed from circulation after the pact of 1939 and restored in 1941. Alexander Dovzhenko's Ukrainian films showed the possibilities and constraints of national cinema (§58.11).

Theater and music operated under similar pressures. The Moscow Art Theatre's repertory, based on the training "system" of Konstantin Stanislavsky, was treated as a model of acceptable "realism," while the experimental director Vsevolod Meyerhold, once a celebrated Soviet innovator, was attacked for "formalism," arrested in June 1939, and shot in February 1940. In classical music the key episode was the condemnation of Dmitri Shostakovich. His opera Lady Macbeth of the Mtsensk District had been performed successfully in Leningrad and Moscow after its premiere in January 1934. After Stalin attended a performance in January 1936, an unsigned editorial in Pravda on 28 January, "Muddle Instead of Music," denounced it as chaotic and "formalist." A second editorial attacked his ballet The Limpid Stream. The effects were swift: critics and colleagues turned on him, performances ceased, and Shostakovich withdrew his Fourth Symphony before its premiere. His Fifth Symphony, first performed in Leningrad on 21 November 1937 under Yevgeny Mravinsky, was received with great emotion and was described in the press as a "creative reply" to just criticism; the ambiguity of its meaning has been debated ever since. Prokofiev, who had returned to the USSR in the mid-1930s, composed Peter and the Wolf (1936) and the ballet Romeo and Juliet, and found, like others, that the conditions of work were both supportive and restrictive.

The record shows that Stalinist culture produced real aesthetic achievement, often by people who understood their work in different ways. It also shows that artistic judgment was inseparable from political risk. A balanced account has to avoid two errors: treating all Soviet work of the 1930s as propaganda, and treating the creators as free agents operating in a normal cultural marketplace. Audiences, too, made choices: the popularity of musical comedies and adventure films suggests that official culture succeeded in part by offering entertainment people wanted.

Section 58.4

Visual Arts, Architecture, Mass Spectacle, and Propaganda

In painting and sculpture the Artists' Union and the exhibition system promoted figurative, narrative styles: portraits of leaders, scenes of industry and collective farm life, historical canvases, and idealized images of youth. Posters, a form with a rich 1920s avant-garde heritage, became more conventional, relying on heroic figures of workers, collective farmers, soldiers, and, increasingly, on portraits of Stalin. Vera Mukhina's sculpture Worker and Kolkhoz Woman, designed for the Soviet pavilion at the Paris International Exposition of 1937, with its two giant figures raising a hammer and a sickle, was among the most recognized Soviet images abroad. This chapter does not repeat the discussion of Stalin's personal cult, which belongs to Chapter 57; it concentrates instead on the wider repertoire of images and spectacles that defined the "ideal Soviet citizen."

Architecture shows the shift from the 1920s especially clearly. Early Soviet architects had embraced Constructivist and functionalist ideas, but in the early 1930s the state moved to monumental, classically inspired design. The competition for a Palace of Soviets, announced in 1931, led to the demolition of Moscow's Cathedral of Christ the Saviour in December 1931 to clear the site and to the eventual selection of Boris Iofan's design for a gigantic structure topped by a statue of Lenin. The building was never completed, in part because of the swampy foundation conditions and the later demands of war. The General Plan for the reconstruction of Moscow, adopted in 1935, widened streets, built new embankments, and planned a ring of monumental avenues. The Moscow-Volga Canal, opened in 1937, supplied the city with water and was built using the forced labor of tens of thousands of prisoners (Chapter 57).

The Moscow Metro, noted at the opening of the chapter, combined a transportation function with political symbolism. Directed by Lazar Kaganovich, it was built by a mixture of volunteers, hired workers, and peasants recruited from surrounding provinces, with engineering expertise borrowed from foreign consultants, in particular the London Underground, before foreign involvement was curtailed amid spy hysteria. Its stations, decorated with mosaics, sculptures, and marble, presented the new society as heir to a monumental high culture. The Metro illustrates a recurring feature of the decade: infrastructure that was truly useful and the product of intense labor also functioned as state theater.

Mass spectacle played a major part in public life. Parades on May Day and the anniversary of the October Revolution were carefully choreographed, and "physical culture" parades displayed ranks of athletes in the capital. The press promoted heroes of exploration, aviation, and labor. The rescue of the crew of the steamer Chelyuskin, which had been trapped in Arctic ice in 1934, produced national celebration. Valery Chkalov's transpolar flight to the United States in 1937 and Ivan Papanin's drifting polar station of 1937–1938 were presented as proofs of Soviet capability. In industry, the Stakhanovite movement, launched after the record coal-cutting claimed by Aleksei Stakhanov in August 1935, offered a labor-hero model (see Chapter 55). Radio, which was widely installed in communal loudspeakers, and illustrated periodicals such as Ogonyok disseminated these images. Mass songs, such as "Wide Is My Native Land" from the film Circus, gave the ideal citizen a soundtrack.

Propaganda in these years also contained a significant element of consumption and celebration. In November 1935 Stalin told a gathering of Stakhanovites that life had become "better and more joyous," a slogan that was widely repeated and that sat uneasily alongside shortages, communal housing, and the arrests that increased in 1936–1938. Historians have used the term "Stalinist modernity" to describe this combination of material aspiration and mobilizational ideology, and they debate how far citizens internalized the official image. Diaries of the period, such as those of Stepan Podlubny and Galina Shtange, which have been studied by scholars, show people employing the official vocabulary of progress while also expressing private anxieties, a reminder that public language was a medium of self-presentation as well as belief. Propaganda is evidence of what the state wished to project and how it organized public life, not a straightforward description of conditions.

Section 58.5

Education, Literacy, Schools, and the New Soviet Citizen

Education was one of the great undertakings of the early Soviet state, and it expanded dramatically. The 1926 census had recorded a literacy rate of about 51 percent among people aged nine and over, with wide gaps between men and women, towns and villages, Russians and some minority groups. In 1930 the government mandated universal compulsory four-year primary education, and the number of students rose rapidly, helped by a massive building program and by the mobilization of literate "cultural brigades" and Komsomol volunteers for literacy campaigns (likbez). By the census of January 1939, around four-fifths of the population aged nine and over was recorded as literate, and the rate among younger cohorts was higher. These figures, like all census data from the period, must be used carefully, since definitions of literacy were minimal and the census of 1937 had been suppressed (§58.7), but the scale of change is not in doubt.

The expansion occurred together with a major reorientation of method. In the 1920s Soviet educators had experimented with progressive techniques, including the "complex method" that organized learning around themes rather than subjects and the "brigade-laboratory" method that emphasized group work. By the early 1930s the leadership judged that these approaches had produced poorly prepared graduates, in a period when industry needed technically trained personnel. Central Committee decrees of 1931 and 1932 restored subject-based curricula, fixed schedules, textbook-based instruction, examinations, and teacher authority. In 1934 a new decree on the teaching of history reintroduced chronological history, and the school history of the party, the Short Course on the History of the All-Union Communist Party (Bolsheviks), published in 1938 under Stalin's editorial supervision, became a canonical text for adult and student political education. In the same years the older historian Mikhail Pokrovsky, whose Marxist schema had once dominated, was denounced posthumously, and a more patriotic narrative that emphasized Russian state-builders and military heroes gained prominence (compare Chapters 21, 23, and 31).

The state also expanded technical and higher education (see Chapter 55). Workers' faculties and quotas brought people of working-class and peasant origins into universities and institutes, while the social background of students became a political consideration. Teachers were often poorly paid and sometimes the targets of political suspicion, and in the countryside school buildings and supplies were often inadequate. Teachers also served as agents of the state in literacy and propaganda campaigns, and some became victims of the repression. In 1936 the government abolished "pedology," a field of child psychology and testing, through a decree that criticized the use of intelligence tests to label children and ordered reform of schools for those regarded as mentally or behaviorally deficient. The decision, justified by claims that pedology was "anti-Marxist," also reflected the state's insistence that environment and instruction could shape any child.

Youth organizations extended education into leisure. The Pioneers, for children from roughly ten to fourteen, and the Komsomol, for older youth, organized camps, clubs, parades, and political study. They taught skills and offered adventure, sports, and ladders of advancement, and they also taught obedience, vigilance, and loyalty to the state. The cult of Pavlik Morozov, a boy said to have been killed in 1932 by relatives whom he had denounced to authorities in a Urals village, held up denunciation of family members as a civic virtue. The facts of the case have been disputed by historians, who have studied the trial records and the way in which the story was shaped by journalists and officials. What is clear is that the official story was widely propagated and that it offered a model, however uneven in practice, of loyalty to the state above family.

Students should keep apart two aspects of the education system. As an expansion of literacy, schooling, and opportunity, it altered the lives of millions, including peasant children who became engineers, teachers, and officers. As an instrument of political socialization and discipline, it narrowed permitted ideas and sought to cultivate political conformity. Both are historically real, and the experience of any individual pupil, parent, or teacher depended on region, social origin, and the political climate of the moment.

Section 58.6

Science, Technology, and the Limits of Intellectual Autonomy

The Soviet state invested heavily in science and technology in the 1930s. The Academy of Sciences, which had inherited institutions of the imperial period, was relocated from Leningrad to Moscow in 1934 and increasingly integrated into the planned economy, with new institutes created for physics, chemistry, geology, and other fields. Research institutes, universities, and industrial laboratories expanded, and a generation of scientists trained under the new regime joined older figures whose careers had begun before 1917. The achievements were considerable. Soviet physicists around Abram Ioffe's Leningrad Physico-Technical Institute made important contributions to solid-state and nuclear physics. Soviet chemists, geologists, and mathematicians were internationally recognized, and engineers, such as the aircraft designer Andrei Tupolev, produced advanced machines. Rocket groups, including the Group for the Study of Reactive Motion (GIRD) and the Reactive Scientific Research Institute, laid foundations on which later space programs would build (Chapter 62).

The political environment imposed limits that varied by field. Scientists' work was subject to ideological review, and Marxist-Leninist philosophy was used as a standard against which theories were evaluated. The "Academy Affair" of 1929–1930 had already purged historians and other academicians. Foreign contacts that had been routine in the 1920s became suspect, and travel abroad became rare. The physicist Pyotr Kapitsa, a Cambridge researcher who visited the USSR in 1934, was not allowed to return to England, and was provided with a new institute in Moscow. In 1936 the mathematician Nikolai Luzin was publicly attacked in a campaign that charged him with placing his work abroad and with political unreliability. During the Great Terror scientists and engineers were arrested, including Tupolev, who worked in a prison design bureau (sharashka), and Sergei Korolev, a rocket engineer, who was imprisoned in 1938 and later released. The physicist Lev Landau was arrested in April 1938 and freed a year later after Kapitsa wrote to Stalin and Molotov on his behalf. Others, such as the physicist Matvei Bronstein, were executed.

Biology and agriculture provide the best-known case of ideological influence. Trofim Lysenko, an agronomist from Ukraine, gained attention in the early 1930s for claims about "vernalization," a method of treating seeds that he said would boost yields, and later for assertions that acquired characteristics could be inherited. His rise reflected the political attractions of his message: he came from a peasant background, promised quick practical gains for hard-pressed collective farms, and framed himself as an opponent of "bourgeois" academic science. His adversaries included the geneticist Nikolai Vavilov, a world-famous plant scientist who led the Lenin All-Union Academy of Agricultural Sciences until 1935. A debate on genetics and plant breeding took place in 1936, and Lysenko became president of that academy in 1938. Yet the later total dominance of Lysenkoism, formalized at the agricultural academy session of August 1948 that condemned classical genetics, did not exist in the 1930s. In those years the dispute was still contested, Soviet geneticists continued to publish and to have international contacts, and Vavilov remained at liberty until his arrest in 1940. Students should therefore avoid reading the 1948 outcome back into the earlier decade (see Chapter 60).

The broader point is that the 1930s combined state patronage and state coercion. Scientists could enjoy resources and status unavailable to most citizens, and many were committed participants in the building of a technologically advanced society. They also operated in a system in which political denunciation could end careers, in which individual patrons could make a difference, and in which the boundaries of acceptable debate shifted unpredictably. Historians of Soviet science therefore avoid both the picture of a uniformly crippled intellectual life and that of an unconstrained scientific enterprise.

Section 58.7

Religion, State Atheism, and the Persistence of Belief

The Bolsheviks inherited a society in which religion was woven into law, calendar, family life, and local identity (compare Chapters 9, 29, and 32), and they were committed to a materialist worldview that treated religion as a survival of the old order. Early decrees had separated church from state and school from church, and the 1920s saw the confiscation of church valuables, trials of clergy, and the splitting of the Orthodox Church by officially encouraged "renovationist" movements. The decade of the 1930s intensified the pressure. The Law on Religious Associations of 8 April 1929 limited religious activity to worship in registered congregations, banning charity, education, and communal activities, and a constitutional amendment of that year guaranteed "freedom of religious worship and of antireligious propaganda" but not of religious propaganda. Churches could be closed by local decision, often with the justification that the community had requested it or that the building was needed for another use, and the heavy taxation of clergy and parishes made survival difficult.

The League of the Militant Godless, headed by Emelian Yaroslavsky, mobilized millions of members, produced journals, posters, and museums of atheism, and sponsored public mock trials and processions. The state changed the calendar: in 1929 it introduced a continuous work week, in which workers rotated through five-day cycles and there was no common day of rest, which undermined Sunday observance and religious holidays; the system was gradually modified and abandoned in 1940. Bells were removed or melted down, icons and relics were confiscated, and church buildings were converted into storehouses, clubs, or factories, or were demolished. The scale of the campaign was uneven. A wave of church closures in early 1930, during collectivization, provoked peasant disturbances, especially among women, and Stalin's article of March 1930, "Dizzy with Success," blamed local officials for "excesses" and led to the reopening of some churches. The pressure resumed in later years and reached extremes in 1937–1938, when the NKVD arrested clergy as part of its mass operations (Chapter 57). Many bishops and priests were shot or imprisoned, and by 1939 only a small remnant of the pre-revolutionary episcopate remained at liberty. Estimates of the number of Orthodox churches open for worship in 1939 are uncertain and range from a few hundred to somewhat over a thousand, in comparison to tens of thousands before 1917.

The Russian Orthodox Church was also divided over how to respond. In 1927 Metropolitan Sergius (Stragorodsky), the acting head of the Moscow Patriarchate, issued a declaration of loyalty to the Soviet state in order to secure legal status; critics inside and outside the Church, including adherents of "True Orthodox" and catacomb communities, condemned it as a compromise. Believers practiced their faith in various ways: some in registered parishes, some in unregistered gatherings, many through domestic rituals such as keeping icons, observing fasts, baptizing children secretly, or visiting holy places. Old Believer communities, with their own history of dissent (Chapter 29), also continued, especially in Siberia and the North.

Other religions were also affected. Islamic institutions, which had already lost their religious courts, schools, and endowments (waqf) in the 1920s, were further constrained, and mosques were closed in large numbers. The campaign for women's unveiling (hujum), begun in 1927 in Central Asia, treated veiling as a symbol of backwardness and led to violence against unveiled women (§58.12). The change of Muslim peoples' alphabets from Arabic to Latin and then to Cyrillic also cut communities off from Arabic-script religious literature (§58.10). Jewish communal life was affected by the closing of synagogues, the banning of Hebrew teaching, and the dissolution of the party's Jewish sections in 1930, though secular Yiddish culture received state sponsorship. Buddhist institutions in Buryatia and Kalmykia, as well as Catholic, Lutheran, Baptist, and Pentecostal communities, experienced closures and arrests; the Buryat monk and diplomat Agvan Dorzhiev was arrested in 1937 and died in custody the following January. Many Protestant communities that had enjoyed some freedom in the 1920s were suppressed.

The census of January 1937 included a question about religion, and the results showed that a majority of respondents aged sixteen and over, about 56 percent according to the figures later reported, described themselves as believers. The authorities rejected the census as defective, suppressed the results, and arrested its organizers. The episode illustrates both the limits of the state's success in changing beliefs and the pressure to avoid documenting this fact. It also needs critical reading: respondents may have been wary of answering, and the question's wording and use varied. Even so, the evidence is among the strongest we have that religious identity persisted widely despite the campaign. Later changes in policy toward the Orthodox Church, during the war, belong to Chapter 60, and the long-term fate of religious life under late Soviet conditions to Chapter 64.

Multiple Perspectives

Belief, Atheism, and the Closing of a Church

Local believers, often women: Evidence from police reports and petitions shows that women frequently led protests against church closures, sought to keep keys and bells, and sustained domestic ritual when public worship was curtailed. Their motives combined faith, attachment to communal institutions, and in some cases a sense that officials were violating legal promises. Their actions carried real risks and were sometimes treated as "kulak" agitation.
Antireligious activists and local officials: Many activists, including Komsomol members and Godless League cells, sincerely regarded religion as superstition that held back progress. Local officials also faced pressure from above to demonstrate results, to close churches, and to repurpose buildings, and their reports suggest a mixture of conviction, careerism, and fear of being accused of leniency. When the center criticized "excesses" in 1930, some officials were blamed for implementing what they had been encouraged to do.
Clergy and church leaders: Priests and bishops faced choices among accommodation, as in Metropolitan Sergius's declaration of loyalty, public resistance, and silence. Those who accommodated were accused by some believers of betraying the Church; those who resisted faced arrest. The wider impact of the 1937–1938 operations was to remove most of the active hierarchy, leaving parishes to lay leadership.
Muslim, Jewish, and Buddhist communities: These communities experienced the campaign alongside the state's nationality and language policies. For Muslims in Central Asia, the attack on religion was joined to the unveiling campaign and the abolition of Arabic script; for Jews, the closing of religious institutions was accompanied by state support for a secular Yiddish culture; for Buddhists, the monastery system was largely dismantled in the late 1930s.

Evidence for each perspective comes from different kinds of sources: police and party reports, petitions, memoirs, and later oral histories. The perspectives are not equally powerful. The state controlled the instruments of coercion, and the narratives of believers were often recorded by those who were investigating them.

Section 58.8

Family Policy, Women, Gender, and the Stalinist Social Settlement

The early Soviet state had proclaimed legal equality for women and had enacted liberal family laws: civil marriage, easy divorce, recognition of de facto marriage in the 1926 code, and legalization of abortion in 1920, the first major state to do so. The Women's Department of the party (Zhenotdel) had worked to mobilize women, especially in Central Asia and the Caucasus, until it was abolished in 1930 on the ground that the "woman question" had been solved. In the First Five-Year Plan and afterward, women entered the industrial and agricultural labor force in great numbers, as Chapter 55 described, and were also asked to bear most of the household work. Tractor drivers like Pasha Angelina, who organized an all-women brigade in 1933, and textile and beet-field workers who became celebrated Stakhanovites, were promoted as models of the new Soviet woman.

During the mid-1930s the leadership began to emphasize the stability of the family as a social unit and the importance of childbearing. A draft law on abortion and family support was published on 26 May 1936 and was submitted to a nationwide "discussion" in the press and in workplaces. The decree that followed, of 27 June 1936, had several components that must be distinguished. First, it prohibited abortion except when the woman's life or health was at risk or when there was a risk of hereditary disease, and it imposed penalties on practitioners and on those who coerced women into the procedure. Second, it provided state assistance to mothers of large families, with cash allowances beginning at a high number of children, and it promised to expand maternity homes, nurseries, and kindergartens. Third, it tightened enforcement of alimony payments. Fourth, it modified divorce procedures by requiring both spouses to appear for registration and by imposing increasing fees on successive divorces, beginning at 50 rubles. These measures did not abolish divorce, but they made it more difficult and more costly.

Historians offer different explanations for the shift. Demographic concerns were important: the losses of the famine, the declining birth rate in cities, and the anticipation of war all motivated an emphasis on population growth. Ideological and social factors also mattered. The state saw the family as a stabilizing institution and wished to reduce social disorder associated with high divorce rates and abandoned children. Some contemporary physicians and women pointed to the health risks of illegal abortions and to the inadequacy of contraception and childcare. The public discussion produced thousands of letters, many of which opposed the ban on the basis of material hardship, crowded housing, and the burden of work. The final law included some changes, but the abortion ban remained. The practical effects were mixed. Registered births rose in the short term, but illegal abortion increased as well, and the childcare facilities promised were far from adequate. The 1944 Family Law, which made divorce even more difficult and introduced new rules for illegitimacy, belongs to Chapter 60.

Other changes affected gender and sexuality. Male homosexual acts were criminalized in 1933–1934 under Article 121 of the criminal code, reversing the position of the early 1920s. A decree of April 1935 allowed the prosecution of juveniles from the age of twelve for serious crimes, a response to the problem of homeless children left by civil war, famine, and dekulakization. Women's actual conditions varied by region. In the cities, many women combined factory or office work with long hours of shopping, queuing, and domestic labor, often in shared communal apartments. In collective farms, women formed the majority of the labor force in many areas as men were drafted into industry and the army, yet received fewer labor-day credits and fewer leadership posts. In Central Asia and the Caucasus, the state's campaigns against veiling, bride-price, and child marriage often provoked resistance and violent backlash while giving some women new educational and professional opportunities (§58.12).

The official image of women thus encompassed several roles: worker, activist, mother, and symbol of Soviet abundance. The constitution of 1936 declared women equal to men in all spheres of life, but it did not alter the household division of labor. The gap between proclaimed equality and lived experience, often described by historians as the "double burden," is one of the best-documented features of Soviet social history in these years (compare Chapters 55 and 64).

Everyday Life and Social History

School, Family, and Leisure in a Soviet Community

Consider a working-class family in an industrial town of the Urals or the Donbas in the mid-1930s. Both parents might work in a factory or mine, one possibly as a Stakhanovite or "shock worker" with extra rations and a better apartment, the other in a lower-paid position. The family likely lived in a barracks room or a shared apartment, with a communal kitchen, and perhaps with an aging relative or a child from the countryside. Their children attended school in shifts because of overcrowded buildings, used textbooks that were in short supply, and might belong to the Pioneers, with its summer camps and sports meets. Parents who had grown up in villages might be semi-literate; their children, schooled in the Soviet system, were often the first in the family to read fluently and might bring home new ideas, creating both pride and generational tension.

Leisure combined official and informal forms. Workers' clubs offered films, lectures, theater, and sports; radio loudspeakers broadcast music and news; and the new parks of culture and rest provided public recreation. Families might attend a showing of Chapaev or Circus, dance to popular tunes, and gather for holidays that mixed Soviet celebrations with older traditions, such as the New Year tree, rehabilitated in 1935 as a children's festival after having been condemned as religious. Meanwhile, shopping required long queues, shortages of consumer goods persisted, and households relied on kitchen gardens, informal trade, and extended family networks. In 1937–1938 the arrest of a neighbor or relative could suddenly change a family's circumstances, and denunciation or silence became part of ordinary calculations. Evidence for this account comes from memoirs, diaries, oral histories, and police and party reports, each of which shows different aspects of experience. Regional variation was large: a village in Ukraine recovering from famine, a Kazakh pastoralist settlement, and a Moscow apartment building offered very different versions of "Soviet life."

Section 58.9

The Soviet Nationalities System and the Legacy of Korenizatsiya

The Soviet Union was constructed in 1922 as a union of formally equal republics, and its structure embedded nationality in its administrative geography (Chapter 53). The country was divided into union republics, autonomous republics, autonomous regions (oblasti), and national districts, each named for a "titular" nationality and each with its own institutions, to differing degrees. The Russian SFSR itself contained numerous autonomous republics, including the Tatar, Bashkir, Yakut, Buryat, Kalmyk, and Volga German ASSRs. In Central Asia, the "national-territorial delimitation" of 1924–1925 had replaced the older Turkestan and the protectorates of Bukhara and Khiva with national republics (Uzbek, Turkmen, and later Tajik, Kyrgyz, and Kazakh), a process shaped by Soviet ethnographers and political calculations as well as by local elites. By the end of 1936 the Soviet Union had eleven union republics (§58.15).

The policy that gave this system content in the 1920s was korenizatsiya. Adopted at the Twelfth Party Congress in 1923 and elaborated in following years, it aimed to promote the languages, schools, press, theaters, and cadres of each titular nationality, to staff local administrations with members of the indigenous population, and to make the party and state "native" in the national territories. It rested on the Bolsheviks' conviction that nationalism, especially the resentment created by Russian imperial domination, could best be disarmed by giving it cultural form while keeping political power centralized in the party. The practical results were considerable. The number of schools and publications in languages such as Ukrainian, Belarusian, Georgian, Uzbek, and Kazakh grew rapidly; written languages were created or standardized for many peoples who had not had them; the Latin script was introduced for many Turkic and other languages; and national scholars and writers found institutional support. In the same period, the party remained fiercely hostile to "local nationalism" as well as "Great Russian chauvinism," and the political police acted against those it considered nationalist.

The system had contradictions that became more visible in the 1930s. It required the promotion of local cadres in territories where literate, party-trained local people were few, and it required the rapid creation of national intelligentsias. It also created fixed ethnic categories: Soviet institutions assigned individuals to nationalities, and, from 1932, the new internal passport recorded each holder's nationality, a feature that would have major consequences both for the individual and for the state's ability to classify and target groups. Nationalities were regarded as legitimate, enduring, and in some versions of Marxist theory, temporary, to be superseded eventually in a socialist future. Moreover, the line between acceptable "national in form" culture and unacceptable "nationalism in content" was drawn by party officials and could be moved. The famous formula, "national in form, socialist in content," captured the intended boundary and its inherent ambiguity.

Historical Vocabulary
Korenizatsiya and Titular Nationality

Definition: Korenizatsiya (from the Russian koren', "root") was the Soviet policy, formally adopted in 1923, of promoting local languages, cultural institutions, and indigenous personnel in the national republics and regions. A titular nationality was the nationality that gave its name to a national territory, such as the Uzbeks in the Uzbek SSR.

Historical context: The policy was strongest in the 1920s and early 1930s and was modified and curtailed unevenly in the 1930s. The Russian terms "indigenization" and "nativization" are common English equivalents. The policy did not apply equally to all minorities: members of non-titular groups living in a republic, such as Poles in Ukraine or Armenians in Georgia, had varying status.

Why it matters: Students should not equate korenizatsiya with either national independence or simple Russification. It was an attempt to create loyal national elites within a centralized party-state, and it left lasting institutions, languages, and categories that shaped the later history of the USSR and its successor states (Chapters 64–66).

Section 58.10

The Retreat from Korenizatsiya and the Changing Language Policy

The retreat from korenizatsiya was not a single decision but a series of shifts between the late 1920s and 1938, differing by republic and by sphere of activity. Several developments combined. The collectivization campaign, which treated resistance in non-Russian regions as evidence of "nationalist" counterrevolution, brought charges of local nationalism against republican leaders. The growing sense of external threat after 1933 sharpened suspicion of border nationalities with ties to neighboring states. The party leadership concluded that the national cadres promoted in the 1920s were unreliable, that the multiplication of languages and scripts complicated industrial planning, the army, and the party apparatus, and that the primary danger had shifted from "Great Russian chauvinism" to local nationalism. A change in the state's emphasis also took place: Russian people and culture were increasingly presented as the leading elements in a family of Soviet peoples, a concept that would be fully developed after 1945 (Chapter 60).

Language and script policy shows the pattern. In the late 1920s many Turkic-language peoples, including Azerbaijanis, Tatars, Uzbeks, Kazakhs, and Turkmen, had adopted a Latin-based alphabet, partly to separate them from Arabic-script religious tradition and partly to align them with modernizing models such as the new Turkish Republic. In the late 1930s the state reversed course. Between roughly 1936 and 1941 these scripts were replaced with Cyrillic-based alphabets, a process that made the languages' written forms closer to Russian and made Russian loanwords easier to integrate. In Ukraine and Belarus, orthographic reforms in 1933 revised earlier standards, removing features that had distinguished Ukrainian and Belarusian spelling from Russian and condemning the work of earlier linguists as nationalist. These changes were presented as technical improvements, but they were also political acts.

The decree of 13 March 1938, issued by the Council of People's Commissars and the Central Committee, made the study of Russian compulsory in the schools of the national republics and regions. It did not ban instruction in non-Russian languages or replace them with Russian as the medium of instruction. Rather, it required Russian to be taught as a subject from an early grade, with the aim of ensuring that all graduates could read and speak the language of the army, the party, and the wider Union. Its implementation was uneven because of a shortage of teachers and textbooks, and its effects were incremental: over the following decades Russian gained a larger role in higher education, technical training, and administration, while non-Russian languages remained in use in many schools and in local publishing. Historians stress that the decree should not be described as an immediate prohibition of all non-Russian languages, nor treated as having the same effect across the Union.

The reversal also affected appointments. In 1937–1938 many first secretaries and other officials of the national republics, who had been promoted under korenizatsiya, were arrested as "bourgeois nationalists" or "enemies of the people," and were frequently replaced by cadres from outside the republic, often Russians or Russian-speaking functionaries sent from Moscow. At the same time, other native cadres, trained in the new institutions, were promoted into the posts left vacant. The result was not a simple replacement of non-Russians by Russians but a thorough reshuffling, with the central party leadership exercising tighter control over appointments (compare Chapter 57). By the end of the decade, the formal structures of national republics and cultural institutions remained, but their autonomy of action was sharply reduced.

Section 58.11

Ukraine, Belarus, and the Repression of National Cultural Elites

Ukraine was the largest non-Russian republic, with a rich national movement, a significant peasant population devastated by the famine (Chapter 56), and a cultural life that had flourished in the 1920s. Under Commissar of Education Mykola Skrypnyk, Ukrainian-language schooling, publishing, and scholarship expanded, a standard orthography was agreed at Kharkiv in 1928, and writers such as Mykola Khvylovy argued, in a famous debate, that Ukrainian literature should look to Europe rather than to Moscow. At the same time the political police had already acted against what it called nationalist organizations: the 1930 trial in Kharkiv of the so-called Union for the Liberation of Ukraine accused prominent intellectuals, including the scholar Serhiy Yefremov, of conspiracy, and the Ukrainian Autocephalous Orthodox Church was pressed to dissolve itself in January 1930. Historian Mykhailo Hrushevsky, the leading figure of the Ukrainian national movement before 1917, was moved to Moscow in 1931 and died in 1934.

The years 1932–1934 brought a sharp turn. In January 1933 Pavel Postyshev was sent from Moscow as second secretary of the Ukrainian party and given wide powers to purge the apparatus and curb "nationalist deviation," which the center blamed in part for the failures of grain procurement. Khvylovy, a leading writer, killed himself in May 1933, and Skrypnyk, subjected to relentless criticism, took his own life on 7 July 1933. Orthographic changes in 1933 aligned Ukrainian spelling more closely with Russian norms. In 1934 the republic's capital was moved from Kharkiv to Kyiv, a decision that reflected the city's historical and symbolic importance as well as administrative considerations. In 1937–1938 the Great Terror removed much of the republic's remaining political and cultural leadership; among those shot were the theater director Les Kurbas, the dramatist Mykola Kulish, the poet Mykola Zerov, and the novelist Valerian Pidmohylny. A large group of Ukrainian writers and artists was executed in late October and early November 1937 at Sandarmokh, in Karelia, an event that later generations of Ukrainian historians called the "Executed Renaissance." This phrase is a later interpretive label, and historians debate how far the killings reflect a coordinated attack on a generation of national culture versus the broader logic of the Terror. Their documentary base, which includes NKVD files opened since 1991, shows that national identity was explicitly cited in many accusations.

Not all Ukrainian cultural life was extinguished. Ukrainian-language schooling remained widespread, the republic's publishing and theaters continued to function, and filmmakers such as Alexander Dovzhenko worked, though under heavy supervision. The Soviet state also promoted a version of Ukrainian history and culture, emphasizing folk traditions and the "reunification" of Ukrainian lands, which became more prominent after the annexation of eastern Poland in 1939 (Chapter 59). This is a good example of the paradox in the nationality system: a national culture could be officially celebrated while its autonomous voices were silenced.

In Belarus, the 1920s had seen the creation of a Belarusian-language educational system and an Academy of Sciences, building on a much weaker national movement than Ukraine's. In 1930 the authorities fabricated a case against a supposed "Union for the Liberation of Belarus," and later arrested many of the republic's cultural and scientific leaders. A 1933 orthographic reform brought Belarusian spelling closer to Russian. The Terror of 1937–1938 was especially severe in Belarus, which had a long border with Poland and a significant Polish minority. Prominent political leaders, scholars, and writers were arrested, shot, or driven to suicide, and the Kurapaty site near Minsk became one of the best known mass-execution sites. Western Belarus, then within Poland, joined the Soviet republic in 1939 (Chapter 59). Historians should avoid describing the experiences of these two republics as identical: Ukraine's size, history, and the famine made its situation distinct, and Belarus's smaller national movement meant the dynamics of cultural policy there were different. Chapter 56 provides the context of the famine, which is not repeated here.

Section 58.12

Kazakhstan and Central Asia: National Development and Central Control

The Central Asian republics were created in the 1920s from lands that the Russian Empire had conquered or dominated in the nineteenth century (Chapter 39). They were multi-ethnic, mostly Muslim, with significant pastoralist and oasis-agricultural societies, and had a thin layer of modern education. Soviet policy included campaigns for schooling, women's emancipation, and economic transformation, and it sought to create national intelligentsias and institutions. The Uzbek SSR was established in 1924 with Samarkand as its first capital, and Tashkent became the capital in 1930. The Tajik ASSR, initially part of Uzbekistan, was raised to union republic status in 1929, with its capital at Dushanbe (renamed Stalinabad in 1929). The Kazakh ASSR (named in 1925) and the Kyrgyz ASSR remained autonomous republics until they became union republics in December 1936. The Karakalpak ASSR, formed in 1932 within the Russian SFSR, was transferred to Uzbekistan in 1936.

Education and language policy were important instruments. Schooling expanded in titular languages, new written standards were created, and newspapers, theaters, and academic institutions were established. In the late 1920s Latin-based alphabets replaced Arabic script; in 1940 the Kazakh, Kyrgyz, Uzbek, Tajik, and Turkmen languages were moved to Cyrillic. The party also tried to change social practices, and the hujum campaign of 1927 urged women to unveil. The campaign produced new opportunities for some women in education and employment, but also assaults and murders of unveiled women by relatives and neighbors and widespread resistance, and it reveals the limits of coercive social engineering. Collectivization and the settlement of nomads had devastating results in Kazakhstan, where the famine of 1930–1933 killed, by the estimates that most scholars accept, roughly 1.3 to 1.5 million people, a very large proportion of the Kazakh population, and drove many others into flight to China, Uzbekistan, and elsewhere (Chapter 56). As a result, ethnic Kazakhs became a minority in their own republic, a situation that persisted into the late Soviet period.

National elites were also targeted. In Kazakhstan, leaders associated with the short-lived Alash Orda government of 1917–1920, such as Alikhan Bukeikhanov and Akhmet Baitursynov, had been under surveillance and repression since the late 1920s; Baitursynov was shot in 1937. Turar Ryskulov, a prominent Kazakh Communist, was arrested and shot in 1938. In Uzbekistan, the writers Abdulla Qodiriy, Cho'lpon, and Abdurauf Fitrat were executed in 1937–1938; the republic's leaders Faizulla Khodzhaev and Akmal Ikramov were tried and shot as part of the "Bloc of Rights and Trotskyites" trial in March 1938 (Chapter 57). The Tajik, Kyrgyz, and Turkmen leaderships also experienced extensive purges. At the same time, the economic orientation of Central Asia was shaped by central planning: Uzbekistan and Turkmenistan became cotton-producing regions, tied to textile industries elsewhere, and the republics were integrated into the Soviet economy as suppliers of raw materials, even as industry and urban life grew in cities such as Tashkent and Alma-Ata. The experience was thus mixed: the promotion of literacy, education, and new professional classes occurred alongside the destruction of leadership groups and a harsh economic and demographic transformation.

Regional Context

Contrasting Nationality Policies within Central Asia

Central Asia was not a single case. In Kazakhstan, a pastoralist society, the central issue was collectivization and sedentarization, which produced catastrophic mortality and the demographic transformation noted above; the republic also became a destination for deportees and a location for camps such as Karlag. In Uzbekistan, with its old oasis cities and dense agricultural population, cotton and irrigation dominated economic policy, and the urban literary and educational elite was a main target of repression. In Tajikistan, a mountainous and economically poorer republic with a majority Persian-speaking population, the new national institutions were built on a very thin base of trained personnel. Turkmenistan combined a pastoralist society with newly irrigated agriculture and the early suppression of armed resistance, and the Kyrgyz republic included both highland pastoral and agricultural populations.

These differences affected language policy, the pace and form of collectivization, relations between local elites and Moscow, and the effects of repression. Students should avoid treating "Central Asia" as a block, and should also note that Russians, Ukrainians, and other settlers, along with deportees, were already present in significant numbers, especially in northern Kazakhstan and the cities.

Section 58.13

The Caucasus, Volga-Ural Region, and Other National Communities

In the South Caucasus, the Transcaucasian Socialist Federative Soviet Republic had united Georgia, Armenia, and Azerbaijan from 1922 until its dissolution in December 1936, when the three became separate union republics under the new constitution. Lavrentiy Beria, a Georgian who rose through the Caucasian security services, became head of the Georgian party in 1931 and of the Transcaucasian party in 1932, and he used the position to purge local leaders and promote a version of Georgian and Caucasian Bolshevik history that centered on Stalin. In Georgia, the Terror of 1936–1938 killed or removed many cultural figures and political leaders, including the old Bolshevik Budu Mdivani, the writer Mikheil Javakhishvili, and the poets Titsian Tabidze and Paolo Iashvili, the latter of whom killed himself in 1937. The Georgian language and literature continued to be taught and published, and Georgia, as Stalin's homeland, enjoyed a special status within the official culture. Abkhazia, which had been a Soviet republic with treaty ties to Georgia, was downgraded in 1931 to an autonomous republic within Georgia, and in 1938 the Abkhaz script was changed from Latin to Georgian, a decision that Abkhaz writers and later nationalists remembered as hostile to their language.

Armenia suffered similar political purges: its party leader Aghasi Khanjian died in Tbilisi on 9 July 1936 amid conflict with Beria; Soviet authorities reported a suicide, while allegations that Beria was responsible for his death remain disputed, and the poet Yeghishe Charents was arrested in 1937 and died in prison. The Armenian Church, centered at Echmiadzin, was weakened by arrests, and its head, Catholicos Khoren I, died in 1938 in circumstances widely believed to involve foul play. In Azerbaijan, the party leader Mir Jafar Baghirov, first secretary from 1933 to 1953, directed a purge of the local intelligentsia and officials. Poets such as Mikayil Mushfig were shot, and the playwright Huseyn Javid was arrested in 1937 and died in exile. The Azerbaijani language was moved from Latin to Cyrillic script around 1939–1940, and Baku, with its oil industry, remained one of the Union's key economic assets. In the North Caucasus, the Chechen-Ingush ASSR was created in 1936 from the earlier autonomous region, after years of suppressed rebellions and collectivization conflicts, and the republic's leadership was purged in 1937–1938. The later wartime deportation of the Chechens and Ingush belongs to Chapter 60.

In the Volga-Ural region, the Tatar and Bashkir autonomous republics had developed national institutions and strong intellectual traditions. Tatar intellectuals, with the legacy of Jadidism, a Muslim reformist movement, had played a major role in the early Soviet period, and Mirsaid Sultan-Galiev, an early Tatar Communist who had proposed ideas of a Muslim national communism, was arrested in 1923 and again in 1928 and was shot in 1940. "Sultan-Galievism" became a standing accusation against Muslim Communists. The Tatar written language was shifted from Arabic to Latin and then, in 1939, to Cyrillic. The Bashkir republic's leadership was also purged in 1937–1938. The Volga German ASSR, created in 1924 with its capital at Engels, had a population of about 400,000 and a network of German-language schools, newspapers, and churches; it suffered severely in the famine of 1932–1933, and its German population was targeted in the "German operation" of the NKVD in 1937–1938 (Chapter 57). Its deportation in 1941 is covered in Chapter 59.

Other communities show further variants. Jews, who had been subject to restrictions and pogroms under the empire (Chapters 43 and 45), gained legal equality after 1917 and entered the Soviet administration, professions, and cities in large numbers. The state promoted secular Yiddish culture while dismantling religious institutions, and in 1934 it established the Jewish Autonomous Oblast, with its center at Birobidzhan, in the Far East, a project that attracted only limited settlement and whose Yiddish institutions were later closed. Poles in Ukraine and Belarus had national districts that were dissolved in the mid-1930s and were subject to the "Polish operation" of 1937–1938. Finns in Karelia, Germans, Greeks, Latvians, and others experienced similar combinations of institutional recognition and suspicion. In each case, the question of how national institutions functioned under central authority had different answers, depending on the group's size, location, and relationship to foreign states.

Section 58.14

Nationalities, Border Security, and the Deportations of the 1930s

The Soviet state used forced population removal as an instrument long before the Second World War. Chapter 56 treated the deportation of "kulaks" during collectivization. In the mid-1930s the government began to deport entire communities identified by nationality from border regions, on the basis of the assumption that members of nationalities with ties to neighboring states were potential spies or saboteurs. In 1935, Finns and Estonians were removed from areas near the Leningrad border. In early 1936, according to the usual research estimates, about 15,000 Polish and German households, roughly 70,000 people (households and persons should not be confused), were moved from the western border zone of Ukraine to Kazakhstan. Kurds and other groups were removed from border areas of the Transcaucasus in 1937, and Iranians in 1938. These actions were carried out by the NKVD under decisions of the party and government, and the people deported were sent to remote areas, usually with limited resources and with significant losses.

The most thoroughly documented case is the deportation of Soviet Koreans from the Far East. Koreans had settled in the Russian Far East from the 1860s, and by the 1930s a community of roughly 170,000 or more lived near the border with Japanese-controlled Korea and Manchuria. Korean-language schools and newspapers had been promoted in the 1920s, and Koreans had been active in agriculture, especially rice growing. During the 1930s, the NKVD arrested many Korean leaders and intellectuals, and in the summer of 1937 the Council of People's Commissars and the Central Committee, in a decision of 21 August signed by Molotov and Stalin, ordered the removal of the Korean population from the border districts of the Far Eastern Territory. The stated justification was to prevent the penetration of Japanese espionage. Between September and November 1937, NKVD reports state, about 172,000 people were transported in freight trains, largely to Kazakhstan and Uzbekistan, where they were settled in agricultural areas, often in difficult conditions. The mortality of the first winters was significant, and survivors gradually built new communities; estimates of the number who died vary, and the source base is imperfect. The Koreans were not allowed to return, and the deportation remained a subject of official silence for decades.

The historical debate concerns the motivation. The government's own language emphasized espionage and security. Historians note that Japanese intelligence did recruit agents in the region, and that the Soviet leadership was genuinely concerned about the threat from Japan, particularly after the outbreak of the Sino-Japanese war in July 1937 (§58.22). But the documentary record has not shown that Koreans as a group posed the threat claimed, and the removal of an entire population on the basis of its ethnicity, including loyal Soviet citizens and party members, was a form of collective punishment. Some scholars view the deportations as part of a Soviet "ethnic cleansing" of borderlands, others as a security measure of the Stalinist state that employed a nationality-based logic, and others emphasize the broad shift to treating ethnicity as a marker of political reliability. These interpretations are not mutually exclusive, and all rest on the documentary evidence that the nationality of the deportees was the key criterion.

These removals should be distinguished from the NKVD's "national operations" of 1937–1938, discussed in Chapter 57, in which hundreds of thousands of people, such as Poles, Germans, Latvians, and others, were arrested and either shot or sent to camps under quotas set by the center. The deportations of the 1930s moved populations to new places of settlement under administrative decision, generally preserving them as communities, albeit with heavy mortality and loss of property, while the national operations were mass arrests with executions. Both were based on the premise that certain nationalities were suspect, and both preceded the wartime deportations of 1941–1944, which affected Germans, Chechens, Ingush, Crimean Tatars, and others (Chapters 59 and 60).

Section 58.15

Soviet Identity, Russian Cultural Influence, and the Contradictions of Federalism

The constitution adopted on 5 December 1936 (Chapter 57) restated the federal structure of the Soviet Union and increased the number of union republics to eleven by upgrading the Kazakh and Kyrgyz ASSRs and dividing the Transcaucasian Federation. It created a bicameral Supreme Soviet, with a Soviet of the Union and a Soviet of Nationalities, and it affirmed in principle that each union republic had the right to secede, a provision that had no practical mechanism for use. It also guaranteed equal rights to citizens "irrespective of nationality or race." The constitution's wording reflected the leadership's claim that the nationalities question had been solved by the Soviet system, in contrast to the capitalist world. The cultural result was the doctrine of the "friendship of peoples," visible in festivals of national art in Moscow, in "decades" of national literature, and in the performances of folk ensembles that celebrated each republic's costume and music.

The same years also strengthened the central place of Russian culture. The Russian language was the language of the army, the all-Union party, and interrepublic communication, and the compulsory study decree of 1938 formalized its role in schooling. Official history increasingly highlighted the Russian people as the Union's "first among equals," and tsarist-era heroes such as Alexander Nevsky, Peter the Great, and Dmitry Donskoy were rehabilitated as examples of patriotism (Chapters 15, 20, and 31). This did not imply the revival of the imperial ideology of Official Nationality (Chapter 38). The state still condemned the tsarist empire as a "prison of nations," and it supported national cultures in form. But the combination of a more prominent Russian cultural role with the repression of national elites produced a hierarchy within the formally equal family of nations.

The 1939 census gives a snapshot of the outcome. It recorded a population of about 170 million. Russians made up a little under three-fifths of the total, Ukrainians about one-sixth, and Uzbeks, Belarusians, Tatars, Kazakhs, Jews, Azerbaijanis, Georgians, Armenians, and others smaller shares. The census used a much smaller number of nationality categories than that of 1926, consolidating many small groups into larger ones, which reflects the state's classificatory strategy. Census figures must be treated carefully because the 1937 census had been suppressed, because of the famine and repression losses, and because of the possibility of manipulated counts. They are nonetheless the principal quantitative source on the state's population in these years.

The key contradictions are institutional. The republics had constitutions, legislatures, languages, and cultural institutions, yet the party was centralized, and republican leaders were appointed or removed from Moscow. The state upheld national equality in principle while using nationality as a ground for suspicion and punishment. It promoted national cultures while limiting their autonomy. These were not simply hypocrisy; they reflected the leadership's belief that the Soviet system could contain national forms within a unified socialist state and its fear that national movements might threaten that unity. Scholars disagree on how to characterize the result, with some describing the USSR as a multinational state with genuine, if constrained, federal and national institutions, others emphasizing a centralized empire with national disguises, and others pointing to a "state-sponsored evolution of ethnic identities" that built the nations it claimed to represent. The legacy of those institutions, including republican borders, titular languages, and national elites, mattered greatly when the system weakened in the 1980s (Chapters 65 and 66).

Section 58.16

The Soviet Union's Strategic Position in the Early 1930s

Soviet foreign policy in the 1920s had rested on a mixture of revolutionary hopes, cautious diplomacy, and pragmatic cooperation with other outsiders to the postwar order. The Treaty of Rapallo (1922) with Germany had normalized relations, and the Treaty of Berlin (1926) renewed the commitment to neutrality. Secret military cooperation allowed German forces, forbidden by the Treaty of Versailles from having certain weapons, to test aircraft and armor at Soviet facilities such as the Lipetsk flying school and the Kama tank school near Kazan, while the Red Army learned from German staff methods and technology. The relationship was never free of mistrust, but it gave the USSR a significant partner in a Europe that otherwise viewed it with hostility. Relations with Britain, France, and the United States were interrupted by disputes over debts, propaganda, and the activities of the Comintern, and the United States did not recognize the Soviet government until November 1933.

Two developments undermined this position. In September 1931 Japanese army units in Manchuria staged an incident and began the conquest of the region, creating the puppet state of Manchukuo in 1932. This put a powerful and ambitious state on the Soviet Far Eastern border and raised the question of Japanese designs on Soviet Siberia and on Mongolia, where the USSR had a protective relationship. The Soviet government, seeking to avoid war, sold its interest in the Chinese Eastern Railway to Manchukuo in 1935, even as it reinforced its Far Eastern forces. The second development was the rise of Hitler. The Nazi movement was explicitly anticommunist and its leader had written of expansion in the East. After January 1933 the military collaboration with Germany ended, and relations cooled, though the Soviet leadership initially hoped that economic ties and the old Rapallo habits might survive. These hopes faded as Germany repressed its Communists, withdrew from the League of Nations and the disarmament conference in October 1933, and concluded a declaration of nonaggression with Poland in January 1934.

Soviet leaders analyzed these challenges through a framework that mixed Marxist-Leninist assumptions and traditional state interests. Official ideology held that capitalist states were driven toward conflicts among themselves and toward hostility to the USSR; Stalin's report to the Seventeenth Party Congress in January 1934 stated that the USSR sought peace but stood ready to respond to aggression, and that the situation was increasingly dangerous. At the same time, Soviet diplomats and military planners confronted practical issues. The Red Army was large but was being reequipped and mechanized under the Five-Year Plans (Chapter 55), and the Soviet western frontier was long and was separated from Germany by Poland and Romania, states hostile to the USSR and suspicious of its aims. The country's relative strength depended on its economy and on relations with potential partners who could support, or hinder, any Soviet military action to the west.

The leadership's response in the early 1930s was a series of nonaggression pacts. The USSR concluded such agreements with Finland, Latvia, Estonia, and Poland in 1932, and with France in November 1932, as well as with other neighbors. It also pursued recognition and trade. The Roosevelt-Litvinov exchange of November 1933 established diplomatic relations with the United States, though hopes for substantial cooperation, including on credits, were not realized. Meanwhile, the most significant change was a reorientation of strategy from limiting exposure to isolation toward seeking partnerships with the Western democracies against the revisionist powers. The next section examines how this appeared in diplomacy.

Section 58.17

Maxim Litvinov, Collective Security, and the League of Nations

Maxim Litvinov, an old Bolshevik who had lived in Britain and spoke several languages, served as People's Commissar for Foreign Affairs from 1930 to 1939. He was a skilled and experienced diplomat who argued that the Soviet Union's interest lay in organizing the powers that were opposed to aggression into a common front, a policy known as collective security. In 1933–1934 he and other officials began to promote closer ties with France, which saw Germany as its main threat, and with the smaller states of Europe. The Soviet government dropped its earlier criticism of the League of Nations, which it had called an instrument of imperialist powers, and in 1934 accepted an invitation to join it. On 18 September 1934 the League Assembly voted to admit the USSR, with a few states, including the Netherlands, Portugal, and Switzerland, voting against, and the Soviet Union received a permanent seat on the Council.

Litvinov used the League as a platform. He proposed definitions of aggression, argued for the indivisibility of peace, and urged that members act together against violations. He spoke in 1935 and 1936 on the Italian invasion of Ethiopia, supported sanctions on Italy, and criticized the failure of members to act decisively. The Soviet position was consistent in rhetoric: it aimed at making a possible German attack on the USSR or its neighbors costly by creating a coalition. The limitations were equally visible. The League lacked its own armed force, depended on the willingness of its major members, and was already weakened by the absence of the United States, the departure of Japan and Germany, and the failure to restrain Italy. Soviet policy also faced skepticism, since many in Western capitals doubted the sincerity of a government whose Comintern simultaneously called for revolution abroad.

Historians have asked what the policy represented. One reading sees a real conversion by the leadership to the view that cooperation with democracies against fascism was necessary for Soviet security. Another emphasizes that collective security was a tactic that left room for other options, including an arrangement with Germany, and that Stalin, who did not share Litvinov's personal convictions, kept a degree of flexibility. The record of Soviet contacts with German officials in 1933–1935, including discussions of trade and of improved relations, shows that the possibility of rapprochement was never wholly abandoned. These readings are compatible. The evidence indicates that Soviet leaders pursued collective security as the preferred course while hedging, and that the policy's success depended on conditions beyond Soviet control, in particular the readiness of France and Britain to commit to a common anti-German front.

Collective security also coexisted with the domestic repression discussed in Chapter 57. The terror of 1936–1938, including the Moscow trials and the purge of the officer corps in June 1937, damaged Soviet credibility in the eyes of foreign observers, some of whom concluded that the Red Army had been weakened to the point where it was a doubtful ally. Others, including some Western diplomats and journalists, accepted the official Soviet account of the trials. How much the purges influenced the decisions of Britain and France is debated (§58.23).

Section 58.18

The Soviet-French and Soviet-Czechoslovak Agreements of 1935

France was the pivotal partner. French Foreign Minister Louis Barthou, in 1934, advanced the idea of an "Eastern Locarno," a regional pact in which the USSR, Germany, Poland, Czechoslovakia, and the Baltic states would guarantee one another's borders, with France guaranteeing the arrangement and the USSR guaranteeing the Locarno treaties. Germany and Poland rejected the proposal. Barthou was killed in October 1934 in Marseille, in the assassination of King Alexander of Yugoslavia, and his successor, Pierre Laval, was more cautious, but the French government concluded that a treaty with the USSR was a necessary element of its security. Alarm grew when Germany announced the existence of its air force and the reintroduction of conscription in March 1935, in violation of the Versailles settlement.

The Franco-Soviet Treaty of Mutual Assistance was signed in Paris on 2 May 1935. It obliged each party to consult and to give immediate assistance if the other were the victim of an unprovoked attack by a European state, but its provisions were hedged with references to the League of Nations Covenant, and no military convention accompanied it. Two weeks later, on 16 May 1935, the Soviet Union and Czechoslovakia signed a similar treaty, with a key condition: the Soviet obligation to assist Czechoslovakia would take effect only if France had first honored its own commitment to Czechoslovakia. The condition reflected Czechoslovak reluctance to rely on Soviet help alone, since the Czechoslovak government did not wish to be seen as an instrument of the USSR, and the Soviet reluctance to become involved in a war in Central Europe without French participation. It also created a structure of conditionality that mattered in 1938.

The treaties were a significant diplomatic achievement but proved limited in practice. The French Chamber of Deputies ratified the Franco-Soviet treaty only in February 1936, after prolonged debate in which the conservative opposition argued that it would provoke Germany and would involve France in the USSR's quarrels. Hitler seized on the ratification as a pretext to remilitarize the Rhineland on 7 March 1936, and the French and British governments did not respond militarily. Staff talks between the French and Soviet armies were slow and cautious, and French military leaders doubted the USSR's reliability and capacity, particularly after the military purges. A further obstacle was geography: the Red Army could reach Germany or Czechoslovakia only by crossing Poland or Romania, whose governments refused to allow the passage of Soviet troops. The treaties therefore created legal commitments and political symbols but did not create a workable military alliance. In March 1936 the USSR also signed a protocol of mutual assistance with Mongolia, extending its security commitments in the East.

Section 58.19

The Communist International and the Popular Front Strategy

The Communist International, or Comintern, was founded in 1919 to coordinate revolutionary parties worldwide, and by the late 1920s it was dominated by the Soviet party. Its Sixth Congress in 1928 adopted the "Third Period" line, which held that capitalism was entering a final crisis, that Communists should compete with social democrats, regarded as "social fascists," and that cooperation with them was a betrayal. In Germany this line led the Communist Party (KPD) to treat the Social Democrats as its main enemy, rather than the Nazis, and the divisions of the left are one of the factors historians cite when explaining the failure to prevent Hitler's rise. The Nazi seizure of power and the destruction of the KPD in 1933 were a political disaster that provoked reassessment, though the shift in the Comintern's line was gradual.

The impetus for change came from several directions. In France, the riots of February 1934 by right-wing groups alarmed both Communists and Socialists, and the two parties moved toward joint action, signing a unity-of-action pact in July 1934. Georgi Dimitrov, the Bulgarian Communist who had defended himself with striking effect at the Reichstag fire trial in Leipzig in 1933 and had been acquitted, arrived in Moscow in 1934 and became an influential voice for broader alliances. The Seventh Congress of the Comintern, held in Moscow from 25 July to 20 August 1935, endorsed the Popular Front: cooperation of Communists with socialists and, where possible, with liberal and democratic forces against fascism. Dimitrov, elected general secretary, defined fascism in terms of the interests of the most reactionary and imperialist sections of finance capital, a definition that preserved Marxist doctrine while making alliances more feasible. In 1936 Popular Front governments won elections in Spain (February) and France (May–June, under Léon Blum).

The Popular Front illustrated the tension between revolutionary ideology and Soviet state interest. For foreign Communists, it opened opportunities for mass politics, trade-union cooperation, and electoral success, and it brought in intellectuals who admired the USSR as an antifascist force. For the Soviet government, it served to support collective security by showing that Communists would cooperate with democratic governments and would not seek revolution at their expense. At the same time, Comintern parties remained subordinate to Moscow, and Soviet priorities could override those of local parties. The terror in the USSR reached the Comintern itself: many foreign Communists living in Moscow were arrested and shot in 1937–1938, including most of the leadership of the Polish Communist Party, which was dissolved in 1938 by the Comintern on false charges of infiltration, and the Hungarian leader Béla Kun. Scholars disagree about whether the Comintern was simply an instrument of Soviet foreign policy or retained a meaningful degree of autonomy; the archival evidence available since 1991 shows extensive Soviet control, but also considerable local initiative and variation in practice.

Popular Front

Definition: A political strategy, endorsed by the Comintern in 1935, of forming broad antifascist coalitions among Communists, socialists, and liberal or democratic parties.

Historical context: It replaced the "Third Period" policy of 1928–1934 that had opposed cooperation with social democrats. Popular Front governments were elected in France and Spain in 1936, and the strategy shaped Communist politics in several countries and the support network for the Spanish Republic.

Why it matters: The Popular Front is often misunderstood as a Soviet commitment to democracy or as a mere trick. It is better understood as a response to the fascist threat that reflected both real shifts in Communist thinking and Soviet state needs, and it remained subject to Soviet control and to sudden reversal, as the events of 1939 showed.

Section 58.20

The Spanish Civil War and Soviet Intervention, 1936–1939

The Spanish Civil War began on 17–18 July 1936 with a military uprising against the Popular Front government of the Spanish Republic. The rebels, soon led by General Francisco Franco, received military assistance from Italy and Germany almost from the start, including aircraft and, later, the German Condor Legion and Italian ground forces. France and Britain, fearing a wider war, promoted a policy of nonintervention, and in the summer of 1936 twenty-seven European states, including Germany, Italy, and the USSR, joined a Non-Intervention Committee that met in London. The Soviet government adhered to the agreement in August 1936 but, as evidence mounted that Germany and Italy were violating it, concluded that it should not be bound, and from October 1936 began to supply the Republic. The decision was made at the highest level, and the operation, which has been called Operation X, was organized by Soviet military intelligence and the Commissariat of Defense, working through Soviet shipping and secret arrangements with the Republican authorities.

Soviet aid included aircraft, tanks, artillery, machine guns, and ammunition, as well as military advisers, pilots, tank crews, and technical specialists, probably numbering a few thousand over the course of the war (with about two thousand present at the peak). Soviet fighters and tanks, such as the I-15 and I-16 aircraft and the T-26 and BT-5 tanks, made a significant difference in the defense of Madrid in late 1936. In October–November 1936 the Spanish Republican government shipped a large portion of its gold reserve, in the range of 500 tonnes, to the USSR, where it was held as payment for arms and services. The gold became a subject of long controversy, with Franco's government alleging that the USSR had cheated the Republic and Soviet sources maintaining that the sums were applied against the cost of supplies; historians have worked from Spanish and Soviet archival records, which indicate that the financial arrangements were opaque and favorable to the Soviet side, although the precise accounting is disputed.

The Comintern organized the International Brigades, volunteer units drawn from more than fifty countries, who began arriving in the autumn of 1936. Most estimates place the total number of volunteers at roughly 35,000 or more at different times, with many killed. They included Communists, other leftists, antifascists, and idealists, and they helped to bolster the defense of Madrid and fought in battles such as Jarama and Guadalajara in 1937 and the Ebro in 1938. The brigades were organized under Communist leadership, with their base at Albacete, and they became a symbol of international antifascism. Soviet policy was constrained by distance, by the difficulty of shipping arms past hostile naval patrols and across a closed French border, and by the diplomatic imperative of not provoking a general war, which the Soviet leadership could not wish while engaged in collective-security diplomacy. As the war went on and Soviet attention shifted to the European crisis of 1938, Soviet aid declined, and the International Brigades were withdrawn in the autumn of 1938, with a farewell parade in Barcelona on 28 October.

Russia and the World

Soviet Participation in the Spanish Civil War

The Spanish Civil War was a European conflict that tied the USSR to a country at the far end of the continent, and it drew in all the major powers. For the Soviet Union, intervention offered several things: support for a Popular Front government that fit the new antifascist line, an opportunity to test equipment and train officers against German and Italian forces, and a chance to counter the accusation that the USSR stood aside from the fascist threat. It also carried risks, including diplomatic entanglement and the diversion of resources.

Interaction was not one-directional. Soviet advisers and equipment influenced Republican military organization, while Soviet officers gained experience of modern air and tank warfare, though the lessons they drew, and the consequences of the executions of many of them on return in 1937–1938, remain debated. Spanish Republican officials, politicians, and ordinary citizens made their own choices, and the Soviet role was greatest in the military and intelligence spheres. Spanish children were also evacuated to the USSR, where several thousand lived through the war years. The episode shows how Soviet policy operated on a transnational stage, interacting with other states, communist and noncommunist organizations, and international public opinion.

Section 58.21

Spain, International Communism, and the Divisions of Antifascism

The Republican side was not a single movement. It included liberal Republicans, Socialists, Communists, Catalan and Basque nationalists, and anarchists, who in the early months of the war carried out social revolution in parts of Spain, collectivizing land and industry in Catalonia and Aragon. The Spanish Communist Party, small before 1936, grew rapidly because it offered discipline, organization, and Soviet arms, and because it argued that winning the war had to take priority over revolution. Others, including the anarchist CNT-FAI and the Marxist but anti-Stalinist POUM (Workers' Party of Marxist Unification), argued that the war and the revolution could not be separated. The Soviet position was to support a Popular Front government in a form acceptable to the Western democracies, and to restrain what it considered revolutionary excess.

These disagreements produced violence within the Republican camp. In early May 1937 street fighting in Barcelona, known as the May Days, pitted anarchists and POUM members against Communist-led forces and the Republican government. The aftermath saw the replacement of Prime Minister Francisco Largo Caballero by Juan Negrín, the suppression of the POUM, and the arrest and murder of its leader Andrés Nin in June 1937 by agents working with Soviet security officers. Soviet advisers and NKVD personnel, including Alexander Orlov, took part in security operations in Spain, and Soviet officials such as the consul-general in Barcelona, Vladimir Antonov-Ovseenko, and the ambassador, Marcel Rosenberg, were recalled in 1937 and later executed, as the Terror reached the Spanish operation itself. Writers who were in Spain, including George Orwell, whose Homage to Catalonia (1938) is a firsthand source, described the suppression of the POUM; other participants and Soviet and Communist accounts presented it as the suppression of a dangerous fifth column. Historians evaluate these competing accounts through Spanish, Soviet, and Comintern records and a wide range of memoirs, and they continue to debate how far the Communists' policy was a cause of the Republic's difficulties or a necessary response to wartime needs.

The war ended in Republican defeat. Franco's forces took Catalonia in early 1939 and Madrid on 28 March, and he declared the war over on 1 April 1939. The consequences were wide. For the Republic and its supporters, defeat meant exile for hundreds of thousands, including many who crossed to France, and repression within Spain. For the Soviet Union, the outcome reinforced doubts about the reliability of the Western democracies, which had not helped the Republic, and strengthened the argument, already voiced in Moscow, that France and Britain were willing to allow Germany to expand eastward. It also contributed to the view among some Western policymakers that Soviet involvement in Spain was a matter of ideological ambition rather than security. Both interpretations shaped perceptions in 1938–1939, and they are part of the background to the negotiations discussed below. For antifascists, Spain remained a moral and political reference point, and its memory later became a contested subject in both Soviet and Western narratives.

Section 58.22

The Soviet Union, Japan, and East Asian Security, 1931–1939

East Asia was the second front of Soviet strategic concern. After the Japanese seizure of Manchuria, the USSR faced a long frontier with Manchukuo and a state that regarded itself as an anticommunist power. Japan also sought influence in Mongolia, where the USSR had maintained a Soviet-aligned Mongolian People's Republic since the 1920s and where Soviet troops were stationed after the 1936 protocol. In November 1936 Japan and Germany signed the Anti-Comintern Pact, which was presented as a defense against communism and contained a secret protocol on consultation in the event of a Soviet attack on either party; Italy joined in 1937. The Soviet leadership saw it as a step toward coordinated pressure on the USSR from both east and west.

The outbreak of the full-scale Sino-Japanese War in July 1937 transformed the situation. The Soviet Union signed a nonaggression pact with Chinese Nationalist government of Chiang Kai-shek on 21 August 1937 and provided substantial aid, including aircraft, pilots, artillery, and credits, and sent several thousand military personnel as advisers and volunteers. The assistance served Soviet interests by keeping Japanese forces occupied in China and reducing the likelihood of an attack on Siberia, and it was provided despite the Soviet Union's ideological connection to the Chinese Communist Party, which was allied in a united front with the Nationalists. Soviet aid declined after 1941, when the USSR and Japan signed a neutrality pact.

The tension on the border produced two major armed clashes. At Lake Khasan, near the point where the borders of the USSR, Manchukuo, and Korea meet, Japanese and Soviet forces fought from 29 July to 11 August 1938 over disputed heights. The Soviet Far Eastern Front, led by Marshal Vasily Blyukher, eventually prevailed, but the fighting revealed command problems, and Blyukher was arrested in October 1938 and died in custody. A larger conflict occurred in 1939 on the Mongolian-Manchurian frontier, along the Khalkhin Gol river, from May to September. Soviet and Mongolian forces, under the command of Georgy Zhukov, used massed tanks, aircraft, and artillery in a large offensive in late August to defeat the Japanese Sixth Army, and a cease-fire was agreed in mid-September. The two battles are significant for several reasons. They showed that the Soviet Union could defend its Far Eastern frontier and strengthened the position of those in Japan who favored expansion southward rather than northward. They also provided experience for Soviet commanders, notably Zhukov. Their chronology should be distinguished from that of the European crises: Khalkhin Gol's decisive phase coincided with the final days of the Anglo-French-Soviet and German-Soviet negotiations, and Soviet leaders may have been influenced by the desire to avoid a two-front war, a point discussed in §58.25.

Section 58.23

The Munich Crisis of 1938 and the Failure of Collective Security

In 1938 Germany's expansion accelerated. On 12–13 March German troops entered Austria, and the union (Anschluss) was proclaimed; the western powers protested but did not act. The Soviet government responded by proposing, through Litvinov, a conference of states to consider collective action, but the British government rejected the idea. Hitler then turned to Czechoslovakia, where he supported the demands of the Sudeten German minority, led by Konrad Henlein, for autonomy and, increasingly, for union with Germany. Czechoslovakia was a parliamentary democracy with a capable army and well-fortified frontier defenses, and it was allied with France and, conditionally, with the USSR (§58.18).

During the summer and September, Britain's Prime Minister Neville Chamberlain pressed Czechoslovakia to make concessions, and the French government under Édouard Daladier, reluctant to fight without Britain, followed. The Soviet government stated repeatedly that it would fulfill its treaty obligations if France did, and Litvinov made this point at the League of Nations in Geneva on 21 September. Soviet military districts took measures of partial mobilization, according to Soviet and Czechoslovak records. The practical questions were how Soviet forces could reach Czechoslovakia, since Poland and Romania refused transit, and whether Czechoslovakia would resist without French support. President Edvard Beneš, who ultimately accepted Anglo-French demands, did not call on the USSR to act independently. On 29–30 September 1938 Chamberlain, Daladier, Hitler, and Mussolini signed the Munich Agreement at a conference from which both Czechoslovakia and the USSR were excluded. It required Czechoslovakia to cede the Sudetenland to Germany. Poland and Hungary also took slices of Czechoslovak territory. In March 1939 Germany occupied the Czech lands and established a protectorate, and Slovakia became a nominally independent client state.

The Munich crisis is among the most debated episodes in modern diplomatic history, and the Soviet role is contested. Some historians argue that Munich demonstrated that Britain and France were not willing to oppose Germany and were instead directing it eastward, and that the exclusion of the USSR convinced Stalin that collective security was dead. Others point out that Soviet readiness to assist Czechoslovakia was conditional, that it was uncertain how, or whether, the USSR would have acted, and that Soviet policy aimed in part to shift responsibility to the West. Czechoslovak archives, opened after 1989, show that Soviet officials gave Beneš assurances, but the evidence regarding Soviet military preparations is partial and open to different readings. Another debate concerns the effect of the Great Terror: some argue that the purge of the Red Army in 1937–1938 undermined Western confidence in the USSR as a partner, while others stress that Western reluctance had political and ideological roots independent of the purges, including anticommunism and a desire to avoid war. These questions are not settled, and students should treat any single-cause explanation with caution.

Cause and Effect

The Erosion of Collective Security, 1934–1939

Long-Term Conditions

Mutual distrust between the USSR and the Western powers dating from 1917; the geography of Eastern Europe, which separated the USSR from Germany by Poland and Romania; the collapse of the post-Versailles order in the Depression years; Soviet ideological suspicion of capitalist states.

Intermediate Developments

The League's failure over Manchuria and Ethiopia; the Rhineland remilitarization of 1936; nonintervention in Spain; the Terror and the military purge; British and French reluctance to commit to Eastern Europe; Polish and Romanian refusal of Soviet transit.

Short-Term Triggers

The Anschluss of March 1938; the Sudeten crisis of summer 1938; the Munich Agreement of 29–30 September 1938; the German occupation of Prague in March 1939.

Effects

Exclusion of the USSR from European settlement; hardening of Soviet suspicion of Anglo-French motives; the opening of alternative options, including a settlement with Germany; the beginnings of the 1939 negotiations.

Historical complexity: No single cause explains the outcome. Western appeasement, Soviet policy and conditionality, Polish and Romanian security calculations, Czechoslovak decisions, and German aggression interacted. Attributing the failure to one government's bad faith, or treating the outcome as inevitable, obscures the choices and alternatives available to each.

Section 58.24

The Anglo-French-Soviet Negotiations of 1939

After Munich the Soviet leadership adopted a more guarded posture. In a speech to the Eighteenth Party Congress on 10 March 1939, Stalin criticized the Western powers for policies that, in his view, aimed to push Germany toward conflict with the USSR, and he warned that the Soviet Union would not "pull chestnuts out of the fire" for others. The speech was read at the time, and by later historians, as a signal that Moscow was reconsidering its options, though it did not renounce collective security. After the German occupation of Prague on 15 March, Britain and France reversed the policy of appeasement: Britain gave a guarantee to Poland on 31 March 1939, and Britain and France later extended guarantees to Romania and Greece. Conscription was introduced in Britain in April. In this context both Western governments reopened discussions with the USSR.

On 17 April the Soviet government proposed a tripartite pact of mutual assistance among Britain, France, and the USSR, backed by a military convention, with guarantees extending to the states of Eastern Europe. London and Paris responded slowly. The British government, led by Chamberlain, doubted the USSR's military value, feared that a pact would provoke Hitler and alienate Poland and Romania, and distrusted Soviet motives, while the French were more favorable but followed the British lead. On 3 May 1939 Litvinov was dismissed and replaced as foreign commissar by Molotov, who kept his post as head of government. The change was widely interpreted as a sign of a shift in policy, and it has been debated whether it marked a decision for a German option or merely a tougher approach. Litvinov was Jewish and was associated with the policy of cooperation with the West, and his removal also removed a diplomat whom Germany regarded as an obstacle. Soviet foreign ministry personnel were purged in the same period.

The negotiations that followed, from April to August, centered on several disputes. The Soviet side wanted a firm alliance with automatic military obligations, and it insisted on including a guarantee of the Baltic states and Finland against "indirect aggression," a term meaning internal subversion or coup by a German-backed party, which the Baltic governments and Britain feared could justify Soviet intervention. The Western governments preferred a more limited commitment and wished to avoid antagonizing states that did not want Soviet guarantees. Most important was the question of military access. A Soviet army could fight Germany in Poland only if it were allowed to enter Polish territory, and the Polish government, under Foreign Minister Józef Beck, refused to agree, fearing that Soviet troops, once in, would not leave. The Romanian government took a similar position. In August an Anglo-French military mission, led by Admiral Sir Reginald Plunkett-Ernle-Erle-Drax and General Joseph Doumenc, traveled to Moscow by slow ship and train, arriving on 11 August, and had limited authority to commit to specifics. The talks with Marshal Kliment Voroshilov stalled on 14 August over the passage question. In the Soviet view, the Western powers were not serious; in the British and French view, the USSR was demanding terms that Poland and Romania would not accept.

Meanwhile contacts between Soviet and German officials had developed. In the spring and summer, German diplomats probed the possibility of improved relations, starting with economic discussions. The Soviet chargé d'affaires in Berlin, Georgi Astakhov, and the German ambassador in Moscow, Friedrich-Werner von der Schulenburg, were among those involved. By the second half of July, trade talks were making progress, and in August political discussions began in earnest. The relationship between the two sets of negotiations is a central problem: whether the Soviet leadership used the German option to pressure the West, or was already committed to it and used the Western talks as cover, or kept both options open until a decisive moment. Evidence available from German, British, French, Polish, and some Soviet sources supports the view that the Soviet leadership had been keeping both channels open and decided in mid-August that the German channel offered more.

Section 58.25

The Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact and the Diplomatic Reversal of August 1939

In early August 1939 Germany was preparing an attack on Poland, with an invasion date set for late August, and wished to be sure of Soviet neutrality. On 14 August, German Foreign Minister Joachim von Ribbentrop indicated that he was ready to travel to Moscow. On 19 August the two countries signed a trade and credit agreement providing Germany with Soviet raw materials in exchange for German manufactured goods and credits. On 20 August Hitler sent a personal telegram to Stalin urging a quick conclusion of the political talks, and on 21 August Stalin replied agreeing to receive Ribbentrop. The foreign minister flew to Moscow on 23 August, was received by Stalin and Molotov, and in the night, with Molotov, signed a ten-year Treaty of Non-Aggression, dated 23 August 1939, together with a Secret Additional Protocol. The signing ceremony was photographed, with Stalin present and smiling in some of the images, and the treaty was announced the next day.

The public treaty was brief. Its articles bound the parties to refrain from any act of force or aggression against each other, whether alone or together with other powers, to remain neutral if one of them became the object of hostilities by a third power, not to join any grouping of powers directed against the other, to consult on matters of common interest, and to resolve disputes peacefully. It was to remain in force for ten years, with automatic renewal for five years unless denounced, and it took effect immediately on signing. The secret protocol, which was not made public, divided Eastern Europe into spheres of influence. It stated that in the event of a territorial and political reorganization of the Baltic states (Finland, Estonia, Latvia, Lithuania), the northern boundary of Lithuania would form the boundary between the German and Soviet spheres, with Lithuania's interest in the Vilna region recognized. It provided that in the event of a reorganization of the territories of the Polish state, the spheres would be bounded roughly by the Narew, Vistula, and San rivers, and that the question of whether the maintenance of an independent Polish state was desirable would be decided later. It recorded the Soviet interest in Bessarabia and the German declaration of complete political disinterest in that territory. These provisions were those of 23 August; they were revised by the German-Soviet Boundary and Friendship Treaty of 28 September 1939, after the invasion of Poland, in which Lithuania was shifted into the Soviet sphere in exchange for additional Polish territory in the German sphere (Chapter 59). The two sets of provisions must not be confused.

For the Soviet leadership, the treaty offered several things: it removed, for the time being, the prospect of facing Germany alone while Britain and France stood aside; it offered the opportunity to recover territories lost in 1918–1921 and to establish a buffer zone to the west; and it promised economic advantages. For Germany it eliminated the threat of a two-front war in the event of an attack on Poland. For Europe the immediate consequences were profound. The treaty enabled Germany to attack Poland on 1 September, and it was followed on 17 September by the Soviet invasion of eastern Poland (Chapter 59). Communist parties abroad, which had campaigned for antifascist unity, were suddenly instructed to support the Soviet line, and many members left in confusion and anger. Soviet propaganda, which had denounced Nazism, fell silent, and films such as Alexander Nevsky were withdrawn. The Soviet government also later denied that any secret protocol existed. It was only in December 1989 that the Congress of People's Deputies of the USSR condemned the secret protocols and declared them legally invalid from the moment of signing, after an investigation led by Alexander Yakovlev, working at first from German-captured copies that had long been published in the West, with the Soviet originals located later (Chapter 65 and Chapter 66).

Analysis: The paired documents let students compare what the governments told the world with what they agreed in private. The documents show the intentions and calculations of the signatories at the time of signing but do not explain their motives, nor do they show how the protocol was later implemented, which depended on events from September 1939 to June 1940. Missing are the perspectives of Poland, the Baltic states, Finland, Romania, and the populations affected. Historical Debate

Why Did the Soviet Union Sign the Pact?

The question: What explains the Soviet decision to sign a nonaggression pact with Nazi Germany and a secret protocol on spheres of influence in August 1939?

Interpretation A: A defensive, security-driven decision. Some historians argue that the Soviet leadership, convinced that Britain and France were unwilling to form a firm alliance and that Munich showed a Western aim of turning Germany eastward, chose the pact to avoid facing Germany alone and to gain time and space for defense. They point to Western reluctance, the Polish refusal of Soviet troops, the delays of the military mission, and the vulnerability of the Soviet army after the purges and during the Khalkhin Gol battle.
Interpretation B: An opportunistic or expansionist decision. Others stress that the secret protocol gave the USSR the opportunity to regain territory and extend its influence into Eastern Europe, and that Soviet leaders were willing to partition Poland and to occupy the Baltic region. They note that the Soviet side demanded a share of territory and that the protocol went beyond what defensive needs required. Some also emphasize Stalin's belief that a war among capitalist powers would favor the USSR.
Interpretation C: A combination within a multi-sided breakdown. A third group of scholars stresses that the outcome resulted from the interaction of several governments' decisions, including British and French hesitation, Polish and Romanian refusals, German offers, and Soviet calculations, and that both defensive and expansionist motives were present.

Evidence: Historians use German, British, French, and Polish diplomatic archives, Soviet records released since the 1990s, the texts of the treaty and protocol, memoirs, and the observable conduct of the signatories after August 1939.

Points of agreement: The documents are authentic; the secret protocol existed and its terms were carried out in large part in 1939–1940; the Anglo-French-Soviet negotiations failed over military access and mutual distrust; and the pact enabled the German invasion of Poland.

Points of disagreement: The relative weight of defensive and expansionist motives; the timing of the Soviet decision; whether the USSR could have obtained a workable alliance; and how far Stalin's ideological expectations shaped the choice. The archival record on internal Soviet deliberations is incomplete.

Why it matters: The debate bears on how the Second World War began, on the status of the territories annexed in 1939–1940, and on later political memory. Official Soviet and Russian narratives have at times presented the pact as a forced response to Western policy, and some European governments and parliaments have condemned it as a joint act of aggression. These are political positions that should be distinguished from the evidence (Chapters 65, 66, and 72).

Student task: Identify two pieces of evidence that support the defensive interpretation and two that support the expansionist one, and explain whether the interpretations are mutually exclusive.

Whatever view one takes of the pact's motives, explaining the Soviet leadership's reasoning does not require accepting that its security fears justified the secret partition of neighboring states. This chapter ends here, on the eve of the events of September 1939, which Chapter 59 begins.

Section 58.26

Chapter Review and Historical Thinking Checkpoint

This chapter has examined three fields of Soviet history that are usually taught separately: cultural policy and social life, nationality policy, and foreign relations. Placing them together shows that they developed within one political system, under the same leadership, at the same time, and under the pressure of the same events. The consolidation of cultural institutions in 1932, the restructuring of the nationality system, and the turn to collective security in 1933–1934 were all responses to the aftermath of the First Five-Year Plan and the rise of Hitler. The Terror of 1937–1938 affected all three: it removed writers, republican leaders, foreign Communists, diplomats, and army commanders alike. Students should therefore be able to explain how internal policies and foreign relations interacted, for example by asking how the suspicion of border nationalities related to fears of Japan and Germany, or how the destruction of the officer corps and the Comintern leadership affected the credibility of Soviet diplomacy.

The chapter also invites comparison between the state's intentions and institutional practice. The regime proclaimed a unified aesthetic, but practice was uneven and permitted work of lasting quality, as well as silence and loss. It proclaimed the friendship of peoples and equal rights, while deporting populations and executing national elites. It proclaimed antifascism, then signed a treaty with the Nazi government. Historians explain these contradictions in several ways: as consequences of an ideology that subordinated every domain to state goals, as products of institutional competition and of the personal preferences of the leader, as responses to external threats, or as a combination. The evidence supports caution about monocausal explanations. Cultural and nationality policies had roots in the 1920s and in long-standing imperial legacies (Chapters 38, 43, 45, and 53); the diplomacy of the late 1930s was shaped by the decisions of other governments as well.

A third task is distinguishing the documentary record from later interpretation. The Writers' Union statute, the decree on Koreans, and the treaty and protocol of 1939 are documents whose texts are firmly established. Their motives, effects, and significance are matters of interpretation, which has been shaped by Cold War politics, Soviet secrecy, post-Soviet archival openings, and contemporary political debates over memory. Labels such as "Executed Renaissance," "ethnic cleansing," "appeasement," and "defensive pact" are interpretive and should be examined as such. Students should ask who is using a term, for what purpose, and on what evidence.

Chapter Summary

This chapter examined how the Soviet state of the 1930s tried to reshape culture and society, governed a multinational population, and responded to a worsening international environment. Its central argument was that the decade combined achievement and coercion in ways the leadership did not regard as contradictory: expanding literacy and cultural participation alongside censorship and terror, national institutions alongside centralization and deportation, and antifascist diplomacy followed by a pact with Nazi Germany.

In cultural life, the decree of April 1932 ended the contest among literary and artistic factions and created unified unions under party supervision. The First Writers' Congress of 1934 proclaimed socialist realism, an elastic doctrine that demanded truthful depiction of reality in its revolutionary development while serving the ideological education of the population. Within this framework Soviet literature, cinema, music, and the arts produced works of real merit and wide popularity, but artists faced censorship, patronage, denunciation, and, in 1937–1938, arrest. The attack on Shostakovich in 1936 and the fates of Mandelstam, Babel, and Meyerhold illustrate the stakes.

Schooling and literacy expanded massively, with the 1930 decree on universal primary education and the 1939 census showing literacy at about four-fifths of those aged nine and over. At the same time, schools returned to traditional subject teaching and discipline, history was reoriented toward a more patriotic narrative, and youth organizations promoted loyalty to the state. Science and technology were generously supported, yet political oversight and denunciation constrained research, and Lysenko's rise began in this decade, though the full institutional victory of Lysenkoism came later. Religious life was subjected to severe pressure, with closures of churches, mosques, synagogues, and monasteries and the arrest of clergy, but the 1937 census showed that a majority of respondents still described themselves as believers.

Family policy shifted from the liberal legislation of the 1920s to a pronatalist emphasis, with the decree of June 1936 restricting abortion, increasing state assistance for large families, and making divorce more difficult. Women worked in industry and agriculture and in the professions, but continued to carry the burden of domestic labor, and the gap between proclaimed equality and lived experience was large.

In nationality policy, the system of union and autonomous republics and the policy of korenizatsiya that built national institutions in the 1920s were modified in the 1930s. The state criticized local nationalism, revised orthographies, shifted scripts from Latin to Cyrillic, and required the study of Russian in 1938. The experiences varied. In Ukraine, the attack on cultural autonomy in 1933 and the executions of 1937 devastated a cultural generation; in Belarus, a smaller movement was similarly decimated; in Kazakhstan, famine and the destruction of the elite produced demographic and political upheaval; in Central Asia and the Caucasus, republican leaderships and writers were purged even as schooling and new institutions expanded. Security-driven deportations, notably of some 172,000 Soviet Koreans in 1937, applied a nationality-based logic to border regions, distinct from the NKVD's mass national operations.

In foreign policy, the rise of Hitler and Japanese expansion led the Soviet Union to pursue collective security under Litvinov: entry into the League of Nations in September 1934, treaties with France and Czechoslovakia in May 1935, and the Comintern's Popular Front line from 1935. Intervention in the Spanish Civil War, from autumn 1936, showed the opportunities and the limits of this policy, as did the divisions within the Republican camp. Border clashes with Japan at Lake Khasan and Khalkhin Gol demonstrated Soviet commitments in the east.

The Munich Agreement of September 1938 excluded the USSR and, in the Soviet view, confirmed Western unwillingness to resist Germany. The Anglo-French-Soviet talks of 1939, hampered by mutual distrust and the refusal of Poland and Romania to admit Soviet troops, failed to produce an alliance, while Soviet-German contacts grew. The nonaggression treaty and secret protocol of 23 August 1939 reversed the antifascist diplomacy of the preceding years and enabled the German attack on Poland. Historians disagree about the balance between defensive and expansionist motives, and the archival record is incomplete.

The chapter's lessons concern both method and substance. Policies in different fields had common sources and mutual effects, but outcomes were contingent on decisions by many actors. Regional and national variation shaped experience. The distinction between a document's text and its interpretation, and between official claims and evidence, was needed throughout. These themes continue in Chapter 59, which treats the Soviet invasion of Poland, the Winter War, and the annexations that followed the pact, and in Chapter 60, which treats wartime and postwar changes in nationality, religious, and cultural policy.

Cultural Revolution (1928–1931)
A campaign in which militant proletarian organizations, with party backing, attacked "bourgeois" specialists and the older intelligentsia in culture, education, and scholarship.

The Russian Association of Proletarian Writers, a dominant literary organization of the late 1920s, dissolved by the Central Committee in April 1932.

The official method of Soviet art and literature, requiring concrete depiction of reality in its revolutionary development and ideological education.

A label for art judged excessively concerned with form or technique at the expense of accessible, politically useful content, used against Shostakovich and Meyerhold.

A non-party writer who accepted the Revolution but did not join the party.
Likbez
The campaign for the "liquidation of illiteracy," involving courses and volunteer teachers.
Pedology
A field of child psychology and testing, condemned and abolished in 1936.
Pioneers and Komsomol
Mass youth organizations for children and for older youth, combining recreation, education, and political training.
Vernalization
A treatment of seeds that Lysenko promoted as a method of improving yields.
Sharashka
A prison design bureau in which arrested scientists and engineers worked under NKVD supervision.
League of the Militant Godless
A mass antireligious organization that promoted atheist propaganda.

Legislation restricting religious activity to worship in registered congregations and banning charitable and educational activity.
Hujum
The campaign begun in 1927 for the unveiling of Muslim women in Central Asia.
Pronatalism
Policy aimed at increasing the birth rate, as in the family legislation of 1936.
Korenizatsiya
The policy of promoting local languages, institutions, and personnel in national territories.
Titular nationality
The nationality for which a national territory was named.
Union republic
A republic with the highest formal status in the USSR; there were eleven at the end of 1936.
Autonomous republic (ASSR)
A national republic within a union republic, with fewer rights than a union republic.
Latinization
The introduction of Latin-based alphabets for languages of the USSR in the 1920s, reversed by the shift to Cyrillic in 1936–1941.

The official doctrine and cultural display of harmony among Soviet nationalities.

The identity document introduced in 1932 that recorded nationality and residence.

A policy of organizing multilateral action against aggression, pursued by Litvinov.
Comintern
The Communist International, an organization of Communist parties dominated by the Soviet party.
Popular Front
The antifascist coalition strategy endorsed in 1935.
Nonintervention
The policy of declining to supply either side in the Spanish Civil War, applied by Britain and France and formally accepted by others.
International Brigades
Volunteer units that fought for the Spanish Republic, organized largely by the Comintern.
Appeasement
The British and French policy of making concessions to Germany to avoid war, associated with Munich.

The confidential part of the 1939 German-Soviet agreement delimiting spheres of interest.
Indirect aggression
A term in the 1939 negotiations for internal subversion or a coup supported by a foreign power, a point of dispute over the Baltic states.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows the Soviet 1930s as a whole rather than as a sequence of separate stories. Culture, nationality, and diplomacy were connected by the leadership's anxieties, ambitions, and methods, and also by their human consequences. The Soviet citizen of the 1930s might attend a new school, ride a new subway, read an officially approved novel, belong to a youth organization, and at the same time live in fear of denunciation, find a church closed, lose a relative, or be deported. Historical understanding requires holding these experiences together.

The chapter's developments shaped later Soviet history. The institutions of the 1930s, including the Writers' Union, the republic structure and nationality categories, the central role of the Russian language, and the habits of diplomacy under strict central control, persisted in modified form until 1991. Some policies were reversed or transformed: wartime concessions to the Orthodox Church, the postwar family law, the deportations of 1941–1944, and the cultural campaigns of the late 1940s are treated in Chapters 59 and 60, and the thaw after 1953 in Chapter 61. Memory of the period also changed. The secret protocol, denied for decades, became a central subject of the late 1980s (Chapters 65 and 66), and the deportations and the repression of national elites became part of the national histories of the successor states.

The chapter also reveals larger themes of this textbook: the interaction of center and periphery; the coexistence of state-building and violence; the importance of evidence and of disagreement among historians; and the connection of Russian and Soviet history to Europe, the Caucasus, Central Asia, and East Asia. Unresolved questions remain, including how far the Soviet leadership believed its own justifications for the deportations, how much room artists and scientists had, what Stalin intended in August 1939, and how the experiences of ordinary people can be recovered from sources produced under censorship. Readers should resist two temptations: to treat the pact, or the 1930s generally, as inevitable, and to treat the later outcomes of 1941 and after as having been settled by the choices examined here. As Chapter 59 shows, the events of September 1939 opened a new and different set of possibilities.
