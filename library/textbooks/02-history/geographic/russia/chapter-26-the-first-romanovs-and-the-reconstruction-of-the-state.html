Part V — Crisis, Recovery, and the Seventeenth Century
26

The First Romanovs and the Reconstruction of the State

A History of Russia: From the Eurasian World to the Russian Federation

Chapter Opening

A tsar had been chosen. But who would collect the taxes, command the armies, administer the provinces, resettle the abandoned lands, negotiate peace with Muscovy's neighbors, and make Moscow's orders carry weight in towns and villages hundreds of miles from the Kremlin?

Chapter 25 ended in February 1613 with an Assembly of the Land electing sixteen-year-old Mikhail Romanov to the Muscovite throne, resolving, after years of famine, pretender politics, civil war, and foreign occupation, the acute question of who would rule. That election was a genuine achievement, and this chapter does not retell it. But an election, however broadly accepted, is not the same thing as a functioning state. On the morning after the assembly's decision, Mikhail's government inherited a realm whose finances had collapsed, whose taxable population had scattered or died, whose military institutions had been shattered by more than a decade of internal fighting and foreign invasion, whose administrative records in many regions had been destroyed or fallen out of date, and whose borders remained contested by Sweden and the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth. This chapter examines how the early Romanov government converted the restored legitimacy of 1613 into the practical capacity to govern.

The story this chapter tells is not one of simple restoration. Muscovy after 1613 did not merely reassemble the institutions that had existed before Ivan IV's later reign and the Time of Troubles; it rebuilt them under new pressures, with new urgency, and, in several important respects, on a larger and more demanding scale than before. Fiscal administrators had to relocate and reassess a population that had fled, died, or lost its former status during the crisis. Military planners had to rebuild an army capable of defending long, contested frontiers while absorbing new ideas about firearms, fortification, and troop organization circulating across contemporary Europe. Central chancelleries had to expand their reach into provinces where local government had been disrupted for a decade or more. None of this reconstruction happened automatically, and none of it happened without continued reliance on the same elite families, service institutions, and consultative practices that had helped reconstruct authority during the crisis itself.

This chapter's central argument is that the early Romanov period should be understood as a process of reconstruction that gradually became transformation. In its first years, Mikhail's government relied heavily on broad consultation through the zemsky sobor, on cooperation with boyar elites and provincial servicemen, and on urgent, improvised measures to locate revenue and rebuild military forces. As reconstruction succeeded across the following decades, some of the emergency mechanisms that had been indispensable during the crisis became less central to ordinary government, while the tsar's administration acquired steadily greater fiscal reach, bureaucratic specialization, and military capacity. By the middle of the seventeenth century, under Mikhail's son Alexei, Muscovy possessed a monarchy substantially stronger in practical governing capacity than the state Boris Godunov had inherited in 1598 — though not yet the fully bureaucratic, standing-army empire that Peter I would build at the century's end, a story reserved for Part VI of this textbook.

Students should resist two tempting oversimplifications as this chapter unfolds. The first is to imagine that the Romanovs simply restored a preexisting Muscovite order, as though 1613 reset the clock to some stable prior condition; the devastation of the Troubles was too severe, and the adaptations required too significant, for restoration alone to describe what occurred. The second is to treat the strengthening of central authority across this period as the inevitable, linear unfolding of Russian absolutism, marching steadily and predictably toward Peter the Great and beyond; this chapter will show instead that stronger monarchy emerged through specific, contingent responses to specific problems — fiscal collapse, military vulnerability, administrative disruption, and the practical challenge of governing a vast territory with limited communications — rather than through any predetermined historical destiny.

This chapter also introduces a distinction that will matter throughout the remainder of this textbook's treatment of Muscovy and the Russian Empire: the difference between formal claims to authority and actual governing capacity. A tsar could be formally acknowledged as sovereign while lacking the fiscal resources, administrative reach, or military strength to make that sovereignty effective across the realm. The early Romanov period is best understood as the process by which formal authority, secured in 1613, was gradually filled in with the practical instruments of rule: money, soldiers, officials, and records.

By the chapter's end, students should be able to trace how Mikhail's government moved from crisis management toward stable administration, explain the continuing and changing role of the zemsky sobor, describe the reconstruction of fiscal and military institutions, assess the growth and limits of seventeenth-century Muscovite bureaucracy, and evaluate what "autocracy" meant in practice by the reign of Alexei Mikhailovich.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain the practical governing challenges Mikhail Romanov's government inherited after the 1613 election, beyond the question of dynastic legitimacy already resolved.
  2. Describe Filaret Romanov's political role after his return from captivity and evaluate his partnership with his son without reducing the reign to Filaret's sole rule.
  3. Analyze the changing function of the zemsky sobor during and after the reconstruction period, distinguishing its usefulness from any claim of sovereign, independent authority.
  4. Explain why fiscal reconstruction — surveys, assessments, and revenue collection — formed the practical foundation for every other institution examined in this chapter.
  5. Trace the incremental rebuilding and adaptation of Muscovite military institutions after the Time of Troubles.
  6. Evaluate the role of boyars and the Boyar Duma as participants in, rather than simple opponents of, early Romanov government.
  7. Assess the institutional significance of the succession from Mikhail to Alexei Mikhailovich.
  8. Describe the growth and specialization of the prikaz system while avoiding anachronistic comparison to a modern civil service.
  9. Explain the selective employment of foreign military specialists and technology as adaptive responses to specific strategic problems rather than wholesale Westernization.
  10. Evaluate what "autocracy" meant in practice in seventeenth-century Muscovy, distinguishing strengthened governing capacity from unlimited personal rule.
  11. Analyze the relationship between reconstruction and transformation, explaining why the early Romanov state was neither a simple restoration of the pre-Troubles order nor an inevitable march toward later absolutism.
  12. Connect the institutional developments examined in this chapter to Chapter 27's account of law and serfdom and Chapter 28's account of Siberian expansion.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. What did the Romanov government actually need to rebuild after 1613, beyond simply holding the throne?
  2. Why did the zemsky sobor prove especially useful during reconstruction, and why might its role change as central institutions strengthened?
  3. Why does fiscal reconstruction underlie nearly every other institutional development examined in this chapter?
  4. In what ways did early Romanov government depend on, rather than simply overcome, the same boyar elites who had shaped Muscovite politics before and during the Troubles?
  5. What does the relatively smooth succession from Mikhail to Alexei in 1645 reveal about how far reconstruction had progressed?
  6. How did the expansion of the prikaz system increase governing capacity without producing a modern, uniform bureaucracy?
  7. Why did Muscovy selectively adopt foreign military expertise and technology rather than either rejecting or wholly imitating European practice?
  8. Does the strengthening of central authority across this period best support a story of restoration, of inevitable absolutism, or of contingent, problem-driven state-building?

Historical Context

Chapter 25 traced the collapse of central Muscovite authority between 1598 and 1613: the elected reign of Boris Godunov, the Great Famine, two successive pretenders claiming to be Tsarevich Dmitry, the socially mixed Bolotnikov rebellion, the fragmentation of sovereignty during the Tushino period, formal Polish-Lithuanian and Swedish intervention, the occupation of the Moscow Kremlin, and the eventual reconstruction of authority through the First and Second National Militias, culminating in the 1613 election of Mikhail Romanov. That chapter emphasized that the 1613 settlement resolved the question of who would occupy the throne without resolving the practical work of governing a realm that years of famine, civil war, and foreign occupation had devastated.

Students should recall from Chapter 22's earlier treatment of Muscovite institutions that the pre-Troubles state had never rested on the person of the tsar alone. It had operated through a Boyar Duma of leading noble families, a developing system of prikazy (chancelleries or administrative offices) handling finance, military affairs, foreign relations, and other functions, provincial governors and local administrators, a service gentry holding conditional pomestie landholdings in exchange for military obligation, and the institutional weight of the Orthodox Church. Chapter 25 showed how thoroughly the Troubles had disrupted these institutions: chancellery records destroyed or scattered, tax rolls hopelessly out of date as population fled or died, military service obligations impossible to enforce amid civil war, and provincial administration interrupted for years at a time in regions touched by rebellion, pretender armies, or foreign occupation.

The scale of the damage deserves emphasis before this chapter's numbered sections begin. Regions along the western and southwestern frontier, including areas around Smolensk lost to the Commonwealth and territory near Novgorod affected by Swedish occupation, required not merely administrative reassertion but, in some cases, renegotiation of Muscovy's actual territorial boundaries. Agricultural production in many districts had fallen sharply as a result of population flight, military requisitioning, and the general disruption of the preceding fifteen years. The treasury that Mikhail's government inherited possessed neither reliable current information about who owed taxes nor, in many cases, the administrative personnel needed to collect them. It is against this background of practical collapse, not merely political uncertainty, that this chapter's account of reconstruction must be understood.

Finally, students should hold in mind a methodological point carried forward from Chapter 25: the sources documenting early Romanov government, including chancellery records, land surveys, and diplomatic correspondence, become progressively more abundant and more administratively detailed across this period, itself a form of evidence for the reconstruction this chapter traces. Where Chapter 25 relied heavily on retrospective chronicle accounts requiring careful source criticism, this chapter draws increasingly on the routine administrative record-keeping of a functioning, if still developing, state.

Section 26.1

Mikhail Romanov

The 1613 election, examined in Chapter 25, gave Mikhail Romanov formal recognition as tsar. It did not give his government money, soldiers, reliable administrators, or secure borders. The new tsar was sixteen years old and inexperienced in government; his court and chancelleries had to be staffed substantially from among the same boyar, gentry, and clerical elites who had navigated the shifting loyalties of the preceding decade, some of whom carried compromised records of service to rival claimants or occupying powers during the Troubles. The practical task facing Mikhail's government in its first years was not to invent new institutions from nothing, but to reactivate, restaff, and, where necessary, adapt institutions whose personnel, records, and revenue base had been severely disrupted.

Securing peace on Muscovy's contested frontiers was an early and urgent priority, since continued warfare would have made fiscal and administrative reconstruction far more difficult. The 1617 Treaty of Stolbovo with Sweden ended northern military pressure, though at the cost of Muscovite access to the Baltic coast, a loss that would remain significant until Peter I's later campaigns examined in Part VI. The 1618 Truce of Deulino with the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth ended active warfare on the western frontier, though it left Smolensk and other territory under Commonwealth control, an unresolved loss that would motivate the Smolensk War examined in Section 26.4. These settlements should be read primarily as evidence of reconstruction rather than as a narrative of foreign policy for its own sake: each treaty removed an active military threat that would otherwise have consumed resources the new government urgently needed for internal recovery.

A major turning point in Mikhail's reign came in 1619, when his father, Filaret Romanov, returned from years of Polish-Lithuanian captivity — a captivity dating from his earlier role, examined in Chapter 25, in the negotiations surrounding Prince Władysław's candidacy for the Muscovite throne. Upon his return, Filaret was installed as Patriarch of Moscow and became, alongside his son, a central figure in governing the realm until his death in 1633. Contemporary and later accounts sometimes describe this arrangement as one in which Filaret effectively ruled while Mikhail reigned in name only; students should treat this characterization with some caution, since it risks erasing Mikhail's own participation in government and reducing a genuine political partnership to a simple transfer of authority. What can be said with confidence is that Filaret's return brought to the government a figure of substantial political experience, ecclesiastical authority, and personal connection to the ruling family, and that the years of his patriarchate saw sustained, intensifying efforts at fiscal, military, and administrative reconstruction, examined in the sections that follow.

By the early 1630s, roughly two decades after the 1613 election, Muscovy possessed a government capable of contemplating renewed offensive warfare against the Commonwealth in an attempt to recover Smolensk — a capacity that would have been unimaginable amid the collapse Chapter 25 described. That the attempt, examined in Section 26.4, ultimately failed should not obscure the more fundamental point: the very fact that Muscovy could raise, supply, and deploy a substantial army by 1632 demonstrates how far reconstruction had already progressed in less than twenty years.

Section 26.2

The Zemsky Sobor

The zemsky sobor did not originate in 1613; assemblies of this general kind had been convened at earlier points in Muscovite history, including, as Chapter 25 discussed, the assemblies that elected both Boris Godunov and Mikhail Romanov. What distinguishes the early Romanov period is the unusually frequent and sustained use of the assembly during the 1610s and 1620s, a pattern directly connected to the practical demands of reconstruction examined throughout this chapter.

Assemblies convened during this period served several overlapping purposes. They allowed the young government to broaden consultation beyond the narrow court circle, drawing in representatives of the service gentry, townspeople, and at times Cossacks, whose cooperation remained essential after the fractured loyalties of the Troubles. They provided a mechanism for communicating royal policy to a wide cross-section of the realm's political nation and, reciprocally, for the government to gather information about local conditions, taxable resources, and popular sentiment that the disrupted administrative apparatus could not easily supply through ordinary channels. Most concretely, assemblies were repeatedly used to secure support for extraordinary taxation and military mobilization, needs that recurred constantly during the reconstruction period as the government sought funds and manpower for continuing warfare and for the rebuilding examined in Sections 26.3 and 26.4. Finally, and perhaps most importantly during the years immediately following 1613, broad assembly participation reinforced the legitimacy of the new dynasty's decisions after more than a decade in which rival claimants, occupying garrisons, and competing courts had repeatedly demonstrated how contested legitimate authority could become.

Students should approach a natural question carefully: did this frequent consultation mean Muscovy was developing toward a constitutionally limited monarchy, in which the zemsky sobor possessed independent sovereign authority comparable to a European parliament? The evidence does not support this conclusion, though the more precise formulation matters. Assemblies normally depended on royal initiative for their convocation and agenda and possessed no standing legal authority to convene themselves or bind the tsar's decisions; at the same time, their composition and procedures varied considerably from one gathering to another, and historians continue to debate the exact membership and character of several individual assemblies. Their influence rested on practical usefulness to the government during a period of acute need for consultation, information, and legitimation, not on any constitutional doctrine of shared or divided sovereignty. This distinction matters directly for Section 26.9's discussion of autocracy: a government could rely heavily and repeatedly on consultative assemblies while remaining, in formal and practical terms, an autocracy in which ultimate authority rested with the tsar.

The assembly's history across the middle decades of the seventeenth century is better described as continued, if less constant, usefulness for particular major questions than as a steady, linear decline beginning in the 1620s. Assemblies continued to address extraordinary finance and war through the 1630s, deliberated over the Don Cossack seizure of the Ottoman fortress of Azov in 1642, participated in recognizing Alexei Mikhailovich's accession in 1645 (examined further in Section 26.6), contributed to the deliberations behind the 1649 Sobornoe Ulozhenie, addressed unrest at Pskov in 1650, and took up Polish-Lithuanian and Cossack affairs in 1651 before the major 1653 assembly that considered the incorporation of the Zaporozhian Cossack Hetmanate, examined further in Chapter 30. Assemblies remained important for particular major questions into the early 1650s, even as the expanding chancelleries, fiscal apparatus, and stabilized dynasty reduced the government's dependence on broad assemblies for routine governance. This change is best explained through institutional development rather than through any dramatic act of royal suppression; the zemsky sobor was not abolished so much as gradually rendered less indispensable for ordinary administration as other instruments of governance grew stronger, a pattern examined further in Section 26.9.

Historical Vocabulary
Prikaz

Prikaz (plural prikazy) refers to a Muscovite administrative chancellery or office responsible for a defined governmental function — among many others, the Ambassadorial Prikaz handling foreign relations, the Military Service Prikaz overseeing service-gentry obligations, and various regional and fiscal prikazy. The system had existed before the Time of Troubles, examined in Chapter 22, but expanded considerably in number, specialization, and administrative reach during the reconstruction period examined in this chapter, particularly in Section 26.7.

Students should resist projecting a modern civil service's uniformity, career structure, or impersonal rule-bound operation onto the prikaz system. Seventeenth-century Muscovite administration could be genuinely sophisticated in its record-keeping and its capacity to extract revenue and mobilize resources, while remaining, at the same time, overlapping in jurisdiction, heavily dependent on personal networks and status relationships, and uneven in its reach across different regions of the realm.

Section 26.3

Reconstructing the Fiscal System

No other task examined in this chapter mattered more directly to the government's practical survival than the reconstruction of reliable revenue. Chapter 24 had already described the fiscal strain accumulating in Muscovy before the Troubles, and Chapter 25 had shown how completely the crisis years disrupted whatever fiscal capacity had existed: tax rolls fell out of date as population fled famine, warfare, and occupation; taxable communities were destroyed, depopulated, or transferred between rival authorities; and the ordinary machinery of assessment and collection ceased to function reliably across large parts of the realm for years at a time. Without dependable revenue, the government could not pay soldiers, maintain chancellery staff, conduct diplomacy, or fund the fortification and frontier defense examined in Section 26.4. Fiscal reconstruction was therefore not a narrow financial matter but the practical foundation on which every other institution examined in this chapter depended.

The government's response combined several overlapping efforts. Renewed cadastral surveys attempted to reestablish, region by region, who held land, who owed service or taxation, and what a given community's productive capacity actually was after years of disruption — work considerably complicated by the destruction of earlier records, the physical scattering of population, and the practical difficulty of verifying claims in a period when many peasants and townspeople had fled their original places of residence and settled, legally or illegally, elsewhere. Officials worked to locate and, where possible, reattach displaced taxable populations to their obligations, an effort directly connected to the tightening peasant-mobility restrictions examined in Chapter 24 and further developed in Chapter 27's account of the 1649 law code. The government also relied on extraordinary levies and special assessments, frequently negotiated or at least announced through the zemsky sobor examined in Section 26.2, to meet urgent military and administrative needs beyond what ordinary revenue could supply.

This fiscal reconstruction proceeded gradually and unevenly rather than through any single decisive reform. Some regions, particularly those closer to Moscow and less severely affected by the fighting, recovered administrative normalcy more quickly than frontier and borderland districts, where continued military tension, population flight, and administrative disruption persisted for years after 1613. By the 1630s, however, the government's demonstrated capacity to fund a substantial military campaign for the Smolensk War, examined in the following section, provides concrete evidence that fiscal reconstruction had progressed far enough to support renewed state ambition, even though the campaign itself would expose continuing weaknesses in both fiscal and military capacity.

Section 26.4

Restoring the Military

Muscovy's military institutions had suffered as severely as its fiscal system during the Time of Troubles, examined in Chapter 25: service cavalry drawn from the pomestie-holding gentry had fought on multiple, shifting sides; streltsy garrison troops had been drawn into factional conflict; frontier fortifications had gone without adequate maintenance or garrison for years; and continuous warfare, rebellion, and foreign occupation had disrupted the ordinary mechanisms of recruitment, supply, and command. Rebuilding military capacity therefore required more than simply recalling existing institutions to duty; it required restoring the fiscal and administrative foundations, examined in Sections 26.3 and 26.7, on which any sustained military effort depended.

The early Romanov government's approach combined restoration of inherited institutions with incremental adaptation rather than wholesale reinvention. Service cavalry and streltsy garrisons remained the core of Muscovite military organization through the middle decades of the seventeenth century, and frontier fortification lines, including the continued development of defensive systems against Crimean Tatar raiding examined in Chapter 24, received renewed attention and resources as fiscal capacity permitted. At the same time, the government began, from the 1630s onward, to organize new units — the polki novogo stroya, or "new formation" regiments — trained, drilled, and in some cases commanded according to contemporary Western European military practice, incorporating foreign officers and instructors examined further in Section 26.8.

The Smolensk War of 1632–1634, an attempt to recover the territory lost to the Commonwealth under the 1618 Truce of Deulino, tested this reconstructed military capacity directly. The campaign initially achieved some success but ultimately failed, with the Muscovite army besieging Smolensk becoming itself encircled and forced into a costly capitulation. The war's failure should not be read simply as evidence that reconstruction had failed; rather, it demonstrated both how far military capacity had been rebuilt since 1613 — sufficient to mount a substantial offensive campaign with newly organized formations — and how much further reconstruction still needed to go before Muscovy could reliably compete with better-organized European rivals. The lessons drawn from this failure fed directly into the continued military adaptation examined in Section 26.8, illustrating this chapter's broader theme of incremental, problem-driven institutional development rather than sudden transformation.

Section 26.5

Boyars and Central Government

It is tempting, especially after Chapter 25's account of shifting boyar loyalties during the Troubles, to imagine early Romanov government as a story of the tsar gradually subduing or sidelining a rival boyar class. This chapter, building on Chapter 22's earlier treatment of Muscovite institutions, argues instead that boyars remained central and largely indispensable participants in the reconstructed state, serving simultaneously as potential rivals, trusted advisers, provincial administrators, and military commanders.

The Boyar Duma, the council of leading noble families, continued to function as a core institution of government throughout the early Romanov period, advising the tsar on major policy questions, participating in judicial matters, and supplying much of the senior personnel who staffed provincial governorships and military commands. Far from being displaced by the expanding prikaz system examined in Section 26.7, boyar families frequently headed the most important chancelleries themselves, combining council membership with direct administrative responsibility. The same elite families whose shifting loyalties during the Troubles Chapter 25 examined — moving, in some cases, between Moscow, Tushino, and eventually the National Militias — reappear in the 1620s, 1630s, and 1640s as governors, military commanders, and Duma members of the reconstructed Romanov state, their earlier compromises largely set aside in the interest of stabilizing the new dynasty's government.

This continuity should not be mistaken for the absence of tension. Individual boyar families continued to compete for influence, office, and royal favor, and the government had genuine reasons for caution regarding any family whose power or independent following might rival the throne's own authority. But the relationship between tsar and boyars during this period is better understood as a negotiated, mutually dependent partnership than as a contest between an ambitious monarchy and a resistant aristocracy. The tsar needed boyar families to supply administrators, commanders, and legitimating support; boyar families needed royal favor, office, and landholding to sustain their own status and wealth. Reconstruction succeeded, to the degree that it did, substantially because this partnership held together through the difficult years examined throughout this chapter.

Section 26.6

Tsar Alexei Mikhailovich

Mikhail Romanov died in 1645, and his son Alexei Mikhailovich succeeded him through an orderly hereditary succession rather than a renewed contest among rival claimants or the kind of fragmentation that had followed the death of Ivan IV's line half a century earlier. An Assembly of the Land still participated in the accession process, formally recognizing Alexei's succession; its involvement demonstrates that consultation and stabilized hereditary succession were not mutually exclusive; what had disappeared since 1613 was not assembly participation as such but the open-ended succession crisis that had made a full elective assembly necessary in the first place. This section's significance lies less in Alexei's personal biography, examined more fully in Chapter 27's treatment of law and society and in Chapter 29's treatment of the Church schism associated with his reign, than in what his accession reveals institutionally: by 1645, the Romanov dynasty had achieved a level of stability, and Muscovite government a level of institutional continuity, sufficient to absorb a change of ruler through recognized hereditary succession rather than renewed nationwide crisis.

This achievement should not be understated. Chapter 25 traced, in painful detail, how the extinction of the Rurikid line had contributed directly to the collapse of central authority after 1598. That the Romanov succession in 1645 produced no comparable crisis reflects genuine institutional accomplishment: a Boyar Duma and prikaz apparatus experienced in the routine business of government, a fiscal system capable of sustaining continuity of administration through a transition, and a dynasty whose legitimacy, established through the 1613 election and reinforced across three decades of increasingly normal governance, now rested on secure hereditary succession within the new line rather than on the kind of open, contested selection among rival claimants that had made 1613 an existential crisis.

Alexei's reign should be understood, for the purposes of this chapter, as the period in which institutions rebuilt and stabilized under Mikhail were expanded and used more intensively rather than fundamentally reinvented. The continued growth of the prikaz system examined in Section 26.7, the continued selective adoption of foreign military expertise examined in Section 26.8, and the promulgation of the major Sobornoe Ulozhenie law code in 1649 — examined briefly here as evidence of expanding administrative and legal capacity and treated in full in Chapter 27 — all occurred within Alexei's reign and built directly on foundations established during his father's reconstruction. Urban unrest, including the 1648 Moscow Salt Riot sparked by fiscal grievances, demonstrates that reconstruction had not eliminated social and economic strain; it shows instead that the state now possessed sufficient administrative and coercive capacity to respond to such unrest, codify law in response to some of its underlying grievances, and continue governing without the kind of total fragmentation examined in Chapter 25.

Section 26.7

Expansion of the Bureaucracy

The growth of the prikaz system across the early and middle seventeenth century provides some of this chapter's clearest evidence of increasing state capacity. New chancelleries were created or expanded to handle specific administrative needs — including offices dedicated to managing the newly organized military formations examined in Section 26.4, to overseeing frontier administration in regions such as Siberia, whose early expansion is examined in Chapter 28, and to managing the government's growing correspondence, recordkeeping, and revenue-collection functions examined in Section 26.3. Clerical personnel (d'iaki and pod'iachie) staffing these offices grew in number and, in many cases, in specialized administrative skill, producing an increasingly dense record of government activity that provides historians with far richer documentary evidence for this period than for the crisis years examined in Chapter 25.

This expansion represented genuine growth in administrative reach: the government could gather more information, issue more instructions, and expect more consistent compliance across a wider territory by the 1640s and 1650s than it could in the immediate aftermath of 1613. At the same time, students should preserve the caution Chapter 22 established regarding Muscovite administration more generally. The prikaz system remained, throughout this period, considerably less uniform, rule-bound, and impersonally organized than the term "bureaucracy" might suggest to a reader familiar with later administrative states. Jurisdictions among different prikazy frequently overlapped, creating redundancy and occasional confusion; personal relationships, patronage networks, and the status distinctions inherent in the Muscovite chin (rank or office) system continued to shape how offices operated in practice; and administrative capacity remained considerably stronger near Moscow than in distant frontier regions, where local governors (voevody) exercised considerable practical discretion regardless of formal chancellery instructions from the center.

The most useful way to characterize this development, consistent with this chapter's central argument, is as greater administrative reach without modern bureaucratic uniformity. The seventeenth-century Muscovite state could accomplish considerably more than its pre-Troubles predecessor — collecting more reliable revenue, mobilizing larger and better-organized military forces, and enforcing law with greater consistency, as Chapter 27's account of the 1649 code will show — while still operating through personal networks, overlapping jurisdictions, and status-conscious relationships that a later, more thoroughly bureaucratized imperial state, examined in Part VI's treatment of Peter the Great's reforms, would attempt to systematize further.

Section 26.8

Foreign Specialists and Military Reform

Muscovy's selective employment of foreign military officers, technical specialists, and organizational practices during this period should be understood as a pragmatic response to specific strategic problems rather than as evidence of any general cultural turn toward Western Europe. The failures exposed by the Smolensk War, examined in Section 26.4, demonstrated concretely that Muscovite forces organized along traditional lines struggled against opponents employing more systematically drilled infantry, coordinated artillery, and disciplined tactical formations characteristic of contemporary European warfare, itself transformed by the broader military developments of the era's prolonged continental conflicts.

In response, the Muscovite government recruited foreign officers, primarily from Western and Central Europe, to help organize, train, and at times command the polki novogo stroya examined in Section 26.4, and employed foreign technical specialists in areas including artillery production, fortification design, and, increasingly across the following decades, manufacturing enterprises connected to military supply. This borrowing was selective and instrumental: the government sought specific expertise to address specific deficiencies exposed by military experience, rather than pursuing any broader program of cultural transformation. Foreign specialists typically served under Muscovite authority, within institutions the government controlled, rather than reshaping Muscovite society or governance more broadly.

Students should resist reading this seventeenth-century military adaptation solely as a preliminary rehearsal for Peter I's much more extensive reforms, examined in Part VI. The employment of foreign specialists and the organization of new-formation regiments addressed genuine seventeenth-century military problems in their own right and achieved meaningful, if incomplete, results within their own historical context, including improved performance in later conflicts beyond this chapter's scope. At the same time, this chapter's account should make clear why later Petrine reform did not emerge from nothing: the practice of selectively recruiting foreign military expertise, adapting specific European organizational and technical practices, and integrating them within existing Muscovite institutions had a substantial precedent in the reconstruction and adaptation examined throughout this chapter, even though Peter's own reforms would go considerably further and reach much more broadly into Russian government and society.

Section 26.9

The Strengthening of Autocracy

This chapter's final analytical section asks what "autocracy" actually meant in seventeenth-century Muscovy, a question that requires more careful handling than simply equating the term with unlimited personal despotism. The evidence assembled across this chapter's preceding sections points toward a specific, historically grounded answer: Muscovite monarchy strengthened across the early and middle seventeenth century because the state's practical governing capacity grew — fiscal collection became more reliable, examined in Section 26.3; military institutions became more capable, examined in Sections 26.4 and 26.8; administrative reach expanded, examined in Section 26.7; and dynastic succession stabilized, examined in Section 26.6 — not because the tsar somehow escaped dependence on the elites, institutions, and consultative practices examined throughout this chapter.

Indeed, the tsar continued to govern through the Boyar Duma examined in Section 26.5, through an expanding but still personally networked prikaz apparatus examined in Section 26.7, through the Orthodox Church and its patriarchal leadership examined in Section 26.1, and through service relationships with provincial gentry and military commanders whose cooperation reconstruction had never ceased to require. Autocracy, in this seventeenth-century Muscovite sense, described a system in which ultimate formal authority rested unambiguously with the tsar, without rival, independent, legally established institutions capable of checking that authority — not a system in which the tsar ruled alone, without institutions, elites, or consultation.

The changing role of the zemsky sobor, examined in Section 26.2, illustrates this dynamic precisely, though the change was gradual and uneven rather than a clean, steady decline. Assemblies had proven extraordinarily useful during the acute reconstruction of the 1610s and 1620s, when the government urgently needed broad consultation, information, and legitimation, and they continued to address major questions well into the early 1650s, including Alexei's own accession in 1645, examined in Section 26.6, and the great decision of 1653 concerning the Zaporozhian Cossacks taken up further in Chapter 30. What changed across these decades was not the disappearance of the assembly through any dramatic act of suppression, but a gradual reduction in the government's dependence on this particular form of broad, emergency-driven consultation for ordinary governance, as other instruments — a more reliable fiscal system, a more extensive bureaucracy, a more capable military, a more securely established dynasty — expanded to handle much of the work that assemblies alone had once been needed to accomplish. This is a more historically accurate explanation than the older narrative claiming that Muscovite "autocracy defeated representative government," a formulation that projects a constitutional conflict onto a period whose sources do not support it.

By the middle of the seventeenth century, then, Muscovy possessed a monarchy substantially stronger in practical governing capacity than the state Boris Godunov had inherited in 1598 — but this strengthened autocracy rested on successful reconstruction, elite cooperation, and gradually expanding institutional capacity, not on the tsar's escape from dependence on the governing structures this chapter and Chapter 22 have together described. The fully bureaucratized, standing-army, more thoroughly centralized state that Peter I would construct at the century's end, examined in Part VI, built upon these seventeenth-century foundations; it did not simply repeat them.

Historical Vocabulary
Autocracy (Samoderzhavie)

Samoderzhavie, conventionally translated "autocracy," designates the principle that ultimate formal sovereign authority in Muscovy rested with the tsar alone, without any rival, independent, legally established institution — no parliament, estate assembly with binding sovereign power, or constitutional body — capable of checking that authority as a matter of established law.

Students should take particular care not to read samoderzhavie as meaning that the tsar governed alone, without institutions, elites, or consultation. As this chapter has shown throughout, the tsar governed through the Boyar Duma, an expanding prikaz apparatus, the Orthodox Church and its Patriarchate, service relationships with provincial gentry, and, especially during reconstruction, the zemsky sobor. Autocracy in this Muscovite sense describes where ultimate formal authority was understood to reside, not the practical absence of elite partnership, negotiation, or consultative government — a distinction central to Section 26.9's argument and to this textbook's caution, introduced in Chapter 22, against treating any Muscovite or later Russian government as possessing unlimited practical control merely because its formal ideology claimed centralized authority.

Primary Source Window

The Cadastral Book of the City of Kostroma (Pistsovaia Kniga g. Kostromy), 1627/28–1629/30

Creator: Scribes and surveying officials (pistsy) dispatched from Moscow, working under prikaz authority · Date: compiled 1627/28–1629/30 (surviving copies held in regional archives and in the Russian State Archive of Ancient Acts, RGADA; a scholarly edition was published in 2004) · Type: Administrative cadastral survey and tax-assessment book (pistsovaia kniga)

Context: This survey of the city of Kostroma and its surrounding district was compiled roughly a decade and a half after the 1613 election, as part of the renewed cadastral surveying examined in Section 26.3. Teams of scribes recorded, street by street and household by household, who occupied each urban holding, which properties stood empty or ruined since the Troubles, and what tax and service obligations attached to each. Kostroma had been directly affected by the fighting and disruption of the Troubles, making its post-crisis survey a useful, concrete window onto the fiscal reconstruction this chapter describes.

Paraphrase of a representative entry (not a verbatim translated quotation):

The survey records a given urban plot as belonging to a named townsman, describes the dwelling and any workshop or trading structure standing on it, and notes whether the household was currently inhabited and paying its assessed obligations or whether the plot stood vacant, burned, or "waste" (pusto) since the Troubles, with the surrounding entries showing a mix of restored, still-vacant, and newly resettled holdings within the same section of the city.

Analysis: This survey is valuable precisely because it is not a narrative or commemorative account but a working administrative document, produced for the practical purpose of establishing what revenue and service obligation the state could actually expect to collect from a specific, named place. The mixture of restored, vacant, and newly resettled holdings recorded within the same district provides direct, concrete evidence for the uneven pace of recovery this chapter has described, while the survey's household-by-household thoroughness illustrates the growing administrative reach examined in Section 26.7. Its limitation is that it reflects official assessment categories and official priorities — what the state needed to know in order to tax and mobilize a specific city — rather than a full picture of how Kostroma's townspeople themselves experienced reconstruction.

Historical Debate

Was Seventeenth-Century Muscovy Becoming Absolutist?

The question: Does the strengthening of central authority traced in this chapter represent the early development of a European-style absolutist state, a distinctively Muscovite form of autocracy, or a more limited, still heavily negotiated form of monarchy that later imperial developments would transform further?

The absolutism-in-formation interpretation: Some historians emphasize the expanding prikaz apparatus, growing fiscal extraction, the declining frequency of the zemsky sobor after the 1650s, and the increasingly centralized legal codification of the 1649 Sobornoe Ulozhenie as evidence that Muscovy was moving, gradually but unmistakably, toward the kind of centralized, bureaucratically administered monarchy associated with European absolutism, a trajectory that would culminate in Peter I's reforms.
The negotiated-monarchy interpretation: Other historians caution against this framing, arguing that the tsar's continued, structural dependence on the Boyar Duma, on personally networked chancellery administration, on the Church, and on service elites throughout this period shows a monarchy that remained deeply embedded in negotiated relationships with elites rather than one escaping such dependence, and that the label "absolutism," developed to describe particular European monarchies, risks obscuring rather than clarifying the distinctively Muscovite character of seventeenth-century autocracy.

Evidence: Historians weigh the administrative record of prikaz expansion and fiscal collection, the frequency and content of zemsky sobor convocations across the century, the actual practice of boyar participation in government, and comparative study of contemporary European state-building processes.

Points of agreement: Most historians agree that Muscovite governing capacity grew substantially across this period and that the government's dependence on emergency broad-based consultation declined somewhat as other institutions matured.

Points of disagreement: Historians differ over how useful the European term "absolutism" is for describing this specifically Muscovite process, and over how much weight to give continued elite negotiation versus growing central capacity in explaining the period's political character.

Student task: Using evidence from Sections 26.2, 26.5, 26.7, and 26.9, construct an argument for which interpretation the evidence in this chapter better supports, while acknowledging the strongest point of the interpretation you find less persuasive.

Multiple Perspectives

Rebuilding the Realm

A prikaz clerk conducting a provincial land survey: For an official sent from Moscow to reassess a district's population and tax liability, reconstruction meant painstaking, often frustrating work: verifying claims about abandoned land, resolving disputes over who currently held a given holding, and producing the detailed written records that Section 26.3 and this chapter's Primary Source Window identify as the concrete foundation of fiscal recovery.
A provincial service gentryman returning to a damaged pomestie estate: For a member of the service gentry whose estate had suffered during the Troubles, reconstruction meant seeking confirmation of landholding, locating fled peasant labor, and balancing continued military service obligations against the practical difficulty of restoring an estate's productive capacity — concerns directly connected to the mobility restrictions examined further in Chapter 27.
A boyar serving simultaneously in the Duma and as a provincial governor: For a member of the elite families examined in Section 26.5, reconstruction offered opportunities to combine court influence with practical administrative authority, while also requiring careful navigation of a court still sensitive to any family's prior compromised loyalties during the Troubles.
A foreign officer recruited into a "new formation" regiment: For a foreign military specialist examined in Section 26.8, service in Muscovy offered employment and opportunity, but required operating within an unfamiliar administrative and social system, under Muscovite command structures, for purposes defined entirely by the host government's specific strategic needs.

These perspectives illustrate that reconstruction was not a single, centrally directed program experienced uniformly, but a set of overlapping practical processes experienced differently depending on one's place in Muscovite society.

Cause and Effect

From Fragmentation to Fiscal Capacity

Long-Term Conditions

The pre-Troubles institutional foundation examined in Chapter 22 — Boyar Duma, prikaz system, pomestie service landholding, and Church institutions — which reconstruction reactivated and adapted rather than reinvented.

Intermediate Developments

The 1613 election's restoration of formal legitimacy, examined in Chapter 25; peace settlements with Sweden and the Commonwealth removing active military threats; and Filaret's return in 1619 strengthening governing leadership.

Short-Term Triggers

Urgent fiscal necessity — the practical need for revenue to pay soldiers, staff chancelleries, and fund diplomacy — driving renewed cadastral surveys, tax assessment, and extraordinary levies negotiated through the zemsky sobor.

Effects

A government capable, by the 1630s, of mounting a substantial military campaign; continued prikaz expansion through the 1640s and 1650s; stabilized dynastic succession in 1645; and, by mid-century, a codified legal order in the 1649 Sobornoe Ulozhenie.

Historical complexity: Fiscal reconstruction alone could not have succeeded without simultaneous peace-making, elite cooperation, and administrative expansion; each reinforced the others across the decades this chapter examines.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Mikhail to Alexei

What Stayed the Same

  • The Boyar Duma and prikaz system remained the core institutional framework of government across both reigns.
  • Pomestie service landholding continued to bind the gentry to military obligation throughout the period.
  • The Orthodox Church, through the Patriarchate, remained closely intertwined with political authority.

What Changed

  • Fiscal collection moved from emergency, ad hoc levies toward more systematic, survey-based assessment.
  • Military organization expanded to include Western-style "new formation" regiments alongside traditional service cavalry and streltsy.
  • The zemsky sobor's centrality to government diminished as other institutions matured, and dynastic succession in 1645 occurred without crisis for the first time in decades.

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both continuity and change equips students to see the early Romanov period as neither pure restoration nor sudden transformation, but as gradual institutional maturation built on inherited foundations.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Provincial Chancellery Clerk

For the growing corps of d'iaki and pod'iachie staffing prikaz offices in Moscow and the provinces, reconstruction meant steady, often tedious work: copying and maintaining records, drafting correspondence between central chancelleries and provincial governors, processing petitions from peasants, townspeople, and servicemen seeking resolution of disputes arising from the Troubles, and compiling the survey and assessment records examined in this chapter's Primary Source Window. This clerical labor, unglamorous as it may appear beside the military and diplomatic narratives that dominate many historical accounts, was the practical substance of reconstruction: without it, the fiscal, military, and legal capacities examined throughout this chapter could not have developed.

Clerical office also offered a distinctive, if limited, avenue of social advancement in Muscovite society, since administrative skill and experience, rather than birth alone, increasingly mattered for staffing an expanding chancellery system. At the same time, clerks operated within a status-conscious hierarchy structured by rank and patronage, and their working lives remained shaped by the personal networks and overlapping jurisdictions this chapter's account of the prikaz system has described, rather than by any impersonal, modern bureaucratic career structure.

Regional Context

The Devastated Borderlands

Reconstruction did not proceed evenly across Muscovite territory. Regions near Moscow, less directly touched by prolonged occupation or repeated campaigning, generally recovered administrative and economic normalcy more quickly than frontier and borderland districts. The western frontier near Smolensk remained contested territory, alternately under Commonwealth and Muscovite control, well into the 1630s and beyond. The northwestern region near Novgorod, affected by Swedish occupation examined in Chapter 25, required particular attention to reestablish secure administration after the 1617 Treaty of Stolbovo. The southern frontier, examined in Chapter 24 as a zone of Crimean Tatar raiding and Cossack autonomy, continued to demand ongoing investment in fortification and defense even as fiscal reconstruction elsewhere allowed the government to consider renewed offensive action, as in the 1632–1634 Smolensk War.

This regional unevenness matters for understanding the pace and character of reconstruction: a government that could reassert reliable administration near Moscow by the early 1620s might still be working to stabilize frontier districts a decade or more later, a pattern that helps explain why full fiscal and military recovery took decades rather than years, and why Chapter 28's account of simultaneous Siberian expansion during this same period should be understood as occurring alongside, rather than only after, the internal reconstruction this chapter examines.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how the early Romanov government converted the formal legitimacy secured by Mikhail Romanov's 1613 election into the practical capacity to govern a realm devastated by the Time of Troubles. It argued throughout that this reconstruction was neither a simple restoration of a preexisting Muscovite order nor an inevitable, linear march toward later absolutism, but a contingent, problem-driven process of institutional adaptation.

The chapter began with Mikhail Romanov's reign, examining the peace settlements — the 1617 Treaty of Stolbovo and 1618 Truce of Deulino — that removed active foreign military pressure, and the political partnership between Mikhail and his father Filaret, who became Patriarch of Moscow in 1619. It then examined the zemsky sobor's particular usefulness during acute reconstruction, distinguishing this practical, situational importance from any claim that the assembly possessed sovereign authority independent of the tsar.

The chapter treated fiscal reconstruction as the practical foundation underlying every other institutional development, tracing renewed cadastral surveys, tax reassessment, and efforts to relocate displaced taxable populations. It examined the incremental rebuilding of military institutions, including the organization of "new formation" regiments from the 1630s, and the Smolensk War of 1632–1634 as both a demonstration of reconstructed capacity and a revelation of continuing weakness.

The chapter argued that boyars remained central, largely indispensable participants in the reconstructed state rather than rivals gradually subdued by an ambitious monarchy, and it treated Alexei Mikhailovich's smooth 1645 accession as evidence of genuine institutional stabilization after decades in which succession crisis had repeatedly threatened Muscovite political order. It examined the expansion and specialization of the prikaz system as evidence of growing administrative reach, while cautioning against anachronistic comparison to a modern, uniform bureaucracy, and it examined the selective employment of foreign military specialists as a pragmatic, problem-driven adaptation rather than a program of general Westernization.

Finally, the chapter offered an interpretive account of what "autocracy" meant in seventeenth-century Muscovy: strengthened governing capacity built on continued dependence on boyars, chancellery officials, and Church institutions, with the declining frequency of the zemsky sobor explained through institutional maturation rather than any dramatic act of royal suppression. This account was reinforced by the chapter's Historical Debate feature examining competing interpretations of whether this period represents an early Muscovite form of European absolutism or a distinctively negotiated form of monarchy.

Throughout, the chapter connected its account to Chapter 22's earlier treatment of Muscovite institutions, Chapter 24's account of pre-Troubles vulnerabilities, and Chapter 25's account of collapse, while previewing Chapter 27's detailed treatment of the 1649 Sobornoe Ulozhenie and Chapter 28's account of simultaneous Siberian expansion.

The chapter closed by emphasizing that the strengthened Romanov monarchy of the mid-seventeenth century, while substantially more capable than the state Boris Godunov had inherited in 1598, remained a negotiated, elite-dependent form of government — the foundation upon which, but not the equivalent of, the more thoroughly centralized and bureaucratized state Peter I would construct at the century's end.

These lessons—distinguishing formal legitimacy from practical governing capacity, recognizing incremental rather than sudden institutional change, and understanding autocracy as strengthened capacity rather than isolated personal rule—prepare students directly for Chapter 27's account of law, serfdom, and social order, and for this textbook's later treatment of Peter the Great's more sweeping transformation of the Russian state in Part VI.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has carried students from the fragile, newly elected government of 1613 through more than three decades of practical, often unglamorous institutional reconstruction, culminating in a monarchy capable, by mid-century, of absorbing a dynastic succession without crisis and codifying a comprehensive law code. The early Romanovs did not simply restore the Muscovite state that had existed before Ivan IV's later reign and the Time of Troubles; they rebuilt it through fiscal surveys, military adaptation, expanding chancelleries, and sustained cooperation with the same boyar and gentry elites whose shifting loyalties had characterized the crisis years.

The chapter's central methodological lesson — that formal legitimacy and practical governing capacity are distinct achievements, and that strengthened central authority typically emerges through incremental, problem-driven institutional development rather than sudden transformation or inevitable historical destiny — will recur throughout this textbook's later chapters, particularly in Part VI's treatment of Peter the Great's far more sweeping reforms, which this chapter's account of seventeenth-century military and administrative adaptation directly anticipates without simply rehearsing.

As the textbook proceeds to Chapter 27's detailed examination of the 1649 Sobornoe Ulozhenie and the consolidation of serfdom, students should carry forward this chapter's recognition that the same reconstructed state capacity that rebuilt Muscovy's finances, military, and administration after 1613 also equipped the government to codify and enforce an increasingly rigid social and legal hierarchy — a development this chapter has previewed but deliberately left for Chapter 27 to examine in full.
