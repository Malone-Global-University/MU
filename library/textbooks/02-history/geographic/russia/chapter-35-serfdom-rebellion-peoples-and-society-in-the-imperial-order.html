Part VI — Peter the Great and the Eighteenth-Century Empire

Serfdom, Rebellion, Peoples, and Society in the Imperial Order

A History of Russia: From the Eurasian World to the Russian Federation

Chapter Opening

The following is a composite, illustrative reconstruction rather than an account of specific documented individuals. A serf household in central Russia might divide its autumn labor among three obligations at once: days owed to the lord's own fields, a cash payment owed at the manor house, and a portion of the harvest set aside against the winter and against the tax collector. Several hundred miles to the southeast, along the Yaik River, a Cossack community argued in its own assembly over a state order that threatened privileges its members considered their birthright. Such households and communities had no knowledge of one another, yet within a few years the strains affecting communities like these would become visible in the largest rebellion the Russian Empire had ever experienced.

Chapter 34 examined Catherine the Great's empire chiefly from above: her seizure of power in 1762, her engagement with Enlightenment ideas, her Legislative Commission, her reorganization of provincial government, her expansion of noble privilege through the Charter of 1785, and her wars and annexations that pushed Russian authority into new lands along the Black Sea and into partitioned Poland. That chapter necessarily told a story centered on the empress, her ministers, her legislation, and her diplomacy. This chapter turns the same period around and asks a different set of questions. What did Catherine's empire look like, and feel like, to the people who lived inside it rather than at the summit of it? What obligations bound a serf to a landlord, and how had those obligations tightened over the eighteenth century? What did it mean to belong to a legally defined estate—noble, clerical, merchant, townsman, peasant, Cossack—in a state that had not yet developed anything resembling common citizenship? How did women's lives differ according to wealth, legal status, and geography? How did the empire actually govern the enormous variety of peoples, languages, and religions that fell within its expanding borders? And why, in 1773, did a Cossack claiming to be the murdered emperor Peter III find himself at the head of the largest and most destructive rebellion in Russian history before the twentieth century?

These questions matter because the imperial order Catherine presided over was not a single, uniform society governed by a single set of rules. It was instead a layered structure of legally differentiated statuses, each carrying its own rights, obligations, courts, and relationship to the state. A wealthy magnate governing thousands of serfs on several estates, a modest provincial noble scraping by on a small property, a state peasant paying dues directly to the treasury, a privately owned serf subject to a landlord's nearly unchecked authority, a Yaik Cossack defending customary fishing and military privileges, a Bashkir community defending ancestral land against encroaching settlement, an Old Believer community maintaining a faith the state officially persecuted, and a Jewish community newly absorbed through the partition of Poland-Lithuania all lived under the same monarch and within the same set of shifting borders, yet their daily obligations, legal protections, and prospects differed enormously. Understanding Catherine's Russia requires holding all of these experiences in view at once, rather than allowing the empress's court, her correspondence with European philosophers, or her territorial acquisitions to stand in for the whole of imperial society.

This chapter also asks students to think carefully about the word "serfdom" itself. The institution examined here was not a single timeless condition inherited unchanged from the medieval past; it was a set of legal categories and social practices that had tightened considerably across the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, culminating in a system that, by the reign of Catherine II, gave many landlords a degree of practical authority over their serfs that approached, without being formally identical to, chattel slavery in certain respects, while remaining legally and institutionally distinct from it in others. The chapter distinguishes private serfs bound to noble landlords from state peasants who owed dues directly to the treasury, and distinguishes both from household serfs (dvorovye liudi) who worked inside a lord's residence rather than on the land. These distinctions are not pedantic. They shaped how heavily the eighteenth-century state and its social order pressed upon different rural populations, and they shaped who would eventually rally, and who would not, behind Pugachev's rebellion.

The chapter's central rebellion, led by the Cossack Emelian Pugachev between 1773 and 1775, deserves careful treatment precisely because it is often remembered as a simple uprising of "the oppressed peasantry" against "the oppressive state." The reality was considerably more complicated and, for that reason, more historically revealing. Pugachev's movement drew together Yaik Cossacks defending eroding frontier privileges, industrial workers at Ural mining and metallurgical establishments facing harsh labor conditions, Bashkir communities resisting settlement and administrative pressure on their lands, Old Believers maintaining a persecuted religious tradition, and serfs and state peasants angry at specific local grievances, alongside others. These groups did not necessarily want the same outcome, and their alliance under Pugachev's banner was as much a product of shared frustration with specific policies and officials as of any single shared ideology. Examining why such different populations could temporarily unite, and why that unity eventually broke down, illuminates the fault lines running through Catherine's entire social order.

At the same time, this chapter resists reducing the eighteenth-century countryside, frontier, and imperial periphery to a story of oppression met by rebellion. Most people, most of the time, responded to the pressures of estate society and imperial administration not through armed revolt but through an enormous range of other strategies: petitioning officials and even the empress herself, litigating disputes in available courts, negotiating informally with landlords and local administrators, migrating to escape especially burdensome conditions, maintaining religious and communal institutions quietly outside state sanction, adapting household economies to shifting demands, and, in many cases, participating actively and even successfully within the imperial system as soldiers, officials, merchants, or skilled artisans. Rebellion was a real and important possibility within this social order, but it was one option among many, and understanding the full spectrum of responses—accommodation, negotiation, evasion, and resistance together—offers a more accurate picture of how an early modern empire actually functioned than attention to open revolt alone.

Finally, this chapter positions Catherine's Russia within a broader historical and historiographical conversation about how early modern empires governed diverse populations across vast distances with limited direct administrative reach. The Russian Empire of this period depended heavily on delegated authority: landlords policed and taxed their own serfs on the state's behalf; Cossack hosts maintained their own institutions in exchange for military service; religious and communal leaders mediated between local populations and distant officials; and legally differentiated estates each managed much of their own internal affairs according to custom and privilege. This pattern of governance through delegation and negotiated privilege, rather than through uniform, centrally administered law, will recur throughout this textbook's treatment of the Russian Empire and, in transformed form, the Soviet Union, and understanding it here prepares students for later chapters examining Russification, nationality policy, and the tensions between central authority and local autonomy across the empire's long history.

Historical Context

Chapter 27 examined the legal consolidation of Russian serfdom in the seventeenth century, centered on the Sobornoe Ulozhenie of 1649, which eliminated the fixed statute of limitations on the recovery of fugitive peasants and thereby bound the rural population to the land, and to their landlords, in a manner earlier Muscovite law had not fully achieved. Chapter 32 examined how Peter the Great's fiscal and administrative reforms, particularly the introduction of the poll tax and the associated census, or "revision," of the taxable population, further entrenched serfdom by making landlords directly responsible for their serfs' tax obligations, a responsibility that gave landlords new administrative power over the very peasants they had previously controlled chiefly through customary and manorial authority. Chapter 33 traced the political instability of the "Age of Palace Revolutions" following Peter's death, during which successive rulers, dependent on noble support to secure and hold the throne, steadily expanded noble privileges, including a 1762 decree freeing nobles from the compulsory state service Peter had imposed upon them, while leaving unchanged, and in practice strengthening, noble authority over serfs.

Chapter 34 then examined how Catherine II, who came to power through a palace coup in 1762, engaged with Enlightenment political ideas while simultaneously deepening this social settlement. Her Legislative Commission of 1767–1768 invited representatives from many social groups, including some state peasants, to submit grievances (nakazy) and participate in deliberation, but private serfs, who by this period constituted roughly half the peasant population, were excluded from direct representation, and the Commission itself was suspended without producing a new law code. Her provincial reform of 1775, prompted in part by the very rebellion this chapter examines, reorganized local administration to give the state, and especially the nobility serving in local institutions, greater capacity to govern the countryside. Her Charter to the Nobility of 1785 confirmed and extended noble exemption from compulsory service, from personal taxation, and from corporal punishment, while formally recognizing noble ownership of land and the serfs upon it as a secure, protected property right. These were the political and legal decisions examined "from above." This chapter now examines what those same decisions, and the broader social order surrounding them, meant "from below," for landlords navigating a complex hierarchy of wealth, for serfs and state peasants bearing an intensifying set of obligations, for women across different social positions, for religious and ethnic minorities incorporated through conquest and diplomacy, and for frontier communities such as the Yaik Cossacks whose customary privileges increasingly conflicted with the state's drive toward administrative uniformity.

Students should keep two points firmly in mind while reading this chapter. First, "the Russian Empire" in this period was not a homogeneous national society but a vast, religiously and ethnically plural polity stretching from the Baltic and Poland in the west to Siberia in the east and from the Arctic to the Black Sea and the Caucasus foothills in the south, a scope examined further in this chapter's Frontier Context feature and returned to at length in Chapters 39 and 45. Second, "serfdom" and "peasant" are not synonyms: alongside privately owned serfs bound to noble landlords, the empire contained state peasants who paid dues directly to the treasury and enjoyed somewhat greater personal mobility and legal standing, as well as other rural categories including monastery peasants (whose status changed substantially after Catherine's secularization of church lands in 1764) and various frontier and colonist populations. Holding these distinctions in mind is essential to understanding both the peasant economy examined in Section 35.4 and the uneven, multi-constituency character of Pugachev's Rebellion examined in Section 35.8.

Section 35.1

The Expansion of Serfdom

By Catherine II's reign, roughly half of the East Slavic peasantry was enserfed to private landlords, with the other half divided chiefly among state peasants and other non-privately-owned categories examined below. This balance had not always existed in its late eighteenth-century form, nor did it emerge suddenly. The legal foundation for binding peasants permanently to the land, examined in Chapter 27, rested on the Sobornoe Ulozhenie of 1649, which consolidated a longer process of enserfment already under way across the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries by eliminating the statute of limitations on the recovery of fugitive peasants, greatly strengthening what became a hereditary, essentially permanent attachment of peasants to landlords and estates. Over the following century, successive rulers and legal decisions steadily expanded landlord authority: Peter the Great's introduction of the poll tax and the associated census made landlords responsible for their serfs' tax payments, giving them new administrative leverage; eighteenth-century legislation progressively restricted serfs' ability to bring legal complaints against their owners, to enter contracts independently, or to leave their landlord's estate without permission; and by Catherine's reign, landlords held the power to sell serfs, in some cases separately from the land they worked and even separately from their own families, to exile serfs to Siberia for perceived insubordination, and to impose corporal punishment largely without external oversight.

Students should resist collapsing all of this into a single undifferentiated category of "the peasantry." Russian rural society in this period recognized several distinct statuses. Private serfs (krepostnye krest'iane) were bound to an individual noble landlord, owed that landlord either labor service or cash and in-kind dues, and possessed only limited legal standing independent of their owner. State peasants (gosudarstvennye krest'iane), by contrast, were not subject to a private landlord but instead owed dues and taxes directly to the treasury; while still burdened with heavy obligations and subject to significant administrative control, state peasants generally possessed somewhat greater personal mobility, could petition state institutions more freely, and were not subject to a private individual's discretionary authority over their persons in the same way private serfs were. The ranks of state peasants grew substantially in 1764, when Catherine secularized church and monastery lands, transferring a former monastery peasant population estimated at roughly one million male taxable "souls" (and correspondingly more when counting entire households) into a new category of state-administered "economic peasants"—a reform that improved conditions for this particular population even as it strengthened the state's fiscal reach and, not incidentally, won Catherine goodwill among nobles who had long resented the church's independent landholding. A third category, household serfs (dvorovye liudi), worked within a landlord's residence as domestic servants, cooks, coachmen, and similar roles rather than laboring in the fields; while legally still serfs, their daily lives, close proximity to their owners, and complete separation from independent agricultural production distinguished their circumstances considerably from those of field-working serfs.

What did these legal categories mean in practice? A useful way to approach the question is to ask whether eighteenth-century serfdom is best understood as a labor system, a legal status, a social hierarchy, or a form of political control. The honest answer is that it functioned as all four simultaneously, and separating these dimensions analytically helps explain why the institution proved so durable and so consequential. As a labor system, serfdom extracted agricultural production and craft labor from the rural population through a combination of unpaid work on the lord's own land (examined further in Section 35.4 as barshchina) and cash or in-kind payments owed from a peasant household's own independent production (obrok). As a legal status, serfdom fixed a person's rights, obligations, and standing before the law according to birth, denying serfs many of the legal protections and mobility available to other social estates. As a social hierarchy, serfdom reinforced a rigid distinction between a landowning elite and a laboring rural majority, shaping status, marriage patterns, education, and social expectation on both sides of the divide. And as a form of political control, serfdom delegated to individual landlords much of the coercive and administrative authority that a state with limited direct reach into the countryside could not easily exercise itself—landlords collected taxes, maintained local order, administered rough justice, and provided army recruits, functioning, in effect, as an extension of the state's own governing capacity. This last dimension connects directly to Section 35.9's broader argument about how the empire governed such an enormous territory and population with limited direct administrative reach: through delegating authority to intermediaries, of whom the serf-owning nobility was the most important on the level of rural society.

This section should be read directly alongside Chapter 34's account of the Charter to the Nobility of 1785, which formally confirmed noble ownership of land and the serfs upon it as a secure, legally protected property right. Where Chapter 34 examined that Charter as an act of state policy strengthening noble loyalty and administrative service, this chapter now examines its lived consequence: an institution in which perhaps half the population of the Russian Empire lived under the near-total discretionary authority of another private individual, an arrangement with few close parallels among contemporary European societies and one that, as this chapter's later sections will show, generated real and, in 1773, explosive tension.

Section 35.2

Estate Society

Serfdom was the most consequential, but not the only, legal division structuring eighteenth-century Russian society. The empire organized its population through a system historians call soslovie, a term referring to legally differentiated social estates, each possessing its own rights, obligations, courts, tax status, and relationship to the state. Unlike a modern legal system, which generally presumes that citizens are governed by broadly common legal categories regardless of birth or occupation, Catherine's Russia assigned fundamentally different legal treatment according to which estate a person belonged to, and movement between estates, while not entirely impossible, was difficult, exceptional, and closely regulated by the state.

The major estates recognized in this period included the nobility (dvorianstvo), whose privileges—confirmed and extended by the 1785 Charter examined in Chapter 34—included exemption from personal taxation, from compulsory state service, and from corporal punishment, along with the exclusive right to own populated estates, meaning serfs, in most circumstances. The clergy formed a distinct hereditary estate with its own courts, educational institutions, and tax exemptions, though the church's own landed wealth and independent authority had been sharply curtailed by Catherine's 1764 secularization. Merchants (kupechestvo), organized into guilds according to registered capital, enjoyed certain tax and legal privileges tied to their commercial registration but remained subject to obligations, including a poll tax until later reforms, from which nobles were exempt. Townspeople (meshchane) formed a broad urban estate encompassing artisans, small traders, and other city dwellers who did not qualify for merchant guild status. The peasantry, as already discussed, divided further into private serfs, state peasants, and other subcategories. Cossacks constituted a distinct, semi-military estate with its own internal institutions, discussed at length in Section 35.7. Beyond these broad categories, particular groups—foreign colonists invited to settle sparsely populated regions, certain non-Russian and non-Christian populations governed under distinct administrative arrangements, and others—occupied further specialized legal niches.

The Legislative Commission of 1767–1768, examined in Chapter 34 as an episode in Catherine's engagement with Enlightenment political thought, illustrates the practical boundaries of this estate system with particular clarity. Delegates were elected from the nobility, from townspeople, from state peasants, from Cossacks, and from certain other groups, each submitting a nakaz, or set of instructions and grievances, for the Commission's consideration. Private serfs, however, were excluded from direct representation entirely; their landlords were assumed to speak, insofar as anyone spoke, for their interests. This exclusion was not an oversight but a direct expression of estate logic: private serfs were understood in law as falling under their landlord's authority rather than possessing an independent civic voice of their own. The gap between Catherine's public engagement with Enlightenment ideas of natural rights and reasoned law, examined in Chapter 34, and this structural silencing of half the peasant population in her own consultative assembly, is one of the eighteenth century's most instructive contradictions, and it recurs as a theme across this chapter's remaining sections.

Understanding soslovie also clarifies why later reform, discussed in Chapter 42's treatment of the Great Reforms of the 1860s, required such sweeping legal change: the emancipation of the serfs and the associated judicial and local-government reforms of that later era amounted to a fundamental reworking of a social architecture that had been developing, and hardening, since at least the seventeenth century and that reached one of its most fully articulated forms under Catherine II.

Historical Vocabulary
Soslovie

Soslovie refers to the system of legally recognized, hereditary social estates that structured Russian imperial society from the eighteenth century into the early twentieth. Each soslovie—nobility, clergy, merchantry, townspeople, peasantry (itself subdivided), and Cossacks, among others—carried distinct legal rights, tax obligations, court jurisdictions, and relationships to state service.

Why it matters: Soslovie status, not wealth or occupation alone, determined a person's fundamental legal standing in Catherine's Russia. A poor noble retained legal privileges denied to a prosperous serf, and movement between estates was tightly restricted by the state. Understanding soslovie helps explain why reforms that modern readers might expect to apply universally—such as Enlightenment-influenced legal consultation—instead applied selectively, and why later nineteenth-century reform of Russian society required dismantling this entire legal architecture rather than adjusting a single policy.

Section 35.3

Noble Culture and Provincial Life

Chapter 34 examined why Catherine II found it politically necessary and ideologically congenial to expand noble privilege, particularly through the 1785 Charter to the Nobility. This section asks a different question: what did being a noble actually mean, socially and culturally, away from the glittering court society of St. Petersburg that dominates many popular images of the era?

The Russian nobility of this period was far from socially uniform. At one extreme stood a small number of enormously wealthy magnate families, some owning tens of thousands of serfs across multiple provinces, maintaining palatial residences, extensive libraries, private theaters and orchestras staffed by serf performers, and close connections to the imperial court. At the other extreme stood a much larger number of modest provincial nobles who might own only a handful of serf households, farm alongside their own peasants in practice if not in law, and never travel beyond their home province in their entire lives. Between these poles lay a broad middling nobility of moderate landowners who formed the backbone of provincial society, staffed local administrative and judicial offices, and increasingly, after the 1775 provincial reform examined in Chapter 34, exercised expanded local governing authority through elected noble assemblies and courts.

Provincial noble life centered on the management of estates and the households and dependents attached to them. A noble landowner's daily concerns typically included overseeing agricultural production, managing accounts, adjudicating disputes among serfs, arranging labor and dues obligations, and maintaining the household's social standing within a local community of similarly situated families. Marriage among the provincial nobility served as much to consolidate landholding, alliances, and social position as to reflect personal preference, and the management of a family's estate across generations, including questions of inheritance and dowry, occupied considerable attention. Education for noble children, especially sons destined for military or civil service, might include tutors, sometimes French-speaking, reflecting the broader elite fashion for French language and culture examined in Chapter 34's discussion of Catherine's cultural patronage; daughters' education varied enormously depending on family wealth and inclination, ranging from minimal instruction to more substantial exposure to languages, music, and literature among wealthier families.

Freedom from compulsory state service, granted in 1762 and confirmed in 1785, did not mean that nobles universally withdrew from service; many continued to pursue military or civil careers as a path to status, income, and imperial favor, since a noble family's standing depended not only on landholding but on visible service and connection to the state. For others, however, the end of compulsory service made possible a genuinely private, estate-centered existence: managing property, engaging in provincial social life, corresponding with distant relatives and, among the more literate and cosmopolitan, with figures in the wider European republic of letters, and participating in the new provincial institutions—assemblies, courts, and boards—that the 1775 reform created and staffed predominantly with noble officeholders.

It is important not to romanticize this picture. Beneath even modest noble comfort lay the labor and dependency of serfs, and the legal and physical power a landlord held over serf households—including, in the era's most notorious cases, documented instances of extreme cruelty and abuse that Catherine's own government occasionally, though inconsistently, prosecuted—was real and largely unchecked by external oversight. The gap between a wealthy magnate's cultivated European sensibility and the physical coercion that sustained his estate's income was one of the eighteenth-century Russian nobility's most striking, and most consequential, contradictions, a contradiction that would become a central theme of Russian literature and social criticism examined in later chapters, including Chapter 40's treatment of the nineteenth-century intelligentsia.

Section 35.4

Peasant Economy and Household

The basic unit of rural economic life in eighteenth-century Russia was not the individual laborer but the peasant household and, above it, the village community; in many central Russian communities, village institutions could organize landholding communally, periodically reallocating strips of land among member households according to family size and labor capacity, though the frequency of such repartitions and the specific forms of landholding varied considerably by region and estate. This communal structure, together with heavy and often collective tax and dues obligations, meant that peasant economic decisions were made within a web of household and communal constraints rather than by isolated individuals responding only to market signals, even as market participation, examined below, remained a genuine and growing feature of peasant life.

Serf and state peasant households owed their obligations chiefly through two mechanisms, and landlords generally chose whichever combination suited their estate's location and economic character. Barshchina, or labor service (a form of corvée), required serfs to work a fixed number of days, often three per week, cultivating the lord's own demesne land using their own tools and draft animals; this arrangement predominated in the fertile black-earth regions of central and southern Russia, where grain cultivation on the lord's land for market sale was especially profitable. Obrok, by contrast, required serfs to pay a fixed cash or in-kind due, leaving them freer to organize their own labor, including, in many cases, seasonal wage labor or craft production away from the estate; this arrangement predominated in less fertile northern and central regions, where agriculture alone offered landlords less profit and where peasant participation in trades, crafts, and seasonal migrant labor could generate cash income landlords could then tax through dues. Many estates combined elements of both systems, and the balance between them shifted over the century as landlords sought to maximize returns from increasingly commercialized grain markets, particularly following Russia's eighteenth-century gains of Black Sea ports examined in Chapter 34.

Historical Vocabulary
Obrok and Barshchina

Barshchina refers to unpaid labor service a serf owed a landlord, typically several days each week spent cultivating the lord's own demesne land using the serf's own tools and draft animals. Obrok refers to a cash or in-kind due a serf owed instead of, or alongside, labor service, leaving the serf's own labor freer to organize independently, including through craft production, trade, or seasonal wage work.

Why it matters: The choice between these two systems was not incidental; it shaped a serf household's daily rhythms, its exposure to market forces, and its practical, if narrow, room for economic maneuver. Barshchina predominated in the fertile black-earth regions where grain cultivation for market sale on the lord's own land was especially profitable; obrok predominated in less fertile northern and central regions, where landlords instead captured value from peasants' independent commercial and craft activity. Many estates combined both, and the balance shifted across the century as markets for grain expanded.

Beyond these formal obligations, the peasant household economy encompassed a wide range of activity: growing grain and vegetables for subsistence and for sale; raising livestock; producing textiles, tools, and other craft goods for household use and local exchange; participating in seasonal migrant labor (otkhodnichestvo) that took some peasants, particularly from obrok estates, to distant towns and cities for wage work during agricultural off-seasons; and engaging in local and regional markets both to acquire goods the household could not produce itself and to sell surplus production for cash needed to meet dues and taxes. Far from a static, purely subsistence "traditional village," the eighteenth-century Russian countryside was increasingly integrated into wider commercial networks, even as most households retained subsistence production as their primary economic foundation and safety margin against bad harvests.

Households facing especially heavy demands or difficult circumstances employed a range of strategies beyond simple compliance or open resistance. Peasants petitioned landlords and, in some documented cases, attempted to petition the state or even the empress directly regarding especially severe abuses, though such petitions against one's own landlord were legally restricted and risky. Households negotiated informally with estate stewards over the timing and scale of demands, particularly around harvest failures. Some peasants fled to the frontier, to Siberia, to Cossack territories, or into the growing cities, seeking to escape particularly burdensome estates, despite the legal and practical risks of detection and forcible return. Village communities also managed internal risk-sharing, redistributing communal land and adjusting obligations to help households facing hardship, insofar as the community's own collective tax liability allowed. These strategies, taken together, demonstrate that peasants navigated their circumstances as economic and social actors making calculated choices under real constraints, rather than as simply passive recipients of landlord demands—an important corrective to older, more static images of the eighteenth-century Russian village.

Section 35.5

Women and Family

There was no single "women's experience" in eighteenth-century Russia; gender intersected with estate, wealth, religion, and geography to produce sharply differing lives. This section compares noblewomen, urban women, and peasant and serf women, while noting that women in frontier, Cossack, and non-Russian communities experienced further distinct patterns examined more fully in Section 35.6 and this chapter's Frontier Context feature.

Noblewomen, particularly among wealthier families, could hold and inherit property in their own right under Russian law to a degree that distinguished Russia from several contemporary Western European legal systems, and widowed noblewomen not infrequently managed substantial estates independently, exercising real authority over stewards, serfs, and household finances. Catherine's own reign, and her patronage of the Smolny Institute for the education of noble daughters (examined in Chapter 34), reflected a broader elite interest in expanding women's education, though such opportunities reached only a small fraction of even noble society and remained essentially unavailable to women of lower estates. Noblewomen nonetheless operated within a patriarchal legal and social order: marriage remained central to family strategy and status, and while marriage did not automatically transfer a noblewoman's legal ownership of property to her husband—Russian law, reinforced by a 1753 ruling, allowed married women to retain, acquire, sell, and independently manage separate property to a degree unusual by contemporary European standards—patriarchal household authority and family pressures could still substantially constrain a woman's practical independence in exercising these rights. Women, whatever their property rights, were formally excluded from state and military service and from the direct exercise of political authority the Legislative Commission and provincial institutions afforded noblemen.

Urban and merchant women's lives varied considerably by family wealth and occupation. Merchant and artisan households often depended on women's labor in workshops, shops, and household production, and merchant widows, like noble widows, sometimes continued to manage family businesses after a husband's death, a pattern documented in numerous eighteenth-century Russian towns. Urban women of more modest means, including domestic servants and the wives of lower townspeople, combined household labor with wage or craft work under generally more constrained legal and economic circumstances than merchant or noble women.

Peasant and serf women's lives centered on the household economy examined in Section 35.4: agricultural labor alongside men during planting and harvest, primary responsibility for textile production, food processing, childcare, and household management, and, for many, participation in craft production or seasonal labor that contributed to the household's ability to meet its obligations. Serf women faced an additional layer of vulnerability specific to their legal status: a landlord's authority over his serfs extended, in practice if not always in clearly codified law, to matters of marriage timing and partner selection, to labor assignment, and, in documented and troubling cases, to physical and sexual abuse largely unchecked by external legal recourse. Marriage among serfs was nonetheless a genuine social institution shaped by community custom, family strategy, and household labor needs, and village and family structures provided some measure of collective protection and negotiation even within an oppressive legal framework.

The unifying analytical point across these varied experiences is that gender in eighteenth-century Russia cannot be examined in isolation from estate and legal status: a noblewoman's constrained but real property rights and household authority differed enormously from a serf woman's near-total legal subjection to both a husband and a landlord, even though both lived under the same broader patriarchal assumptions about women's proper role. This intersection of gender with legal status recurs as a theme in later chapters, including Chapter 42's treatment of women and the Great Reforms and Chapter 55's treatment of women in the Soviet industrial workforce.

Section 35.6

Religious and Ethnic Diversity

Catherine II's empire was not a religiously or ethnically uniform Orthodox Russian society; it was a vast, plural polity whose population, by the later eighteenth century, included Russians, Ukrainians, and Belarusians of varying regional identity and legal status; Tatars, Bashkirs, Chuvash, Mari, Mordvins, and other Volga-Ural peoples, many but not all Muslim; Kalmyks, practicing Tibetan Buddhism, in the lower Volga steppe; numerous Siberian indigenous peoples; Baltic Germans, Estonians, and Latvians in territories acquired earlier in the century; Ukrainian Cossacks and the population of the former Hetmanate, examined in Chapter 30; and, following the partitions of Poland-Lithuania beginning in 1772 and examined in Chapter 34, a substantial new Polish Catholic population and, of particular long-term significance, a large Jewish population previously living under Polish-Lithuanian rule and now, for the first time, incorporated in large numbers into the Russian state.

The central historical question this section addresses is how an early modern imperial state, with limited direct administrative reach, actually governed such a religiously and ethnically varied population. The general pattern combined selective toleration, administrative categorization, taxation, and negotiation with local elites rather than uniform assimilation or systematic persecution, though the specifics varied considerably by group, region, and period. Catherine's government generally tolerated Islam as practiced among Volga-Ural and steppe Muslim populations, going so far as to establish, in 1788, the Orenburg Mohammedan Spiritual Assembly, a state-sponsored institution intended to organize and, to a degree, supervise Islamic religious life within a framework the state could recognize and work through—an approach reflecting pragmatic governance rather than religious conviction, since Catherine's government judged that formal engagement with, rather than suppression of, Islamic religious institutions would produce more stable and cooperative relations with Muslim populations whose land and manpower the empire needed. Old Believers, despite continuing formal legal disabilities and periodic local persecution stemming from the seventeenth-century church schism examined in Chapter 29, found somewhat greater practical toleration under Catherine than under some earlier rulers, though as Section 35.8 will show, communities among them still harbored grievances substantial enough to support rebellion.

The Jewish population newly incorporated through the Polish partitions received distinctive and increasingly restrictive treatment. Catherine's government initially confirmed many of the legal and communal privileges Jewish communities had held under Polish-Lithuanian rule, but in 1791 introduced the first version of what would become known as the Pale of Settlement, a designated territory outside of which Jewish residence was generally prohibited—a policy examined at greater length in Chapter 45's treatment of the Pale in the context of the later nineteenth-century empire, but whose origins in Catherine's reign students should recognize as continuous with this chapter's broader theme of legally differentiated, spatially and administratively bounded treatment of distinct populations.

Bashkir communities in the Volga-Ural region occupied a particularly contested position. Bashkir lands had been subject to escalating Russian settlement, fortress construction, and administrative encroachment across the eighteenth century, provoking a series of earlier Bashkir uprisings that predated Pugachev's rebellion and that shaped Bashkir grievances against the imperial state well before 1773. Understanding this longer history of tension is essential to understanding why Bashkir communities joined Pugachev's movement in large numbers, examined in Section 35.8, rather than treating their participation as a spontaneous or incidental feature of the uprising.

Across these varied populations, the imperial state's basic governing strategy relied on administrative categorization (assigning legal status and tax obligations by recognized group), negotiation with religious and communal leaders who could mediate between distant officials and local populations, selective toleration calibrated to political usefulness rather than principle, and, where these mechanisms failed or where the state's own encroachment provoked resistance, coercive military response. This pattern, examined further in this chapter's Historical Debate feature, sets the stage directly for Section 35.7's discussion of the Yaik Cossacks and Section 35.8's account of how so many of these distinct populations, sharing different grievances but common frustration with the same imperial pressures, converged around Pugachev's rebellion.

Section 35.7

The Yaik Cossacks

Cossack communities, examined earlier in this textbook's treatment of the Ukrainian Hetmanate (Chapter 30) and Siberian conquest (Chapter 28), occupied a distinctive institutional position within the Russian Empire: self-governing frontier military communities that provided the state with valuable border defense and light cavalry service in exchange for substantial autonomy over their own internal affairs, landholding, and customary privileges. The Yaik Cossacks, settled along the Yaik River (renamed the Ural River after the rebellion examined below, precisely in order to erase the association) in the southern Ural steppe, exemplified this arrangement. Their community managed its own internal governance through a council (krug), elected or recognized its own leadership (though the state increasingly asserted the right to appoint or confirm Cossack officers, a growing source of friction), controlled valuable fishing rights along the Yaik that formed an important economic foundation of the community, and organized military service on terms that Cossacks themselves regarded as a matter of customary right rather than simple obedience to distant imperial command.

By the mid-eighteenth century, this arrangement had come under mounting strain. The imperial state, seeking greater administrative predictability, more reliable taxation, more standardized military organization, and firmer control over frontier populations whose loyalty and internal governance it found increasingly difficult to monitor from a distance, pressed to reduce Cossack self-governance, to interfere with fishing rights and other economic privileges, and to impose officers and administrative procedures many Yaik Cossacks regarded as violations of long-standing custom. This tension between frontier privilege and imperial standardization is the essential background to the rebellion examined in the next section: the empire valued the Yaik Cossacks precisely because their military service and frontier presence were useful, but the same administrative logic that made Catherine's government seek uniform provincial institutions across Russia (examined in Chapter 34) also drove it to erode the customary autonomy that gave Cossack communities their distinctive identity and cohesion.

Specific disputes in the years immediately preceding 1773 sharpened this general tension into active grievance. Conflicts over the election and conduct of Cossack officers, disputes over military obligations and pay, and state interference in internal Cossack affairs provoked unrest among the Yaik host well before Pugachev's arrival, including a serious disturbance in 1772 that the state suppressed with executions and further restrictions—restrictions that, rather than resolving the community's grievances, left a population primed for further rebellion when a plausible leader and cause presented themselves the following year. Understanding this immediate background helps explain why the Yaik Cossacks, rather than some other population, became the rebellion's initial base, and why they proved receptive to Pugachev's claim to be the rightful emperor Peter III, examined in the next section.

Frontier Context

The Volga-Ural and Yaik World

From the vantage point of Moscow or St. Petersburg, the Volga-Ural region and the Yaik frontier might appear as a distant periphery, significant chiefly for its fortress lines, its mineral wealth, and its Cossack military service. From within the region itself, the picture looked considerably different. This was a densely layered frontier world in which Russian Cossacks and settlers, Bashkir and Tatar communities defending customary land rights, Kalmyk pastoralists on the steppe to the south, Ural mining and metallurgical enterprises drawing on both free and coerced labor, and Old Believer communities that had fled earlier religious persecution into the region's more remote areas all lived in close, and often tense, proximity.

The imperial state's growing presence in this region—new fortress lines, expanding settlement, administrative reorganization, and mounting fiscal and labor demands on Ural industrial enterprises—did not simply extend Moscow's authority outward into empty space; it intersected with, and frequently disrupted, existing communities, land claims, and customary arrangements. This is precisely why the region, rather than the agricultural heartland of central Russia, became the epicenter of the empire's largest eighteenth-century rebellion: multiple populations, each with distinct grievances against distinct aspects of the same expanding imperial administration, occupied the same contested territory simultaneously.

Section 35.8

Pugachev's Rebellion

In September 1773, Emelian Pugachev, a Don Cossack who had served in the Russian army during the Seven Years' War and the ongoing war against the Ottoman Empire (examined in Chapter 34) before deserting and living for a time among Old Believer communities, appeared among the Yaik Cossacks claiming to be Peter III, the emperor deposed and killed in the 1762 coup that had brought Catherine II to power (examined in Chapters 33–34). This was not the first such claim in Russian history—several earlier pretenders had similarly invoked Peter III's supposed survival—but Pugachev's version found unusually receptive ground among Yaik Cossacks already primed by the grievances examined in Section 35.7. Students should understand why this specific claim mattered: a pretender claiming the imperial title asserted not opposition to monarchy itself but a rival, and in this telling more legitimate, claim to it, promising that the "true tsar," once restored, would grant the freedoms, privileges, and relief from oppressive landlords and officials that the current government had denied. This framing allowed diverse populations with different specific grievances to imagine that Pugachev's victory would address their particular concerns, without requiring them to articulate a shared political program beyond loyalty to the "true emperor."

From its Yaik Cossack base, the rebellion spread with remarkable speed across the Volga-Ural region during the following year, drawing in a coalition whose diversity is the central analytical point of this section. Bashkir communities, already aggrieved by decades of encroaching settlement and administrative pressure examined in Section 35.6, joined in large numbers, seeing in the rebellion an opportunity to resist further loss of land and autonomy. Workers at Ural mining and metallurgical enterprises, many of them serfs or state-assigned laborers bound to industrial establishments under harsh conditions, joined in significant numbers, some providing the rebellion with captured artillery and metalworking expertise. Old Believer communities, including some in whose midst Pugachev himself had spent time before the rebellion, offered support reflecting their own long-standing grievances against state religious policy. Serfs and state peasants across the affected region rose against individual landlords and local officials, in many documented cases killing landlords, estate stewards, and government officials in a wave of local violence that historians describe as jacquerie-like: not centrally coordinated attacks on the imperial order as an abstract system, but immediate, local acts of violence against specific figures of authority and oppression close at hand.

It is essential that students resist the temptation to describe this coalition as though it wanted a single, shared outcome. Yaik Cossacks sought restoration of their eroded frontier privileges and autonomy. Bashkirs sought protection of their land and reduced settlement pressure. Ural workers sought relief from harsh industrial labor conditions. Serfs sought freedom from landlord authority and, in many articulations attributed to Pugachev's manifestos, the abolition of serfdom itself and the granting of land to those who worked it. Old Believers sought religious toleration. These were overlapping but distinct grievances, held together for a time by a shared enemy—the existing structure of landlord authority, state administration, and the empress who, in Pugachev's telling, had usurped the throne from its rightful, more benevolent occupant—rather than by a unified political vision of what should replace that structure.

The rebellion reached its greatest extent in 1774, when Pugachev's forces besieged the fortress city of Orenburg for several months and, in July 1774, stormed and briefly occupied most of the city of Kazan, a major regional center, though the fortified Kazan Kremlin itself remained in government hands and relief forces soon defeated the rebels outside the city. Government forces, substantially reinforced once the Russo-Turkish War (examined in Chapter 34) concluded and freed experienced troops for domestic deployment, then began a sustained counteroffensive. Catherine's government treated the rebellion as an existential threat to imperial authority, and the military response, once fully mobilized, proved decisive: rebel forces suffered a series of major defeats through the latter half of 1774, Pugachev's coalition began to fracture as different constituencies' specific goals proved difficult to reconcile under mounting military pressure, and Pugachev himself was betrayed by former associates, captured, transported in a cage to Moscow, and executed in January 1775 following a public trial.

The rebellion's suppression involved extensive and often brutal reprisals across the affected region, including mass executions, punitive expeditions against Bashkir and other communities judged to have supported the uprising, and a systematic effort to erase the rebellion's memory from official commemoration—reflected symbolically in the renaming of the Yaik River as the Ural River, discussed in the preceding Frontier Context feature, and in Catherine's broader determination, examined in Chapter 34, to reorganize provincial administration in 1775 partly in direct response to the rebellion's demonstration of how thin the state's actual governing presence in much of the countryside had proven to be.

The earliest proclamations, addressed to the Yaik Cossacks in 1773, emphasized restoration of frontier privileges; the movement's most radical promises to serfs, including explicit freedom from landlord dependence and grants of land, date specifically from mid-1774, after the rebellion had already spread widely. Early manifestos attributed to Pugachev promised Cossacks the restoration of their traditional rivers, lands, and self-governance free of new taxation. Their promises to different groups were not always mutually consistent—for instance, the July 1774 promises of land and freedom to serfs implied a transformation of noble landholding that would have had little direct bearing on Cossack or Bashkir grievances—which helps explain both the movement's broadening appeal over time and the strains that emerged as it grew. Section 35.9

Empire, Social Control, and Resistance

Pugachev's Rebellion was the most dramatic response available to populations aggrieved by Catherine's imperial order, but it was only one point on a much broader spectrum of responses that ordinary people employed in dealing with landlords, officials, and the state. This section draws together that spectrum, which included petition and litigation, negotiation and bargaining, evasion and flight, religious and communal separatism, everyday noncompliance, and active participation and collaboration within imperial institutions, alongside outright rebellion.

Petition (the chelobitnaia tradition, examined further in Chapter 22's discussion of Muscovite institutions) remained a legitimate, if constrained, channel through which subjects—including, in limited circumstances, serfs regarding especially severe abuses—could seek redress from officials or, in rare and celebrated cases, directly from the monarch. Litigation, though the courts available to serfs were sharply limited compared to those available to other estates, offered state peasants, townspeople, and other non-serf populations a further avenue for resolving disputes over land, taxation, and obligations. Negotiation with estate stewards over the pace and scale of demands, particularly during difficult harvest years, formed a routine, largely undocumented but historically significant feature of landlord-serf relations, since even landlords with near-total legal authority generally recognized that pushing demands beyond a certain point risked flight, reduced future productivity, or worse. Flight to frontier regions, Cossack territories, Siberia, or the growing cities offered an exit option for particularly aggrieved individuals and families, despite the substantial legal and practical risks of detection and forcible return that serfdom's legal apparatus, especially after the 1649 Ulozhenie examined in Chapter 27, was specifically designed to minimize. Religious and communal separatism, exemplified by Old Believer communities examined in Section 35.6, allowed groups to maintain distinct identity and practice at some distance from full state or church oversight, particularly in remote regions. Everyday noncompliance—working slowly, concealing production, manipulating communal land allocation, or quietly resisting specific demands—constituted a pervasive, low-visibility form of resistance operating beneath the threshold of open confrontation. And active participation within imperial institutions—as soldiers, as estate stewards, as merchants, as provincial officials, and, for some fortunate serfs, as skilled artisans or even, in exceptional individual cases, as freed and prosperous former serfs—represented a further and equally real response available to at least some members of even the most legally constrained estates.

This spectrum matters because it corrects a picture, sometimes implicit in accounts that move directly from serfdom's oppressive legal structure to Pugachev's dramatic rebellion, in which ordinary people appear either passively submissive or violently rebellious with little in between. The historical reality was that most people, most of the time, navigated the imperial order through the full range of intermediate strategies described above, and rebellion occurred when specific, acute local grievances—Cossack privilege erosion, Bashkir land pressure, harsh industrial labor conditions, particular landlord abuses—converged geographically and temporally, as they did in the Volga-Ural region in 1773, rather than as a simple, general, and continuous product of serfdom's existence everywhere it was practiced.

This observation leads to the chapter's deepest analytical question: how did the Russian imperial state, with a relatively small corps of professional officials and vast distances to govern, actually maintain control over such an enormous and diverse population? The answer, consistent with this chapter's recurring theme, is that it did so chiefly through delegation rather than direct, uniform administration: delegating disciplinary and fiscal authority over serfs to individual landlords; delegating military and frontier governance to Cossack hosts in exchange for autonomy and service; delegating religious administration to church hierarchies, and, for Muslim populations, to institutions such as the Orenburg Mohammedan Spiritual Assembly examined in Section 35.6; and delegating much local governance, after 1775, to noble-staffed provincial institutions examined in Chapter 34. This system of governance through privilege, delegation, and negotiated authority, rather than uniform centralized law, proved capable of sustaining an enormous multiethnic empire across ordinary times, but Pugachev's Rebellion revealed how quickly it could unravel when multiple delegated intermediaries—Cossack officers, industrial managers, landlords, local officials—simultaneously lost the confidence and cooperation of the populations beneath them.

Historical Debate

Empire, Colonialism, or Negotiated Rule?

The question: Is the expansion of Russian authority over the Volga-Ural region, the Cossack frontiers, and other non-Russian populations examined in this chapter best analyzed through the language of empire, of colonialism, of negotiated rule, or through some combination of these frameworks?

The colonial-framework position: Some historians argue that Russian expansion into Bashkir lands, the steppe, and other non-Russian territories shares important features with European overseas colonialism: settler encroachment on indigenous land, extraction of resources and labor, administrative categorization of subject populations, and the use of coercive force to suppress resistance, as seen in the suppression of both earlier Bashkir uprisings and Pugachev's rebellion.
The negotiated-rule position: Other historians emphasize that Russian imperial governance incorporated diverse populations as recognized legal estates and communities with negotiated institutions—Cossack hosts, Muslim spiritual assemblies, Bashkir landholding arrangements—rather than governing them as a categorically separate colonized "other," and that the empire's frontier expansion often proceeded through gradual settlement, fortress-line construction, and administrative absorption alongside, rather than purely instead of, military conquest. These scholars caution that such features complicate direct, one-to-one comparison with particular overseas colonial models, though they note that many overseas empires also governed substantially through intermediaries and negotiated local institutions, so the comparison remains a matter of degree and specific historical circumstance rather than a categorical difference.

Evidence: Historians examine administrative records, land disputes, the terms of specific treaties and agreements with frontier communities, patterns of settlement and resistance, and comparative study of contemporary European colonial empires.

Points of agreement: Most historians agree that Russian expansion involved genuine coercion, land pressure, and cultural and administrative asymmetry between Russian settlers/officials and many non-Russian populations, and that neither a purely benign "unification" narrative nor a purely predatory "colonial conquest" narrative captures the full historical reality.

Points of disagreement: Historians differ over how much weight to give structural similarities with overseas colonialism versus features—shared Christian Orthodox political culture in some regions, contiguous land expansion rather than overseas conquest, incorporation of local elites into imperial service—that some scholars argue distinguish the Russian case.

Student task: Using evidence from Sections 35.6 and 35.7 regarding Bashkir and Yaik Cossack relations with the imperial state, evaluate which elements of this debate the evidence most strongly supports, and identify what additional evidence would help resolve remaining disagreement.

Multiple Perspectives

Inside the Rebellion

A Yaik Cossack rank-and-file member: For a Yaik Cossack who had watched the state erode his community's fishing rights, officer elections, and internal autonomy over the preceding decade, Pugachev's claim to be the "true tsar" offered a route back to customary privilege framed as loyal restoration rather than treasonous rebellion against the crown itself.
A Bashkir community elder: For a Bashkir community that had already resisted decades of Russian fortress construction and settlement on ancestral land, joining Pugachev represented a further, larger-scale attempt to halt an encroachment that earlier, smaller uprisings had failed to stop, with the rebellion's chaos offering an opportunity, however uncertain, to reverse recent losses.
A serf on a central Russian estate: For a serf hearing distant news of the rebellion and Pugachev's promises of freedom and land, the uprising offered the prospect, however unlikely to be realized in practice, of ending the specific landlord authority that structured daily life, prompting some serfs to rise locally against individual landlords even without direct contact with Pugachev's main forces.
A provincial noble landowner: For a provincial noble, the rebellion represented a terrifying and, in some documented cases, lethal threat to person, family, and property, confirming fears about the fragility of the existing social order and reinforcing support for the very system of landlord authority and state coercion the rebellion had risen against.

These perspectives illustrate why the rebellion's coalition proved both broad and, ultimately, unstable: shared hostility to the existing order did not translate into a shared vision of what should replace it.

Cause and Effect

Why the Rebellion Grew So Large

Long-Term Conditions

Decades of hardening serfdom, expanding noble privilege, and eroding Yaik Cossack and Bashkir autonomy created multiple distinct populations with deep, accumulated grievances against the imperial order.

Intermediate Developments

Specific disputes over Cossack officer elections and fishing rights, continued Bashkir land pressure, and harsh Ural industrial labor conditions sharpened these grievances in the years immediately before 1773.

Short-Term Triggers

Pugachev's appearance and claim to be Peter III offered a unifying, legitimizing framework—loyalty to a "true tsar"—that allowed distinct aggrieved groups to join a single movement without requiring a shared political program.

Effects

Rapid territorial spread across the Volga-Ural region in 1773–1774, the siege of Orenburg and the storming of much of Kazan, extensive local violence against landlords and officials, and, ultimately, a massive state military and administrative response, including the 1775 provincial reform.

Historical complexity: No single grievance—Cossack, Bashkir, serf, or industrial—explains the rebellion's scale; its size resulted from the temporary convergence of several distinct populations' separate grievances around a shared symbolic leader.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Peasant Dependency Since the Ulozhenie

What Stayed the Same

  • The basic legal principle established in 1649—that peasants were permanently and hereditarily bound to the land and to their landlord—remained in force throughout this period.
  • Village communal structures and household-based economic organization persisted as the basic unit of rural life.
  • Landlords continued to function as the state's chief agents of rural taxation and order.

What Changed

  • Peter I's poll tax and revision system (1718–1724) gave landlords new fiscal responsibility over serfs and thereby new administrative leverage.
  • Eighteenth-century legislation progressively restricted serfs' legal standing, including their ability to bring complaints against landlords.
  • The 1762 freeing of nobles from compulsory service, without a corresponding change for serfs, widened the practical asymmetry between the two estates.
  • The 1785 Charter to the Nobility formally secured noble ownership of serfs as protected property, the fullest legal expression of this long process.

Why the pattern matters: Serfdom did not spring into its late eighteenth-century form all at once; it developed cumulatively across more than a century of legal and fiscal change, a trajectory students should keep in mind when Chapter 42 examines its abolition in 1861.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Serf Village and a Provincial Estate

A representative, though not universal, central Russian serf village of this period might consist of several dozen households organized in part around a communal land system, with strips of arable land periodically reallocated among households according to family size and labor capacity in communities that practiced repartition. A household's year followed the agricultural calendar—spring plowing and planting, summer haying, autumn harvest and threshing, and a winter season devoted to indoor craft work, textile production, and, on obrok estates, sometimes travel to towns for wage labor. Village elders and communal assemblies handled internal disputes, land reallocation, and the distribution of collective tax and dues burdens among households, operating with real, if bounded, autonomy so long as the village met its overall obligations to the landlord and the state.

The manor house at the center of a modest provincial estate might house a noble family of moderate means, along with several household serfs performing domestic labor. The lord or his steward oversaw the collection of dues or the organization of labor service, adjudicated minor disputes among serfs, and represented the estate's interests to visiting officials and neighboring landowners. The physical and social distance between the manor house and the serf village was often surprisingly small in practical terms—stewards and even landlords frequently had direct daily knowledge of individual serf households—even as the legal gulf between lord and serf remained immense and, for the serf, inescapable except through the limited strategies examined in Section 35.9.

Chapter Review and Historical Thinking Checkpoint

This chapter has examined Catherine II's empire from below, tracing the legal architecture of serfdom and estate society, the varied experiences of nobles, peasants, and women, the plural religious and ethnic composition of the empire, and the largest rebellion in Russian history before the twentieth century. Before proceeding to Chapter 36's examination of Russia and the Napoleonic Wars, students should synthesize this chapter's central argument: that Catherine's empire was held together not by uniform law or a homogeneous population but by a layered social order of estates, privileges, dependencies, local institutions, religious communities, and negotiated relationships with imperial authority, and that this same order, precisely because it depended on delegated and negotiated authority rather than direct uniform administration, proved capable of producing an explosive, if ultimately unsuccessful, convergence of grievances in 1773–1775.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined Catherine II's Russian Empire as a social order experienced very differently by different populations, complementing Chapter 34's political and diplomatic account of the same reign. It began with the legal development of serfdom, tracing how the Sobornoe Ulozhenie of 1649, Peter the Great's fiscal reforms, and successive eighteenth-century legislation cumulatively bound roughly half the peasant population to individual noble landlords, while distinguishing private serfs from state peasants and household serfs.

The chapter then examined soslovie, the system of legally differentiated social estates that structured Russian society without any common framework resembling universal citizenship, and showed how even Catherine's Enlightenment-influenced Legislative Commission operated within, rather than against, this estate logic by excluding private serfs from direct representation.

Turning to noble life, the chapter distinguished wealthy magnates from modest provincial nobles and showed that freedom from compulsory service, secured in 1762, created space for a genuinely private, estate-centered noble existence resting on serf labor and dependency.

The chapter examined peasant household economy in detail, distinguishing barshchina and obrok obligations, describing communal landholding and household survival strategies, and emphasizing peasants' active economic agency within severe constraints.

It then compared women's experiences across noble, urban, and peasant or serf status, showing that gender intersected inseparably with estate and legal standing to produce sharply different lives.

The chapter surveyed the empire's religious and ethnic diversity, examining toleration, administrative categorization, and the specific and increasingly restrictive treatment of the newly incorporated Jewish population, alongside the longer-standing tensions surrounding Bashkir land and Muslim communities in the Volga-Ural region.

It examined the Yaik Cossacks' eroding frontier autonomy as the immediate background to Pugachev's Rebellion of 1773–1775, presented as a multi-constituency uprising—Cossacks, Bashkirs, Ural industrial workers, Old Believers, serfs, and state peasants—unified temporarily by a shared pretender claim rather than a single coherent program, and traced its dramatic rise, its brutal suppression, and its consequences for state policy.

Finally, the chapter placed rebellion within a broader spectrum of responses to imperial authority—petition, litigation, negotiation, flight, religious separatism, everyday noncompliance, and active participation—and argued that the empire governed its vast and diverse population chiefly through delegated and negotiated authority rather than uniform direct administration, a pattern whose strengths and vulnerabilities the Pugachev rebellion exposed with particular clarity.

These themes—legal differentiation, delegated governance, multi-constituency social conflict, and the full spectrum of accommodation and resistance—prepare students for Chapter 36's examination of Russia's confrontation with revolutionary and Napoleonic France, a very different kind of crisis that would nonetheless test many of the same social and administrative structures examined in this chapter.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has turned Chapter 34's account of Catherine the Great's reforms, diplomacy, and territorial expansion inside out, asking not what the empress did but what her empire meant for the people who lived within it. The answer is neither a simple story of uniform oppression nor a simple story of stable, well-ordered hierarchy. It is a story of legally differentiated estates, unequal burdens, negotiated privileges, and a state that governed an enormous and diverse territory chiefly by delegating authority to landlords, Cossack hosts, religious institutions, and local elites, rather than through direct, uniform administration.

Pugachev's Rebellion mattered not because it succeeded in overturning this order—it did not—but because it revealed, with unusual clarity, how many different populations harbored genuine grievances against specific aspects of that order, and how quickly those grievances could converge, however briefly and unevenly, when a plausible leader and cause presented themselves. The rebellion's suppression, and the state's subsequent tightening of both provincial administration and noble privilege, shows that Catherine's government drew from the crisis a lesson about strengthening control rather than fundamentally reforming the social relationships that had produced the uprising in the first place.

This chapter's themes—legal differentiation by estate, the layered and unequal position of women, the plural religious and ethnic character of the empire, and the wide spectrum of responses available to subordinate populations—will recur throughout the remainder of this textbook. Chapter 39 will return to Caucasus and Central Asian expansion in the following century; Chapter 42 will examine the eventual emancipation of the serfs and the dismantling of much of the estate system examined here; and Chapter 45 will return in greater depth to the empire's late nineteenth-century nationalities question, building directly on the plural imperial order this chapter has described.

As the textbook proceeds to Chapter 36's account of Russia's confrontation with revolutionary and Napoleonic France, students should carry forward this chapter's central lesson: that no single narrative of oppression, unity, benevolence, or conquest adequately describes an early modern empire as vast and varied as Catherine's Russia, and that understanding such an empire requires attention to the full range of social positions, institutions, and human choices operating beneath the level of high politics.
