Part VIII — The Late Imperial Crisis

The Duma Monarchy, Stolypin, Reform, and Repression

A History of Russia: From the Eurasian World to the Russian Federation

Chapter Opening

Did the Revolution of 1905 transform the Russian Empire into a constitutional monarchy, or did it merely force the autocracy to govern through new representative institutions while preserving decisive imperial power?

On 27 April 1906 (O.S.; 10 May N.S.), the deputies of the first elected legislature in Russian history assembled in the Winter Palace in St. Petersburg to be received by the emperor. The hall was divided by more than politics. Court officials, grand dukes, and members of the newly constituted State Council stood on one side in uniforms and decorations; on the other stood several hundred deputies, many of them in the plain dress of provincial lawyers, schoolteachers, zemstvo employees, and peasants who had traveled to the capital with little experience of palaces. Nicholas II delivered a brief address, and the Duma then moved to the Tauride Palace to begin its work. Within days it would become clear that the two sides had arrived with incompatible understandings of what had just been created. Only a few days earlier, on 23 April, the emperor had promulgated a revised set of Fundamental Laws that defined his own powers in terms that many deputies regarded as a retreat from the promises of the previous October.

This chapter studies the political system that emerged from that moment and lasted until the outbreak of the First World War in the summer of 1914. Historians have called it the Duma monarchy, the June Third system, the Russian constitutional experiment, and, more skeptically, a façade constitutionalism. Each label carries an argument. The chapter does not settle the question by choosing a label. Instead, it asks students to examine what the institutions of 1906–1914 actually did: what powers the emperor, the ministers, the State Council, and the State Duma possessed; how elections were organized and manipulated; which laws were passed and which were blocked; how the government used reform and repression together; and how workers, peasants, national minorities, and educated society responded.

The period is dominated by one statesman, Pyotr Arkadievich Stolypin, who served as minister of the interior and, from July 1906, as chairman of the Council of Ministers until his assassination in September 1911. Stolypin has been remembered in sharply opposed ways: as the last great reformer of the old regime, as the executioner of the revolution, as a conservative nationalist, and as a pragmatic administrator who wished to build a stable, property-owning rural society. This chapter treats him as a figure whose policies illustrate the central contradiction of the age, the simultaneous use of coercion and reform to preserve a political order that had been shaken but not overthrown. It also insists that Stolypin was not the whole story. Deputies, party organizers, zemstvo activists, factory workers, peasant households, newspaper editors, imperial borderland elites, and the emperor himself shaped these years.

The chapter also continues the textbook's attention to the Russian Empire as a multinational state. Elections to the Duma were organized differently in Poland, the Caucasus, Central Asia, and Siberia; revisions of the electoral law in 1907 reduced the representation of non-Russian regions; and nationalist politics, including Russian nationalism, became an organizing principle of the Third and Fourth Dumas. The empire's Muslim, Jewish, Polish, Ukrainian, Baltic, and other populations experienced the Duma monarchy through the specific restrictions and opportunities that applied to them, not as a single undifferentiated "Russian" political experiment.

Evidence for this period is unusually abundant and unusually contested. The Fundamental Laws, electoral statutes, Duma stenographic reports, ministerial memoranda, police files, newspaper runs, party programs, memoirs, strike statistics, and village surveys survive in large quantities. They were produced by participants with strong interests: liberal leaders writing to justify their strategies, officials writing to defend the state, revolutionaries writing to claim the future, and statisticians whose categories reflected state priorities. Students must therefore read laws against administrative practice, official figures against alternative estimates, and memoirs written after 1917 with awareness that their authors knew how the story ended.

That last point is the chapter's most important methodological warning. Because the Romanov monarchy fell in February 1917, it is tempting to read every event of 1906–1914 as a step toward collapse, or alternatively to read the period as a promising modernization tragically interrupted by war. Both approaches impose an ending on the evidence. Chapter 46 explained the revolutionary crisis of 1905; Chapter 48 will explain how the First World War strained the system to breaking point; Chapters 49 to 51 will trace the revolutions of 1917. This chapter's task is narrower and, in some ways, harder: to understand the system on its own terms, in the years when its future was still open.

Finally, the chapter connects Russia to wider patterns. In the same decades, Ottoman, Iranian, Chinese, and Japanese political elites experimented with constitutions, parliaments, and legal reform; Germany and Austria-Hungary maintained parliaments alongside strong monarchical executives; and many European states debated the relationship between mass politics, property, nationalism, and empire. Russia's Duma monarchy was a distinctive case, but not an isolated one.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain the institutional structure created by the Fundamental Laws of 1906, including the respective powers of the emperor, the Council of Ministers, the State Council, and the State Duma.
  2. Analyze how Article 87 and other provisions allowed the monarchy to preserve executive authority alongside representative legislation.
  3. Distinguish the major political parties and movements of the Duma period by social base, program, attitude toward the monarchy, and position on land reform and empire.
  4. Describe how the electoral systems of 1905–1906 and 1907 worked, and explain why the law of 3 June 1907 was controversial.
  5. Compare the First, Second, Third, and Fourth Dumas in composition, relations with the government, and legislative record.
  6. Assess Stolypin's career as a combination of repression, reform, and political management rather than as either simple liberalism or simple reaction.
  7. Evaluate the agrarian reforms of 1906–1911 by separating the government's intentions from peasant responses and measurable outcomes by 1914.
  8. Interpret the revival of strikes and labor politics after 1910, including the Lena Goldfields shootings of 1912, as evidence about the limits of political stabilization.
  9. Contextualize economic, educational, and cultural modernization as developments that did not automatically produce democratization.
  10. Evaluate competing historiographical classifications of the post-1905 system as constitutional monarchy, modified autocracy, or hybrid regime.
  11. Connect the Duma monarchy to the experience of non-Russian peoples of the empire and to comparable constitutional experiments elsewhere in Eurasia.
  12. Avoid teleological reading of 1906–1914 as either an inevitable road to 1917 or a lost path to stable liberal government.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How could the same constitutional settlement both restrict autocracy and preserve it?
  2. Where did sovereignty reside after 1906: in the emperor, in the emperor acting together with the legislature, or in some contested combination?
  3. Why did the regime survive the revolutionary crisis of 1905–1907, and what did it cost in political legitimacy?
  4. Was Stolypin attempting to preserve autocracy, construct a conservative constitutional order, modernize Russia, or all three at once?
  5. Had Stolypin's agrarian reforms begun a fundamental rural transformation by 1914, or had they only started to alter a much larger and more persistent agrarian system?
  6. Why did the Third Duma legislate effectively while the First and Second Dumas did not, and what does that difference reveal about the nature of the system?
  7. What does the revival of strikes after 1910 reveal about the relationship between economic growth and political stability?
  8. How did the post-1905 system treat the empire's non-Russian and non-Orthodox populations?
  9. What kinds of evidence let historians distinguish what the law said from how the state actually governed?
  10. How should historians evaluate the period 1906–1914 without simply reading backward from 1917?

Historical Context

The system studied in this chapter was created in the shadow of defeat and revolution. Chapter 46 traced how the Russo-Japanese War (1904–1905), Bloody Sunday (9 January 1905, O.S.), the strike movement, the St. Petersburg Soviet, mutinies, peasant disturbances, and the general strike of October 1905 pushed the government to concede. The October Manifesto of 17 October 1905 (O.S.) promised civil liberties, a broadened franchise, and the principle that no law would take effect without the approval of an elected State Duma. Sergei Witte, who drafted the manifesto's framework, became the first chairman of a unified Council of Ministers. The manifesto divided Russian society. Moderate liberals and many property-holders accepted it as a basis for reform; radicals regarded it as a trick; conservatives regarded it as a calamity; and national and religious minorities read it according to their own prospects. By December 1905 the government had defeated the Moscow uprising and begun punitive expeditions in the Baltic provinces, the Caucasus, and Siberia, but it had also issued an electoral law and was committed to holding elections.

The political inheritance was complicated by the imperial legal tradition. Chapters 32 and 38 described how Peter the Great's colleges, the Senate, and later the bureaucratic apparatus of Nicholas I had created a state in which law was largely an instrument of administration. The emperor's will was the source of law, and ministers answered to him alone. Administrative discretion mattered more than judicial independence in many areas of life, a pattern only partly altered by the judicial reform of 1864 discussed in Chapter 42. Provisions for states of emergency, such as the Statute on Measures to Preserve State Order and Public Tranquillity of 1881, gave governors and police broad powers in large parts of the empire and remained in effect after 1905. Any constitutional order created in 1906 therefore had to operate alongside a deeply entrenched administrative state.

Social conditions shaped the political field. The great majority of the empire's population, perhaps four in five, lived in the countryside, where land shortage, communal tenure, redemption payments left over from the emancipation (Chapter 42), and tensions between peasants and landowners had fueled the agrarian disturbances of 1905–1906. The industrial working class, discussed in Chapter 44, was small relative to the total population but concentrated in large factories in St. Petersburg, Moscow, the Urals, the southern mining and metallurgical region, Baku, Poland, and the Baltic. An educated public of professionals, zemstvo activists, university students, journalists, and entrepreneurs had grown since the Great Reforms and had been politicized by the Union of Liberation and the zemstvo congresses. The empire's national and religious minorities had their own political agendas, from Polish demands for autonomy to Jewish struggles against legal disabilities, Muslim organization for religious and educational rights, and Finnish defense of constitutional autonomy.

The state's capacity to coerce was also real. The army, despite mutinies in 1905, had largely remained loyal at the decisive moments; the Okhrana and Gendarme Corps had extensive informant networks; and revolutionary parties had been badly damaged by arrests and by their own losses in the uprisings of December 1905. At the same time, the fiscal situation was precarious: war costs and increased debt had strained the treasury, and the government relied on foreign loans, notably a large French loan concluded in April 1906. A ministry that wanted to continue borrowing abroad had incentives to present a stable political order.

Finally, the era's intellectual environment mattered. Educated Russians knew of the constitutional experiments of Japan (1889), the Ottoman Empire (1908), Iran (1906), and China's late-Qing reforms; of the German Reichstag with its limited powers over the government; and of the Habsburg Reichsrat. Liberal leaders such as Pavel Milyukov looked to western European parliaments. Conservatives pointed to the German model of monarchy with a parliament that could be managed. These comparisons do not predict outcomes, but they show that the actors of 1906 were reasoning about available models and not simply improvising.

Section 47.1

The Fundamental Laws of 1906

The October Manifesto of 1905 had promised a Duma with real legislative power, but it was a brief declaration of principles. It did not define the relationship between the new legislature and the emperor, the status of ministers, or the limits of imperial decree. Between October 1905 and April 1906, officials, including Witte and a group of jurists, drafted a revision of the Code of Fundamental Laws, the section of the Russian legal code that defined the structure of supreme power. The revised text was issued on 23 April 1906, only four days before the Duma's opening, and without any consultation of the Duma. For many liberals this timing was itself an insult: the framework within which the Duma would operate was fixed before the Duma could speak.

The Fundamental Laws created a legislature of two chambers. The State Duma, elected for a term of five years unless dissolved earlier, was one chamber. The State Council, transformed from an appointed advisory body of the nineteenth century into an upper house, was the other. Half of its members were appointed by the emperor; the other half were elected by designated corporate and institutional constituencies, including the Orthodox Church, provincial noble assemblies, zemstvo assemblies, universities and the Academy of Sciences, and organizations of commerce and industry. Both chambers possessed equal rights in legislation. A bill required the approval of the Duma and the State Council and the sanction of the emperor. This was a substantial departure from the earlier system, in which the emperor legislated on the advice of a purely consultative body. It meant that, for the first time, ordinary legislation could be blocked by an elected assembly.

At the same time, the Fundamental Laws reaffirmed that supreme power belonged to the emperor. The text retained the phrase "autocratic" in describing imperial authority but dropped the word "unlimited" that had appeared in the earlier version, a change on which commentators placed great weight. The emperor retained the exclusive right to initiate revisions of the Fundamental Laws themselves; the Duma could not amend the constitution. He controlled foreign policy, including the declaration of war and the making of peace and treaties; he was supreme commander of the armed forces and exercised control over military organization; he appointed and dismissed ministers, who were responsible to him and not to the Duma; and he had the right to dissolve the Duma and to set the date for new elections. Parts of the budget, including funds for the imperial court and the servicing of the public debt, were placed beyond the Duma's control, and if the budget was not approved in time the previous year's figures remained in force. Military expenditures, though debated, were in practice difficult for the Duma to reduce.

The most important device of executive authority was Article 87. When the Duma was not in session, the Council of Ministers could ask the emperor to issue emergency decrees that had the force of law, provided that they did not alter the Fundamental Laws or the rules of the Duma and State Council or the electoral law. Such decrees had to be submitted to the legislature within two months of the Duma's reconvening, and if they were not approved they lapsed. In theory this was a safeguard for genuine emergencies. In practice, as Section 47.5 and Section 47.7 show, it allowed the government to enact major policy, including the agrarian reform of November 1906 and the field courts-martial of August 1906, during recesses and, where necessary, to schedule recesses with that purpose in mind.

The legislature's power of scrutiny over the executive was real but limited. Deputies could submit interpellations, formal questions demanding that ministers explain administrative actions; the ministers were obliged to answer or to explain why they would not. A debate on a ministerial reply could embarrass the government and gain publicity. But the Duma could not dismiss a minister or force a change of cabinet. Parliamentary control over the executive, in the sense known in Britain or France, was absent.

Civil liberties were another area of ambiguity. The October Manifesto had promised freedom of conscience, speech, assembly, and association, and subsequent temporary regulations in 1905–1906 legalized trade unions, abolished preliminary censorship of most periodicals, and allowed political parties to form. Yet these regulations were hedged with exceptions. The emergency statutes of 1881 stayed in force in many provinces; governors could close newspapers and fine editors; unions could be closed by administrative order; and a permissive law of association did not prevent police harassment. In other words, the same text could be read as a charter of rights or a set of conditional concessions, depending on how it was administered. This gap between written law and administrative practice is a central evidentiary problem for historians of the period.

Historians have therefore disagreed about classification. One tradition, associated with liberal scholars and many Western historians of the later twentieth century, saw 1906 as the beginning of a constitutional order whose development was cut short. A second tradition, influential in Soviet writing and among many political opponents of the regime, treated the Duma as a decorative arrangement in a state whose real character had not changed. A third, more recent line of interpretation describes the system as a hybrid: neither parliamentary nor merely sham, an experiment in which institutions could harden into precedent or be hollowed out depending on political choices. This chapter keeps all three possibilities in view.

The question raised at the start of this section remains central: how could the same constitutional settlement both restrict autocracy and preserve it? The answer is that the Fundamental Laws did both by dividing authority in ways that neither side believed to be settled. The legislature had real veto power over ordinary laws, but the executive controlled appointment, foreign policy, the army, constitutional amendment, emergency legislation, and the timing of dissolution. Whether the Duma's veto would grow into broader control or would be contained by the executive was a matter of political struggle, not of text alone.

Historical Vocabulary
Article 87

Definition: A provision of the Fundamental Laws of 1906 permitting the emperor, on the proposal of the Council of Ministers, to issue emergency decrees with the force of law while the Duma was not in session, provided that the measure did not change the Fundamental Laws or the electoral law and was submitted to the legislature within two months of its reconvening.

Historical context: Article 87 was used in 1906 to introduce field courts-martial and to launch the first stage of the agrarian reform, and it was invoked again in March 1911 to bypass the State Council in the western zemstvo dispute. Critics complained that governments arranged parliamentary recesses to enable its use.

Why it matters: Article 87 shows how a constitutional text can recognize a legislature while keeping a reserve of executive lawmaking. It is a key piece of evidence in debates over whether the post-1905 order was constitutional or autocratic. It should not be confused with the 3 June 1907 change to the electoral law, which was not issued under Article 87 at all but by imperial manifesto, which is why opponents regarded it as unlawful.

Primary Source Window

The Fundamental Laws of 23 April 1906 (O.S.)

Creator: Imperial government, drafted by officials and jurists and issued in the name of Emperor Nicholas II · Date: 23 April 1906 (6 May N.S.) · Type: Constitutional law text

Context: The revised Fundamental Laws were issued after the October Manifesto and before the First Duma convened. They were not debated by the Duma, which had not yet met. Their authors sought to define a stable relationship between the emperor and the new institutions at a time when the revolutionary movement had been defeated in the cities but the countryside remained disturbed.

Paraphrase of two key provisions (not a verbatim translated quotation; students should consult an authoritative published translation):

One provision affirms that the emperor possesses supreme autocratic power and that obedience to this authority is a duty of conscience. Another provides that no new law may take effect without the approval of both the State Council and the State Duma, and that every law requires the emperor's sanction.

Analysis: Read together, these provisions state the contradiction of the period. The first reflects a conception of monarchy rooted in religious and dynastic tradition; the second subjects ordinary legislation to representative institutions. The source reveals what the government was willing to put in law and what it chose to preserve. It does not reveal how the provisions operated in practice, which requires evidence from Duma debates, ministerial correspondence, and administrative records. It also represents the perspective of the state; the views of deputies, party leaders, workers, peasants, and national minorities appear only indirectly, as responses to the text. Historians use it to ask whether the word "autocratic" retained substantive meaning after the introduction of mandatory legislative approval, and whether the removal of the word "unlimited" was a real concession or a rhetorical adjustment. It cannot by itself show how these questions were answered in politics.

  1. What does the source say about the emperor's authority, and what does it say about the legislature's authority?
  2. Who created it, when, and for what possible purpose?
  3. What can historians learn from it about how the regime understood the post-1905 settlement?
  4. What additional evidence, such as Duma debates or police records, would help evaluate how the laws worked in practice?
Section 47.2

Political Parties and Electoral Politics

Legal political parties were a novelty in the empire. Before 1905, political organizations had been illegal or semi-legal: the Marxist and populist parties operated underground or in exile, while liberals met in zemstvo gatherings and professional unions. The October Manifesto and subsequent regulations allowed parties to form, publish newspapers, and contest elections. The result was a sudden and varied political spectrum. These parties were not simply Western models transplanted; they differed from each other and from Western analogues in social base, organization, and attitude toward the existing order.

The Constitutional Democratic Party, known as the Kadets (from the Russian initials), was founded in October 1905. Its leaders included professors, lawyers, zemstvo figures, and publicists, notably Pavel Milyukov, Vladimir Nabokov, and Ivan Petrunkevich. Its program called for a constitutional monarchy with a government responsible to the legislature, civil rights, universal suffrage, and land reform that would transfer land to peasants with compensation to owners at fair prices where state or private lands were expropriated. Kadet strength lay in the educated urban and provincial middle classes, and the party saw itself as the principal parliamentary opposition. It also contained significant internal divisions over tactics and over how far to cooperate with revolutionaries.

The Union of 17 October, known as the Octobrists, took its name from the manifesto and regarded it as a sufficient basis for constitutional change. Alexander Guchkov, a Moscow industrialist's son and activist, became its most prominent leader. Octobrists drew support from landowners, businessmen, and moderate zemstvo figures. They supported a strong state, the preservation of the monarchy, and gradual reform, and they were willing to cooperate with the government when its policies were reformist. They were often described as the party of the "constitutional order" that existed rather than the one that opponents wished to create.

To the right stood a range of monarchist and conservative organizations, including the Union of the Russian People, led by Alexander Dubrovin, and, among Duma politicians, figures such as Vladimir Purishkevich and Nikolai Markov. These groups defended autocracy, Orthodoxy, and Russian national primacy, and some engaged in antisemitic agitation and violence. Their relationship to the government was complex: officials sometimes tolerated or subsidized them, while others considered their methods a liability. More moderate nationalist groups, which gained influence from 1909, sought to use the Duma to promote Russian national interests in the western borderlands while accepting a constitutional framework.

On the left, the Socialist Revolutionaries (SRs) drew on the populist tradition and claimed to represent peasants as well as workers; they favored the socialization of land and had a combat organization that conducted assassinations. The Russian Social Democratic Labor Party, divided since 1903 into Bolshevik and Menshevik factions, aimed at a Marxist revolution but disagreed about tactics, the role of the bourgeoisie, and participation in elections. The Socialist Revolutionary leadership and much of the Social Democratic movement boycotted the First Duma elections, although the boycott was incomplete; both movements participated more fully in the elections of 1907, with varying degrees of enthusiasm. The Trudoviks, or Labor Group, emerged from peasant and radical-democratic circles and advocated land redistribution and democratic reform without adopting a fully socialist program.

National and regional parties and groupings added further complexity. The Polish deputies, organized in the Polish Circle (Koło), pursued autonomy and the protection of Polish interests, although they were divided between conservatives and nationalists of the National Democratic party. Ukrainian deputies in the First and Second Dumas formed a short-lived Ukrainian group. Muslim political organization, notably the All-Russian Muslim Union (Ittifaq), brought together Tatar, Azerbaijani, Crimean, and Central Asian figures, many of them concerned with religious freedom, education, and the status of Muslims in a Christian-dominated empire. Jewish parties and the Bund promoted civil equality and national cultural rights in the context of the Pale of Settlement and legal restrictions discussed in Chapter 45. These groups did not fit a simple left-right axis.

The electoral system shaped what these parties could achieve. Elections to the Duma were indirect and based on curiae, or classes of voters. Under the law of 11 December 1905, voters were assigned to curiae of landowners, urban property-holders and taxpayers, peasants, and workers in larger industrial enterprises. In most provinces voters in each curia chose electors, who then met in provincial assemblies to choose deputies. The system gave unequal weight to different groups; commonly cited calculations suggest that a landowner's vote counted for many times that of a peasant, and that urban and worker votes were weighted differently again. The exact ratios varied by region, and any single figure should be treated as an illustration rather than a universal rule. Women, soldiers, many young men, nomads in some regions, and other groups were excluded. Special rules applied to Poland, the Caucasus, Siberia, and Central Asia, where representation was reduced or modified.

Students should keep three points in mind. First, indirect voting created opportunities for administrative influence and for the moderation of results, because electors in provincial assemblies could be pressured or swayed. Second, the peasantry, though underrepresented in proportion to its population, had enough weight in the first two Dumas to make peasant deputies a major bloc, which is partly why the land question became so politically explosive. Third, the system was not static: the government's ability to change the electoral law in 1907 meant that the very rules of representation were part of the political struggle.

Historical Vocabulary
Curia

Definition: A class or category of voters who chose electors separately from other categories. In Russian Duma elections, the principal curiae were landowners, urban voters, peasants, and, in industrial centers, workers.

Historical context: The curial system was used in the elections of 1906 and 1907 and, in a revised form favoring landowners and wealthier urban voters, in the elections of 1907, 1912, and later. It reflected an assumption, common in nineteenth-century European politics, that different social estates or classes should be represented as separate categories rather than as equal individual voters.

Why it matters: The curial structure helps explain why Dumas differed so much in composition and why changes to the electoral law could produce a much more conservative chamber without altering the formal powers of the Duma itself. It should not be confused with modern one-person, one-vote systems.

Section 47.3

The First Duma

Elections to the First Duma took place in March and April 1906. The Socialist Revolutionary leadership and much of the Social Democratic movement boycotted the elections, arguing that participation would legitimate a manipulated constitutional façade, although the boycott was incomplete. This left much of the field to the Kadets and to non-party and peasant candidates. The Kadets emerged as the largest party, with roughly 180 of about 500 deputies. A bloc of peasant deputies and radical democrats, many of them associated with the Trudovik group, numbered around one hundred. Smaller groups included Poles and other national representatives, a limited number of Octobrists, and a substantial number of non-party deputies. Peasants made up a large share of the Duma by occupation, perhaps a third or more of the membership. The right-wing parties won few seats. The result was a chamber far to the left of what the government had expected.

The opening on 27 April set the tone. The deputies had hoped that the emperor's speech would acknowledge the Duma as a partner. Nicholas's remarks were brief, referring to the new institution in terms of loyal service. The Duma's formal reply, the address to the throne drafted by Kadet and Trudovik deputies, called for a ministry responsible to the Duma, a political amnesty, the abolition of the death penalty, guarantees of civil liberties, and, above all, a program of land reform involving compulsory alienation of private estates. The ministry, led by Ivan Goremykin, refused to accept the Duma's demands. Goremykin reportedly regarded the Duma as an unwelcome obstacle; his government declared the proposals for compulsory expropriation to be inadmissible and said that the Duma lacked the right to demand a change of ministry. The emperor was not willing to accept responsible ministry because he believed it contradicted his sovereign authority.

The land question dominated debate. Peasant deputies spoke of the hunger for land in their villages and of the failure of the emancipation to provide sufficient allotments, themes familiar from Chapter 42. Kadet leaders proposed compensation to owners, while Trudoviks pressed for more radical redistribution. The government, in contrast, defended private landownership and proposed only modest measures such as increased credit for peasants. The Duma and the government could not agree on a framework. Nor was there agreement on civil liberties: deputies demanded that the ministry stop using emergency powers, interpellated ministers about pogroms and police violence, and called for investigation of the pogrom in Białystok in June 1906. Such debates were reported in newspapers across the empire and gave the Duma a public voice far beyond its formal powers.

The crisis was ultimately about sovereignty. Many liberals believed that the October Manifesto had inaugurated a constitutional regime in which the elected legislature represented the nation and ministers should answer to it. Nicholas believed that he had voluntarily granted a limited form of consultation and legislative participation while retaining sovereign authority. These positions were incompatible, and neither side was prepared to abandon its reading. The government also had a calculation: if the Duma could be portrayed as radical and irresponsible, its dissolution would find support among moderate and conservative groups. After some ten weeks, on 8 July 1906 (O.S.), the emperor dissolved the Duma by decree. On the same day he replaced Goremykin with Stolypin as chairman of the Council of Ministers. The government justified the dissolution by saying that the Duma had exceeded its mandate and incited unrest, particularly by appealing directly to the population on the land question.

A large group of deputies, mostly Kadets and Trudoviks, then traveled to Vyborg, in the Grand Duchy of Finland, where the Russian police had less direct authority. There they issued the Vyborg Manifesto, which called on the population to refuse to pay taxes and to resist military conscription until the Duma was reconvened. The appeal produced little practical response; the population did not rise, and troops and police remained under control. The government prosecuted the signatories, and those convicted were barred from standing for the Duma in subsequent elections. The affair deprived the Kadet party of some of its most prominent leaders for the next Duma and illustrated the limits of legalistic opposition when the executive was willing to disregard the expectations of its critics. It also suggested that the opposition lacked a strategy for forcing change once the Duma had been dissolved, and that the population at large was not ready to follow a call for passive resistance.

The First Duma's significance does not lie in its legislation, since it passed none of consequence. It lies in what it revealed: that a broadly but unequally elected national assembly in Russia would press for sweeping reform, that the ministry would not accept parliamentary responsibility, and that the emperor would use his dissolution power rather than compromise on sovereignty. Some historians argue that a different course, such as bringing Kadet leaders into the government in 1906, might have led to a more genuine constitutional order; negotiations with Kadet figures did occur but failed over the land question and ministerial responsibility. Others believe that the gap between the Duma's expectations and the monarch's convictions was too wide to bridge. The question of contingency here is real, and students should evaluate the evidence rather than assume the outcome was inevitable.

Section 47.4

The Second Duma and the Coup of 3 June 1907

Elections to the Second Duma occurred in January and February 1907 in an atmosphere shaped by field courts-martial, arrests, and administrative pressure on opposition candidates. This time the Social Democrats and Socialist Revolutionaries participated, convinced that the Duma could be used as a platform. The result was a chamber more polarized than the first. The Kadets lost seats, falling to roughly one hundred, while socialist and Trudovik deputies together held a large share of the seats, with Social Democrats numbering around sixty and Socialist Revolutionaries around thirty-five. The right and moderate conservative groups also gained. The chamber convened on 20 February 1907 (O.S.), and its mood combined radical rhetoric with a recognition that the government might dissolve it at any time.

Stolypin's strategy toward the Second Duma has been debated. He presented a program of reforms, including measures on civil equality for peasants, local government, religious toleration, and agrarian change, indicating a desire to demonstrate that the government was willing to legislate. Yet the Duma's left wing, particularly the Social Democrats, used the platform to attack the government and to proclaim revolutionary aims, and many Kadets and Trudoviks were reluctant to condemn revolutionary violence. The majority of deputies were thus unwilling to give the government the cooperation it sought, and the government was unwilling to accept the Duma's demands, especially on the land question. Some historians argue that Stolypin intended from the beginning to provoke a dissolution and to change the electoral law; others believe that he would have preferred a workable Duma but that the Duma's composition and the pressure of conservative circles led him to act.

In May and June 1907 the police claimed to have uncovered a plot involving Social Democratic deputies and a group of soldiers in the St. Petersburg garrison. The government demanded that the Duma remove fifty-five Social Democratic deputies from the chamber and lift the immunity of sixteen of them for arrest. The Duma set up a committee to examine the evidence rather than comply immediately. Before it could respond, the emperor dissolved the Duma on 3 June 1907 (O.S.; 16 June N.S.) and issued a new electoral law the same day. The evidence presented against the deputies was thin, and many historians regard the affair as at least partly provoked or exploited by the police and the government; others stress that the SD party did maintain contacts with soldiers' groups. Social Democratic deputies were arrested and tried, and many received sentences of hard labor or exile.

The new electoral law was the crux. According to Article 86 of the Fundamental Laws, no law could take effect without the approval of the State Council and Duma. The Fundamental Laws also gave the emperor the exclusive right to initiate revisions of the Fundamental Laws, but the electoral law was an ordinary statute rather than part of that code. Issuing it by manifesto without the legislature's approval therefore violated the procedural rules the government itself had established in 1906. For this reason, opponents called the act a coup d'état (in Russian, gosudarstvennyi perevorot), and the date 3 June became shorthand for the government's willingness to alter the rules to secure the desired political result. Stolypin and his supporters defended the measure by saying that the Duma's refusal to cooperate and its alleged links to revolution created an emergency, and by arguing that only a more reliable electorate could make the legislature function.

The new law drastically changed representation. It raised the weight of landowners and wealthier urban voters, reduced the representation of peasants and workers, cut the number of deputies from Poland, the Caucasus, and Siberia, and removed representation for Central Asia and the Kirgiz (Kazakh) steppe regions altogether. Muslim representation fell substantially. The details are discussed further in Sections 47.9 and the Regional Context feature. For now it is enough to note the implication: the government could, without altering the formal powers of the Duma, change its composition by changing who elected it.

Was 3 June the end of the constitutional experiment, or the beginning of a more workable but less democratic constitutional order? Historians have given different answers. Those who emphasize the coup view the act as an illegal restoration of autocratic control and a rejection of the 1905 principle that the legislature represented the population. Those who emphasize the system's functioning point out that the Third Duma went on to serve a full term, to debate budgets, and to pass legislation, and that the monarchy did not abolish the Duma. The two views are not mutually exclusive: the law of 3 June demonstrated that the constitutional order depended on the executive's acceptance of the legislature's composition, and it also enabled a period of political stability that would not have occurred if the second Duma's conflict had continued. The tension between legality and practicality is the central feature of the period.

Historical Debate

Did 1905 End Autocracy?

The question: After the October Manifesto, the Fundamental Laws, and the creation of the State Duma, should the Russian Empire be classified as an autocracy, a constitutional monarchy, or something else?

Interpretation A — A constitutional monarchy was emerging: Some historians stress that after 1906 the emperor could no longer legislate in ordinary matters without the consent of two chambers; that the Duma debated budgets, interpellated ministers, and passed laws; that parties, trade unions, and a freer press became part of political life; and that the Duma's institutional presence created expectations that could not easily be reversed. In this view, the system was unfinished and the conflicts of the period were part of a normal process of constitutional development.
Interpretation B — A modified autocracy: Others emphasize that ministers were responsible to the emperor alone; that Article 87 allowed executive lawmaking; that the emperor controlled the army, foreign policy, and constitutional revision; that the electoral law was changed by decree in 1907; and that civil liberties were repeatedly curtailed by administrative action. In this view, the Duma was a consultative and legislative instrument that the regime used while preserving its essential power.
Interpretation C — A hybrid system whose character was not fixed: A third approach treats the post-1905 regime as a hybrid in which constitutional and autocratic elements coexisted, and in which the practical outcome depended on contingent political choices and on how far each side accepted the new rules. This approach asks not which label is correct in the abstract but how the system changed between 1906 and 1914.

Evidence: Historians examine the text of the Fundamental Laws; the number and kind of laws passed by the Duma and State Council; the use of Article 87; stenographic records of Duma debates; ministerial and imperial correspondence, including Nicholas II's private letters and diaries; police records; press censorship rulings; and electoral statistics.

Points of agreement: Most historians agree that the system of 1906–1914 was different from the pre-1905 order, that the Duma was more than a mere façade, and that the emperor and ministers retained very extensive powers.

Points of disagreement: Historians differ about whether the system was moving toward parliamentary government, whether the emperor's personal convictions or structural features of the regime did more to limit change, and how much weight to give to the manipulation of elections.

Why it matters: The classification affects how historians judge the political options open to Russians in 1914, the responsibility of different actors for the crisis of 1917, and comparisons with other constitutional experiments.

Student task: Select one piece of evidence, such as the Fundamental Laws, the electoral law of 1907, or the Third Duma's legislative record, and explain which interpretation it best supports and which it complicates.

Section 47.5

Stolypin as Prime Minister

Pyotr Arkadievich Stolypin was born in 1862 into a landed noble family with extensive connections. He studied natural sciences at St. Petersburg University and entered state service in the Ministry of State Domains and the Ministry of Internal Affairs. He served as marshal of the nobility in Kovno province and then as governor of Grodno (1902) and Saratov (1903–1906). In Saratov he confronted the peasant disturbances of 1905, combining direct personal appearances with the use of troops and, in the view of his admirers, an ability to restore order through personal authority. His performance attracted attention in St. Petersburg, and in April 1906 he was appointed minister of the interior in the Goremykin ministry. In July he also became chairman of the Council of Ministers.

Stolypin's political outlook is best understood as a form of conservative reformism. He accepted that the old order could not be restored unchanged. He believed that the monarchy needed a legislature to channel public opinion and a reformed countryside to stabilize society, but he also held that the state must retain strong authority and that order must precede reform. He regarded the revolutionary movement as a threat that required firm repression, and he argued that reform without order would fail. His view of the Duma was pragmatic: he sought a legislature that could be worked with and, if necessary, shaped by changes in the electoral system. His nationalism, which became more explicit after 1907, included support for Russian predominance in the western provinces and for policies that limited the autonomy of Finland and the political role of Poles and Jews, a stance that put him at odds with many liberals even as he opposed the extreme right.

His relationship with Nicholas II was unstable. The emperor valued Stolypin's success in restoring order and recognized his abilities, but he was uneasy about a strong minister who commanded public attention and sought to shape policy. Stolypin's attempts to introduce reforms in local government, religious toleration, and legal status of the peasants encountered resistance from the court, from the State Council, and from conservative nobles. The emperor also resented what he saw as pressure from the prime minister, and by 1911 Stolypin's position was weaker than outward appearances suggested. Historians have debated how secure he was: his dependence on the emperor's confidence was always the fundamental fact of his position, in a system in which ministers served at the monarch's pleasure.

His relationship with the Dumas varied. With the Second Duma he sought a program of reform while preparing a change in the electoral law. With the Third Duma he worked closely with the Octobrist majority and promoted legislation on peasant land tenure, local courts, and military and naval funding. The Duma debates of this period gave him opportunities for notable speeches. One frequently cited line, attributed to him in a speech of 1907 and often quoted in paraphrase, held that opponents wanted great upheavals while the state needed a great Russia. Another remark, usually dated to 1909 and reported in the press, expressed the view that if the country were given a long period of peace, its transformation would be unrecognizable. Because the precise wording of such sayings varies by source, students should treat them as paraphrases of political claims rather than as verified quotations, and should remember that they were public statements designed to build support.

A broader reform agenda existed alongside the agrarian program. The government broadened peasants' civil and legal rights in October 1906 by removing a range of estate-based disabilities, including restrictions involving residence and passports, access to state service and education, and communal obligations tied to peasant status. Proposals to reform local administration, to create a smaller rural zemstvo unit, to reform police structures, and to extend religious toleration, including legal recognition of Old Believers and other dissenters, were put forward, although many ran into obstacles in the State Council or from the court. Legislation on insurance for workers against sickness and accident was passed in 1912. Education also advanced in this period: the Third Duma expanded primary-school funding beginning in 1908 and later approved a broader universal-primary-education bill in 1911, although disagreement with the State Council prevented the full measure from becoming law. These measures showed that the government did not rely only on coercion.

The most notable political crisis of Stolypin's tenure occurred in 1911 over the western zemstvo bill. The bill extended elected zemstvo institutions to nine western provinces, where the Polish-dominated landowning elite was to be balanced by new electoral arrangements favoring Russian and Ukrainian peasants and landowners. The Duma approved it, but in the State Council the measure was defeated by a coalition of conservative nobles, including figures close to the court, who disliked its curial system or opposed Stolypin personally. Stolypin offered his resignation. The emperor refused it and, at Stolypin's urging, prorogued both chambers for three days in March 1911 so that the bill could be enacted under Article 87. The action was legal under the letter of the law but widely viewed as a misuse of it. Guchkov resigned as chairman of the Duma in protest, many Octobrists felt betrayed, and Stolypin's support from both the right and the center weakened.

Stolypin faced violence throughout his tenure. On 12 August 1906 (O.S.), a bomb exploded at his dacha on Aptekarsky Island in St. Petersburg, killing and wounding dozens of people, among them members of his household, though he survived. He was the target of other plots as well, many of them traced to revolutionary organizations. On 1 September 1911, while attending a performance at the Kyiv Opera House in the presence of the emperor, he was shot by Dmitry Bogrov, a young man who was both an anarchist or revolutionary sympathizer and an informant of the Kyiv Okhrana. Stolypin died on 5 September. Bogrov was tried and executed within days. The events raised questions about the security police's role and negligence, which a senatorial investigation did not resolve to the satisfaction of critics; no authoritative evidence has established an official conspiracy, but the case remains a notorious example of the opaque relationship between the revolutionary underground and the police.

Was Stolypin attempting to preserve autocracy, construct a conservative constitutional order, modernize Russia, or all three? The evidence supports a combination. He wanted to preserve the monarchy and the empire; he accepted a legislature and legal institutions as part of a stable monarchy; and he pursued economic and social modernization, especially in the countryside. What he does not seem to have wanted was a government accountable to the Duma, or a society in which the political power of the traditional elite and the monarchy was reduced. His fall in influence before his death and the difficulty his successors had in continuing his policies illustrate that his program depended on a particular alignment of forces, including the emperor's support, that proved fragile.

Section 47.6

Repression and the Restoration of Order

The restoration of order after 1905 involved both coercion and the removal of the immediate causes of mass revolt. Repression was not an incidental accompaniment of Stolypin's reforms; it was a major historical process in its own right, and its scale and methods shaped the political culture of the period. The government faced real violence: revolutionary groups, including the Socialist Revolutionaries' combat organization, anarchists, and local militant groups, carried out assassinations, bombings, and armed robberies, often called expropriations. In 1905–1907, thousands of officials, police, and private individuals were killed or wounded in political attacks, with estimates varying according to what was counted. Many attacks targeted police and local officials, but others killed bystanders or were criminal in character.

The state's response was extensive. The most notorious measure was the introduction of field courts-martial by an Article 87 decree of 19 August 1906 (O.S.). These tribunals, composed of military officers, could try civilians accused of armed robbery, murder, and attacks on officials when guilt was considered evident. Trials were to begin within twenty-four hours of the crime and be concluded within a few days, defendants had limited rights of defense, and sentences, principally death, were to be carried out within twenty-four hours with no appeal. The decree was not submitted to the Duma for approval and lapsed in April 1907 after the Second Duma convened, but the practice of death sentences by ordinary military courts continued. Estimates of the number of executions in this period vary, in part because categories and sources differ; the field courts-martial carried out about 680 executions during their eight months of operation, while broader totals for executions under military and other courts during 1906–1909 run into the low thousands and vary according to source and category. Students should treat any single figure with caution and note the range.

Hanging became the characteristic method of execution, and in November 1907 the Kadet deputy Fyodor Rodichev is usually credited with using the phrase "Stolypin's necktie" in the Duma. The phrase was a hostile political expression referring to the gallows. It entered popular language and was later used by revolutionary and liberal critics as shorthand for the prime minister's policies. It is important evidence of how opponents perceived the regime, but it was a polemical label and not a neutral description. Stolypin challenged Rodichev to a duel, and the deputy retracted the words, a small illustration of the political atmosphere.

Beyond executions, the state used imprisonment, katorga (hard labor), exile, and administrative banishment on a large scale. Prisons were crowded, and thousands of political prisoners were sent to Siberia. Punitive expeditions in the Baltic provinces, where revolutionary movements among Latvian and Estonian peasants and workers had been particularly strong, produced numerous executions and mass arrests, and many activists fled abroad. Similar measures were used in the Caucasus, Poland, and the western regions, and they interacted with local national tensions. The police expanded their surveillance and infiltration of revolutionary groups; the Okhrana used informants, including highly placed ones, whose activities sometimes complicated the distinction between police and revolutionaries. The discovery in 1908 that Yevno Azef, the head of the Socialist Revolutionary combat organization, had been a police agent, shocked the revolutionary movement and caused deep distrust.

The state also restricted the legal freedoms promised in 1905. Provincial governors used emergency statutes to ban meetings, close newspapers, fine editors, and dissolve trade unions and societies. Universities were placed under stricter control after a brief period of autonomy. Many trade unions were forced to close or operate under severe constraints. The authorities did not abolish the new legal framework, however; they limited its use. The result was a more constrained but still partly open public sphere, in which newspapers continued to publish, associations continued to exist, and the Duma allowed a degree of political expression.

The effects of repression are debated. There is a general agreement that revolutionary organizations were severely weakened by 1908–1909, that the number of terrorist acts fell sharply, that strike activity declined, and that peasant disturbances subsided. Historians differ on how much credit to assign to repression, how much to changing circumstances (including exhaustion after the revolutionary years, improved harvests, and economic recovery), and how much to the government's concessions and reforms. They also disagree about the long-term costs. Some argue that harsh repression discredited the regime among educated Russians and radicalized opponents, while others note that the political stability of 1908–1911 gave the state a margin in which reforms could develop.

This section should avoid two extremes. Stolypin was not merely a liberal reformer who regrettably had to use force; he personally oversaw and defended an apparatus of extraordinary justice that many contemporaries and later historians have condemned as unlawful and brutal. Nor was he merely an executioner; he was also a politician who pursued legislative and economic reform and who believed that repression alone could not stabilize Russia. The chapter's central point is that repression and reform occurred together, and that the combination was a deliberate governing strategy, not a contradiction that the regime failed to notice.

Section 47.7

Stolypin's Agrarian Reforms

The peasantry was the largest social group in the empire and the source of the most alarming threat to the state in 1905–1906, when peasant disturbances, including the burning of manor houses and seizures of land and timber, spread across many provinces. The government's agrarian policy therefore addressed both economic and political concerns. To understand it, students must first understand the rural institutions the reform sought to change.

The village commune (obshchina or mir) was, in much of European Russia, the legal and administrative unit of peasant life. After the emancipation of 1861 (Chapter 42), land allotted to former serfs was in many regions held collectively by the commune, which periodically redistributed strips among households according to household size or labor capacity, was collectively responsible for taxes and redemption payments, and regulated agricultural practices such as crop rotation and grazing. Individual households typically held land in numerous scattered strips, which made consolidation or innovation difficult. In some regions, particularly in the western provinces and parts of the south, hereditary household tenure was already common, and the commune's functions varied widely. The reform thus addressed a system that was not uniform.

Several problems were connected. Rural population growth was rapid, and allotment sizes per person had declined since 1861 in many areas, producing what contemporaries called land hunger. Agricultural productivity was low compared with western Europe, though it varied by region and had been rising. The commune restricted the departure of members, which the government feared impeded labor mobility and agricultural improvement. Redemption payments had burdened peasant finances, and in the revolutionary crisis the government canceled them in stages from 1905, with full abolition from 1 January 1907. The state also saw the commune as a source of collectivist habits and of politically explosive solidarity, although some officials, including conservatives, regarded it as a bulwark of social stability. The reform debate therefore reflected disagreement about whether the commune was an obstacle to progress, a protection for peasants, or both.

The principal legal act was the decree of 9 November 1906 (O.S.), issued under Article 87 and later approved by the Third Duma and State Council in modified form (the law of 14 June 1910), with further legislation in 1911. It gave each household head in a commune that practiced periodic redistribution the right to demand that his allotment be fixed as his personal property, and it allowed him to withdraw from the commune with his land. Further provisions allowed the household to demand consolidation of scattered strips into a single holding. Households could consolidate land in the village, forming an otrub, or move their dwelling onto the consolidated land, forming a khutor. Where communal redistribution had not occurred for many years, the land was automatically treated as held in hereditary household tenure. Land settlement commissions, staffed with surveyors and officials, mediated between households and the commune and implemented the consolidation.

The state supported these changes with other measures. The Peasant Land Bank, established in 1882, expanded its lending in 1906 to help peasants, especially those departing from the commune, purchase land, including land from noble estates that were being sold, and it received additional state support. The government supported agricultural improvement through agronomists, credit cooperatives, and educational programs. It encouraged the resettlement of peasants from land-short regions of European Russia to Siberia and parts of Central Asia, with financial aid for travel and settlement and with a Resettlement Administration to organize land allocation. The head of the Main Administration of Land Organization and Agriculture, Alexander Krivoshein, played a major role in the program, and Stolypin visited Siberia in 1910 to promote it.

The government's goals were multiple. Economically, it sought to increase agricultural productivity by encouraging individual proprietors to invest in improvements. Socially and politically, it aimed to create a class of prosperous, property-owning peasants who would support the existing order and serve as a counterweight to radicalism; a phrase usually attributed to Stolypin summarized the idea as relying on the strong and prosperous rather than on the weak and poor. Politically, it also sought to avoid the more radical alternative of expropriating noble estates, which the Duma's left and many peasant deputies demanded. It was thus a policy designed to resolve the land question in a way that preserved the noble landowners' property and the political order.

Students should avoid saying that Stolypin simply intended to destroy the commune. His goal was more complex. He sought to give peasants the option of individual proprietorship and to create conditions in which a stratum of stable farmers could emerge, while recognizing that the process would be gradual. The measure's voluntary form, which allowed but did not compel withdrawal, reflected this. Some of his officials and later enforcement practices, however, pressured communes and peasants in ways that went beyond voluntarism, and the extent to which pressure rather than peasant choice drove consolidation is a matter of historical debate.

Historical Vocabulary
Khutor and Otrub

Definition: Both terms refer to forms of consolidated peasant landholding created or encouraged by the reforms of 1906–1911. A khutor was a consolidated farm on which the household moved its dwelling onto its own land, outside the village. An otrub was a consolidated holding whose owner continued to live in the village.

Historical context: The terms were used in the legislation and by land-settlement officials. Khutors were more common in western regions where individual farmsteads had earlier precedents; otrubs were more common in central and southern regions where peasants preferred to remain in villages.

Why it matters: The distinction shows that consolidation could take different forms and that peasants adapted state policy to local needs. It also warns students against equating the reform with a single outcome, such as the creation of isolated farmsteads.

Section 47.8

Peasant Responses, Resettlement, and the Limits of Agrarian Reform

A reform and its effects are different historical questions. The statutes tell what the state intended and what options it offered; peasants' decisions, local conditions, and economic circumstances determined what happened. Evidence for outcomes comes from statistics compiled by the Ministry of Agriculture and land settlement commissions, zemstvo surveys, reports of officials and agronomists, peasant petitions, and later studies by historians. Each has limitations: official statistics often counted applications or decisions rather than genuine changes in farming, and the categories used shifted over time.

The following pattern emerges from the evidence, though the figures are estimates and should be read as orders of magnitude. By 1915, roughly a quarter of the households in the communes of European Russia had formally applied for or acquired individual title to their allotments. However, a substantial share of those who obtained title did so without leaving the commune or consolidating their land; many acted to secure legal ownership of land they intended to sell or because they had left the village for work. The proportion of households that actually consolidated their holdings into separate farms was smaller, on the order of a tenth of all households in the affected regions. The pace of withdrawal peaked in 1908–1909 and declined thereafter, partly because the most eager applicants had already acted, and partly because resistance and administrative obstacles accumulated.

Regional variation was marked. Withdrawal and consolidation were more common in the western and southern provinces, where hereditary household tenure had precedents, where land was more productive, and where market integration was higher. In the central agricultural region and the north, where communal practices were strong, withdrawals were less frequent. Some communes resisted fiercely, using social pressure, denial of grazing rights, or threats. Others compromised, allowing individual consolidation while maintaining communal oversight of crops and pasture. Peasants who left the commune were not always the successful farmers the government expected: they included wealthier households with commercial interests, but also poor households that sold their land and left for towns or Siberia, and in some cases households that had lost their position in redistributions and sought a legal claim to their land.

Resettlement to Siberia and Central Asia was among the larger demographic movements of the period. Between 1906 and 1914 roughly three million people moved east of the Urals, with a significant share going to western Siberia and to the steppe regions. The state provided cheap rail tickets, loans, and land, and Siberian agriculture, especially dairying and grain, expanded; the export of Siberian butter to Europe grew significantly. Yet resettlement also had costs and limits. Many migrants faced inadequate preparations, disease, and difficult conditions; a notable proportion, perhaps a fifth or a quarter in some years, returned to their home villages. The migration also affected indigenous populations, including Kazakhs, Buryats, and other peoples, whose land and pasture were reallocated or restricted, a process discussed from the borderland perspective in the Regional Context feature. The Kazakh steppe, in particular, saw conflicts over land that contributed to tensions leading to the revolt of 1916.

Agricultural performance in the years 1909–1913 was generally strong. The grain harvest of 1913 was exceptionally good, and Russia remained among the world's leading grain exporters. Productivity per hectare increased in some regions, and the use of improved implements, such as steel plows and mechanical reapers, expanded. Cooperatives, including credit and dairy cooperatives, grew substantially. Historians debate how much of this growth should be attributed to Stolypin's reforms. Favorable weather, global grain prices, expanding railways, and market development all contributed, and improvements occurred in regions where the reforms had little effect. Some scholars regard the reforms as a significant stimulus; others see them as marginal to changes that were already under way.

The persistence of the commune and the continuing agrarian grievances are equally important. Most peasants in the central regions remained in communes in 1914. The land hunger that had driven the revolution of 1905 had not been resolved; the noble estates, which still owned a substantial share of arable land, remained a focus of resentment, and the population continued to grow. When the monarchy fell in 1917, peasants' demands for the redistribution of estates and the resurgence of communal institutions in the revolutionary years (Chapter 50) showed that the reform had not eliminated these attitudes. This does not prove that the reform was a failure; it indicates that its impact was incomplete by 1914.

Older histories sometimes imply that Stolypin was on the verge of creating a prosperous farming society before war interrupted him. That claim is too certain. The evidence shows a significant policy shift with some real results, an expansion of individual landholding and migration, and signs of agricultural dynamism, but also limited coverage, regional unevenness, and persistent communal structures. A better formulation is the question: had Stolypin's reforms begun a fundamental rural transformation by 1914, or had they only begun to alter a much larger and more persistent agrarian system? The answer remains evidence-based and contested. The reform's long-term trajectory is unknowable, because the war, revolution, and the Soviet policies of collectivization (Chapter 56) intervened.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Peasant Household Under the Reforms

For a peasant household in the central black-earth provinces, the reforms of 1906–1911 did not mean a sudden break. The household's work followed the agricultural calendar: plowing, sowing, haymaking, harvest, threshing, and winter crafts or seasonal labor. Its land consisted of strips in the commune's several fields, subject to a common rotation that dictated when fields were fallow and when livestock grazed them. The household head represented the family in the village assembly, which met to allocate land, assign tax shares, and settle disputes. Women were responsible for the household, garden, livestock, and, during harvest, field labor; men often left for seasonal work in towns or on distant estates.

When the new laws arrived, households reacted according to their circumstances. A household with several strong workers, some capital, and a desire to expand its output might see advantages in consolidating its land and escaping communal restrictions. A household with little land or many dependents might apply for title in order to sell and leave for a city or Siberia. A household that relied on the commune's protection in bad years, or whose neighbors would be disadvantaged by redistributions, might oppose withdrawals. Disputes between those who wished to consolidate and those who did not could be bitter: surveyors arrived, boundaries were marked, and local officials were often present. Reports from the period describe conflicts over grazing rights, damaged fences, and social ostracism of those who left.

Not all experiences were uniform. In the western provinces, where individual farmsteads had earlier precedents, moving to a khutor might be a normal option; in the central and northern regions, isolation from the village and its church, school, and mutual aid was a deterrent. Literacy and schooling were expanding, and young men who had served in the army or worked in factories brought new expectations. The evidence for peasants' own views is limited, since most sources were produced by officials, agronomists, and educated observers, and peasant voices are often mediated through petitions or surveys. Historians therefore need to read such sources critically and avoid assuming that officials accurately represented peasant motives.

Section 47.9

The Third Duma and the June Third System

The Third Duma, elected in autumn 1907 under the new law, convened on 1 November 1907 and served its full five-year term until June 1912, the only one of the four Dumas to do so. Its composition reflected the changed electoral system. Of about 440 deputies, the Octobrists formed the largest group, with roughly 150 seats, and conservative and nationalist deputies held a substantial additional share. The Kadets fell to around fifty seats; the Trudoviks, SRs, and Social Democrats together held a much smaller proportion than in the Second Duma. Landowners and urban property-holders dominated; peasants and workers had reduced representation. The Duma was thus a chamber in which the center-right, supported by moderate nationalists and, depending on the issue, by conservatives, could form a working majority.

The new electoral rules were the foundation of this outcome. The law of 3 June 1907 increased the weight of the landowners' curia, so that landowners and the wealthier urban voters together controlled a majority of electors in many provinces. It reduced the number of deputies for the empire's non-Russian regions: Poland's representation fell from thirty-six to fourteen, the Caucasus lost a large share, Central Asia and the steppe regions lost representation entirely, and Muslim representation was greatly diminished. Within the Russian provinces, the weight of peasants and workers was reduced. In some areas the law arranged separate sections of electors by nationality to favor Russians. The change was not a minor adjustment; it altered the political composition of the legislature while leaving the formal powers of the Duma intact.

In practice, this produced a Duma that cooperated with the government on many matters. The Octobrist leader Guchkov and others supported military reform and rearmament, partly because they favored an effective army and navy after the defeat of 1905. The Duma debated and approved budgets, increased funding for education, passed legislation on peasant land tenure that consolidated the agrarian reform, and approved measures on workers' insurance (1912), local courts, and other administrative matters. It scrutinized ministers through interpellations and sometimes reduced or delayed government proposals. In the military sphere, the Duma's attempts to reform the army's administration and to assert oversight led to conflicts with the emperor and the military establishment. In one well-known episode in 1909–1912, disputes over naval staff legislation and army commands revealed how the emperor guarded military prerogatives. The Duma's legislative role was therefore real in civilian domains and limited in military ones.

The Third Duma's record shows that representative government in this system was not simply theatrical. It legislated, funded, debated, and influenced policy. But it did so in a body whose electoral basis had been deliberately constructed to produce a conservative majority, and in a structure where the State Council, half appointed and dominated by conservative figures, could reject Duma bills. A considerable number of the Duma's measures, including proposals for religious toleration, local government reform, and expanded civil equality, were blocked or diluted in the State Council or by the court. The Third Duma's limits were therefore built into the system, not merely a result of personalities.

Cooperation also had boundaries because of nationalism. Many Octobrists and nationalists supported policies that favored Russian predominance in the borderlands: the Duma approved measures to restrict Finnish autonomy, supported the separation of the Kholm region from the Kingdom of Poland in 1912 to form a new province with a majority of Ukrainian Orthodox population, and endorsed policies that limited the rights of Jews and other minorities. These actions interacted with the electoral law's reduced representation of non-Russians and contributed to the alienation of minority groups from the constitutional order. Chapter 45 discusses the broader nationalities question. It is important not to treat this nationalism as a single phenomenon: it included defense of Russian interests, support for a unified imperial state, and sometimes antisemitic and anti-Polish attitudes.

The period 1907–1912 is often called the June Third system after the date of the electoral change. It combined a controlled legislature with a government that governed through a mix of cooperation and executive action. Political stability increased: strikes declined, revolutionary parties were weakened, harvests were good, the economy recovered, and the state's finances improved. For this reason, some historians see the period as a time when the regime found a workable formula. Others argue that the stability was shallow, because it rested on exclusion of large groups from representation and on repression, and because the emperor and conservative elites remained hostile to genuine parliamentary government. The ambiguity is central: the Third Duma proved that representative institutions could function in Russia, and it also showed how they could be limited by manipulating the electorate.

Regional Context

Borderlands and the Duma Monarchy

From the perspective of the empire's borderlands, the Duma monarchy offered both opportunities and disappointments. In Poland, the first two Dumas included a Polish Circle that pursued autonomy and protested martial-law conditions; after 1907, its representation was cut sharply, and Polish politics divided between those who sought accommodation with the imperial government and nationalists who looked to the possibility of future independence. In Finland, which had a distinct constitutional order as a Grand Duchy, the Russian government under Stolypin's tenure pursued measures to reduce Finnish autonomy, and the Finnish Diet and political elites resisted, arguing that the imperial legislation violated Finland's constitution. The Baltic provinces experienced harsh repression after 1905; German landowning elites, Latvian and Estonian peasants and workers, and Russian officials had conflicting interests, and Baltic deputies used the Duma to raise grievances.

In the Caucasus, Georgian Social Democrats, such as Nikolai Chkheidze, entered the Duma and became figures in all-Russian politics, while Armenian and Azerbaijani political organizations had their own priorities, shaped by oil-field labor, ethnic tension, and the recent violence of 1905. Muslim deputies in the first Dumas, including Tatars from the Volga region, pressed for religious and educational rights and for improved status for Muslims; after 1907, Muslim representation was greatly reduced, and the Duma became less accessible to them. In Central Asia and the Kazakh steppe, where imperial rule was recent and colonial in character, residents were excluded from the Duma after 1907, and the land policies of resettlement affected pastoral communities directly. Jewish communities, subject to legal restrictions on residence and occupation in the Pale of Settlement, continued to face discrimination; the Beilis trial of 1911–1913 in Kyiv, in which a Jewish worker was prosecuted on a ritual murder accusation and ultimately acquitted by the jury, showed how antisemitic charges could be used within the legal system and also how public opinion in Russia and abroad mobilized against them.

These regional experiences suggest that the Duma monarchy was not simply a Russian national political experiment but a system through which an empire of diverse peoples was governed. Its institutions operated differently in different places, and its restrictions fell most heavily on groups whose political demands the government was least willing to accommodate.

Multiple Perspectives

The Duma Monarchy as Experienced by Different Groups

A Kadet deputy and liberal professional: Liberal politicians saw the Duma as the beginning of a constitutional order and expected that ministerial responsibility would follow. The dissolutions of 1906 and 1907 and the law of 3 June frustrated these hopes and reduced the party's influence, but many Kadets continued to work within the system, hoping that legality and public opinion would eventually move the regime. Their sources are largely speeches, newspapers, and memoirs, and they often present their own strategy sympathetically.
An Octobrist landowner: For moderate landowners and businessmen, the Duma after 1907 offered a way to influence policy, secure budgets, and support military strength while preserving social order. Many welcomed the Third Duma, though some became uneasy when the government bypassed the legislature in 1911. Their position illustrates how the June Third system made the Duma attractive to propertied groups.
A peasant household: For rural families, the Duma's importance lay chiefly in the land question. Peasant deputies in the first two Dumas voiced demands for redistribution; after 1907, peasants' political voice in the Duma was reduced, and the government's agrarian policy offered individual ownership rather than estate expropriation. Evidence of peasants' own views is often mediated by officials and observers.
A socialist worker or party activist: Workers and socialist organizers experienced the period as a time of repression followed by gradual, restricted recovery. The legal framework allowed some unions, newspapers, and insurance funds, but police surveillance and prosecution were constant. Social Democratic deputies used the Duma as a platform even as party members debated the value of legal activity.
Nicholas II and conservative officials: The emperor and many conservative officials regarded the Duma as a concession that needed to be contained, and they were suspicious of ministers who seemed too closely tied to it. Nicholas's private correspondence and diaries indicate a firm belief in his sovereign responsibility, though historians differ about how rigid his position was.
A Polish, Muslim, or Jewish activist in the empire: For minority representatives, the system was shaped by reduced representation, nationalist legislation, and legal disabilities. Many engaged with the Duma to defend rights while recognizing its limits.

These perspectives show why the post-1905 system cannot be judged from a single vantage. Power, coercion, and exclusion shaped how each group experienced the same institutions.

Section 47.10

The Fourth Duma and the Revival of Opposition

Stolypin's death in September 1911 removed the figure around whom the government's strategy had been organized. His successor as chairman of the Council of Ministers was Vladimir Kokovtsov, previously minister of finance, a cautious and experienced administrator who was less politically ambitious and who held a different view of relations with the Duma, though he continued to defend the government's fiscal and economic policy. Kokovtsov lacked Stolypin's political profile, and the emperor, who was increasingly influenced by court circles, treated him with reserve. In January 1914, Kokovtsov was dismissed and replaced by the elderly Ivan Goremykin, a move that many in the Duma regarded as a sign that the court preferred a minister who would not seek to cooperate with the legislature.

Stolypin's death also altered the balance of the reform program. Some of his initiatives continued, notably the land settlement work and the resettlement administration, which were headed by officials who remained in post. Other reform plans, including those for local government and religious toleration, stalled. The emperor and the conservative majority of the State Council were not inclined to approve legislation that expanded civil equality or changed local institutions. The political momentum behind systematic reform slowed. This does not mean that policy became static, since measures on education, insurance, and military reorganization continued, but the sense that a coherent program was being pursued diminished.

The elections to the Fourth Duma, held in autumn 1912 under the same electoral law, reflected shifts in political opinion. The Octobrists lost seats, falling to under one hundred, partly because of disillusionment over the government's treatment of the legislature in 1911 and partly because of intensified activity by the right and by newer groupings. Right-wing and nationalist deputies increased their share, and a Progressist group, representing industrial and commercial circles and more liberal-minded landowners, emerged as a new center-left force. The Kadets had around fifty-odd seats. Social Democrats, divided between Bolshevik and Menshevik factions, held a small number of seats; their divisions grew sharper in 1913–1914, when the Bolshevik deputies formed a separate group. The Duma's chairman, Mikhail Rodzianko, an Octobrist landowner, supported a strong Duma role and often criticized the government.

Relations between the Fourth Duma and the ministry were marked by friction. The Duma used interpellations to raise cases of administrative abuse, press restrictions, and the handling of strikes. Conflicts occurred over the treatment of the press, over restrictions on the Duma's rights to question ministers, and over the government's rejection of proposals to enhance legislative oversight. The government also faced criticism from the right: some conservative circles wanted to reduce the Duma's role, and discussions in the court and the State Council in 1913–1914 considered turning it into a purely consultative body. Such proposals were not adopted, but their existence indicates the instability of the political settlement.

Public criticism was also stimulated by scandals and by the growing influence at court of the Siberian wanderer Grigory Rasputin, whose reputed intimacy with the imperial family, based on the belief that he could ease the hemophilia of the heir Alexei, became a subject of press commentary and Duma speeches even before the war. The Duma and the press were constrained from discussing the matter freely, but accusations of court influence on policy circulated widely. Such rumors, together with the perception that ministers served the court rather than the nation, eroded respect for the monarchy among sections of the elite. The wartime effects of this process belong to Chapter 48; here, students should note that the issue emerged before 1914.

A further source of tension was the persistent question of whether the Duma could expand its role over time. Octobrists and Progressists wanted to move toward a government that took account of the Duma's views and drew ministers from public figures; the emperor and many ministers were unwilling. This dispute became more acute because the Octobrists, who had been the government's main ally in the Third Duma, felt that their cooperation had not been rewarded. By 1913–1914 the political center was in an uncomfortable position: it had accepted the limits of the system but received few concessions, and its supporters were increasingly critical.

Students should be cautious about how to read this evidence. The revival of opposition was real, but it did not amount to a collapse of the political order in 1914. The Duma continued to meet, legislate, and approve budgets; the government maintained control of the administration and the armed forces; and political groups continued to operate within a legal framework. The period 1912–1914 was one of polarization and growing public criticism, not of institutional breakdown. This section stops at 1914, with a brief forward reference: the Duma's wartime role, its prorogation, and the formation of the Progressive Bloc in 1915 are discussed in Chapter 48.

Cause and Effect

The Stabilization of the Regime After 1905

Long-Term Conditions

A bureaucratic state with extensive police powers; rapid but uneven economic growth; a large peasantry with land grievances; an educated public seeking legal rights; a multinational empire with competing demands.

Intermediate Developments

Military defeat in 1904–1905 and revolutionary mobilization in 1905; concessions in the October Manifesto; division of the opposition between moderates and radicals; continued loyalty of most of the army; a foreign loan in 1906.

Short-Term Triggers

Defeat of the Moscow uprising (December 1905); issuance of the Fundamental Laws; dissolution of the First and Second Dumas; field courts-martial; the electoral law of 3 June 1907; the agrarian decree of November 1906.

Event or Transformation

Political stabilization under the June Third system and a limited, controlled constitutional order.

Immediate Consequences

Decline in terrorism and strikes, a cooperative Third Duma, expanded agrarian and educational programs, but reduced representation for peasants, workers, and national minorities.

Long-Term Consequences

A durable legislature and public sphere; a political center that felt unrewarded; unresolved questions of sovereignty and ministerial responsibility; renewed labor activism after 1910.

Historical complexity: No single cause explains stabilization. Repression alone does not explain why strikes subsided, since economic recovery and exhaustion also mattered; reform alone does not explain why revolutionary parties weakened. Nor does this sequence lead straight to 1917. It describes a political order that functioned for several years and encountered new difficulties, whose outcome depended on events after 1914.

Section 47.11

Labor Unrest, the Lena Goldfields, and the Return of Mass Politics

Without attention to labor, students might conclude that Russia became steadily calmer between 1907 and 1914. That conclusion would be wrong. Industrial workers, whose numbers were growing, experienced the post-1905 period as one of repression followed by gradual recovery of organization and protest. Chapter 44 described the formation of the industrial working class and the conditions of factory labor; this section examines how that class re-entered national politics.

After the strike peak of 1905–1906, when millions of workers took part in strikes, activity fell sharply in 1908–1909. The causes were varied: arrests of activists, dismissals and blacklisting by employers, the closure of many unions, an economic downturn in 1908–1909 that increased unemployment, and the exhaustion of the revolutionary wave. Union membership declined from its 1906 peak. Yet legal institutions persisted: sickness funds, cooperative societies, workers' clubs, educational circles, and legally published newspapers continued to function, giving socialists and trade unionists channels for activity. The Social Democratic parties were divided, with Mensheviks emphasizing legal work and Bolsheviks maintaining underground organization, and each used Duma deputies and legal press to reach workers.

From 1910 the economy recovered strongly, and industrial output expanded, while wages rose more slowly than prices. Strike activity revived. Workers pressed for wage increases, shorter hours, and better treatment, and in many cases also for the dismissal of foremen or for recognition of their representatives. The demographic composition of the workforce was changing: in the large factories of St. Petersburg and other centers, workers who had experienced 1905 or who were literate and urban in outlook formed a core of organized activists, while recent peasant migrants kept ties to the village. Both were part of the labor force's complexity, and generalizations about workers' consciousness require care.

The event that crystallized this revival occurred in Siberia. The Lena Goldfields, in the Bodaibo region of the Lena river basin, were operated by the Lena Goldfields Company (Lenzoloto), a joint-stock company with significant foreign investment, including British interests. Several thousand workers labored in remote and harsh conditions, with low wages, long hours, high prices at company stores, poor housing, and inadequate food. In February and March 1912 a strike began after complaints about the quality of food supplied by the company. On 4 April 1912 (O.S.; 17 April N.S.), a column of strikers marched to submit complaints to a prosecutor, and troops under the command of an army officer fired on the crowd. Estimates of the number killed vary from about 170 in official accounts to over 250 in some other reports, with a further large number wounded. The precise figures are disputed because of limited and politicized documentation.

The shootings generated national outrage. The Duma established a commission, and a senatorial inquiry led by Senator Sergei Manukhin, sent by the government, reported critically on the conduct of the local officials and the company, a response that shows that parts of the state recognized the gravity of the event. Strikes and demonstrations protesting the shooting occurred in cities across the empire, with hundreds of thousands of participants in the spring of 1912. The Lena events became a symbol of the exploitation of labor and the use of force against workers. The newspaper Pravda, founded in St. Petersburg in April 1912, grew in this context as a legal Bolshevik daily, and the Social Democratic parties used the protests to extend their influence. The Bolsheviks, who had held a separate conference in Prague in January 1912 and claimed to represent the party, were developing an organization oriented toward factory workers.

Strike statistics from official sources, compiled by factory inspectors, show a dramatic rise: from roughly a hundred thousand strikers in 1911 to several hundred thousand in 1912, and to more than eight hundred thousand in 1913, with a further rise to over a million in the first half of 1914. These figures should be treated as approximate and as dependent on how strikes, including political demonstration strikes, were counted. In July 1914, a major strike wave in St. Petersburg, accompanied by street clashes and barricades in some districts, was under way when the international crisis led to war. Many historians discuss whether this pre-war strike movement indicates a revolutionary situation. The evidence shows significant militancy and growing influence of socialist organizations among workers, but also employers' and police countermeasures, divisions among workers, and the fact that the movement had not reached the level or coordination of 1905.

The chapter should not present this as proof that another revolution was inevitable. Some historians argue that Russia in 1914 was on the verge of a new revolutionary crisis, driven by worker militancy; others contend that the strike wave was subsiding, was responsive to economic fluctuations, and would have been contained or accommodated, perhaps through labor legislation and negotiation. What the evidence supports is the narrower statement that Russia experienced renewed labor militancy despite economic expansion and institutional reform. That contradiction is precisely what this chapter seeks to emphasize: modernization produced new social forces and new grievances at the same time as it produced new institutions.

Section 47.12

Economic and Cultural Modernization and the Unresolved Crisis of Autocracy

Late imperial Russia was not simply decaying. In the years between 1906 and 1914 the empire experienced substantial economic, social, educational, and cultural change, and any assessment of the political system must account for it. Chapter 44 traced the state-led industrialization of the 1890s; this section examines the broader modernization of the Duma years.

Industrial output grew rapidly after the recovery of 1909–1910, at rates commonly estimated at several percent a year, with notable expansion in metallurgy, coal, oil, engineering, textiles, and chemical industries. Joint-stock banks, many of them with foreign participation, expanded credit and investment. Railways grew, linking agricultural regions to ports and industrial centers, and the Trans-Siberian line facilitated migration and trade. Foreign investment, particularly from France, Belgium, Britain, and Germany, remained substantial, and Russia's state budget was relatively stable. Agricultural commercialization advanced: grain exports, dairy production in Siberia, and cooperative societies expanded. Cities grew; St. Petersburg and Moscow each had populations of well over a million, and cities such as Odesa, Kyiv, Warsaw, Baku, and Riga were major commercial and industrial centers. Urban consumer culture, with department stores, cinemas, popular newspapers, sport, and entertainment, was developing.

Educational expansion was especially significant. The Third Duma expanded primary-school funding beginning in 1908 and later approved a broader universal-primary-education bill in 1911; disagreement with the State Council prevented the full measure from becoming law, but appropriations and enrollment continued to rise substantially. Enrollment grew rapidly in primary schools and, more slowly, in secondary schools and universities, with an increase in the number of women students despite restrictions. Literacy rates rose, with estimates suggesting that perhaps two-fifths of the population was literate by 1914, with much higher rates among urban populations and young men and far lower rates among rural women and in many non-Russian regions. The publishing industry grew, newspapers reached wider audiences, and professional associations of doctors, lawyers, engineers, teachers, and others flourished. Zemstvo and municipal institutions expanded medical and educational services.

Cultural life flourished in what is often called the Silver Age. Symbolist and modernist poets such as Alexander Blok and Andrei Bely, Acmeists including Anna Akhmatova and Nikolai Gumilev, painters such as Wassily Kandinsky and Kazimir Malevich, composers such as Igor Stravinsky and Alexander Scriabin, and impresarios such as Sergei Diaghilev, whose Ballets Russes performed in Paris from 1909, gave Russian culture a prominent international presence. Scientists such as Ivan Pavlov and Ilya Mechnikov received Nobel Prizes in 1904 and 1908, respectively. Religious and philosophical thought, including debates about the role of the Orthodox Church and spirituality, was lively. These developments show that Russian society was not culturally stagnant and that it was integrated into European and global exchanges.

Yet this modernization coexisted with unresolved political problems. The first was the monarchy's resistance to responsible government. Nicholas II believed that he was accountable to God and history for the fate of Russia and was reluctant to accept ministers dependent on parliamentary confidence. His preference for ministers who were loyal to him personally, and his repeated replacement of ministers, produced instability in the cabinet and weakened continuity of policy. Tensions between the emperor and Duma politicians were reinforced by the influence of court circles, and by the perception that appointments were made for reasons of favor.

The second problem was the persistence of unresolved social questions. The land question had been addressed by Stolypin's reforms only partially; labor unrest had returned; and the national question was complicated by Russian nationalist policies and reduced representation. Civil liberties were uneven: the press was freer than before 1905 but subject to fines and closures, associations could be dissolved, and religious minorities faced restrictions, though legal toleration measures advanced in some respects. A tension between law and administrative authority remained: the rule of law was strengthened in some areas, such as the courts and legal profession, but administrative discretion and emergency powers continued to limit it.

The central distinction of the chapter follows from this: a regime can be modernizing without becoming democratizing. Economic growth, education, and cultural vitality did not by themselves produce a government accountable to the population. At the same time, political conflict does not prove that the state was already collapsing. Many European monarchies of the period experienced conflicts between executives and parliaments, labor unrest, and nationalist agitation without collapse. Russia in 1914 was not obviously doomed. Its outcome depended on the war, on the decisions of the emperor and his ministers, and on the response of society to the war's pressures, which are the subject of Chapter 48. The long-term question of whether the Duma monarchy could have evolved toward a more fully constitutional system, or would instead have faced a crisis of legitimacy even without the war, is one that historians can discuss but cannot resolve with evidence.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Russia in 1904, 1907, and 1914

What changed?

  • 1904: No elected national legislature; political parties illegal; trade unions largely illegal; preliminary censorship in force; the emperor legislated on the advice of the State Council.
  • 1907: An elected Duma and a reformed State Council existed; parties and unions were legal but constrained; the electoral law had been altered by decree; revolutionary movements were suppressed; the agrarian reform had begun.
  • 1914: A Duma had completed a full term and a fourth was in session; the press, associations, and professional organizations were more developed than in 1904; literacy and schooling had expanded; the economy had grown; labor protest had revived.

What remained?

  • The emperor's control of ministers, the army, and foreign policy.
  • The bureaucracy and police apparatus, with emergency powers.
  • The rural majority's reliance on small-scale agriculture and the persistence of communal tenure in many regions.
  • The legal disabilities of Jews and other minorities and the unequal treatment of non-Russian regions.

When and for whom: The greatest change occurred in 1905–1907 and was most visible in the capital cities and among educated and organized groups. For many peasants in the central provinces, continuity was more apparent than change, while peasants in regions of consolidation or resettlement experienced significant disruption. Workers in large factories experienced both legal opportunities and repression. Why the pattern matters: Comparing three dates, rather than two, shows that change was neither a single event nor an uninterrupted progression, and that stability in 1907–1911 and renewed tension in 1912–1914 belong to the same period.

Russia and the World

Constitutionalism in Eurasia, 1905–1914

The Russian Revolution of 1905 was part of a wave of constitutional movements across Eurasia. Iran's Constitutional Revolution of 1905–1906 produced a parliament (the Majlis) and a constitution, followed by a Russian-supported coup against the parliament in 1908 and, in 1911, Russian and British pressure that ended the first constitutional period. The Ottoman Empire restored its constitution after the Young Turk revolution of 1908. China adopted constitutional reforms in its final years under the Qing dynasty before the revolution of 1911. Japan, whose victory in 1905 inspired many Asian observers, had had a constitution since 1889 under which the emperor held extensive powers and the Diet had limited authority. These comparisons show that Russian developments were embedded in a broader trend, but they also reveal differences: Russia was simultaneously a participant in this movement and, as a great power, a force that influenced constitutional experiments in its neighbors, particularly Iran.

In Europe, the German Empire combined a parliament elected by universal male suffrage with a government responsible to the emperor and not to the Reichstag; Austria-Hungary had parliaments in both halves of the monarchy alongside strong imperial prerogatives and the use of emergency decree. Britain and France had parliamentary governments, though with limited franchises and imperial administrations that functioned differently from metropolitan politics. Such comparisons indicate that a legislature coexisting with a powerful monarchical executive was not unique to Russia. They also illustrate the danger of treating the Western parliamentary model as the only measure of constitutionalism. At the same time, the comparison cannot erase differences: in Russia the electoral law was altered by decree, ministerial responsibility to the legislature was absent, and the legal protections for civil rights were weaker than in most of western Europe. Finance and diplomacy also connected Russia to the world: the French alliance, loans, and the international system that would shape the outbreak of war in 1914 (Chapter 48).

Section 47.13

Chapter Review and Historical Thinking Checkpoint

This chapter has asked whether the Russian Empire between 1906 and 1914 became a constitutional monarchy, a modified autocracy, or a hybrid system that never resolved the contradiction between the two. Before turning to the chapter review, students should synthesize the evidence and reasoning developed in Sections 47.1 through 47.12.

Transformation. The Revolution of 1905 permanently changed Russian political life. It produced an elected legislature with a veto over ordinary laws, legal political parties, a more public press, trade unions and professional associations, and expectations of constitutional government that persisted even when governments limited them. A new generation of politicians gained experience in parliamentary debate, and the Duma became a national forum.

Continuity. The emperor retained control over ministers, foreign policy, and the armed forces, and the exclusive right to initiate constitutional change. The bureaucracy, police, and army remained the instruments of executive power. Emergency statutes, administrative discretion, and Article 87 provided means to bypass or limit the legislature. In this sense, essential features of the older order survived.

Causation. The regime stabilized after 1905 through a combination of factors: the loyalty of most of the army, the defeat of urban uprisings, division within the opposition, foreign credit, a policy of reform and repression, and a manipulated electoral system. Economic recovery after 1909 also helped. No single cause suffices.

Evidence. Laws and administrative practice must be read together. The Fundamental Laws, the electoral statutes, Duma records, and ministerial correspondence reveal what was formally permitted and how it was used. Police reports, strike statistics, land-settlement records, memoirs, and newspapers help assess how the system affected different groups, but each source has limits: officials had interests in presenting order, opponents in presenting oppression, and memoirists wrote with hindsight.

Competing interpretations. Historians differ about whether to emphasize constitutional development or restored autocracy, and whether the system was moving in one direction or another. The three interpretive positions presented in the Historical Debate remain defensible, and students should evaluate them against specific evidence rather than choosing a label in the abstract.

Contingency. Whether meaningful political evolution remained possible in 1914 is a question that cannot be answered with certainty. Some factors, including the emperor's convictions, the power of conservative elites, and unresolved social grievances, limited reform. Others, including economic growth, a functioning legislature, and a developing civil society, suggest that alternative paths were not closed. The war transformed the context, and the outcomes of 1917 were not determined by the events of 1906–1914 alone.

Broader significance. The Duma period shows that meaningful representative institutions can coexist with strong monarchical executive power, and that such a coexistence can be stable for years without resolving who is sovereign. That unresolved question mattered because, once war placed extraordinary demands on the state in 1914, the relationship among emperor, ministers, and legislature became a practical problem of legitimacy and coordination, not merely a constitutional puzzle (Chapter 48).

Students should now be able to defend a reasoned answer, using evidence from at least the Fundamental Laws, the First and Second Dumas, the electoral change of 3 June 1907, the Third Duma, Stolypin's repression, the agrarian reform, and the labor revival, to the question of how best to describe the political system of the Duma monarchy. The review sections that follow provide further exercises in this reasoning, and Chapter 48 will examine how the system responded to the stress of war.

Chapter Summary

This chapter examined the political system of the Russian Empire between the Revolution of 1905 and the outbreak of the First World War, asking whether it was a genuine constitutional transformation or an adaptation of autocracy designed to preserve imperial authority. The question arose from the contradictory settlement of 1906, in which the Fundamental Laws recognized a legislature with a veto over ordinary laws while reaffirming the emperor's supreme authority, his control over ministers and the armed forces, and his power to legislate by emergency decree.

The legal framework set the terms of political conflict. The State Duma and the reformed State Council shared legislative power, but ministers answered to the emperor, Article 87 allowed emergency decrees, and the emperor controlled dissolution. The electoral system was indirect and organized by curiae, and it could be changed, as it was in June 1907, in ways that altered the composition of the Duma without changing its formal powers. A new spectrum of legal parties, including Kadets, Octobrists, Trudoviks, socialists, nationalist and monarchist groups, and regional and national organizations, developed within and around this framework.

The first two Dumas revealed that a broadly elected assembly would press for responsible ministry, civil liberties, and land reform, and that the emperor and his government would not accept these demands. The dissolution of the First Duma, the Vyborg Manifesto, the confrontation of the Second Duma with the government, and the law of 3 June 1907 showed that the incompatibility concerned sovereignty itself. Historians debate whether the 1907 coup ended the constitutional experiment or created a more workable but less democratic order.

Pyotr Stolypin personified the regime's strategy. As prime minister he restored order by repression, including field courts-martial, executions, imprisonment, exile, and administrative restrictions, and he promoted reforms, notably the agrarian legislation that allowed peasants to leave the commune and consolidate their land, expanded credit through the Peasant Land Bank, and encouraged resettlement to Siberia and Central Asia. His approach combined conservative aims with modernizing methods, and it cannot be reduced to either liberalism or reaction.

The agrarian reforms must be evaluated by their outcomes as well as their intentions. By 1914, a substantial minority of communal households had acquired individual title, a smaller share had consolidated their land, and millions had migrated eastward. Agricultural production and commercialization grew, but the commune persisted in much of central Russia, land hunger remained, and regional variation was large. The evidence does not support the claim that a prosperous farming society had been created, nor does it show that the reforms were without effect.

The Third Duma, elected under the new law, served a full term and cooperated with the government. It legislated on budgets, education, insurance, and land tenure, showing that representative institutions could function in Russia. Yet its limits were built into the electoral system, the State Council, and the court. Nationalist policy in the borderlands and the reduced representation of non-Russian regions contributed to the alienation of minority groups, demonstrating that the Duma monarchy was a system for governing a multinational empire as well as a Russian political experiment.

After Stolypin's death in 1911, reform momentum slowed, the Octobrist center felt disappointed, and the Fourth Duma, elected in 1912, experienced increased friction with ministers. At the same time, labor militancy revived after 1910, symbolized by the Lena Goldfields shootings of 1912 and the strike movement of 1912–1914. These developments indicate that stabilization was incomplete and that economic growth and institutional reform produced new social forces and grievances.

The period also saw genuine modernization: industrial growth, expanding banking and railways, rising literacy and school enrollment, a flourishing press, professional associations, and the cultural achievements of the Silver Age. The chapter's key distinction is that a regime can be modernizing without becoming democratizing, and that political conflict does not prove that a state is already collapsing. Comparison with constitutional experiments in Iran, the Ottoman Empire, China, Japan, Germany, and Austria-Hungary shows that the Russian case was distinctive but not isolated.

Historiographically, the period remains contested. Interpretations range from a constitutional monarchy in formation, to a modified autocracy, to a hybrid system whose character depended on political choices. The chapter has urged students to evaluate evidence, to distinguish law from practice, and to avoid reading the years 1906–1914 either as a straight road to 1917 or as a lost liberal opportunity. Chapter 48 examines how the First World War placed extraordinary stress on this system, and Chapters 49 through 51 trace the revolutions of 1917.

Key Terms

Fundamental Laws
The portion of the Russian legal code defining the structure and powers of supreme authority; revised on 23 April 1906 to incorporate the State Duma while reaffirming imperial prerogatives.
October Manifesto
The declaration of 17 October 1905 (O.S.) promising civil liberties, a broader franchise, and legislative approval of laws by an elected Duma.
State Duma
The elected lower house of the imperial legislature; four Dumas met between 1906 and 1917.
State Council
The upper house after 1906, half appointed by the emperor and half elected by corporate bodies.
Council of Ministers
The unified cabinet whose members were appointed by and responsible to the emperor.
Article 87
The provision allowing emergency decrees with the force of law while the Duma was not in session.
Interpellation
A formal question to a minister, submitted by deputies, demanding explanation of administrative actions.
Curia
A category of voters (landowners, urban voters, peasants, workers) who chose electors separately.
Indirect election
A system in which voters choose electors who then choose deputies.
Kadets
The Constitutional Democratic Party, liberal and the main parliamentary opposition in the early Dumas.
Octobrists
The Union of 17 October, a moderate conservative-liberal party supportive of the manifesto and cooperative with the government.
Trudoviks
The Labor Group, a peasant-based democratic group advocating land redistribution.
Socialist Revolutionaries
A party in the populist tradition favoring socialization of land and using terrorism through a combat organization.
Mensheviks and Bolsheviks
The two factions of the Russian Social Democratic Labor Party, divided over organization and tactics.
Vyborg Manifesto
The appeal of July 1906 by dissolved First Duma deputies for passive resistance; its signatories were barred from later elections.
June Third system
The political order based on the electoral law of 3 June 1907, characterized by a conservative-leaning Duma and cooperation with the government.
Field court-martial
A summary military tribunal introduced in August 1906 to try civilians for violent crimes, with rapid proceedings and no appeal.
Okhrana
The security police department responsible for surveillance and infiltration of political opposition.
Obshchina (mir)
The peasant commune, which in many regions held land collectively and periodically redistributed it.
Khutor
A consolidated peasant farm with the household's dwelling located on its own land.
Otrub
A consolidated peasant holding whose owner continued to live in the village.
Peasant Land Bank
A state bank providing credit for peasant land purchase.
Resettlement Administration
The state agency organizing peasant migration to Siberia and Central Asia.
Land hunger
A term describing peasants' perceived shortage of land relative to population; the term is used by contemporaries and historians, though its extent varied by region.
Constitutional monarchy
A monarchy in which the monarch's powers are defined or limited by constitutional law and representative institutions; it does not necessarily imply that ministers are responsible to the legislature.
Autocracy (samoderzhavie)
Rule by a monarch whose authority is not formally derived from or limited by a representative body; its meaning in Russia after 1906 was contested.
Progressists
A center-left group in the Fourth Duma associated with industrial and commercial circles.
Silver Age
A term for the flourishing of Russian poetry, art, music, and thought from roughly the 1890s to the 1910s.
Lenzoloto
The Lena Goldfields Company, operator of the mines where troops fired on strikers in 1912.
Katorga
Penal servitude, a form of hard-labor sentence typically served in Siberia.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a regime that had been shaken by revolution adapted rather than collapsed, and how the adaptation created institutions that changed political life without resolving the question of who held sovereign power. The Duma monarchy introduced elections, parties, parliamentary debate, and a more public press into the empire, and it preserved executive authority through law, administration, and manipulation of the electorate. Its history cannot be reduced to either triumph or sham.

What continued afterward was the unresolved question of accountability. When the First World War placed extraordinary demands on the state, the relationship between the emperor, the ministers, and the legislature became a central political issue, as Chapter 48 will show. The experience of 1906–1914 also shaped the expectations of the politicians, officials, workers, and peasants who would act in 1917. At the same time, the period's reforms, including land reform and expanded schooling, left lasting effects that differed from the outcomes their designers intended.

The chapter also reveals larger themes of the textbook: the interaction of reform and coercion, the importance of regional and national variation in the empire, the need to distinguish law from practice, and the limits of teleological explanation. Questions remain unresolved, including how much political evolution was possible, how deeply agrarian change had penetrated by 1914, and how to weigh structural constraints against the choices of individuals. Students should carry forward the habit of asking these questions without assuming that the war and revolution that followed were inevitable consequences of what came before.
