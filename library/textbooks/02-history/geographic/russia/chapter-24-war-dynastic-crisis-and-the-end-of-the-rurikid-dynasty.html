Part IV — The Rise of Moscow and the Muscovite State
24

War, Dynastic Crisis, and the End of the Rurikid Dynasty

A History of Russia: From the Eurasian World to the Russian Federation

Chapter Opening

In 1584, the new tsar of Muscovy inherited a state larger than any of his predecessors had ruled — and a treasury, a countryside, and a court deeply strained. The crown he wore was secure. The foundations beneath it were not.

When Ivan IV died in March 1584, he left behind a Muscovite state whose territorial reach — from the Volga basin to the edge of Siberia, from the White Sea to the southern steppe frontier — would have astonished a Muscovite ruler of a century earlier. He also left behind a state whose countryside bore the marks of a quarter-century of warfare, whose fiscal registers recorded districts abandoned by the peasants who had once farmed them, whose southern frontier remained exposed to Crimean Tatar raids capable of striking Moscow itself, and whose royal family had been reduced, through war, illness, and a disputed death within the royal household, to a single reigning branch resting on unusually thin dynastic ground. The tsar who succeeded him, his son Fyodor, was widely portrayed by contemporary and later observers as pious and less personally engaged in daily government, although the extent of his actual political role remains difficult to establish and is examined critically later in this chapter; what is certain is that he was without an heir who would survive him. Fourteen years later, in January 1598, Fyodor's death without a surviving child brought to an end the Moscow ruling line of the Rurikid dynasty, a lineage that Muscovite political culture had traced, however uncertainly, back through centuries of princely succession to the semi-legendary founder examined in Chapter 7.

This chapter is not primarily about Ivan IV, whose reforms, conquests, and repressions Chapter 23 has already examined in detail. It is about what came after: the accumulated condition of Muscovy in the years between Ivan's death and the extinction of his dynasty, and the specific developments — economic disruption, peasant flight, frontier warfare, and a fragile royal household — that together explain why the loss of an heir in 1598 did not remain a narrow dynastic technicality but instead opened the door to the sustained political, social, and military crisis that Chapter 25 will trace as the Time of Troubles. Readers who expect a chapter built around a single dramatic reign, a single decisive battle, or a single villain will not find one here. Instead, this chapter asks students to hold several developments in view simultaneously: a countryside disrupted by war taxation and flight; a legal and administrative system increasingly restricting peasant movement in response to that disruption; a southern frontier requiring constant, expensive defense against Crimean Tatar raiding; an eastern frontier where Muscovite-backed forces were, in the very same years, opening a vast new zone of conquest and fur-tribute extraction in western Siberia; a court whose effective direction passed gradually into the hands of a capable but non-dynastic figure, Boris Godunov; and a royal household in which the death of a young half-brother of the tsar, under disputed circumstances, would later become one of the most politically explosive questions in all of Russian history.

None of these developments alone caused what followed. Muscovy had survived difficult successions before — the death of Vasily III in 1533 had left the throne to a three-year-old child, and the state had weathered the resulting regency crises examined in Chapter 23 without dissolving into prolonged civil conflict. What made the situation after 1598 different was the convergence of dynastic uncertainty with economic exhaustion, social dislocation, and frontier insecurity at the same historical moment, compounded by a political culture in which legitimacy had rested for generations on the assumption of continuous Rurikid succession. This chapter's central task is to explain that convergence carefully, resisting both the older narrative that treats 1598 as a sudden, inexplicable catastrophe and the equally misleading narrative that treats the Time of Troubles as the automatic, inevitable result of dynastic extinction alone.

The chapter also resists a narrative of simple decline. Even as war-related fiscal strain, peasant flight, and frontier raiding strained Muscovite society, the same state was extending its authority eastward across the Ural Mountains into western Siberia, a process this chapter introduces through the expedition associated with the Cossack leader Yermak and the Stroganov merchant family, and which Chapter 28 will follow in far greater depth. A state capable of simultaneously absorbing military strain in the west and south while opening an enormous new frontier in the east was neither simply collapsing nor simply thriving; it was a large, complex, and increasingly overextended political system whose long-term resilience depended on institutions, described in Chapter 22, that were themselves under growing pressure.

Finally, this chapter asks students to practice a specific kind of historical reasoning: distinguishing an immediate trigger from the deeper structural conditions that make a trigger dangerous. The death of a single childless tsar is, by itself, a demographic and biological fact. Whether that fact becomes a political catastrophe depends on the institutions, social relationships, and material conditions surrounding it. By the chapter's end, students should be able to explain why the succession crisis of 1598 proved far more dangerous than earlier Muscovite succession problems, using evidence about war, economy, society, frontier security, and political culture rather than dynasty alone.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain the political and economic condition of Muscovy during the final decade of Ivan IV's reign and at his death in 1584.
  2. Analyze the causes and regional unevenness of late sixteenth-century economic disruption and population movement, distinguishing depopulation from relocation.
  3. Trace the tightening of restrictions on peasant mobility, including the significance and limitations of the "forbidden years" (zapovednye leta), without projecting the fully consolidated seventeenth-century system of serfdom backward.
  4. Describe the military and economic burden of Crimean Tatar raiding on the southern frontier, including the 1571 burning of Moscow and the 1572 Battle of Molodi.
  5. Explain the beginnings of Muscovite expansion into western Siberia through the Stroganov family and Yermak's Cossack expedition, while distinguishing this beginning from the later, fuller conquest examined in Chapter 28.
  6. Evaluate Fyodor I's reign, distinguishing his documented dynastic and ceremonial role from later caricatures of him as wholly powerless.
  7. Trace how Boris Godunov accumulated political authority during Fyodor's reign through kinship, patronage, and administrative competence.
  8. Critically evaluate the evidence surrounding the 1591 death of Tsarevich Dmitry of Uglich, distinguishing the official investigation's findings from later political accusations.
  9. Explain why the death of Fyodor I in 1598 ended the Moscow Rurikid line and analyze why this dynastic extinction proved politically dangerous.
  10. Connect economic, demographic, social, military, and dynastic developments into a single causal explanation for Muscovy's vulnerability by 1598.
  11. Distinguish the immediate trigger of a historical crisis from its deeper structural preconditions.
  12. Anticipate how the developments examined in this chapter set the stage for the Time of Troubles examined in Chapter 25.

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. What condition did Ivan IV leave Muscovy in at his death in 1584, and how does that condition help explain what followed?
  2. Why might state records of "depopulation" actually reflect population movement rather than population loss, and why did this distinction matter for Muscovite fiscal and social policy?
  3. What pressures pushed Muscovite landholders and the state toward increasingly restricting peasant movement in the late sixteenth century?
  4. How did the southern steppe frontier and the eastern Siberian frontier place different, simultaneous demands on Muscovite military and administrative resources?
  5. How should historians evaluate Fyodor I's reign given the limits and biases of the available evidence?
  6. Through what mechanisms did Boris Godunov accumulate power without holding the throne?
  7. Why does the death of Tsarevich Dmitry at Uglich remain historically disputed, and what does the controversy reveal about the relationship between evidence and political interest?
  8. Why did the extinction of the Moscow Rurikid line in 1598 prove more dangerous than earlier succession problems in Muscovite history?
  9. How did economic, social, military, and dynastic developments combine to produce a fragile state by the end of the sixteenth century?

Historical Context

Chapter 21 traced Ivan III's transformation of Moscow into a large territorial state and the ideological language, including the idea of the "gathering of the Rus' lands," that accompanied that transformation. Chapter 22 examined the institutions — the Boyar Duma, the service nobility, the pomestie landholding system, the central administrative offices known as prikazy, and the Orthodox Church — that sustained the expanding Muscovite state before the reign of Ivan IV. Chapter 23 then traced Ivan IV's reign as a period of contradictory achievement: legal and administrative reform through the Chosen Council, territorial conquest at Kazan and Astrakhan, prolonged and ultimately unsuccessful war for the Baltic coastline in the Livonian War, and the political terror and social disruption of the Oprichnina.

By the early 1580s, each of these threads had produced consequences that outlasted Ivan IV's own reign. The Livonian War, begun in 1558 in pursuit of Baltic access, had drawn Muscovy into prolonged conflict with Poland-Lithuania, Sweden, and Denmark, and ended in 1583 with Muscovy surrendering its Baltic gains without achieving its original objective. The Oprichnina, formally ended in 1572 but leaving lasting institutional and social scars, had involved the forced relocation of populations, the confiscation and redistribution of land, and periods of extraordinary violence, including the 1570 sack of Novgorod examined in Chapter 23. Decades of taxation to fund war, combined with military service obligations that pulled labor away from agriculture, had placed sustained pressure on the peasant and townsman households that formed the fiscal foundation of the Muscovite state.

At the same time, Muscovy's southern frontier remained a zone of chronic insecurity. The Crimean Khanate, itself linked to Ottoman power, conducted regular raids into Muscovite territory both for plunder and for captives who could be sold into slavery through Black Sea markets, a pattern examined further in this chapter's Section 24.4. Muscovite defense against these raids required a standing system of fortified lines, garrison towns, and mobile cavalry forces whose maintenance consumed resources that might otherwise have supported the western war effort or internal reconstruction.

Students should carry forward from Chapter 22 the basic structure of Muscovite service-state institutions: a service nobility holding conditional pomestie estates in exchange for military obligation, a tax and labor system resting heavily on peasant households, and a central administration whose capacity depended on accurate registers of population, land, and resources. This chapter examines what happened to that structure under sustained strain, and how a political culture built around continuous Rurikid succession confronted the sudden absence of an heir in 1598.

Section 24.1

The Later Reign of Ivan IV

The formal end of the Oprichnina in 1572, examined in Chapter 23, did not restore Muscovy to the conditions that had prevailed before its introduction a decade earlier. The last twelve years of Ivan IV's reign were years of continuing warfare, continuing taxation, and a royal household increasingly marked by instability. The Livonian War, which Ivan had launched in 1558 to secure Muscovite access to the Baltic coast, dragged on through repeated campaigns against Poland-Lithuania, Sweden, and, from 1569, the newly unified Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth. Muscovite forces, worn down by decades of fighting and by the military and administrative disruptions of the Oprichnina years, suffered a series of reverses in the war's final phase, including the loss of previously held territory. The war ended in 1582–1583 through a set of truces that left Muscovy without the Baltic outlet it had sought and without significant territorial gain to show for a quarter-century of sustained military effort.

The fiscal and social costs of this prolonged conflict fell heavily on the same peasant and townsman households examined in Chapter 22 as the foundation of Muscovite state finance. Continuous taxation to fund campaigns, combined with the conscription of labor and resources for military transport and fortification, drew productive capacity away from agriculture in war-affected regions, a development examined in greater depth in Section 24.2. The Oprichnina's own disruptions — forced population transfers, confiscated estates, and periods of extreme violence, including the sack of Novgorod in 1570 — compounded this strain in the specific regions it had touched most heavily.

Ivan IV's royal household grew correspondingly fragile during these same years. His marriages, numerous by the standards of Orthodox canon law and a subject of contemporary and later controversy in their own right, produced several sons, but by the early 1580s only two survived to adulthood or near-adulthood: Ivan Ivanovich, his capable and politically visible eldest surviving son, and Fyodor Ivanovich, generally described in the sources as gentle and devout. According to a tradition first recorded by contemporary and near-contemporary observers, Ivan IV struck and fatally injured his son Ivan Ivanovich in a fit of rage in November 1581, a death traditionally dated to that year, though the precise circumstances, and even certain details of the traditional account, remain the subject of historical debate and cannot be treated as fully established fact. What can be said with confidence is that by the early 1580s, Ivan IV's most experienced and politically visible heir was dead, leaving Fyodor as the presumptive successor — a ruler whose degree of personal engagement in government would later be portrayed very differently by contemporary and later observers, a question this chapter examines with appropriate caution in Section 24.6, alongside a much younger son, Dmitry, born to a later marriage whose canonical legitimacy was itself questioned by contemporaries, examined further in Section 24.8.

Ivan IV died in March 1584. He left behind a state territorially larger than any of his predecessors had governed, encompassing the conquests of Kazan and Astrakhan examined in Chapter 23 and, as Section 24.5 will show, already extending tentatively into western Siberia. He also left behind a treasury strained by decades of warfare, a countryside marked by tax-driven and war-driven population movement, a service nobility whose landholding system faced growing pressure from labor scarcity, a southern frontier requiring constant and expensive defense, and a royal succession resting on a single heir whose capacity for independent rule was, and remains, a matter of disputed characterization. The question this chapter now takes up is how these accumulated conditions developed over the following fourteen years, and why they converted the eventual loss of the dynastic line into a genuine political crisis.

Section 24.2

Economic Disruption and Depopulation

Muscovite fiscal and land registers (pistsovye knigi) compiled during the 1580s and 1590s in many districts of central and northwestern Muscovy record striking numbers of peasant households recorded as "empty" (pusto) — abandoned dwellings whose former occupants no longer appeared on local tax rolls. Taken at face value, these registers might suggest a catastrophic decline in Muscovy's rural population during the final decades of the sixteenth century. Historians treat this evidence with considerable care, because the appearance of an empty entry in a district register reveals only that a household had disappeared from that particular district's records, not that the individuals who had once occupied it had died, nor even that the district's total population had necessarily declined by the same proportion.

Several distinct processes could produce the same fiscal appearance of "depopulation." Sustained warfare, particularly the Livonian War's demands for soldiers, transport labor, and provisioning, drew men away from agricultural households for extended periods, sometimes permanently, through death in campaign or through resettlement elsewhere. Taxation to fund that warfare, combined with the extraordinary levies and confiscations associated with the Oprichnina in the districts it touched, placed direct financial pressure on peasant households, some of which responded by abandoning increasingly unprofitable tenancies. Harvest failures and disease outbreaks in particular years compounded these pressures regionally. And, crucially, many peasants who left one district did not vanish from Muscovy altogether; they relocated — to the estates of wealthier or more favorably positioned landholders able to offer better terms, to expanding frontier regions in the south and southeast where new land and looser oversight offered opportunity, and, in smaller numbers, toward the new eastern frontier opening in Siberia, examined in Section 24.5.

This distinction between depopulation and relocation matters for more than demographic accuracy. From the perspective of the Muscovite state and its service landholders, the practical effect of relocation was often indistinguishable from population loss: a district whose taxable households disappeared could no longer support the fiscal obligations and military service assessments that the state and its pomestie-holding servicemen depended upon, regardless of whether the missing peasants were dead or simply farming someone else's land elsewhere. A service nobleman holding a pomestie grant, examined in Chapter 22 as land held conditionally in exchange for military service, faced a direct threat to his own capacity to fulfill that service if his peasants departed for more attractive estates, particularly those of wealthier boyars or monastic landholders who could offer temporary tax relief or better terms to attract labor.

This dynamic transformed demographic disruption into what historians describe as a state-capacity problem: the Muscovite state's ability to fund its military and administrative apparatus depended on a functioning system of registered, taxable, service-obligated households, and that system was visibly eroding in significant parts of the realm during the very years, examined in Section 24.4, when frontier defense demanded sustained and expensive military commitment. The regional unevenness of this disruption also mattered. Districts most heavily affected by war taxation, Oprichnina confiscation, and military transit — chiefly in the northwest and center — showed the most severe registered abandonment, while southern frontier districts, despite their own security risks, often attracted population precisely because service obligations there could carry different, sometimes more favorable, terms, and because frontier settlement offered a route away from the tightening restrictions examined in Section 24.3.

Section 24.3

Peasant Mobility and Growing Restrictions

Chapters 21 and 23 have already traced the long trajectory by which Muscovite peasants' traditional right to change landlords narrowed over the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries. The Sudebnik of 1497, examined in Chapter 21, had formalized a customary practice by restricting peasant departure to a two-week window around Yuriev Day (St. George's Day, in late autumn), together with payment of an exit fee (pozhiloe) compensating the departing landlord. This arrangement did not abolish peasant mobility; it regulated it, giving both peasants and landholders a predictable, legally sanctioned period and procedure for relocation.

By the final decades of the sixteenth century, the pressures examined in Section 24.2 — labor scarcity produced by war-driven and tax-driven population movement, combined with the service nobility's dependence on a stable peasant labor force to meet their own military obligations — generated sustained pressure from landholders, and particularly from the less wealthy service gentry least able to compete for scarce labor against wealthier boyars and monasteries, for the state to restrict peasant departure further. The state's own fiscal interest reinforced this pressure: a peasantry able to relocate freely toward more favorable estates or frontier regions complicated the accurate assessment and collection of taxes and service obligations.

Beginning in the 1580s, and continuing through the 1590s, the Muscovite government issued a series of measures, imperfectly documented and unevenly enforced across different regions and periods, that came to be known as the "forbidden years" (zapovednye leta) — years, or districts, in which the customary Yuriev Day right of departure was temporarily suspended. Historians continue to debate the precise chronology, scope, and legal mechanism of these measures, since the surviving documentary record is fragmentary and some scholars have questioned whether a single comprehensive decree ever existed or whether the "forbidden years" instead represented a series of more limited, situational suspensions that later observers retrospectively systematized into a single policy. What can be said with reasonable confidence is that by the 1590s, peasant departure rights that had been customary and legally regulated for nearly a century were being progressively curtailed in significant parts of Muscovy. A related but distinct development came in 1597: on 24 November of that year, the government established a five-year limitation period (urochnye leta) for claims to recover fugitive peasants, a search-and-return rule that should not be conflated with the earlier and more obscurely documented zapovednye leta, since it concerned the length of time a landholder had to pursue and reclaim a peasant who had already fled, rather than the suspension of the departure right itself.

Students should resist two tempting oversimplifications. The first is to treat this period as though it had already produced the fully consolidated system of hereditary serfdom that Chapter 27 will examine as a defining feature of seventeenth-century Muscovite society following the Sobornoe Ulozhenie of 1649; the restrictions of the 1580s and 1590s were significant but incomplete, contested, unevenly enforced, and still framed in the language of temporary suspension rather than permanent hereditary bondage. The second is to attribute this tightening process to the personal will of any single ruler, including Ivan IV; the evidence instead points to a structural interaction among war-driven fiscal need, service-gentry labor competition, landholder pressure, and state administrative interest, developing gradually across multiple reigns rather than through a single deliberate act of enserfment. The chapter's central point is that peasant mobility, once a regulated but genuine right, was becoming an increasingly contested and restricted status precisely as the economic pressures examined in Section 24.2 gave peasants the strongest incentive to exercise whatever mobility remained available to them.

Historical Vocabulary
Yuriev Day

Yuriev Day (St. George's Day, falling in late November on the Muscovite calendar) designated the traditional two-week period during which Muscovite peasants held the legally recognized right to leave one landholder's estate for another, provided they had settled outstanding obligations, including the exit payment known as pozhiloe. The Sudebnik of 1497, examined in Chapter 21, formalized this customary practice into law.

Yuriev Day mattered because it represented a legally regulated middle ground between unrestricted peasant mobility and outright bondage: peasants retained genuine, if narrowly timed, freedom of movement, while landholders retained some predictability and compensation for departing labor. The progressive restriction of Yuriev Day rights through the "forbidden years" examined in this section, and their eventual abolition in the seventeenth century examined in Chapter 27, marks one of the central long-term social transformations of Muscovite history.

Section 24.4

Crimean Tatar Raids

While Muscovy's western energies were absorbed by the Livonian War and its internal resources strained by the developments examined in Sections 24.2 and 24.3, the southern steppe frontier presented a distinct and continuous security challenge. The Crimean Khanate, a Turkic successor state to the Golden Horde examined in Chapter 17, had by the sixteenth century become a vassal of the Ottoman Empire while retaining substantial independent military capacity, particularly in mounted raiding warfare well suited to the open steppe terrain examined in Chapter 2. Crimean forces conducted regular incursions into Muscovite and, at times, Polish-Lithuanian territory, seeking plunder, livestock, and, especially, captives who could be marched south and sold into slavery through Black Sea markets, chiefly at the Ottoman-controlled port of Kaffa — a trade in human captives that represented one of the major economic functions of Crimean raiding and imposed enormous human cost on frontier populations.

The most severe of these incursions during this period came in May 1571, when Khan Devlet I Giray led a large Crimean force deep into Muscovite territory and, exploiting the diversion of Muscovite military resources toward the Livonian War and the continuing disruptions of the Oprichnina, reached and burned much of Moscow itself. Contemporary accounts describe massive destruction and casualties, though precise figures cannot be established with confidence given the limitations of the surviving evidence; what is clear is that the raid represented a catastrophic failure of Muscovite frontier defense and a profound shock to the capital.

Devlet I Giray returned with a renewed invasion force in 1572, reportedly seeking outright conquest or at minimum the permanent detachment of Muscovite territory such as Kazan and Astrakhan, examined in Chapter 23, from Muscovite control. This second invasion met a very different outcome. At the Battle of Molodi, fought south of Moscow in the summer of 1572, a Muscovite force under experienced commanders, employing fortified mobile field defenses (gulyay-gorod, or "walking towns" — mobile wooden barricades mounted on wagons) in combination with cavalry and infantry, decisively defeated the Crimean army. The victory at Molodi halted the immediate threat of a repeat catastrophe and demonstrated that Muscovite military institutions, despite the strains examined elsewhere in this chapter, retained genuine capacity for organized defense when properly directed and resourced.

The Molodi victory did not end Crimean raiding, which continued at a lower intensity through the remainder of the sixteenth century and well beyond, requiring Muscovy to maintain and periodically extend an expensive system of fortified defensive lines (zasechnye cherty), garrison towns, and mobile cavalry patrols along its southern frontier. This ongoing commitment of military and fiscal resources to southern defense occurred simultaneously with the western war effort examined in Section 24.1 and the eastern expansion examined in Section 24.5, illustrating a recurring feature of late sixteenth-century Muscovy: a state stretched across multiple, simultaneous military and administrative commitments, each individually manageable but collectively placing substantial strain on the fiscal and social foundations examined in Section 24.2.

Section 24.5

Siberian Expansion Begins

Even as Muscovy absorbed the pressures examined in the preceding sections, the same state was, in these very years, extending its authority eastward across the Ural Mountains into a vast new territorial and commercial frontier. This section introduces the opening episode of that expansion; the fuller process of conquest, administration, indigenous encounter, and settlement across Siberia is reserved for sustained treatment in Chapter 28, and students should understand the developments described here as a beginning rather than a completed conquest.

The Stroganov family, a wealthy Muscovite merchant and industrial dynasty holding extensive land and commercial privileges in the Kama and Chusovaya river basins along the western approach to the Urals, had by the mid-sixteenth century developed substantial interests in salt production, agriculture, and, especially, the fur trade — a commodity of enormous value in both domestic Muscovite and international markets, examined further in Chapter 28's treatment of the fur economy. Seeking to protect and expand their commercial interests against raiding by forces loyal to the Khanate of Sibir, a Turkic Muslim successor state to the Mongol empire controlling territory in western Siberia, the Stroganovs received government charters authorizing them to maintain fortified settlements and armed forces in the region, and in the early 1580s a company of several hundred Cossacks — frontier warrior-adventurers whose social origins and role in Muscovite and Ukrainian history are examined further in Chapter 30 — under the leadership of a Cossack commander known as Yermak Timofeevich emerged from the Stroganov frontier milieu and received support associated with their domains. Historians dispute both the precise chronology of the expedition's launch and how directly the Stroganovs initiated it, since the surviving evidence does not clearly establish whether the initiative came primarily from the Stroganovs or from the Cossacks themselves.

Yermak's expedition crossed the Urals and defeated forces loyal to Khan Kuchum, the khanate's ruler, capturing Kuchum's capital, Qashliq (also called Isker), near the site of modern Tobolsk, in 1582. This success rested on several interacting advantages rather than any single decisive factor: firearms, which gave Cossack forces a real if not absolute tactical edge over cavalry more heavily reliant on the bow; superior river mobility, since the expedition traveled and supplied itself substantially by boat along the Siberian river network; and political divisions within the Khanate of Sibir itself, where Kuchum's authority over subordinate Tatar, Khanty, and Mansi communities was neither uniform nor uncontested, leaving some local groups willing to accommodate or ally with the newcomers rather than resist alongside the khan. This victory did not represent the conquest of Siberia in any complete sense. Khan Kuchum, though driven from his capital, continued armed resistance for years afterward, and Yermak himself was killed in fighting in 1585, after which his surviving forces temporarily withdrew from the region. Muscovite state authority reasserted itself only gradually thereafter, through the construction of fortified towns — including Tyumen (founded 1586) and Tobolsk (founded 1587) — garrisoned by state forces rather than Cossack adventurers, and through the slow extension southward and eastward of Muscovite administrative and tribute-collecting authority over subsequent decades and, in fact, subsequent centuries.

Students should also resist the older, heroic narrative in which Yermak single-handedly "conquered Siberia" for Muscovy in a single dramatic campaign. The indigenous peoples of western Siberia — including Khanty, Mansi, and various Turkic-speaking communities incorporated into or resisting the Khanate of Sibir — were active historical participants in this process, not passive scenery; some communities allied with Muscovite forces against Kuchum, others resisted Muscovite incursion, and the fur-tribute system (yasak) that Muscovite administration extended across the region, examined more fully in Chapter 28, imposed new and often severe obligations on indigenous populations. What this section establishes is the beginning of a process — a Cossack expedition emerging from the Stroganov frontier milieu, followed by state consolidation through fortified towns — that would, across subsequent decades, become one of the most consequential territorial expansions in this textbook's coverage, extending Muscovite and later Russian authority eventually to the Pacific, as Chapter 28 will trace in full. That this expansion began in the very years when war strain, peasant flight, and frontier raiding were placing severe pressure on the Muscovite core illustrates a central analytical point of this chapter: state growth and state vulnerability were not mutually exclusive but were occurring simultaneously.

Section 24.6

Fyodor I

Fyodor Ivanovich acceded to the throne upon his father's death in March 1584, having been, since the death of his elder brother Ivan Ivanovich in 1581, the presumptive heir. Fyodor's dynastic legitimacy as Ivan IV's son was never seriously questioned during his reign; what later commentary, both contemporary and historical, has questioned is the extent of his personal engagement in governance. Foreign observers present at the Muscovite court, together with later Russian historians drawing on chronicle and administrative evidence, generally describe Fyodor as devout, gentle in temperament, more inclined toward religious observance and ceremonial participation than toward the sustained personal direction of military and administrative affairs that had characterized his father's reign.

This chapter treats this characterization with appropriate caution. Contemporary accounts of Fyodor's limitations come substantially from observers, including foreign diplomats and merchants, whose access to the inner workings of the Muscovite court was itself limited, and from later sources shaped by the political interests of the Godunov era and its aftermath, which had reasons of their own to emphasize Fyodor's passivity in order to explain and justify Boris Godunov's subsequent accumulation of authority. What can be established with more confidence is that Fyodor presided over a court structured around a regency-like council of leading boyars in the early years of his reign, that this council's composition shifted over time as certain factions were marginalized, examined further in Section 24.7, and that Boris Godunov emerged as the dominant figure directing day-to-day administration, diplomacy, and military policy for most of the reign — without this necessarily meaning that Fyodor exercised no independent judgment or ceremonial authority of his own.

Several significant developments occurred during Fyodor's reign that should not be attributed to Boris Godunov alone, since Fyodor's dynastic authority provided the essential legitimating framework within which Godunov and other officials operated. The most consequential religious development of the reign was the establishment of the Moscow Patriarchate in 1589, elevating the head of the Russian Orthodox Church from the rank of metropolitan to patriarch; the wider Orthodox hierarchy subsequently recognized Moscow as the fifth patriarchate in order of precedence, ranking after the four ancient Eastern patriarchates of Constantinople, Alexandria, Antioch, and Jerusalem. This achievement, negotiated through careful diplomacy with the Patriarch of Constantinople and reflecting Muscovy's growing self-conception as a leading center of Orthodox Christianity — a theme connected to the "Third Rome" concept examined in this textbook's treatment of Muscovite ideology — required sustained ecclesiastical and diplomatic effort that occurred under Fyodor's nominal authority, whatever the precise division of initiative between the tsar, the church hierarchy, and Boris Godunov's administration.

Fyodor's reign also saw continued frontier development along multiple axes: renewed, if limited, conflict with Sweden that recovered some territory lost during the final phase of the Livonian War, continued construction of fortified towns along the southern and eastern frontiers examined in Sections 24.4 and 24.5, and continued administrative consolidation of conquered and newly settled territories. Fyodor and Irina had one child, a daughter named Feodosia, born in 1592; her death in 1594, before her second birthday, removed the dynasty's immediate prospect of continuation and sharpened the succession anxiety examined further in Sections 24.7 and 24.9. Taken together, the evidence suggests a reign in which the tsar's personal political role was genuinely more limited than his father's had been, but in which state administration nonetheless functioned with reasonable effectiveness — a combination that reflects less on Fyodor's individual capacities than on the growing institutional maturity of Muscovite administrative structures examined in Chapter 22, which could sustain effective government even without an unusually forceful ruler at their head, provided a capable director such as Boris Godunov stood ready to exercise authority in the tsar's name.

Section 24.7

Boris Godunov's Rise

Boris Godunov's path to political dominance during Fyodor's reign rested on a combination of kinship connection, court patronage, administrative competence, and the gradual marginalization of rival boyar factions, rather than on any single decisive event. Godunov belonged to a boyar family of moderate but not preeminent standing; his rise owed much to two specific connections established earlier in his career. First, Godunov had served within the Oprichnina apparatus during its operation in the 1570s, a service that, whatever its later reputational costs, placed him within networks of court favor and administrative experience during Ivan IV's later reign. Second, and more decisively, Godunov's sister Irina married Fyodor Ivanovich, making Boris the tsar's brother-in-law and giving him unusually close access to the ruling household. This connection, though highly significant, was not entirely without rival: Fyodor's mother, Ivan IV's first wife Anastasia Romanovna, belonged to the Romanov (Zakharyin-Yuriev) family, whose members therefore also possessed a substantial maternal kinship claim on the tsar's household, a connection that would matter a great deal for the events examined in Chapter 25.

Upon Fyodor's accession in 1584, governance initially rested with a council of leading boyars appointed, according to tradition, by Ivan IV himself shortly before his death to guide the new, inexperienced tsar. This council included figures from several of Muscovy's most prominent boyar families, and its early years were marked by factional competition for dominant influence. Over the following several years, Godunov's position strengthened relative to his rivals through a combination of political maneuvering, marriage alliances, and, in some cases, the exile, disgrace, or forced monastic tonsure of competing boyars and their supporters — a familiar pattern of court competition examined in earlier chapters' treatment of Muscovite politics, though the surviving evidence for the specific mechanisms of Godunov's rise is filtered through later sources shaped by his subsequent reign and its contested legacy.

By the late 1580s, Godunov's dominance in the direction of Muscovite foreign policy, military affairs, and internal administration was widely recognized both within Muscovy and by foreign observers at the Muscovite court, who increasingly treated Godunov as the effective director of state policy even while continuing to observe the formal protocols of address and deference due to Fyodor as tsar. Godunov's administration is credited with the negotiation of the Moscow Patriarchate's establishment in 1589, examined in Section 24.6, with continued military and diplomatic engagement on the Swedish and southern frontiers, and, as Section 24.3 has examined, with continued development of policies restricting peasant mobility that served the interests of the service gentry whose support Godunov needed to maintain his position.

It is essential for students to distinguish Boris Godunov's status during this period from his later status as tsar. Throughout Fyodor's reign, Godunov's authority, however extensive in practice, remained formally derivative of Fyodor's dynastic legitimacy; Godunov ruled in the tsar's name, not in his own right, and his position, however dominant, remained vulnerable to the same kind of factional reversal that had elevated him over his own rivals. This distinction matters directly for the chapter's argument: Godunov's power before 1598 rested on competence, patronage, and kinship to a legitimate Rurikid tsar, not on any independent claim to the throne. What happened when that legitimating dynastic connection disappeared is the subject of Section 24.9; Godunov's own reign as tsar, beginning in 1598, is examined fully in Chapter 25.

Section 24.8

The Death of Tsarevich Dmitry

Among Ivan IV's sons was Dmitry Ivanovich, born in 1582 to Maria Nagaya, Ivan's seventh or eighth wife (accounts differ), a marriage whose canonical legitimacy under Orthodox Church law was doubtful, since church canons restricted the number of permissible marriages well below this figure. This uncertain canonical status meant that Dmitry's own legitimacy as a potential heir was already contested during his lifetime, a fact that would matter enormously for the political crisis examined in Section 24.9 and Chapter 25. Following Fyodor's accession in 1584, Dmitry and his mother's family were sent to reside at Uglich, a town northeast of Moscow granted to Dmitry as an appanage — a practice with roots in the appanage system examined in Chapter 13, by which junior members of a ruling family received territorial holdings separate from the main line of succession.

On 15 May 1591, the young Dmitry, then eight years old, died at Uglich from a knife wound to the throat. An official government commission, headed by the boyar Vasily Shuisky — a figure who will reappear prominently in Chapter 25's account of the Time of Troubles — was dispatched from Moscow to investigate. The commission's findings, recorded in surviving investigative documents, concluded that Dmitry had died accidentally, suffering a seizure (the boy was reported to have a history of epileptic episodes) while playing with a knife, causing him to fall upon the blade and inflict a fatal wound upon himself. The commission also reported that Dmitry's mother's Nagoi relatives had, in the immediate aftermath, incited a crowd in Uglich to murder several townsmen whom they accused, evidently without foundation according to the commission, of having assassinated the boy — an accusation that led to punitive measures against the town of Uglich itself, including the exile of participants in the disturbance and, according to later, more legendary tradition, punishment even of the town's bell that had been rung to summon the crowd.

Accusations of murder arose immediately at Uglich, though initially directed against alleged local perpetrators rather than against Boris Godunov himself; it was in subsequent political narratives, developing over the following years and especially after 1598, that responsibility for the supposed assassination was increasingly and pointedly attributed to Godunov, who, as this section's evidence makes clear, stood to benefit enormously from the elimination of a rival claimant to the throne, however canonically doubtful that claim might have been, given that Fyodor remained without an heir of his own. This accusation could not be tested by contemporaries in any way that would satisfy a modern historian's evidentiary standard, and it cannot be resolved with confidence by historians today. The official investigation's conclusion of accidental death is consistent with the specific medical detail regarding epileptic seizures and with the absence of any surviving contemporary evidence directly implicating Godunov in a plot. At the same time, historians recognize that a government commission investigating the death of a politically inconvenient claimant, dispatched by the very administration that stood to benefit from his death, cannot be treated as a fully independent or disinterested source, and the swift, forceful suppression of the Uglich disturbance likewise served the interests of the Moscow government regardless of what had actually happened to the boy.

What can be established with confidence, and what matters most for this chapter's argument, is not the unresolved question of Dmitry's actual cause of death but the enormous and lasting political consequence of the uncertainty surrounding it. Because Dmitry's death remained genuinely disputed rather than conclusively explained, it became available, within little more than a decade, as raw material for one of the most consequential episodes of political impersonation in Russian history: the emergence of individuals claiming to be the "true" Dmitry, miraculously escaped from the assassination attempt at Uglich, examined in Chapter 25 as False Dmitry I and, subsequently, False Dmitry II. The Uglich case thus offers students a clear historical-method lesson: a disputed death, investigated under circumstances that compromise the investigation's independence, can become not merely an unresolved historical question but an active political weapon wielded by later claimants and their supporters, with consequences far exceeding the immediate event itself.

Primary Source Window

The Uglich Commission Report (1591)

Creator: Official investigative commission headed by boyar Vasily Shuisky, dispatched from Moscow · Date: May–June 1591 · Type: Official government investigative record and witness testimony

Context: Following the death of Tsarevich Dmitry at Uglich on 15 May 1591, the Moscow government, under the effective direction of Boris Godunov, dispatched a commission to investigate the circumstances, take witness testimony from residents of Uglich, including servants and playmates said to have been present, and report its findings to the Boyar Duma and the church hierarchy.

Brief paraphrase of the commission's conclusion (not a verbatim translated quotation):

The commission reported that Dmitry, known to suffer periodic seizures, had been playing a game involving a knife or pointed implement, described in the record as "tychka," with several other children in the courtyard when a seizure struck him, causing him to fall upon the knife he held and inflict a fatal wound to his own throat. The commission further reported that Dmitry's Nagoi relatives, without waiting for investigation, had accused several townsmen of murder and incited the crowd of Uglich to kill them, an act the commission characterized as unjust violence against innocent men.

Analysis: This source must be read as an official document produced by an investigation dispatched by, and reporting to, the very Moscow government whose leading figure, Boris Godunov, stood to benefit politically from Dmitry's death. This does not prove the commission's findings false; medical detail regarding epilepsy is specific and consistent with an accidental account, and no surviving contemporary evidence directly implicates Godunov. It does mean the source cannot be treated as fully independent, and historians must weigh its findings alongside its origin.

Questions for students:

  1. Why might an official government report be an inherently limited source for investigating a politically sensitive death?
  2. What specific detail in the commission's account lends some support to the accidental-death conclusion?
  3. Why did the swift punishment of the Uglich crowd serve the Moscow government's interests regardless of what actually happened to Dmitry?
  4. How might the same document be read differently by a historian skeptical of Godunov and by one more inclined to credit the official account?
Section 24.9

The End of the Rurikid Line

Fyodor I died on 7 January 1598, without a surviving son or daughter to succeed him. His death brought to a close the Moscow ruling branch of the Rurikid dynasty, a lineage that Muscovite political and religious culture had, for centuries, treated as tracing an unbroken line of legitimate princely authority back through the grand princes of Moscow and Vladimir-Suzdal examined in earlier chapters, to the founding figures of Rus' examined in Chapter 7. Other branches of the broader Rurikid family tree survived among various princely and noble families across Muscovy, but none possessed a claim to the Moscow throne itself comparable to direct descent from Ivan IV and his immediate predecessors.

Students should resist treating this dynastic extinction as though it automatically or mechanically produced the Time of Troubles examined in Chapter 25. Muscovite political history had weathered uncertain successions before without descending into prolonged crisis; the minority of Ivan IV himself, following the death of Vasily III in 1533, had produced years of factional regency conflict, examined in Chapter 23, without threatening the survival of the state itself. What made 1598 different was not dynastic extinction in isolation but its convergence with the specific conditions this chapter has traced: a countryside strained by war taxation and population movement, examined in Section 24.2; a peasantry whose customary mobility rights were being progressively, and contentiously, restricted, examined in Section 24.3; a southern frontier requiring continuous, expensive military commitment, examined in Section 24.4; a state simultaneously extending itself into an enormous and only partially secured eastern frontier, examined in Section 24.5; and a still-unresolved public uncertainty surrounding the death of a rival claimant to the throne, examined in Section 24.8, whose circumstances remained available for political exploitation.

In this specific context, the disappearance of the principle that had underwritten political legitimacy in Muscovy for generations — direct, undisputed descent from a recognized ruling line — removed a source of stability at precisely the moment when other sources of stability were already under strain. Muscovite political culture had developed no well-established alternative mechanism for conferring legitimacy on a non-dynastic ruler; the closest available institution was the Zemsky Sobor, the assembly of the land examined in Chapter 23, which convened in February 1598 and formally elected Boris Godunov as tsar, drawing on his years of demonstrated administrative competence, his kinship connection to the extinguished dynasty through his sister Irina, and the practical reality that Godunov already directed most functions of government.

The Zemsky Sobor's election of Godunov was a genuine and legally significant act within Muscovite political tradition, not a mere formality; it represented an attempt to construct an alternative foundation for legitimate rule through the consent and recognition of the political nation. The 1598 assembly was politically and ceremonially significant and presented Godunov's accession as a collective election, although historians debate its composition, procedures, and how broadly it actually represented the realm, since the surviving documentation of the gathering is itself incomplete and was produced substantially under Godunov's own administration. But it did not, and by its nature could not, fully substitute for the accumulated weight of dynastic legitimacy that Muscovite political culture had relied upon for centuries. A tsar without dynastic descent remained, in the eyes of many contemporaries, a boyar elevated above his peers rather than a ruler set apart by inherited sacral authority — a distinction that would matter enormously once the economic and social pressures examined throughout this chapter combined with famine, examined in Chapter 25, to produce conditions in which rival claimants, beginning with the pretender who claimed to be the miraculously surviving Tsarevich Dmitry, could find genuine popular and elite support against a tsar whose legitimacy rested on election rather than birth.

Historical Debate

What Happened at Uglich?

The question: Did Tsarevich Dmitry die accidentally in 1591, as the official Uglich commission concluded, or was he murdered, whether on Boris Godunov's orders or otherwise?

The accidental-death position: Historians favoring this view point to the specific, medically plausible detail of a documented seizure disorder, the absence of any surviving contemporary evidence directly implicating Godunov, and the observation that a planned assassination of a child regarded by many as canonically illegitimate and politically marginal would have been an unusually risky and, from Godunov's perspective, not obviously necessary act.
The assassination position: Historians favoring this view emphasize Godunov's clear political interest in Dmitry's elimination, the compromised independence of a commission dispatched by the government that stood to benefit, the swift and forceful suppression of the Uglich crowd's own murder accusations, and the broader pattern of later political rumor and accusation that, while itself politically motivated, may preserve a kernel of contemporary suspicion not otherwise documented.

Evidence: Historians weigh the surviving commission report and witness testimony, the political context and interests of the investigating authority, comparative evidence about childhood epilepsy and accidental knife injury, and the later use of the Dmitry question by pretenders and their supporters, examined in Chapter 25.

Points of agreement: Historians agree that the surviving evidence cannot conclusively resolve the question, that Godunov had a clear political motive regardless of his actual involvement, and that the uncertainty itself became historically consequential.

Points of disagreement: Historians differ over how much weight to give a politically compromised official investigation versus the absence of any positive evidence of murder.

Student task: Identify what kind of additional evidence, if it existed, would most help historians resolve this question, and explain why the political consequences of the uncertainty may matter more for understanding the Time of Troubles than the unresolved medical question itself.

Multiple Perspectives

Muscovy in the 1590s

A service gentry landholder in the central districts: For a modest pomestie holder dependent on peasant labor to meet military service obligations, the 1580s and 1590s brought growing anxiety over peasant flight to wealthier estates, and correspondingly strong support for the restrictions on peasant departure examined in Section 24.3, on which his own capacity to serve, and therefore his own landholding, increasingly depended.
A peasant household in a war-affected northwestern district: For a peasant family facing continued taxation, the lingering effects of Oprichnina disruption, and narrowing legal avenues for relocation, the tightening restrictions of these years represented a closing door, making flight toward frontier regions, however risky, an increasingly attractive alternative to remaining on an overburdened estate.
A boyar at the Moscow court: For a leading boyar observing Boris Godunov's steady accumulation of authority during Fyodor's reign, the 1590s presented a delicate calculation between accommodation and resistance, complicated further by the uncertain succession and the unresolved question of Dmitry's death, which offered both a potential future rallying point and a source of genuine political danger.

These perspectives illustrate why the same set of developments — war strain, restricted mobility, frontier expansion, dynastic uncertainty — could appear, from different social positions, as a matter of survival, opportunity, or political calculation.

Cause and Effect

The Making of a Fragile State

Long-Term Conditions

The Muscovite service-state structure examined in Chapter 22, dependent on stable peasant labor and continuous dynastic legitimacy for its fiscal and political functioning.

Intermediate Developments

Decades of Livonian War taxation and the disruptions of the Oprichnina; the death of Tsarevich Ivan in 1581; the progressive restriction of peasant mobility through the "forbidden years."

Short-Term Triggers

Continued Crimean raiding demanding southern military resources; the unresolved death of Tsarevich Dmitry in 1591; the death of Fyodor I without an heir in January 1598.

Effects

A succession resolved only through the non-dynastic election of Boris Godunov; a state entering the seventeenth century with economic strain, restive peasants, frontier insecurity, and contested political legitimacy simultaneously in play, examined further in Chapter 25.

Historical complexity: No single factor—not dynastic extinction alone, not economic strain alone, not frontier pressure alone—explains why 1598 proved so dangerous; each condition made the others harder to manage.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Succession Before and After 1598

What Stayed the Same

  • The institutional structures of Muscovite government — the Boyar Duma, the prikazy, and the service nobility — continued to function, and the Zemsky Sobor could still be convened as a consultative and legitimating mechanism, as it was to resolve the 1598 succession.
  • Court factional competition for influence, a persistent feature of Muscovite politics since at least the minority of Ivan IV, continued after 1598 much as before.

What Changed

  • For the first time since the establishment of Moscow's political preeminence, the throne passed to a ruler without direct Rurikid descent, removing a previously unquestioned source of legitimacy.
  • The accumulated economic and social strain examined in this chapter, largely absent or less severe during earlier Muscovite succession crises, combined with dynastic uncertainty in a way it had not in 1533.

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both institutional continuity and the specific, unprecedented change in the source of legitimacy helps explain why 1598, unlike 1533, opened the door to the sustained crisis examined in Chapter 25.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Deserted Village Household

The fiscal registers examined in Section 24.2 record, district by district, the departure of individual peasant households, but behind each brief "empty" (pusto) entry lay a household's decision, often made under severe pressure, to abandon a home, often generations old, and seek terms elsewhere. A peasant family facing an exit fee it could no longer afford, mounting service and tax obligations, and narrowing legal windows for departure confronted difficult choices: remain and accept worsening terms, negotiate informally with a landholder desperate enough to overlook irregularities in order to retain labor, or flee outright, risking classification as a fugitive subject to the search-and-return provisions accompanying the restrictions examined in Section 24.3.

Flight itself carried substantial risk and hardship: a family moving toward the southern frontier faced the same Crimean raiding danger examined in Section 24.4, while a family moving toward Siberian territory, however distant that region remained from most peasant experience in this period, faced an unfamiliar climate, uncertain relations with indigenous populations, and the physical hardship of the journey itself. For most ordinary Muscovite households in these decades, the large political developments this chapter traces — war finance, frontier policy, and dynastic uncertainty at court — translated into concrete, immediate questions about tax burdens, landlord demands, and the shrinking legal space within which a family could seek a better situation.

Historical Vocabulary
Zapovednye Leta ("Forbidden Years")

Zapovednye leta refers to specific years, or districts, in the late sixteenth century during which the Muscovite state temporarily suspended the customary Yuriev Day right of peasant departure examined in this chapter's Section 24.3 and this chapter's Yuriev Day vocabulary feature. The precise chronology, legal mechanism, and territorial scope of these measures remain incompletely documented and are debated among historians, some of whom question whether a single comprehensive decree ever existed.

The term matters historically because it marks a significant, if incomplete and contested, stage in the long-term restriction of Muscovite peasant mobility, situated between the regulated departure rights of the 1497 Sudebnik examined in Chapter 21 and the fully consolidated hereditary serfdom of the 1649 Sobornoe Ulozhenie examined in Chapter 27. Students should understand the "forbidden years" as an important step in a gradual, contested process rather than as the single moment serfdom was "created."

Regional Context

The Siberian Frontier

From the perspective of Moscow, the events examined in Section 24.5 might have appeared as a promising but distant commercial venture, financed by a private merchant family and defended by state garrisons established only after the fact. From the perspective of the Khanate of Sibir and the indigenous Khanty, Mansi, and Turkic-speaking communities of the region, the arrival of Yermak's Cossacks and the subsequent construction of Muscovite forts represented an entirely different kind of event: the beginning of sustained outside military and administrative intrusion into a political and economic order that had existed independently of Moscow.

Khan Kuchum's continued resistance after the loss of his capital, and the years required for Muscovite administration to establish durable control even over the immediate region around Tobolsk, illustrate that the Siberian frontier was not simply awaiting incorporation into an expanding Muscovite state, but was itself a contested political space whose eventual, gradual absorption — a process Chapter 28 will trace across many subsequent decades — depended on continued investment of resources that the state examined throughout this chapter could only partially spare from its western and southern commitments.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the condition of Muscovy in the years between the death of Ivan IV in 1584 and the extinction of the Moscow Rurikid line in 1598, arguing that the succession crisis of that year cannot be understood as a sudden or purely dynastic event but must instead be explained through the convergence of several accumulated pressures.

The chapter began by tracing the consequences of Ivan IV's later reign: the prolonged and ultimately unsuccessful Livonian War, the lingering disruptions of the Oprichnina, and a royal household narrowed by the disputed death of the capable Tsarevich Ivan in 1581, leaving the less politically forceful Fyodor as heir. It then examined how decades of war taxation and population movement produced fiscal registers recording widespread "depopulation," while cautioning that this term often concealed relocation rather than genuine population loss, and explaining why this distinction nonetheless mattered enormously for a service-state structure dependent on stable, taxable peasant households.

The chapter traced the parallel tightening of restrictions on peasant mobility through the incompletely documented "forbidden years," situating this development within the long-term trajectory from the regulated Yuriev Day departure rights of the 1497 Sudebnik toward the fully consolidated serfdom of 1649, while cautioning against treating the 1580s and 1590s as though they had already produced hereditary bondage. It examined the southern steppe frontier's chronic vulnerability to Crimean Tatar raiding, including the catastrophic 1571 burning of Moscow and the reassuring, but not conclusive, Muscovite victory at Molodi in 1572.

The chapter then turned to the beginning of Siberian expansion through Yermak's Cossack expedition, which emerged from the frontier milieu of the Stroganov family's domains, carefully distinguishing this opening episode from the fuller conquest and settlement examined in Chapter 28, noting that historians dispute how directly the Stroganovs initiated the campaign, and emphasizing that state growth and state strain were occurring simultaneously rather than as alternatives to one another.

Turning to the royal court, the chapter examined Fyodor I's reign with appropriate caution regarding the sources' characterization of his political passivity, while crediting the reign with genuine achievements, above all the establishment of the Moscow Patriarchate in 1589. It traced Boris Godunov's accumulation of effective governing authority through kinship, patronage, and administrative competence, while insisting on the crucial distinction between Godunov's derivative authority before 1598 and his later claim to the throne itself.

The chapter gave sustained, methodologically careful attention to the 1591 death of Tsarevich Dmitry at Uglich, presenting the official commission's finding of accidental death alongside the enduring, unresolvable suspicion of assassination, and emphasizing that the historical significance of the episode lies less in its unresolved factual question than in the political uses to which its uncertainty was later put.

Finally, the chapter examined the death of Fyodor I in January 1598 and the Zemsky Sobor's election of Boris Godunov, arguing that dynastic extinction alone does not explain the ensuing crisis; rather, the disappearance of a centuries-old source of political legitimacy at a moment when economic, social, and frontier pressures were already severe converted a demographic fact into a genuine structural vulnerability.

These lessons—distinguishing structural conditions from immediate triggers, reading fiscal and investigative sources critically, and recognizing that state expansion and state fragility can occur together—prepare students directly for Chapter 25's account of how these accumulated vulnerabilities erupted into the Time of Troubles, including famine, pretenders claiming to be the "resurrected" Dmitry, and the near-collapse of the Muscovite state.

Key Terms

Oprichnina aftermath
The lasting economic, social, and administrative disruption in districts affected by Ivan IV's Oprichnina, persisting after its formal end in 1572.
Livonian War
Ivan IV's prolonged, ultimately unsuccessful war (1558–1583) for Baltic territory, a major source of late sixteenth-century fiscal and military strain.

The historian's distinction between genuine population loss and peasant movement to other districts, both of which could appear identically as "empty" entries in fiscal registers.

Muscovite land and fiscal registers recording households, land, and tax obligations by district, a major source for the demographic evidence examined in this chapter.
Yuriev Day
The traditional two-week autumn period during which Muscovite peasants held a legally regulated right to change landholders, formalized in the 1497 Sudebnik.

The exit payment a departing peasant owed a landholder under the Yuriev Day system.

The "forbidden years," a late sixteenth-century policy temporarily suspending Yuriev Day departure rights in specified years or districts.

A peasant who left an estate without legal authorization, subject to search-and-return provisions that expanded during this period.
Pomestie
Conditional service landholding, introduced in Chapter 22, whose holders' military capacity depended on stable peasant labor, examined here under strain.

Crimean khan who burned Moscow in 1571 and led a second, defeated invasion in 1572.

Fortified defensive lines maintained along Muscovy's southern frontier against steppe raiding.

Cossack leader whose expedition, beginning around 1581, opened Muscovite military engagement with the Khanate of Sibir.

The Muscovite assembly of the land, whose 1598 convocation elected Boris Godunov tsar following the end of the Rurikid line.

Ivan IV's youngest son, whose disputed 1591 death at Uglich became a central, politically exploitable uncertainty.
Rurikid dynasty
The princely lineage, traced to the founding traditions examined in Chapter 7, whose Moscow ruling branch ended with Fyodor I's death in 1598.

Political authority grounded in recognized hereditary descent, the traditional foundation of Muscovite rulership disrupted by the events of this chapter.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has carried students through the fourteen years separating Ivan IV's formidable, exhausting reign from the extinction of his dynasty, tracing how war burdens, peasant flight, frontier warfare, and an increasingly fragile royal household combined to leave Muscovy in a genuinely precarious condition by 1598. The chapter has resisted two tempting but misleading narratives: that the Time of Troubles simply followed automatically from dynastic extinction, and that Muscovy in these years was a state in simple, uninterrupted decline. Instead, it has shown a state simultaneously extending its authority into Siberia and struggling to hold its own social and fiscal foundations together — a genuinely complex historical situation that resists easy summary.

The chapter's central methodological lesson — that a historical crisis requires both a trigger and the structural conditions that make the trigger dangerous — will recur throughout this textbook, particularly in Chapter 25's account of how these accumulated vulnerabilities erupted into famine, rebellion, foreign intervention, and civil war, and in later chapters examining other moments when Russian and Soviet political systems confronted succession or legitimacy crises under conditions of accumulated strain.

As the textbook proceeds to Chapter 25's examination of the Time of Troubles, students should carry forward this chapter's insistence on holding multiple causal threads together, its caution in reading politically compromised sources such as the Uglich commission report, and its recognition that state growth and state vulnerability are not opposites but can occur within the same institutions at the same historical moment.
