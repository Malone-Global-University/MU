Part XI — The Post-Stalin Soviet Union

Gorbachev, Perestroika, and Glasnost

A History of Russia: From the Eurasian World to the Russian Federation

Chapter Opening

A paraphrase of a sentiment Mikhail Gorbachev later reported in his memoirs and interviews, recalling a conversation with his wife Raisa in the early 1980s: that the country could not continue to live as it was. Like all later recollections, it expresses how a participant remembered his motives, not a verifiable record of the words spoken.

On the evening of March 10, 1985, Konstantin Chernenko, the General Secretary of the Communist Party of the Soviet Union, died after months of illness. He was the third holder of that office to die in less than three years. Within hours the Politburo, the party's senior decision-making body, met in Moscow to decide who would succeed him, and on March 11 a plenum of the Central Committee confirmed its choice: Mikhail Sergeyevich Gorbachev, then fifty-four years old, the youngest full member of the Politburo. His selection was not a revolutionary event. It was an ordinary act of Soviet succession, carried out by the same small group of senior officials who had chosen earlier leaders. Yet the man chosen would, within six years, preside over the transformation of the Soviet political system, the end of the Communist Party's constitutional monopoly, the revolutions of 1989 in Eastern Europe, and the end of the Cold War.

This chapter examines that transformation from 1982 to the end of 1990. It is built around a central question: why did an attempt to renew and strengthen Soviet socialism produce economic disruption, unprecedented political openness, institutional conflict, and a fundamental change in the international system? The question deliberately avoids assuming the answer. Gorbachev did not come to power with a program to abolish the Soviet Union or to introduce capitalism. His aims shifted across the late 1980s as early measures failed to deliver the improvements he expected, as the public responded in unanticipated ways, and as opponents and allies pushed in different directions. Understanding that evolution is the chapter's first task.

The chapter's second task is to separate processes that contemporaries and later writers often merged. Perestroika (restructuring) concerned the reorganization of economic, administrative, and eventually political institutions. Glasnost (openness) concerned public information, criticism, and debate. Democratization concerned elections, representative bodies, and the constitutional basis of political authority. They were connected, and Gorbachev came to treat each as necessary to the others, but they had different origins, different timetables, and different consequences. The enterprise law of 1987, the Party Conference of 1988, and the elections of 1989 show why they cannot be told as one story.

The people who experienced these years included party secretaries and factory directors, but also coal miners in Siberia and the Donbas, firefighters at a reactor in northern Ukraine, editors who suddenly could print what had been forbidden, cooperative restaurateurs, pensioners standing in queues for soap and sugar, historians reading newly opened files, and citizens of Warsaw, Budapest, Leipzig, Prague, and Bucharest whose own movements determined the course of 1989. Evidence for the period is unusually rich and unusually contested: Politburo transcripts and diaries released after 1991, contemporary newspapers and television, memoirs by participants who had reasons to shape their reputations, official statistics of uncertain reliability, and the records of Western and Eastern European governments.

The chapter also continues a theme running through Part XI. Chapters 63 and 64 described a late Soviet society that combined material security, expanding education, and considerable global power with economic stagnation, censorship, nationality tensions, and growing cynicism. Gorbachev inherited those conditions, but inheriting them did not determine what he would do with them. Other leaders, drawing on the same evidence, might have chosen continued gradual adjustment, a Chinese-style liberalization of the economy without political change, or more coercive discipline. The chapter therefore treats Gorbachev's choices as choices, made with incomplete information under real constraints.

Chapter 65 ends at the close of 1990, with the Soviet Union still intact, Gorbachev president, Germany reunified, and the Cold War effectively over. It does not narrate the sovereignty declarations of the union republics, the struggle over the Union Treaty, the August 1991 coup, or the dissolution of the USSR; those belong to Chapter 66. Retrospective knowledge of 1991 must not distort the account of 1985–1990. Participants at the time did not know how the story would end, and the chapter asks students to reason as historians, not as viewers who already know the final scene.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Compare the leadership of Yuri Andropov and Konstantin Chernenko as distinct responses to Soviet institutional difficulties.
  2. Explain how Gorbachev reached the General Secretaryship in March 1985 and why his initial program emphasized acceleration rather than systemic change.
  3. Distinguish perestroika, glasnost, and democratization as separate but interacting processes.
  4. Analyze the causes, course, and consequences of the Chernobyl disaster, separating confirmed effects from disputed estimates.
  5. Evaluate why the Law on State Enterprise and the cooperative law did not produce the stable economic improvement their authors expected.
  6. Trace the expansion of glasnost from controlled criticism to open debate over Stalinism, culture, the environment, and religion.
  7. Explain the institutional changes of 1988–1990, including the Congress of People's Deputies, the 1989 elections, the revision of Article 6, and the Soviet presidency.
  8. Assess the claim that democratization either strengthened or destabilized Soviet government, using evidence.
  9. Describe the principles and diplomacy of "new thinking," from Geneva to the Charter of Paris.
  10. Contrast the revolutions of 1989 in Poland, Hungary, East Germany, Czechoslovakia, Bulgaria, and Romania, and weigh Soviet policy against local agency.
  11. Contextualize Gorbachev's decisions within the information and constraints available at the time, avoiding retrospective inevitability.
  12. Evaluate primary sources from the period, identifying authorship, purpose, and limitations.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did a generation of Soviet leaders who had built careers inside the system conclude that the system required fundamental change?
  2. How did Gorbachev's goals change between 1985 and 1990, and what evidence shows that change?
  3. Why did partial economic reform produce shortages and disruption instead of the intended efficiency?
  4. What was the relationship between the Chernobyl disaster and the development of glasnost, and how much weight should historians give it?
  5. How did reopening the historical record on Stalinism affect the legitimacy of Soviet institutions?
  6. In what sense were the 1989 elections competitive, and in what sense were they restricted?
  7. What roles did Soviet policy and local movements each play in the revolutions of 1989?
  8. Did Gorbachev's reforms fail because they went too far, did not go far enough, or were sequenced in a way the existing structures could not sustain?

Historical Context

The Soviet Union of 1982 was a federal state of fifteen union republics, with a population of roughly 270 million, governed through a party-state in which the Communist Party's leading role was written into Article 6 of the 1977 Constitution (Chapter 63). Economic coordination rested on central planning administered by ministries, with prices set administratively and most producer goods allocated rather than sold. This system had built heavy industry, a large military, and a literate urbanized society (Chapters 55–57), and it had survived the Second World War and postwar reconstruction (Chapters 59–60). Under Khrushchev it had attempted partial reform and ideological relaxation (Chapters 61–62); under Brezhnev it had settled into what the leadership called stability and what later critics called stagnation (Chapter 63).

By the early 1980s several pressures converged. Official growth rates had fallen steadily across the 1970s and early 1980s, and independent estimates, both Western and later Russian, suggested that real growth was lower than the official figures. Extensive growth, based on adding labor, capital, and raw materials, was giving way to a situation in which labor supplies were tightening and new resource extraction in Siberia was costly. The economy depended heavily on exports of oil and gas to earn the hard currency needed to import grain, machinery, and consumer goods. Agriculture, despite heavy investment, required grain imports in poor harvest years. Soviet technology lagged behind the West in computing and several civilian sectors, even while the military-industrial complex sustained strategic parity with the United States (Chapters 62–63).

Politically, the leadership was aging. Brezhnev's long tenure had stabilized careers within the party apparatus but also slowed the replacement of officials, producing a Politburo whose average age was close to seventy by the early 1980s. The nomenklatura system, the party's control over appointments to important posts, rewarded loyalty and caution. Society was more educated and urban than in Stalin's time, with a large stratum of specialists and professionals whose expectations exceeded what the system supplied. Dissent, discussed in Chapter 64, was limited in size and heavily repressed, but unofficial culture, informal economies, and widespread private skepticism about official ideology were broader. Nationality tensions existed beneath formal proclamations of friendship among peoples, and the war in Afghanistan, begun in December 1979, was draining resources without a clear political outcome.

The international environment had also deteriorated. The détente of the 1970s had eroded after the Afghan invasion, the deployment of intermediate-range missiles in Europe by both alliances, and the military buildup and rhetoric of the early Reagan administration. In September 1983 a Soviet fighter shot down Korean Air Lines Flight 007, and in the autumn of 1983 a NATO exercise, Able Archer, coincided with a period of acute Soviet anxiety about a possible Western first strike. The Soviet leadership, therefore, faced domestic economic strain and an arms competition with an economy several times larger, at the same time. Historians continue to debate how much each factor weighed in leadership decisions, and this chapter treats the debate as open.

One further context matters: Eastern Europe. Since 1945 the Soviet Union had maintained a bloc of allied communist governments, held together by party ties, economic integration in the Council for Mutual Economic Assistance, the Warsaw Pact, and the demonstrated willingness to use force in Hungary in 1956 and Czechoslovakia in 1968 (Chapters 62–63). The Polish crisis of 1980–1981, in which the independent trade union Solidarity challenged the party before martial law was imposed in December 1981, showed that these governments faced deep domestic legitimacy problems. Whether Moscow would continue to guarantee them by force was an open question in 1985, and its answer would shape the events of 1989.

Section 65.1

65.1 — Yuri Andropov: Discipline, Reform, and the Limits of Renewal, 1982–1984

Leonid Brezhnev died on November 10, 1982, and two days later the Central Committee elected Yuri Andropov General Secretary. Andropov, born in 1914, had served as ambassador to Hungary during the 1956 uprising and then as a Central Committee secretary before leading the KGB from 1967 to 1982. His KGB career gave him an unusually broad view of Soviet conditions: the security service gathered information on corruption, economic failures, popular attitudes, and dissent, and it also suppressed the last. His elevation was the result of a contest in which Brezhnev's long-time associates, notably Chernenko, were outmaneuvered by a coalition including senior figures in the military and the foreign ministry. In June 1983 he added the largely ceremonial post of chairman of the Presidium of the Supreme Soviet.

Andropov's program was one of discipline and improved administration. Its most visible element was a campaign against absenteeism and idleness at work: in 1983 police and volunteer patrols checked shops, bathhouses, and cinemas in Moscow and other cities during working hours and questioned people found there. The campaign was unpopular and its economic effect was limited, but it signaled that the leadership regarded indiscipline as a systemic problem. He also encouraged prosecutions of corruption, including among officials whose patronage networks had flourished under Brezhnev, and replaced a significant share of regional party secretaries and ministers. In speeches he acknowledged that Soviet leaders did not fully understand the society they governed, and he permitted limited experiments in giving enterprises more latitude in planning, such as a 1983 experiment in several ministries.

Andropov also promoted younger officials. Mikhail Gorbachev, whom he had known from the spa region of the North Caucasus where he took holidays, gained influence; Yegor Ligachev was brought into the Central Committee apparatus to supervise cadre policy; and Nikolai Ryzhkov was appointed to the Secretariat. Historians often describe this group as the "Andropov generation" of reformers, but the label hides disagreements: Ligachev and Gorbachev would later part ways sharply over the pace and direction of change. Andropov's approach should be distinguished from later perestroika. It assumed that renewed discipline, better management, and a purge of corrupt cadres could revitalize the existing system. It did not envisage open public criticism, competitive elections, or market institutions.

His capacity to carry out even this limited agenda was constrained by his health. He suffered from kidney failure, was largely absent from public view from the late summer of 1983, and died on February 9, 1984, after about fifteen months in office. The international climate in these months was tense, marked by the Korean airliner shootdown, the deployment of intermediate-range missiles in Western Europe, and the breakdown of arms-control talks. Andropov's brief tenure thus left a mixed legacy: a recognition at the top that change was needed, a generation of promoted officials, and a reform agenda that rested on administrative pressure.

Section 65.2

65.2 — Konstantin Chernenko and the Succession Crisis, 1984–1985

When Andropov died, the Politburo again confronted the succession question. The younger Gorbachev was by then a plausible candidate, and Andropov is reported by several participants to have wished him to chair the Secretariat, but the older members preferred continuity. On February 13, 1984, the Central Committee elected Konstantin Chernenko, born in 1911, who had been Brezhnev's closest administrative aide and head of the Central Committee's General Department. Defense Minister Dmitry Ustinov and Prime Minister Nikolai Tikhonov were among his backers. Chernenko was already gravely ill with a lung disease, and his election has generally been interpreted as an interim solution that allowed the old guard to postpone a generational change.

Chernenko's fourteen months in office restored several Brezhnev-era practices. Pressure on workplaces eased, criticism of past personnel practices was muted, and the language of "developed socialism" returned. He supported an educational reform emphasizing vocational training, endorsed a large land-reclamation program, and did not abandon the 1983 managerial experiments. In foreign policy, the Soviet Union boycotted the 1984 Los Angeles Olympic Games and maintained a hard line, although exploratory contacts with the United States resumed late in the year, and arms-control talks were scheduled to restart in Geneva in early 1985. It would be a mistake to treat his tenure as simply inert. It shows how the existing system, left alone, tended toward caution, bureaucratic bargaining, and delay.

In practice, authority within the leadership was increasingly shared. As Chernenko's illness worsened, Gorbachev frequently chaired meetings of the Politburo and the Secretariat, a role that gave him exposure to the full range of party business and to officials across the country. The deaths of Defense Minister Ustinov in December 1984 and the aging of other senior figures also reduced the weight of Chernenko's backers. Competing power centers included Viktor Grishin, the Moscow party chief; Grigory Romanov, the Leningrad party chief and a Secretary; and Andrei Gromyko, the long-serving foreign minister, whose support for Gorbachev later proved important.

By early 1985, three consecutive deaths in leadership posts had made the issue of age impossible to ignore. Chernenko's final months made the contrast between a dwindling gerontocracy and a society that had changed since 1964 visible even to the leadership. When he died on March 10, 1985, there was no obvious heir within the older generation. Whether the resulting choice reflected a deep consensus for reform or simply a decision that someone younger was needed is a question that the next section addresses.

Section 65.3

65.3 — Mikhail Gorbachev's Rise and the Leadership Transition of March 1985

Mikhail Sergeyevich Gorbachev was born on March 2, 1931, in the village of Privolnoe in the Stavropol region of southern Russia, into a peasant family whose members had experienced collectivization, the famine years, and the war; both of his grandfathers had been arrested during the 1930s repressions and later released. As a teenager he worked on a combine harvester and received a state decoration for his harvest work. He studied law at Moscow State University from 1950, joined the party in 1952, and returned to Stavropol, where he rose through the Komsomol and party ranks. In 1970 he became first secretary of the Stavropol regional party committee, a position that placed him in contact with senior visitors to the nearby spas, including Andropov. In 1978 he moved to Moscow as the Central Committee secretary responsible for agriculture, became a candidate member of the Politburo in 1979, and a full member in 1980.

Gorbachev's agricultural portfolio was not a record of success: harvests under his supervision were uneven, and the sector remained dependent on imports. But the post exposed him to the economy's structural problems, and his later account stresses conversations with officials, economists, and academics in the 1980s that persuaded him that the whole system of administration needed change. He also benefited from the patronage of Andropov and Mikhail Suslov, and from an early reputation for energy, openness to discussion, and a conversational style that contrasted sharply with that of older leaders. In December 1984, speaking at a conference on ideology, he called for more openness in public life and for wider use of the term glasnost, although at that stage he meant improved flow of information within the existing system.

The leadership transition itself was brisk. Chernenko died on the evening of March 10, 1985. The Politburo met that night and the following morning, and by the account of several participants Foreign Minister Andrei Gromyko, a figure of great seniority, spoke in favor of Gorbachev, praising his qualities and noting that he had in effect been running the Secretariat. Rival candidates Grishin and Romanov lacked sufficient support. A Central Committee plenum on March 11 formally elected Gorbachev General Secretary, and a long list of senior positions was soon reshuffled. In July 1985 Gromyko moved to the ceremonial post of chairman of the Presidium of the Supreme Soviet and Eduard Shevardnadze, the Georgian party chief with no diplomatic experience, became foreign minister. Romanov was removed from the Politburo in July, and Grishin was replaced in Moscow by Boris Yeltsin in December. Ryzhkov became chairman of the Council of Ministers in September 1985 and Ligachev was promoted to the Secretariat as the senior party figure for ideology and cadres.

The coalition that elected Gorbachev was not a unified reform movement. Some supported him because they believed a younger man would strengthen the Soviet position against the United States, some because they sought economic improvement, and some because they believed that he would continue the discipline of Andropov. Others preferred the security of established practices. The initial reform coalition, in other words, was a coalition of different reform expectations, and the divergences among its members would become more visible as the program broadened. The significance of the transition was not that a revolution had occurred but that a leader who accepted the need for substantial change now controlled the machinery of appointment, the Politburo agenda, and the party's media.

Section 65.4

65.4 — Diagnosing Soviet Problems: Acceleration, Modernization, and the Reform Imperative

Gorbachev's first concern was economic performance. He and his advisers believed that the Soviet economy could not sustain simultaneously the military competition, the social commitments of the Brezhnev years, and the investment needed to modernize industry. They pointed to falling growth rates, the high consumption of energy and raw materials per unit of output, the poor quality of many manufactured goods, and the growing dependence on imports of food and technology. They also pointed to corruption, absenteeism, and drunkenness, which they regarded as symptoms of a loss of social discipline. Agricultural difficulties, including heavy losses between farm and consumer, persisted despite large investment. Bureaucratic obstruction, in the form of overlapping ministerial controls and the habit of reporting inflated results, made it difficult to know what was actually happening.

The initial program, formally announced at the April 1985 Central Committee plenum, was uskorenie, acceleration of social and economic development. Its central instrument was a large increase in investment in machine-building, with the aim of retooling industry with advanced equipment, supported by scientific and technological progress, personnel renewal, and the strengthening of discipline. This was a program of intensification, not of structural change; the word perestroika appeared in speeches, but it was not yet a settled program. The rhetoric of the period promised to improve socialism, to return to supposed Leninist principles that had been distorted in the Brezhnev years, and to bring the party back to its tasks of leadership. Gorbachev and many of his closest colleagues believed in these goals; reading them as a mere cover for a hidden plan to end socialism misreads the evidence of the period.

Why did the early approach emphasize improving socialism rather than replacing it? The first reason was political. A new General Secretary needed the support of the Central Committee, the regional party organizations, and the ministries, and a radical program would have risked the coalition that had made him leader. The second was intellectual. Gorbachev's advisers drew on earlier reform traditions, including the Kosygin reforms of 1965, Hungarian experiments, and the Leninist New Economic Policy (Chapter 53), which suggested that a socialist state could use market elements without abandoning public ownership. The third was experiential. Officials who had spent careers in the system knew its strengths, including full employment, social security, and military power, and believed they could correct its defects.

Historians nonetheless note that the diagnosis contained a tension. If the problems were failures of discipline and technology, they could be addressed by campaigns and investment. If they were failures of incentives and information built into planning itself, they could not. Over the next two years the leadership gradually moved from the first diagnosis to the second, and with that shift came a widening of what counted as reform. The next sections trace how events, including a failed campaign and a nuclear disaster, accelerated that shift.

Historical Vocabulary
Uskorenie, Perestroika, Glasnost

Definition: Uskorenie ("acceleration") was the 1985–1986 slogan for speeding economic growth through investment, technology, and discipline. Perestroika ("restructuring") meant changing institutions, and by 1987 referred to the broad reform program. Glasnost ("openness" or "publicity") meant public disclosure and discussion.

Historical context: The terms were not new; glasnost had been invoked in Russian reform contexts since the nineteenth century, including during the Great Reforms (Chapter 42). In the Gorbachev period their meanings widened as policy changed.

Why it matters: Treating the three as synonyms hides the sequence by which a program of managerial acceleration became a program of institutional and political change.

65.5 — The Anti-Alcohol Campaign and the Social Costs of Administrative Reform

The first major social policy of the Gorbachev leadership was a campaign against alcohol abuse, launched by a Central Committee resolution of May 1985. Alcoholism was a recognized national problem: it contributed to workplace accidents, absenteeism, domestic violence, and ill health, and Soviet demographers had documented rising adult male mortality since the 1960s (Chapters 63–64). The campaign had supporters in the party, notably Ligachev, in the medical community, and among many women who bore the household consequences of heavy drinking. Its design followed the administrative methods familiar from earlier campaigns: reduce the supply, restrict the hours and locations of sale, raise the minimum drinking age to twenty-one, punish drinking at work and in public places, and require party members and officials to set an example.

The measures were substantial. Production of vodka and other spirits was cut sharply, many liquor stores were closed, and sales were limited to the afternoon hours. Some wine-producing regions, including Moldova, Georgia, and Crimea, saw vineyards uprooted or curtailed, a policy that angered growers and local officials. Alcohol-free weddings and celebrations were promoted. In the first years, official data showed a decline in recorded consumption and, for a time, improvements in male life expectancy and in some indicators of workplace accidents and crime. Demographers continue to discuss how much of the improvement was attributable to the campaign, and how much of it was reversed after 1987.

The campaign also generated costs the planners had not foreseen. State revenue from alcohol sales, an important source of budget income, fell, deepening the budget deficit. Consumers shifted to illicit home distillation (samogon), which increased the consumption of sugar and produced shortages of it; some turned to industrial alcohols, perfumes, and other substitutes, with poisoning cases. Long queues for what remained in shops became a symbol of the campaign, and Gorbachev acquired the sarcastic nickname "mineral water secretary." Public resentment, especially among men, was widespread, and many officials quietly obstructed enforcement. By 1987–1988 the restrictions were eased in practice, and the campaign was largely abandoned.

The campaign matters for the history of perestroika because it showed the limits of administrative moralizing. It treated a social problem as a problem of supply and command, left demand and the incentives of local officials untouched, and ignored fiscal consequences. For some within the leadership, it demonstrated that decrees from above could not alter behavior without changes in incentives. It also belongs to the story of public trust: citizens who had been told that the new leadership would improve their lives experienced instead new restrictions.

Section 65.6

65.6 — The Twenty-Seventh Party Congress and the Search for Socialist Renewal, 1986

The Twenty-Seventh Congress of the CPSU met in Moscow from February 25 to March 6, 1986. Party congresses were ritual occasions at which the leadership presented an authoritative assessment of the country's condition and a program for the next five years, and delegates, selected through the apparatus, approved it. Gorbachev's report differed from its predecessors in tone. It criticized the stagnation of the late Brezhnev years, though without naming him, and spoke of the need for acceleration, technological renewal, and a "radical reform" of economic management. It also contained a new vocabulary, with frequent use of glasnost, democratization, and "new thinking," although these terms were attached to modest practical measures.

The Congress approved a new edition of the Party Program, with a more modest treatment of the transition to communism than earlier versions, and guidelines for the twelfth Five-Year Plan, which emphasized investment in machine-building and energy-saving technologies. It also renewed the Central Committee: a large share of members, as well as a number of senior secretaries and regional leaders, were replaced. Personnel change was a central instrument for Gorbachev, who needed officials committed to his program, and the Congress completed the transformation of the leadership begun after March 1985. The new composition did not eliminate disagreement. Ligachev, who emphasized discipline and ideological orthodoxy, and Yakovlev, who favored broader cultural and intellectual openness, represented diverging tendencies within the group around the General Secretary.

Boris Yeltsin, newly Moscow party secretary, delivered a speech to the Congress that criticized privileges and the slow pace of change. Some delegates also voiced concern about the extent of criticism of the past. These interventions show that the Congress was not a monolithic demonstration of support. The tension between reform-minded officials and the established apparatus, which had to implement the new policies, was visible already in 1986: many regional secretaries and ministers could accept the language of renewal but resisted measures that threatened their authority.

Historians read the Congress in two ways. One view regards it as a conservative event, in which the leadership reaffirmed the party's leading role, the plan, and socialism, and used reformist vocabulary without altering the structure. Another regards it as the point at which a reform language became official and thus available for use by those who wished to push further. Both observations are supported by the evidence; it was possible for the same document to be both.

Section 65.7

65.7 — The Chernobyl Nuclear Disaster, April 1986

At about 1:23 a.m. on April 26, 1986, Reactor Unit 4 of the Chernobyl Nuclear Power Station, in the northern Ukrainian SSR about 100 kilometers north of Kyiv and near the new town of Pripyat (Ukrainian: Prypiat), exploded during a safety test. The plant's four reactors were of the RBMK-1000 type, a graphite-moderated, water-cooled design used in the Soviet Union but not in Western Europe or North America. The test was intended to determine whether a turbine running down after a loss of external power could supply electricity to cooling pumps until emergency generators started. It had been postponed during the day, and the reactor was operated for hours at unstable low power with several safety systems disabled in order to complete the procedure.

Two categories of cause are generally distinguished. The first concerns the operators' actions: the reactor power fell much lower than the test program required, operators withdrew most of the control rods to restore power, and protective systems were bypassed. The second concerns the reactor's design. The RBMK had a positive void coefficient at low power, meaning that steam formation increased reactivity, and its control rods, when inserted from the top, initially displaced water with graphite-tipped sections, which momentarily increased reactivity in the lower core. When operators pressed the emergency shutdown button, the insertion of the rods produced a surge, and a steam explosion followed, then a second explosion that destroyed the reactor and scattered fuel and graphite. The first Soviet report to the International Atomic Energy Agency in August 1986 emphasized operator error; later analysis by the International Nuclear Safety Advisory Group (1992) gave greater weight to design deficiencies and to institutional failings, including incomplete communication of known design problems. Both factors are treated by specialists as contributing.

The immediate response was heroic and uncoordinated. Firefighters from the plant and from Pripyat extinguished fires on the roof of the adjacent turbine hall and prevented spread to Unit 3, receiving very high radiation doses; most of those who died from acute radiation syndrome in the following weeks were plant staff and firefighters. A government commission headed by Deputy Prime Minister Boris Shcherbina arrived on April 26, together with scientists such as Valery Legasov of the Kurchatov Institute. Helicopter crews dropped sand, boron, clay, and lead onto the burning reactor. The residents of Pripyat, about 49,000 people, were evacuated by bus on the afternoon of April 27, roughly thirty-six hours after the explosion, and over the following days and weeks a thirty-kilometer exclusion zone was established, with in total well over 100,000 people evacuated from the zone in 1986.

Mass mobilization followed. Soldiers, reservists, miners, construction workers, and engineers, later collectively called "liquidators," were brought in to decontaminate the area, to bury contaminated material, and to construct a concrete and steel enclosure over the destroyed unit, the "sarcophagus," completed in November 1986. Registered liquidators eventually numbered several hundred thousand. Their exposures varied enormously and were often poorly recorded.

Establishing the scale of consequences requires distinguishing categories of evidence. Two people died in the immediate explosion and building collapse. Acute radiation syndrome was diagnosed in over one hundred workers and firefighters, of whom twenty-eight died within a few months. Among those exposed as children to radioactive iodine, thyroid cancer rose sharply in Belarus, Ukraine, and parts of Russia, with thousands of cases attributed to the accident, though most were treatable and deaths were comparatively few. Projections of eventual cancer deaths are far more uncertain: the 2005 Chernobyl Forum, an assessment by United Nations agencies, estimated that up to several thousand deaths might eventually occur among the most heavily exposed groups, with a larger figure possible across the broader population, while some independent organizations have published estimates substantially higher, and others argue that effects at low doses cannot be reliably attributed. A single casualty figure cannot represent the disaster, and responsible accounts attribute any figure to its source and method.

Section 65.8

65.8 — Chernobyl, Government Secrecy, Public Trust, and Environmental Politics

The political significance of Chernobyl lay not only in the accident but in the way information about it was handled. Soviet practice treated information about accidents, particularly in the nuclear and military sectors, as a state secret. In the first hours, local officials lacked precise information, and some initial reports from the plant underestimated the situation. The Politburo was informed on April 26 and discussed the accident on following days. The Soviet public was not told. Evacuation of Pripyat was announced to residents on April 27 with an instruction that they could expect to return in a few days. In Kyiv and Minsk, authorities allowed normal life to continue, and May Day parades went ahead on May 1, partly to avoid alarm and partly because of institutional habit.

The first public notice came after Swedish monitors at the Forsmark nuclear station detected abnormal radiation on April 28 and the Soviet government was asked for an explanation. Late that evening, Soviet television carried a brief statement, only a few sentences long, that an accident had occurred and that measures were being taken. More detailed reports followed gradually. Gorbachev addressed the nation on television on May 14, nearly three weeks after the accident, attacking Western media coverage as exaggerated and malicious, while also acknowledging the seriousness of the event, praising those who had responded, and promising help for those affected. Historians read the speech as a mixture of necessary reassurance, defensiveness, and a limited advance in disclosure.

The social effects included widespread anxiety. People in affected regions received contradictory instructions and unreliable information about food, water, and safe areas. Rumors circulated and, in some places, supplies of food and medical advice were poorly organized. Scientists argued about radiation thresholds and health effects; some, such as Legasov, who presented a detailed report to the IAEA in Vienna in August 1986, were relatively frank, while others subordinated findings to official interests. In July 1987, a trial in Chernobyl town convicted the plant's director Viktor Bryukhanov, chief engineer Nikolai Fomin, and deputy chief engineer Anatoly Dyatlov, among others, of safety violations, while many critics felt that responsibility for the design and for institutional failings was not adequately addressed.

How much did Chernobyl contribute to glasnost? Gorbachev himself later said in published reflections that the accident was perhaps the real cause of the collapse of the Soviet Union, a statement that should be treated as his considered retrospective judgment, not as an established fact. Historians more cautiously identify several effects. It demonstrated the costs of secrecy and of a system in which bad news was suppressed. It strengthened the arguments of those, like Yakovlev, who wanted wider disclosure. It also generated environmental activism and distrust of official statements, especially in Ukraine and Belarus. It did not, by itself, cause glasnost: the term had been in use since 1984–1985, and the major expansion of press freedom occurred in 1987–1988, after the accident but through other political decisions. Chernobyl was a catalyst, not an origin.

Multiple Perspectives

Experiencing Chernobyl

Scientists and engineers: Specialists such as Legasov had to reconcile loyalty to the institutions that employed them with their technical judgment. Some pressed for candor, while others emphasized operator error, which protected the reactor design and its ministries.
Officials at the center and in the republics: Leaders had to weigh the risk of panic, the limits of their own information, the political cost of admitting a major failure, and the demand to protect the Soviet image abroad. Republic officials in Kyiv and Minsk lacked autonomy and depended on instructions from Moscow.
Emergency workers and liquidators: Firefighters, soldiers, and conscripted workers were exposed to extreme hazards, often with little protection or knowledge of the risk, and many suffered long-term health problems. Official recognition and benefits became a political issue in later years.
Evacuees and civilians in affected areas: Residents of Pripyat lost their homes and belongings; families in Belarus and Ukraine lived with contaminated land and uncertainty about food, children's health, and the reliability of official assurances.

The perspectives show that the same event produced different risks, information, and obligations, and that power over information shaped who bore the cost.

Section 65.9

65.9 — From Acceleration to Perestroika: The Broadening Reform Agenda, 1986–1987

By late 1986 the leadership could see that acceleration was not producing the expected results. Investment increases in machine-building raised costs and strained supply; the oil price fall in 1986, which cut the value of Soviet hard-currency exports by a large share, reduced import capacity and budget revenue; the anti-alcohol campaign had reduced revenue; and Chernobyl imposed substantial additional expense. Gorbachev concluded that personnel changes and technological investment would not suffice, and that managers, workers, and local officials needed incentives and accountability that the existing system did not provide.

Several measures of 1986 and early 1987 foreshadowed a wider agenda: a law on individual labor activity (adopted November 1986, effective May 1987) legalizing certain small-scale private services; a January 1987 decree allowing joint ventures with foreign firms; and measures to strengthen state control over product quality (gospriyemka), which created friction with managers. In December 1986 Gorbachev telephoned Andrei Sakharov in Gorky, where he had been in internal exile since 1980, and invited him to return to Moscow, an event that signaled a new approach to political prisoners and dissenters (Chapter 64).

The January 1987 plenum of the Central Committee was a turning point. Gorbachev proposed "democratization" of the party and of Soviet society, including the use of secret ballots and multiple candidates in elections of some party officials and enterprise managers, and criticized the "braking mechanism" in the system. The plenum was postponed several times, reflecting disagreement, and the resolution adopted was more cautious than his speech. A June 1987 plenum then approved the principles of a "radical reform" of economic management, the basis of the Law on State Enterprise. Opposition within the leadership focused on the pace and scope of change. Ligachev and others supported renewal but resisted measures that would weaken party control, while Yakovlev and other advisers pushed for broader political openness.

Yeltsin's break with the leadership in October–November 1987 revealed the tensions. At an October 21 plenum, he criticized the pace of reform and the conduct of Ligachev and others, and offered to resign from the Politburo; he was condemned at a Moscow party meeting on November 11 and removed from his post as Moscow first secretary. The affair damaged his official career but gave him public sympathy, since rumors of his speech circulated widely. The period therefore ended with a leader who had moved from acceleration to a broad agenda of restructuring, a leadership divided over its scope, and a public increasingly aware of the disagreements.

Section 65.10

65.10 — The Law on State Enterprise of 1987 and Economic Decentralization

The central legal instrument of economic perestroika was the Law on State Enterprise (Association), adopted by the Supreme Soviet on June 30, 1987, after a Central Committee plenum that month, and applied from January 1, 1988. It sought to change the relationship between the production enterprise and the ministry that supervised it. Under the planning system developed since the 1930s (Chapter 55), an enterprise received detailed targets for output, inputs, wages, and investment from the ministry and the state planning agency (Gosplan), and its managers were rewarded for meeting them. The law was intended to replace many of these directives with a framework of enterprise autonomy.

Its main principles were khozraschet, or full cost accounting and self-financing, under which enterprises were to cover their costs from revenue and retain profits for wages, bonuses, and investment; self-management, in which labor collectives would elect managers and form councils; and a shift from binding plan targets to "state orders" (goszakaz) for priority products, with enterprises free to seek other customers for the rest. It also envisaged gradual replacement of central allocation of supplies by wholesale trade among enterprises, reduction of the role of ministries, and a later reform of prices and finance. Enterprises could to some extent determine their own wage funds and, under additional measures of 1987 and 1988, some gained rights to deal directly with foreign partners.

The reform reflected Soviet and Eastern European traditions of economic reform, including debates of the 1960s and Hungarian experiments, and the arguments of economists such as Abel Aganbegyan and Tatyana Zaslavskaya, who had been calling for greater enterprise incentives since the early 1980s. It also reflected a political calculation: the leadership expected that managers and workers, given a stake in results, would support restructuring and counterbalance resistance from the ministries.

From the outset, however, the law left central institutions intact. Ministries continued to exist and to issue state orders; the planning agency still compiled the plan; most prices remained administratively set; and the supply system, the system of allocation of materials and equipment, had not been replaced. The relationship between formal legal autonomy and actual practice would determine the outcome, as the next sections explain.

Section 65.11

65.11 — Planning, Prices, Supply Networks, and the Contradictions of Partial Reform

The principal difficulty in the 1987 reform was that enterprise autonomy depended on conditions that had not been created. An enterprise that is free to choose what to produce, whom to supply, and what to pay needs prices that reflect scarcity, access to inputs through markets, and a credible budget constraint, under which losses cannot be covered automatically by the state. In the late 1980s, none of these existed. Prices of most goods, set by the state, bore little relation to costs or demand; the supply system relied on allocation, so that an enterprise could not simply purchase what it needed; and an enterprise that lost money could generally expect subsidies or bank credit.

State orders, introduced as a limited mechanism for priority output, in practice covered a very large share of production in many sectors during 1988, so that managers' actual freedom was narrow. Ministries retained influence through the allocation of "limits," the control of investment and the assignment of norms. Managers had incentives to maximize wage funds and bonuses, which were now less strictly tied to plan fulfillment, while prices and output decisions were still constrained. They could raise wages faster than productivity, and could improve apparent profits by altering product mix toward items favored by pricing rather than demand. At the same time, they could lose the guaranteed supplies on which they had depended, because the old compulsion on suppliers to fulfill plan deliveries had weakened.

The result, in the words of many economists then and since, was a hybrid in which some features of the plan had been loosened and none of the features of a market had been established. The IMF's historical analysis, and later accounts by Russian economists, emphasize this contradiction between formal decentralization and unreformed price and allocation systems. Leaders were aware of the problem and delayed price reform because raising retail prices, which had been held low by subsidy, risked social protest; the memory of the 1962 Novocherkassk events and of Polish price riots made the leadership cautious. A well-prepared price reform was thus postponed, and the contradictions deepened.

Section 65.12

65.12 — Cooperatives, Joint Ventures, and the Reemergence of Legal Private Enterprise

Alongside state enterprise reform, the leadership legalized several forms of non-state economic activity. The 1986 Law on Individual Labor Activity, in force from May 1987, permitted people to run small-scale services and crafts, such as repairs and tutoring, on their own account. The more consequential change was the Law on Cooperatives of May 1988, which allowed groups of citizens to form cooperatives to produce goods and services, hire members, buy inputs from various sources, and set prices for much of their output. Cooperatives were formally collective, not private, but in practice many functioned as small private firms. Within two years millions of people worked in the sector, in restaurants, repair shops, garment and furniture manufacture, construction, video salons, publishing, consulting, and trade.

Foreign joint ventures were authorized in January 1987 and soon extended. Initially, foreign partners could hold a minority share, and the ventures needed approval; later decrees relaxed restrictions. Joint ventures remained few and small in the early years, and hampered by inconvertibility of the ruble, uncertain legal rules, and the difficulty of obtaining supplies. In 1988–1989 the foreign trade system was also partly opened to enterprises, and the first commercial banks, often established by cooperatives and enterprises, appeared. Some state enterprises created affiliated cooperatives, through which they could sell goods at higher prices, and this practice became a source of both efficiency and abuse.

Reactions were sharply divided. Many consumers welcomed new services and goods, particularly in large cities, but resented cooperative prices, which were often much higher than state prices. Officials and workers in the state sector accused cooperatives of speculation, of diverting scarce inputs, and of profiting from the shortages the state sector was unable to resolve. Local authorities, and in some cases organized-crime groups, extorted payments from cooperatives. Tax policy shifted several times, and public opinion polls recorded ambivalence. It is important to distinguish legal cooperative enterprise from the longstanding illegal "second economy" of black-market trade, theft from workplaces, and informal exchange described in Chapters 63–64. Legalization brought elements of the second economy into the open, but also blurred the boundary, and some sources of initial capital for new businesses came from informal activity and from Komsomol-sponsored youth enterprises.

The importance of the non-state sector was both economic and political. It created experience, networks, and capital that would shape the post-Soviet economy (Chapter 67), and it signaled that the legitimacy of private initiative, long condemned, was being reassessed. But it did not provide a stable alternative to the state sector, and it contributed to the perception of inequality and unfairness that undermined support for reform.

Historical Vocabulary
Cooperative, State Order, and Self-Financing

Definition: A cooperative (kooperativ) was a group enterprise legalized in 1988 that could hire members, set many of its own prices, and keep profits after taxes. A state order (goszakaz) was an obligatory contract for products the state needed, which replaced some plan targets. Self-financing (khozraschet, "economic accounting") meant that an enterprise should cover its costs from its own revenue.

Historical context: The terms belong to the 1987–1990 reforms. "Cooperative" echoed the Leninist NEP-era cooperatives (Chapter 53) and, as a socially acceptable label, helped to legitimize activity that resembled private business.

Why it matters: Students should not read these terms as equivalents of market capitalism. They described hybrid forms that functioned within a still-planned economy.

Section 65.13

65.13 — Agricultural Reform, Farm Autonomy, Leasing, and Food Supply

Agriculture was Gorbachev's old portfolio and a persistent weakness of the Soviet economy (Chapter 56). The collective and state farm system (kolkhozy and sovkhozy) employed a large share of the rural labor force and absorbed substantial investment, but yields and productivity lagged behind those in Western Europe and North America, and large losses occurred in storage, transport, and processing. In November 1985 the leadership created a super-ministry, the State Agro-Industrial Committee (Agroprom), to coordinate farming, processing, and supply. It added another layer of administration without changing incentives, and was dismantled in stages after 1989.

Reform proposals in 1987–1989 turned toward contract and lease arrangements. Families or small groups were allowed to lease land and equipment from a farm and to farm it under contract, with the right to dispose of part of the output. A March 1989 Central Committee plenum on agriculture endorsed lease and contract forms, and a law on leasing was adopted that year. Legislation on land in 1990 granted lifetime inheritable possession to farmers, but did not establish full private ownership of land, which remained a bitterly contested issue. The reforms had to confront the opposition of farm chairmen, district officials, and many rural residents who feared the loss of security and the dismantling of the collective structure; take-up of leasing was limited and varied among regions.

Meanwhile, household plots and the "private subsidiary economy" continued to supply a significant portion of potatoes, vegetables, milk, and meat, though they occupied a small share of the land. Procurement prices rose, but retail food prices remained low and subsidized, so that the state paid heavily to purchase food and sell it cheaply, and consumers often found it unavailable. Grain imports remained high, and by 1989–1990 some cities introduced rationing coupons for basic foods such as sugar, tea, and meat products. Distribution failures, disruptions of supply between republics, and the withholding of produce by some regions contributed to shortages as much as production levels did.

The agricultural case thus repeated the broader pattern: partial reform in a context of unreformed prices and administrative control. It also illustrated regional diversity. Conditions in the Baltic republics, the Russian Black Earth region, Central Asian cotton districts, and the Siberian north differed widely, and republic governments increasingly sought control over their own agricultural resources, an issue that would resurface in Chapter 66.

Section 65.14

65.14 — Wages, Shortages, Inflationary Pressures, and the Deterioration of Consumer Markets

From 1988 consumer conditions deteriorated. Money incomes rose faster than the supply of consumer goods, because enterprises had greater freedom to pay wages and bonuses, cooperatives paid high wages, and the state budget deficit, widened by lost alcohol revenue, falling export earnings, subsidies, and investment commitments, was financed in part by credit and money creation. Because most prices were fixed, the excess of money did not show up primarily as rising prices. It appeared instead as empty shelves, queues, and growing savings that could not be spent on desired goods, a condition economists call repressed inflation, in contrast to open inflation, where prices rise.

Households responded by buying whatever was available, by hoarding durable goods and staples, and by turning to alternatives: collective-farm markets, cooperative shops with higher prices, informal exchange networks (blat), and workplace distribution of scarce goods. Goods that had been intermittently scarce, such as meat, sugar, soap, and detergent, became harder to find, and local governments introduced coupons to limit purchases. Shortages varied by place; Moscow and Leningrad, as showcase cities, were supplied better than many provincial towns, and non-residents were sometimes restricted from buying. This difference fed resentment, as did regional restrictions on exporting goods out of republics and cities, which began to appear in 1989–1990 and contributed to supply breakdowns.

These conditions affected the political climate. Surveys and press accounts showed growing dissatisfaction with the reform leadership, but the distribution of blame was contested: some citizens blamed bureaucrats and cooperatives, some the reformers, some the legacy of the plan. In the miners' strikes of 1989 (§65.26), shortages of soap and food were among the central grievances. The deterioration of consumer markets also reduced the credibility of arguments that reform would quickly raise living standards.

Everyday Life and Social History

Household Shopping and Economic Uncertainty

Shopping for a family in 1989–1990 involved time, networks, and calculation. A worker might stop at several shops on the way home to check for deliveries, carry a string bag in case something appeared, or join a queue without knowing what was being sold. Women, who still performed most household work in addition to paid employment (Chapter 64), carried most of this burden. Families supplemented purchases with produce from relatives' gardens, with goods bought at work through the trade-union or enterprise distribution, and with items obtained through acquaintances. Coupons for sugar, tea, or soap guaranteed a quantity only when the item was in stock.

Experiences differed. Residents of Moscow, with better supply and higher incomes, were able to use cooperative shops and markets; rural residents with large gardens often ate better than urban neighbors, though they faced shortages of manufactured goods; pensioners on fixed incomes struggled with higher market prices. Officials with access to special stores retained advantages, a privilege that glasnost made a public scandal. Historians should treat accounts from large cities, which dominate the memoir and press record, with caution as evidence for the whole union.

Section 65.15

65.15 — The Debate over a Market Economy, 1989–1990

By 1989 the question was no longer whether to move beyond the 1987 reform but in what direction. At the Congress of People's Deputies in December 1989, Prime Minister Nikolai Ryzhkov presented a program for a transition to a "regulated market economy" over several years, with price reform, restructuring of ownership, and strengthening of the financial system, but retaining a large role for the central government and prudence about social protection. Leonid Abalkin, an academic economist, became a deputy prime minister with responsibility for the reform. In May 1990 Ryzhkov announced plans to raise prices of bread and other staples in order to reduce subsidies, and the reaction was panic buying, public hostility, and a decision by the Supreme Soviet to postpone the measures. The episode demonstrated how politically hazardous price reform had become, and how democratization had given the public means to resist.

An alternative approach was developed in the summer of 1990 by a group of economists including Stanislav Shatalin, Grigory Yavlinsky, Nikolai Petrakov, and Boris Fedorov, working with Gorbachev's and Yeltsin's blessing. Known as the "500 Days" program, it proposed rapid privatization, price liberalization, financial stabilization, and a new division of economic power between the union and the republics, with republics controlling their own resources and paying for central functions. It had considerable support from the Russian Republic's new leadership under Yeltsin. Ryzhkov and others criticized its speed and its implications for union authority, and unease in the party, the military-industrial complex, and the ministries was strong.

Gorbachev initially seemed to support a combination of the two programs, then turned away from the 500 Days plan in the autumn. In October 1990 the Supreme Soviet endorsed a compromise set of "Basic Guidelines" that was less radical and ambiguous on key points, and many of its measures were not implemented. Disputes over ownership, prices, and fiscal authority overlapped with disputes about the Union's structure: if republics controlled taxation and resources, the central government would lose the means to fund its commitments. This overlap is why the economic debate belongs to the beginnings of the constitutional crisis addressed in Chapter 66.

Historians' judgments of Gorbachev's economic decisions vary. Some argue that he hesitated at crucial moments and lost time; others that a rapid transition would have caused social disruption and political backlash that the weakened center could not have managed; and others that no sequence of economic measures could have succeeded without a political consensus that did not exist. These are interpretations, not facts; the evidence needed to test them includes Politburo records, advisers' memoranda, and comparison with Eastern European and Chinese experience.

Section 65.16

65.16 — Why Economic Reform Failed to Stabilize the Soviet System

By the end of 1990 the Soviet economy was in a state of acute stress. Output in several sectors was falling, the budget deficit was large, consumer shortages had spread, and supply links between enterprises and between republics were breaking down. This section assesses why the reforms did not produce the stabilization their authors sought. It is important to say what "failure" means: the reforms did not achieve their stated objectives of faster growth, better goods, and higher living standards, and they contributed to instability; it does not follow that any alternative path would necessarily have succeeded.

Several causes interacted. First, the partial and uneven character of reform created incentives that undercut the plan without creating a market. Second, fiscal pressures grew: oil revenues fell after 1986, alcohol revenue fell, subsidies and investment promises remained, the cost of Chernobyl and of the Armenian earthquake of December 1988 added burdens, and the budget deficit increased. Third, political resistance from ministries, regional authorities, and parts of the party slowed implementation, while at the same time public opposition prevented price increases. Fourth, uncertainty about the future caused enterprises to hold inventories, shift to barter, or redirect sales, and led republic and local governments to restrict exports of goods, a "war of laws" and of customs that disrupted a highly interdependent economy. Fifth, the sequencing of political and economic reform interacted: the new legislature, elected in 1989, gave voice to groups opposing unpopular measures, and the weakening of the party reduced the central government's ability to enforce decisions.

Comparisons with China are frequently made. China's reforms from 1978 began with agriculture and local enterprise experiments under a party monopoly that stayed intact, with a much larger peasant population and a smaller industrial base and state-owned sector. The Soviet Union was already urbanized and heavily industrialized, with a complex defense sector, and the party's monopoly was weakened by the political reform itself. Comparison illuminates differences in conditions and choices but does not show that a Chinese path was open to the USSR. Comparison with Poland and Hungary, which also struggled with reform, similarly shows that economic transitions were difficult in different institutional settings.

Historical Debate

Why Did Economic Restructuring Produce Disruption?

The question: Why did the reforms of 1987–1990 fail to stabilize the economy, and could a different approach have succeeded?

Interpretation A — Incoherent partial reform: Many economists argue that decentralizing enterprise decisions without reforming prices, allocation, and financial discipline produced the worst of both systems. On this view the fault was the design and incompleteness of the reform, and faster, more comprehensive liberalization was needed.
Interpretation B — Political sequencing and capacity: Other scholars emphasize that the decisive problem was political. The leadership lacked a consensus, and democratization reduced the center's power to carry out painful measures; a program such as the 500 Days required a strong, agreed authority across the union, which no longer existed. This view often compares China, where the party retained control.
Interpretation C — Structural and external shocks: A third approach stresses the accumulated structural problems of the planned economy, dependence on energy exports, and the oil price decline, arguing that no reform sequence could have quickly overcome them.

Evidence: Historians use statistical series (whose reliability is itself debated), Politburo and government records, memoirs and advisers' papers, enterprise-level studies, and comparisons with other transitions.

Points of agreement: There is broad agreement that the partial reforms generated shortages and imbalances and that fiscal deterioration was serious by 1989–1990.

Points of disagreement: Scholars disagree about whether a different sequence could have worked, about the relative weight of external and internal causes, and about the reliability of official data. The interpretations are not mutually exclusive.

Section 65.17

65.17 — Glasnost: From Controlled Criticism to Expanding Public Debate

Glasnost did not begin as a declaration of freedom of expression. In 1985 and 1986 the leadership used the term to mean greater accountability and information within the existing system: officials should explain their decisions, shortcomings should be exposed so they could be corrected, and the press should help to mobilize society behind reform. This was an old idea in Soviet politics, resembling the "criticism and self-criticism" campaigns of earlier decades, and it assumed that the party would continue to decide what could be said. Censorship remained in force through the state agency Glavlit, and the party's Central Committee continued to direct the media through appointments and instructions.

The practical expansion came through personnel and editorial decisions. Alexander Yakovlev, who headed the Central Committee's propaganda department from 1985 and became a Secretary and Politburo member, promoted editors who favored openness, including Vitaly Korotich at the illustrated weekly Ogonyok, Yegor Yakovlev at Moscow News, and Sergei Zalygin at the literary monthly Novy Mir. They published investigative reports on corruption, crime, drug addiction, prostitution, and the war in Afghanistan, topics previously treated as foreign or marginal. Television introduced live and investigative programs, among them the youth program Vzglyad (Viewpoint) from 1987. Readers' letters poured in, circulation of reformist periodicals soared, and by 1989 newspapers and magazines were among the most influential institutions in public life.

The boundaries of acceptable speech were contested and moved in stages. Some limits were removed by political decisions, as in the release of political prisoners in 1986–1987 and the decision to stop jamming foreign radio broadcasts in 1987–1988. Others were removed in practice when censors hesitated to enforce rules that the leadership no longer clearly supported. Conservatives resisted, and the publication in March 1988 of a letter by a Leningrad chemistry teacher, Nina Andreyeva, in the newspaper Sovetskaya Rossiya defending Stalin-era orthodoxy, seen by many as endorsed by Ligachev's circle, was answered in April by a long editorial in Pravda associated with Yakovlev. The episode showed that glasnost's limits depended on a struggle within the leadership. A new press law in 1990 formally abolished prior censorship and permitted independent publications, but by then the practice had outrun the law.

Glasnost changed the relationship between state and society in at least three ways. It created a public sphere in which ordinary people could debate; it exposed the gap between official accounts and experience; and it provided the opponents of the leadership, from radical reformers to Russian nationalists to conservative communists, with platforms. Gorbachev expected glasnost to support restructuring. Instead it also magnified economic complaints, nationality grievances, and attacks on the party's past. The leadership's response was uneven: it sometimes tolerated criticism, sometimes responded with pressure on editors, but it did not return to systematic suppression.

Section 65.18

65.18 — Reexamining Stalinism, Repression, and Soviet Historical Memory

One of the most powerful effects of glasnost was the reopening of the Soviet past. Since the mid-1960s, official discussion of Stalin-era repression had been restricted, and the Great Terror, collectivization, and the Gulag were treated sparingly in textbooks (Chapters 56–57). In 1987–1988 the situation changed. The Politburo established a commission, led by Yakovlev, to examine the repressions of the 1930s and the early 1950s and to review cases for rehabilitation. The cases of many victims of show trials, including Nikolai Bukharin and others tried in the 1930s, were reviewed, and in 1988 the Supreme Court annulled their convictions and restored their party status. Rehabilitations of millions of victims, already under way after 1956 (Chapter 61), resumed and widened.

Publishing played a central role. Works long banned or circulated only in samizdat appeared in print: Anatoly Rybakov's novel Children of the Arbat and Vasily Grossman's Life and Fate in 1987–1988, Anna Akhmatova's poem Requiem, Varlam Shalamov's stories of the camps, and excerpts of Alexander Solzhenitsyn's The Gulag Archipelago in 1989. Journals printed memoirs of former prisoners, archival documents, and statistical studies. Historians and journalists began to challenge earlier explanations, and public discussion of the numbers of victims, the causes of the terror, and Stalin's role in the war became intense. The estimates of victims of repression, of the 1932–1933 famine, and of other categories were debated, and responsible accounts treated them as ranges, drawing on archival work that continued into the 1990s.

Civic initiatives accompanied scholarship. Groups of young activists, former prisoners, and historians organized the Memorial society in 1987–1989 to collect testimony, to build a monument to the victims, and to research the repressions. It formally held its founding conference in January 1989, with Andrei Sakharov as honorary chairman. Local activists located and publicized mass graves, such as those at Kurapaty near Minsk in 1988 and Bykivnia near Kyiv, which became symbols for national movements as well as for the memory of terror. The new openness also extended to episodes involving other countries: in 1989 the Congress of People's Deputies condemned the secret protocols of the 1939 Molotov-Ribbentrop pact (Chapter 58) after a commission under Yakovlev examined them, and in April 1990 the Soviet government acknowledged that the NKVD had been responsible for the Katyn killings of Polish officers in 1940.

The political implications were large. The legitimacy of the Soviet state had rested on its claim to represent a rational and progressive historical path, on the Revolution, and on victory in the Second World War. Revisiting the repression forced the question of who was responsible and whether the system itself was implicated. Gorbachev's own approach was to distinguish Stalin's crimes from the achievements of Soviet socialism, in his speech on the seventieth anniversary of the Revolution on November 2, 1987, which condemned the repressions while defending the Revolution and the Soviet path. Critics from the radical side held that this distinction was insufficient, while conservatives and some veterans accused critics of slandering the nation's history. Historical debate thereby became one of the main arenas of political conflict.

  • Public organizations such as Memorial pursued research, commemoration, and the location of burial sites.

Section 65.19

65.19 — Literature, Film, Television, and the Cultural Transformation of Perestroika

Culture was both a participant in and an instrument of change. In 1986 the Fifth Congress of the Union of Cinematographers replaced its old leadership with reformers and set up a commission to review films that had been shelved, and the Union of Writers experienced contests over leadership and over publishing priorities. Films that had been withheld for years, such as Tengiz Abuladze's Georgian-language Repentance, an allegory of dictatorship and memory, reached audiences in 1986–1987, and others on social problems, such as drug use, youth alienation, and the war in Afghanistan, drew large audiences. Theater and television featured discussion programs, and rock music, once tolerated at best, became a mass cultural force; groups such as Kino and Aquarium, supported by an expanding network of concerts and recordings, expressed youth skepticism and desire for change.

The literary press played a central role. Novy Mir published Boris Pasternak's Doctor Zhivago in 1988, thirty years after he had been forced to refuse the Nobel Prize, as well as works by émigré and banned authors such as Vladimir Nabokov and George Orwell, whose 1984 and Yevgeny Zamyatin's We reached Soviet readers in 1988–1989. Thick journals reached circulation figures of several million, which collapsed again in the early 1990s when economic conditions and the end of the monopoly on public attention changed reading habits. Readers' demand for the long-suppressed literature of the twentieth century, including the Silver Age and the émigré and camp writers, was extraordinary, and historians regard the publication boom as evidence of the pent-up demand created by decades of restriction.

Cultural debate was not uniform. Liberal and "democratic" intellectuals, associated with periodicals like Ogonyok, emphasized Western models, human rights, and a critique of Soviet history. A nationalist and conservative current, represented by writers connected with the "village prose" tradition and journals such as Nash Sovremennik and Molodaya Gvardiya, emphasized Russian national traditions, religion, and the preservation of rural culture, and often criticized the liberal press and the influence of mass culture and Westernization. The conflict between these tendencies, expressed in debates about the past, anticipated the political divisions of the 1990s. At the same time, cultural figures in non-Russian republics linked language, literature, and historical memory with national revival, a theme taken further in Chapter 66.

The change in cultural authority was significant. Writers and editors, who under the old system had been supervised by party organs, acquired independent public prestige and, in many cases, elected office in 1989. The ending of the party's control over cultural production, however, also exposed cultural institutions to financial difficulties, as state subsidies and guaranteed audiences declined. Cultural change therefore illustrates both the opening of expression and the disruption of institutions that had provided stability.

Section 65.20

65.20 — Environmental Movements, Civic Mobilization, and the Aftermath of Chernobyl

Environmental degradation was among the most visible consequences of the Soviet development model. The drying of the Aral Sea following the diversion of the Amu Darya and Syr Darya for cotton irrigation, air and water pollution in industrial centers, pollution of Lake Baikal by industrial plants, the hazards of nuclear weapons testing and production sites, and the contaminated landscapes of former military facilities were documented by scientists, though often kept from public view. Soviet writers and scientists had raised concerns since the 1960s and 1970s. The decision in 1986 to abandon the large project to divert northern rivers toward Central Asia, after criticism from scientists and writers, was an early sign of the capacity of public criticism to influence policy.

Glasnost and Chernobyl turned environmental concern into a mass issue. Press investigations of pollution and public-health data multiplied. Environmental groups formed, many of them initially as clubs or branches of official organizations, and some became politically assertive. In Ukraine, the association Zelenyi Svit (Green World), founded in 1987, and public campaigns about Chernobyl and nuclear power later contributed to the formation of the Popular Movement of Ukraine (Rukh) in 1989. In Armenia, protests against the Nairit chemical plant and the Metsamor nuclear station in 1987–1988 mixed environmental and national concerns. In Lithuania, campaigns against the Ignalina nuclear station and in Estonia against phosphate mining combined environmental claims with political ones, and in Belarus the consequences of Chernobyl, which had affected a large share of the country's territory, became an enduring political and social issue. In Kazakhstan, the Nevada-Semipalatinsk movement, led by poet Olzhas Suleimenov in 1989, mobilized thousands against nuclear testing at the Semipalatinsk site, and testing was curtailed.

Care is required in interpretation. Environmental concerns were real and widespread, and they cannot be reduced to proxies for nationalism. Yet in several republics, they offered a legitimate language in which people could criticize Moscow's policies, and the movements included activists who later became leaders of independence campaigns. This section therefore describes environmental mobilization as one stream of the growth of civic activity, leaving the subsequent political history of the republics to Chapter 66. The movement also exposed disputes among scientists, officials, and citizens about risk, expertise, and the right to information, and the legacy of those disputes is visible in later Russian, Ukrainian, and Belarusian politics.

Regional Context

Chernobyl's Consequences in Ukraine and Belarus

Seen from Moscow, Chernobyl was a ministry-level and union-level emergency; seen from Kyiv, Minsk, and the affected countryside, it was a crisis of daily life. The station and Pripyat lay in the Ukrainian SSR, but a large share of the fallout fell on southern Belarus, where many villages were contaminated and tens of thousands of residents were eventually resettled. Parts of the Bryansk region of the Russian SFSR were also affected. Republican governments had little autonomy to decide on evacuation, food controls, or public information, and republican scientists and physicians frequently found that they lacked access to data held in Moscow.

These experiences shaped later politics. In Ukraine, public discussion of Chernobyl, nuclear power, and the contrast between central decisions and local costs fed the environmental and civic groups described above. In Belarus, health and resettlement questions became a lasting part of public life, and the revelations about Stalin-era mass graves at Kurapaty in 1988 joined environmental grievance and historical memory in a new civic movement. Historians caution against treating these regional movements only as precursors of independence; they also expressed concerns about health, expertise, and the right to information that were shared by citizens across the union. Their later political development belongs to Chapter 66.

Section 65.21

65.21 — Religion, Public Associations, and the Expansion of Civil Society

The state's attitude toward religion changed in the course of the 1980s. Official atheism remained party doctrine, and religious institutions had been tightly regulated since the 1920s and the antireligious campaigns of the 1930s and Khrushchev years (Chapters 58, 62). Under Brezhnev, believers numbered in the millions and religious practice persisted, but the Russian Orthodox Church was supervised through the Council for Religious Affairs and its hierarchy was constrained. The millennium of the Christianization of Rus' (Chapter 9) in 1988 became an occasion for change. Celebrations in Moscow in June 1988 were extensively covered, Gorbachev received Patriarch Pimen and members of the Holy Synod in the Kremlin in April 1988 and acknowledged the Church's contribution to national culture, and the Danilov Monastery in Moscow, returned to the Church in 1983, was opened in 1988 as its administrative center. The anniversary was commemorated by the Russian Orthodox Church, but the heritage of Rus' also belonged to Ukrainian and Belarusian traditions, a point that would acquire political significance (Chapter 12).

Other communities also gained space. In December 1989, after a meeting between Gorbachev and Pope John Paul II in Rome, the legal status of the Ukrainian Greek Catholic Church, which Stalin had forcibly merged into the Orthodox Church in 1946, was restored, setting off disputes over church property in western Ukraine. Muslims, Jews, Baptists, Buddhists, and others found greater freedom to open prayer houses, teach, publish, and contact co-religionists abroad. The 1990 Law on Freedom of Conscience, adopted in October 1990, guaranteed religious freedom and ended many of the older restrictions. Jewish emigration, which had been tightly restricted, rose sharply in 1989–1990, and Jewish cultural organizations were formed. The new freedoms were uneven in practice: local officials, shortages of buildings, and prejudice persisted, and the religious revival of the 1990s (Chapter 72) was uneven among regions and communities.

Beyond religion, hundreds of thousands of citizens joined informal organizations (neformaly), including historical-preservation societies, environmental clubs, youth groups, discussion clubs, human-rights groups, and political clubs. The Moscow Popular Front and the Club of Social Initiatives appeared in 1987–1988, the Democratic Union claimed to be a first opposition party in 1988, and in the Baltic republics, Armenia, Georgia, and elsewhere popular fronts and national associations mobilized large followings. Some organizations were nationalist and illiberal, such as the Russian nationalist society Pamyat, which promoted antisemitic conspiracy theories, a reminder that civil society's expansion included groups of very different values. The authorities initially treated unofficial groups with suspicion, and registration and use of public space were disputed, but by 1989–1990 laws on public associations and on assemblies gave them a legal basis.

The growth of civil society had a contradictory relation to Gorbachev's program. It built the social base for democratization and for criticism of bureaucracy, yet it also created organizations that were not controlled by the party and that increasingly challenged it. Historians therefore see the expansion of public association as both a condition of democratization and an indicator that the party's role as the organizer of society was ending.

65.22 — The Nineteenth Party Conference of 1988 and the Decision to Democratize

By the beginning of 1988 Gorbachev and his closest advisers had concluded that economic restructuring was stalling against the resistance of the party and state apparatus, and that the apparatus could be overcome only by changing the political system. The Nineteenth All-Union Party Conference, held in Moscow from June 28 to July 1, 1988, was convened for that purpose. A party conference, a less formal body than a congress, had not met since 1941. Its roughly five thousand delegates, chosen in a process in which regional apparatuses retained much influence, were not a reformist body, and Gorbachev needed to persuade them to approve proposals that would reduce the apparatus's power.

The program presented in his report and endorsed in resolutions had several elements. It proposed a distinction between the functions of the party, which would set political direction, and those of the state and soviets, which would administer; the transfer of real authority to elected soviets; the creation of a new legislature, the Congress of People's Deputies, to be chosen through contested elections; limits on the terms of officials; and a "socialist law-governed state" (pravovoe gosudarstvo) with greater judicial independence. A parallel proposal, that party first secretaries should also be elected chairmen of the corresponding soviets, was designed to give party leaders a popular mandate and, in the leadership's view, to make soviets effective while keeping party control. Resolutions also addressed glasnost, legal reform, and relations among nationalities.

The Conference was itself a demonstration of the new openness: major sessions were broadcast, and delegates criticized the leadership publicly. Boris Yeltsin, who had been removed from the Politburo candidate list in February, asked for political rehabilitation, and Ligachev responded sharply. Conservative delegates attacked press criticism of the party and the army, and defended the apparatus. The resolutions passed, but with compromises, and the plan to merge party and soviet positions met resistance. Over the following months, Gorbachev reshaped the leadership: at a September 30, 1988 plenum, Gromyko, Ligachev's responsibilities for ideology, and several others were removed or reassigned, and on October 1 he himself became chairman of the Presidium of the Supreme Soviet, the head of state post. Yakovlev gained influence over foreign policy and ideological commissions.

The Conference marks a turning point because it committed the party to a program that, in effect, would limit its own power. Whether Gorbachev meant at that time to move toward a multiparty system is doubtful: the language remained one of strengthening the soviets and improving the party's role, and he spoke of "socialist pluralism of opinions" not of competing parties. His intentions evolved with events, and by 1990 he accepted what in 1988 he had not envisioned.

Section 65.23

65.23 — Constitutional Changes and the Creation of the Congress of People's Deputies

On December 1, 1988, the Supreme Soviet adopted amendments to the 1977 Constitution and a new electoral law that created a two-tier legislature. The Congress of People's Deputies, with 2,250 members, was to meet for a few weeks each year, to decide constitutional questions, and to elect from among its members a smaller standing Supreme Soviet of 542 deputies with two chambers, the Soviet of the Union and the Soviet of Nationalities, which would work in session for much of the year. The Congress also elected the head of state, the Chairman of the Supreme Soviet. The structure replaced the earlier Supreme Soviet, which had met briefly twice a year to approve decisions made elsewhere.

Of the 2,250 seats, 750 were filled from territorial districts of roughly equal population, 750 from national-territorial districts representing the union republics and ethnic territories, and 750 by public organizations, among them the Communist Party, trade unions, the Komsomol, women's and veterans' organizations, scientific societies, and creative unions. The reserved seats were controversial. They guaranteed representation of institutions the leadership regarded as pillars of the system and provided a channel through which party leaders could secure seats, but they also meant that a third of the Congress was not chosen by voters at large. The method of selecting candidates for the organization seats, usually by organizational leaderships or plenary meetings, drew criticism. When the Academy of Sciences initially omitted Andrei Sakharov and other reformers from its list in January 1989, protests by scientists forced a revision that allowed him to be elected.

For the territorial districts, candidates could be nominated by groups of citizens and workplace collectives, but pre-election district meetings screened them, a procedure that in some places excluded independent candidates. The amendments also left the party's constitutional position intact, since Article 6 still defined it as the leading force of Soviet society. The intended relationship between representation and party leadership was therefore ambiguous: the reform aimed to give soviets real authority and make party officials accountable, while retaining the party as the political center. This ambiguity set the stage for the conflict between the new legislature and the party apparatus, which became explicit in 1989–1990.

Section 65.24

65.24 — The Elections of March 1989 and the Emergence of Electoral Competition

The elections of March 26, 1989, were the first national elections in the USSR since 1917 in which voters had real choices in many constituencies. Previous Soviet elections had offered a single candidate approved by the party, with turnout near unanimity. In 1989, more than one candidate stood in a large majority of districts, although in a substantial minority only one candidate was permitted, and voters could cross out his name. Turnout was high, about nine-tenths of the electorate. Campaigns involved public meetings, leaflets, newspaper debates, and small independent clubs and voter associations, and in many cities the campaigning was the first of its kind that voters could remember.

The results surprised officials. Dozens of established party and state figures were defeated, including some who ran unopposed but failed to win a majority of votes cast. The party first secretary of Leningrad, Yuri Solovyov, a candidate member of the Politburo, was defeated in this way, as were several other regional leaders and senior officials. Boris Yeltsin, running in Moscow's national-territorial district against an official establishment candidate, won about nine-tenths of the vote, converting his political isolation into a public mandate. Reformist intellectuals, journalists, and activists were elected in major cities, while in the Baltic republics, Georgia, and Armenia, candidates supported by popular fronts and national movements did very well. In rural areas and in many regions, local party officials and established figures were more successful.

The elections were therefore both competitive and limited. A third of seats were reserved for organizations; the nomination process had filtered candidates; the Communist Party still dominated the apparatus and resources, and most deputies, perhaps eighty-five percent, were party members. They did not constitute a multiparty election, since no organized opposition parties could stand. But the political significance was large: the voters had demonstrated that they were willing to reject the establishment, and the party's leadership had accepted the results, since the leadership had not manipulated the outcome on the scale it could have. Historians generally treat 1989 as the point at which competitive politics, and with it a public with political expectations, entered Soviet life.

Reform-minded intellectuals and urban activists: For many in Moscow, Leningrad, and other cities the elections offered a chance to bring dissident and reformist figures into public office, and they organized campaigns, voter clubs, and publications. They criticized reserved seats and candidate screening as obstacles.
Party officials and the apparatus: Many regional and district officials treated the elections as a threat to their authority. Some adapted by campaigning; others relied on organizational resources or obstructed independent candidates, and those who were defeated experienced a sudden loss of standing.
Voters in cities, towns, and villages: Voters used the opportunity to express dissatisfaction with shortages, privileges, and the performance of officials. Rural and small-town voters often supported established figures, and experiences varied by region and republic.
National movements in the republics: In several republics the elections became an occasion for national mobilization, as candidates from popular fronts framed contests around language, history, and sovereignty, a development examined in Chapter 66.

The groups did not agree about what the elections meant: reform, protest, institutional change, or the beginning of the end of one-party rule.

Section 65.25

65.25 — The Congress of People's Deputies, Televised Politics, and Parliamentary Opposition

The first Congress of People's Deputies met in the Kremlin from May 25 to June 9, 1989, and was broadcast live on television and radio. Millions followed the proceedings; in factories and offices work reportedly slowed as people listened. For the first time Soviet citizens saw their leaders criticized openly, deputies interrupting and challenging one another, and arguments on subjects such as the army, the KGB, privileges, the war in Afghanistan, and the 1989 events in Tbilisi, where Soviet troops had used force against demonstrators in April, killing about twenty people. Gorbachev was elected chairman of the Supreme Soviet at the start of the Congress, and he presided in a role that required him to balance radical and conservative deputies.

The Congress was a forum for newly prominent figures. Andrei Sakharov, returned from exile and now a deputy, argued for transferring power to the soviets and for ending the party's monopoly, and clashed with Gorbachev over procedure and over the war in Afghanistan, which he criticized. Other deputies, such as the Leningrad law professor Anatoly Sobchak, who led the commission on the Tbilisi events, and the historian Yuri Afanasyev, became known nationally. Conservative and military deputies defended the army and the apparatus, and some representatives of non-Russian republics pressed national claims. The Congress elected the Supreme Soviet, in a process that kept some radical candidates out, which angered reformers, but also saw the rejection of several ministerial nominees by the new body in the following weeks.

In July 1989, radical deputies, including Yeltsin, Afanasyev, Gavriil Popov, Sakharov, and Viktor Palm of Estonia, formed the Interregional Deputies Group, the first organized parliamentary opposition in Soviet history, though it was a loose association inside the legislature and not a party. It advocated rapid market reforms, abolition of the party's constitutional monopoly, and greater republican autonomy. Its membership numbered in the hundreds, but it did not command a majority and it faced the "obedient majority" of deputies who supported the leadership. The legislature also had practical limits: it lacked experienced staff and procedures, and its decisions required implementation by ministries and party structures that it did not control. Sakharov died on December 14, 1989, a loss to the reformist current.

The televised Congress changed the character of politics. Disagreements previously confined to party committees became public, expectations of accountability grew, and authority came to depend on persuasion in public, not only on position within the apparatus. The same visibility, however, exposed the differences in the reformist camp and the difficulty of governing amid constant public argument, themes taken up in §65.28.

Section 65.26

65.26 — Workers' Strikes, Civic Protest, and the Expansion of Popular Politics, 1989–1990

Workers entered political life with the miners' strikes of the summer of 1989. In July, miners in Mezhdurechensk in the Kuzbass coalfield of western Siberia stopped work, and the strike spread across the Kuzbass, to the Donbas in Ukraine, to Vorkuta in the Arctic north, and to Karaganda in Kazakhstan. Participants numbered in the hundreds of thousands. Their demands were mainly economic and local: supplies of soap, food, and protective equipment, higher wages and pensions, safer mines, and greater control over the proceeds of coal sales, including the right to retain a share for local use. Strike committees, rather than official trade unions, coordinated the actions, and the miners behaved in an orderly fashion, which impressed observers. The government, led by Ryzhkov, agreed to many demands, and the strikes ended in a few weeks, although implementation was incomplete.

The strikes had large political consequences. They showed that organized workers could act independently of the official unions and the party, and that the state could not simply suppress them. They highlighted the effects of economic reform on living standards. Later strike committees pressed for political demands, including the removal of Article 6, and independent miners' organizations developed in 1990. The strikes in the coalfields of Russia, Ukraine, and Kazakhstan also reflected regional differences: in the Donbas the miners' movement later had a complicated relation to Ukrainian national politics, while in Siberia it formed ties with Russian reformist deputies.

Mass demonstrations also spread. Rallies in Moscow in 1989–1990, including a large demonstration in February 1990 organized by the new Democratic Russia movement and calling for an end to the party's monopoly, drew very large crowds, with estimates varying widely by organizer and authority. In the republics, demonstrations on national issues were larger and more frequent (Chapter 66). New trade unions, strike laws, and associations of voters, pensioners, veterans, and consumers appeared. Conservative organizations also mobilized; the United Front of Working People and groups of military and party-linked activists, supported by newspapers such as Sovetskaya Rossiya, protested against what they saw as the destruction of socialist institutions.

The expansion of popular politics weakened the traditional party control over public life. Officials accustomed to resolve social disputes by apparatus channels faced independent bodies with claims of legitimacy. This was an important change, but the effects should not be overstated: many Soviet citizens remained outside organized politics, public opinion divided, and regional differences were large. The chapter's concern is how mobilization weakened party control, not yet how it fused with national movements.

Section 65.27

65.27 — The End of the Communist Party's Constitutional Monopoly and the Soviet Presidency, 1990

Pressure to end the party's constitutional monopoly rose after the elections. Sakharov and the Interregional Group demanded the repeal of Article 6, while the events of late 1989 in Eastern Europe, where communist parties gave up their guaranteed roles, shaped Soviet debate. Gorbachev initially resisted, then accepted that political pluralism was necessary. A Central Committee plenum of February 5–7, 1990, approved a platform that accepted the end of the party's monopoly and the possibility of a multiparty system, and presented a program of reorganization of the party. The Third Extraordinary Congress of People's Deputies, meeting March 12–15, 1990, then amended the Constitution on March 14, removing the provision that defined the party as the leading force, and legalizing political parties and public associations in a framework of "political pluralism."

The same Congress established the office of President of the USSR, an executive position with extensive powers over the government, security, and foreign policy, and enlarged the system of presidential advisory bodies and later a Federation Council of republican leaders. On March 15, Gorbachev was elected president by the Congress, not by a nationwide vote. A direct popular election was proposed by critics, among them Yeltsin and radical deputies, but Gorbachev and his allies argued that it would be risky in the circumstances of unrest and that a legislative election was easier to organize. He obtained a majority of deputies' votes, though substantial numbers voted against or did not vote. Historians debate whether the choice of an indirect election weakened his authority at a time when republican leaders, such as Yeltsin in Russia, would shortly obtain direct popular mandates. This is a counterfactual question, but it identifies the way in which institutional design shaped later political relations.

The effects of these changes were uneven. The party remained a large organization with property, personnel, and ties to the security forces, and at the Twenty-Eighth Party Congress in July 1990 it reorganized its leadership structures; but it was no longer the constitutional center of the state. The shift of power from the Politburo and Secretariat toward the president and government created ambiguities in lines of authority, and the new office was built on a legal base still being formed. Republic parliaments elected in the spring of 1990, as discussed in Chapter 66, began to assert their own institutions. In this period, the Soviet political system was therefore moving from a party-state toward a mixed system whose institutions were not yet consolidated.

Section 65.28

65.28 — Reformers, Conservatives, and the Crisis of Political Authority

By 1990 the political spectrum was wide. On the radical side stood democrats such as Yeltsin, Popov, Afanasyev, Sobchak, and economists who advocated rapid market reform and an end to the party's role; they organized in Democratic Russia and in republican parliaments. Gorbachev and his group of supporters, including Yakovlev, Shevardnadze, and advisers, occupied the reformist center, hoping to preserve the union and socialist institutions while expanding political freedoms. On the conservative side were party officials, parts of the military and security services, and the ministries of the defense and heavy industry, with spokesmen such as Ligachev, who had been shifted to agriculture in 1988 and who criticized the press and the direction of reforms, and organizations such as the Soyuz group of deputies. Russian nationalists, communists with socialist convictions, and some trade-union leaders occupied various positions within or between these camps.

Boris Yeltsin's trajectory illustrates the changes. Removed from the Moscow party leadership in 1987, he won a Moscow seat in 1989 with an overwhelming vote, led the Interregional Group, was elected chairman of the Supreme Soviet of the Russian Soviet Federative Socialist Republic in May 1990, and left the Communist Party at the Twenty-Eighth Congress in July 1990. The Russian republic's institutions, which had been marginal in the Soviet system, now offered him an independent base, which Gorbachev's reforms had created. The details of the Russian sovereignty declaration of June 1990 and the conflict between the Russian and union governments belong to Chapter 66.

Gorbachev's position shifted as he sought to balance these forces. In 1989 he often sided with reformers against the apparatus; in 1990 he faced demands from radicals for faster change and from conservatives for restoration of order, and, in the autumn, as economic conditions and nationality conflict worsened, he turned toward the security and party establishment. In December 1990 appointments and reorganizations strengthened conservative figures, and on December 20 Foreign Minister Shevardnadze resigned, warning publicly that a dictatorship was approaching. Those events lie at the edge of this chapter and open the political crisis of 1991.

The deeper issue was the relationship between democratization and effective government. The Soviet state of 1985 had centralized authority, however imperfect its practical control. Democratization created new centers of legitimacy, the legislature, republican parliaments, the press, and public movements, before a stable constitutional order and a revised division of powers had been established. Authority at the center eroded while the capacity to carry out policies diminished. Whether this was an inevitable result of reform, a consequence of sequencing, or the effect of other pressures is the subject of the debate below.

Historical Debate

Did Democratization Strengthen or Destabilize Soviet Government?

The question: Did the political reforms of 1988–1990 renew the Soviet state by giving it broader legitimacy, or did they undermine its capacity to govern?

Interpretation A — Democratization as a necessary renewal: Some historians argue that without political reform the bureaucratic resistance to restructuring could not be overcome, and that the elections and the legislature gave the state a broader base. On this view the instability arose from the unresolved economy, nationality questions, and conservative resistance, not from democratization itself.
Interpretation B — Democratization as destabilizing: Others argue that opening politics before reforming the economy and the federal structure weakened the party, the only institution that held the union together, and created competing centers of legitimacy that the central government could not control.
Interpretation C — Design and sequencing: A third group emphasizes specific choices, such as the creation of an indirectly elected presidency, the dual role of party and state offices, and the failure to settle the federal structure, as determining whether democratization would strengthen or weaken the state.

Evidence: Historians use Politburo records and diaries, stenographic reports of Congresses, election results and opinion polls, memoirs, and comparison with other transitions from one-party rule.

Points of agreement: Scholars agree that the reforms transformed the political system and that central authority diminished between 1989 and 1990.

Points of disagreement: They disagree about causation, about whether alternatives existed, and about the weight to give design choices versus structural conditions.

Section 65.29

65.29 — New Thinking in Soviet Foreign Policy

Gorbachev's foreign policy was announced under the label "new political thinking" (novoe politicheskoe myshlenie). It rested on several propositions that were developed between 1985 and 1988. First, nuclear war could have no winner, and the arms race therefore increased risk without improving security. Second, the world was interdependent, and global problems, among them nuclear danger, environmental damage, and economic development, required cooperation across ideological lines. Third, security could not be achieved unilaterally; it had to be mutual, and the Soviet Union needed to reduce the threat perceived by others. Fourth, by 1988 Gorbachev argued that "universal human values" took priority over class struggle in international relations, a departure from the doctrine of peaceful coexistence as a form of class conflict. He also spoke of a "common European home," in which the divisions of the Cold War would be overcome.

The sources of the policy were domestic and international. The military burden was heavy: the Soviet defense sector consumed a large share of industrial resources, and many reformers believed economic renewal required lower military spending and a calmer international environment. Experts at institutes such as IMEMO and the Institute of the USA and Canada, including Georgy Arbatov and Yevgeny Primakov, and advisers such as Anatoly Chernyaev, had developed arguments for a less confrontational policy. Chernobyl reinforced Gorbachev's sense of nuclear danger, and the setbacks in Afghanistan convinced many that the use of military force had limited political returns. Gorbachev and Shevardnadze, who replaced Gromyko in 1985, also changed the style of Soviet diplomacy toward more flexibility and public outreach.

The policy provoked opposition. Some military officers, party conservatives, and officials in the foreign ministry and defense industry were skeptical that concessions would be reciprocated, and later criticized the loss of Eastern Europe and of Soviet influence. Gorbachev's government sought to limit these objections by emphasizing the security gains, and by building support among reformers. Whether new thinking was a coherent doctrine or a series of improvisations responding to events is debated; the evidence suggests that the general principles were clear but that the specific decisions, on German reunification and Eastern Europe, were reached step by step.

Russia and the World

New Thinking and the Cold War System

Soviet policy did not change in isolation. Western European governments, social-democratic parties, peace movements, and scientific communities, including physicians' and physicists' organizations that met Soviet counterparts, had developed ideas of common security that influenced Soviet advisers. In the United States, President Reagan, who had condemned the Soviet system in strong terms in the early 1980s, shifted toward negotiation in his second term, influenced by advisers such as Secretary of State George Shultz and by his own anxiety about nuclear war. Interaction, adaptation, and rivalry all mattered: each side responded to the other's moves, and personal relations between leaders helped sustain negotiations that bureaucracies might have stalled.

The end of the Cold War is therefore not best understood either as a Soviet capitulation to American pressure or as a purely Soviet decision. Historians continue to weigh the effects of Western military and economic pressure, Soviet domestic needs, and the leaders' choices, a question revisited in the debate in §65.35.

Section 65.30

65.30 — The Geneva and Reykjavík Summits, 1985–1986

Gorbachev and Ronald Reagan met for the first time in Geneva from November 19 to 21, 1985. The meeting produced no major treaty, but the two leaders issued a joint statement that a nuclear war could not be won and must never be fought, agreed to work toward reductions of nuclear arms, and expanded cultural and scientific exchanges. The personal contact established a working relationship that had been absent in the previous years. After Geneva, in January 1986, Gorbachev announced a proposal for the elimination of nuclear weapons by the year 2000 in stages, a public initiative whose sincerity was debated in the West, but which signaled a Soviet willingness to consider radical reductions.

The Reykjavík meeting of October 11–12, 1986 was a hastily arranged encounter in Iceland. The leaders and their advisers discussed deep reductions in strategic nuclear weapons, the elimination of intermediate-range missiles in Europe, and, in the course of the talks, the possibility of eliminating all nuclear weapons within ten years. The discussions broke down over the Strategic Defense Initiative (SDI), Reagan's program to develop defenses against ballistic missiles. Gorbachev demanded that research on SDI be confined to the laboratory; Reagan refused, and the meeting ended without agreement, to the disappointment of both.

Reykjavík was nonetheless significant. It showed that the superpowers could discuss radical reductions seriously, and it clarified the issues remaining. In February 1987 Gorbachev dropped the demand that an agreement on intermediate-range missiles be linked to SDI, opening the way for the INF treaty. Historians regard the summit as a failure in the short term and a foundation for later agreements; for observers in Europe it produced anxiety, as some leaders feared that proposals for nuclear abolition might undercut the deterrence on which their security had rested.

Section 65.31

65.31 — The INF Treaty, Arms Reductions, and Changing Superpower Relations, 1987–1988

The Treaty on the Elimination of Intermediate-Range and Shorter-Range Missiles, signed by Reagan and Gorbachev in Washington on December 8, 1987, required the elimination of all land-based ballistic and cruise missiles with ranges between 500 and 5,500 kilometers, together with their launchers and support facilities. It was the first agreement to eliminate an entire class of nuclear weapons, in contrast with earlier treaties that limited the growth of arsenals. The Soviet Union accepted an asymmetry, since it had to destroy more missiles than the United States, a concession that critics in the Soviet military noted. The US Senate approved the treaty in May 1988, and it entered into force on June 1, 1988. The treaty required the elimination of covered missiles within three years of its entry into force in June 1988, and dismantling and verification were well advanced by the end of 1990.

Verification was central. The treaty provided for exchange of data, on-site inspections of missile bases and production facilities, and continuous monitoring of certain sites, procedures that required the Soviet side to accept intrusive access, a major shift from earlier secrecy. The negotiations involved many technical disputes, and Reagan's phrase "trust but verify" became a slogan of the process. The treaty also required adjustments by NATO allies, which had agreed to deploy US missiles in 1979, and by the Soviet leadership, which had to explain the apparent imbalance to its own military.

The Moscow summit of May 29 to June 2, 1988 exchanged the treaty's ratification documents and signaled the improved relationship; Reagan, who had once described the USSR as an "evil empire," walked in Red Square with Gorbachev. In December 1988 Gorbachev, speaking at the United Nations, announced that the Soviet Union would unilaterally reduce its armed forces by 500,000 troops and withdraw several divisions and thousands of tanks from Eastern Europe, and that the principle of freedom of choice should apply to all states. The announcement was made on December 7, and Gorbachev cut short his American trip to return to Armenia after the earthquake of that day. These steps changed the military balance in Europe and expressed a new approach to security.

Section 65.32

65.32 — Afghanistan, China, and the Reassessment of Soviet International Commitments

Gorbachev described the war in Afghanistan, in which Soviet forces had been engaged since December 1979, as a "bleeding wound," and early in his leadership sought a political settlement that would allow withdrawal while leaving a friendly government in Kabul (the war itself is treated in Chapter 64). The Geneva Accords, signed on April 14, 1988 by Afghanistan and Pakistan with the United States and the USSR as guarantors, provided for a Soviet withdrawal within nine months. Troops began leaving in May 1988 and the last Soviet units crossed the border on February 15, 1989. The accords did not end the conflict between the Kabul government and its opponents, and the withdrawal's costs, in the lives and experience of Soviet soldiers and Afghan civilians, were part of the context for the domestic criticism of the war during glasnost.

The leadership also sought to reduce other commitments. It supported negotiated settlements in southern Africa, including the 1988 agreements concerning Angola and Namibia that led to the withdrawal of Cuban forces, encouraged Vietnam to withdraw from Cambodia, reduced forces along the Chinese border and in Mongolia, and moderated support for allied governments in regional conflicts. These steps were motivated by cost, by a reassessment of the returns on military and economic aid to client states, and by a wish to improve relations with the West and with China.

Relations with China had been strained since the 1960s. Gorbachev's July 1986 speech in Vladivostok proposed improved relations, and negotiations addressed what Beijing called the "three obstacles": Soviet troops on the Chinese border, Soviet support for Vietnam in Cambodia, and the occupation of Afghanistan. Gorbachev visited Beijing from May 15 to 18, 1989, the first Sino-Soviet summit since 1959, formally normalizing party and state relations. The visit coincided with the mass student demonstrations in Tiananmen Square, so that the arrival of the international press shifted attention to the Chinese protests, which the Chinese government suppressed by force in early June. The contrast between the Chinese leadership's reliance on force and Gorbachev's later refusal to use it in Eastern Europe has often been noted, though the circumstances were different.

Section 65.33

65.33 — The Abandonment of Military Intervention and the Revolutions in Poland and Hungary, 1989

The postwar Soviet bloc rested on communist governments allied with Moscow and supported by Soviet forces. After the intervention in Czechoslovakia in 1968, Western analysts called Moscow's claim of a right to intervene to defend socialism the Brezhnev Doctrine (Chapter 63). Gorbachev gradually distanced the Soviet Union from this approach. In 1985–1987 he told East European leaders that each party was responsible for its own policies, and by 1988 he spoke publicly about the freedom of choice of peoples. At the same time, the Soviet leadership expected that reformed socialist governments would remain in power, and it did not at first foresee the collapse of communist rule. The weakening of the Brezhnev Doctrine was therefore less an announced decision than a progressive withdrawal of assurance, interpreted by East European leaders and oppositions in different ways. In October 1989 a Soviet foreign ministry spokesman, Gennady Gerasimov, joked that the "Sinatra Doctrine," a reference to the song "I Did It My Way," had replaced it.

In Poland, the economic crisis of the late 1980s and renewed strikes in 1988 led General Wojciech Jaruzelski's government to negotiate with Solidarity, which had been banned since 1981. Round Table talks between the government and opposition ran from February 6 to April 5, 1989, and produced an agreement to restore Solidarity's legal status, to create an upper house, the Senate, to be elected freely, and to hold elections in which only 35 percent of Sejm seats would be contested, with the rest reserved for the communists and allied parties, and a new presidency. In the elections of June 4 and 18, Solidarity won all but one of the Senate seats and nearly all of the Sejm seats open to contest. The communist-allied majority in the Sejm then partly fell apart, and in August 1989 Tadeusz Mazowiecki, a Catholic intellectual and Solidarity adviser, became the first non-communist prime minister in the Soviet bloc. Gorbachev, in conversations with Polish leaders, did not oppose the transition, and Soviet officials reportedly encouraged the communists to accept the outcome. The developments were driven by the Polish opposition, the Catholic Church, the workers of Solidarity, and the Polish government's calculation, not by Moscow.

In Hungary, the reformist wing of the Hungarian Socialist Workers' Party replaced János Kádár in 1988 and moved toward political pluralism. In June 1989 the reburial of Imre Nagy, executed after the 1956 uprising, drew an enormous crowd and symbolized the repudiation of the Soviet-imposed order. Round Table negotiations among the party and opposition groups ran from June to September. In May 1989 Hungary began dismantling its border fence with Austria, and on September 10–11 it opened the border to East Germans who had gathered in Hungary, allowing tens of thousands to leave for West Germany and breaching the Eastern bloc's controls on movement. In October, the party dissolved itself and re-formed as a socialist party, and Hungary was proclaimed a republic on October 23, the anniversary of the 1956 revolution. Hungary's decisions on the border were taken by Hungarian leaders weighing the risks of Soviet reaction, and show the importance of local agency.

Section 65.34

65.34 — The Fall of the Berlin Wall and the Collapse of Communist Governments in Eastern Europe, 1989

East Germany (the German Democratic Republic) had a leadership, headed by Erich Honecker, that resisted reform and relied on the security service, the Stasi. By the summer of 1989, thousands of citizens sought to leave through Hungary and through the West German embassies in Prague and Warsaw. Domestic protest grew, centered in Leipzig, where Monday prayer meetings at the Nikolai Church turned into demonstrations. On October 9, tens of thousands marched in Leipzig without violence by the security forces, who had prepared for confrontation, a decisive moment. Gorbachev had visited East Berlin on October 6–7 for the republic's fortieth anniversary, and encouraged reform; Soviet troops in East Germany remained in their barracks. Honecker was forced out on October 18 and replaced by Egon Krenz, whose attempts at reform did not satisfy protesters. On November 4 a vast demonstration took place in East Berlin.

On November 9, a government spokesman, Günter Schabowski, announced at a press conference new travel regulations, and in response to a question said, apparently without full information, that they took effect immediately. Crowds gathered at the border crossings in Berlin, and guards, without clear orders, opened the gates that night. The opening of the Wall was thus the result of a confused bureaucratic announcement within a context of massive pressure, and not a deliberate decision to dismantle the barrier. In the following months the movement shifted from demands for reform to demands for unification.

The other revolutions followed distinct paths. In Czechoslovakia, police violence against a student demonstration in Prague on November 17 triggered mass protests, the formation of the Civic Forum led by Václav Havel, a general strike on November 27, and the resignation of the communist leadership; a largely non-communist government took office in December, and Havel was elected president by the Federal Assembly on December 29, in what was called the Velvet Revolution. In Bulgaria, on November 10, the party leadership removed Todor Zhivkov, who had ruled since 1954, in a palace coup, after which a reformist communist leadership negotiated a transition. In Romania, Nicolae Ceaușescu's regime, the most repressive and personalized in the region, faced protest in Timișoara in mid-December after an attempt to remove a Hungarian Reformed pastor, László Tőkés; unrest spread to Bucharest, army units turned against the regime, and Ceaușescu and his wife fled on December 22, were captured, tried by a military tribunal, and executed on December 25. Fighting that followed killed many people; the number is commonly given as over a thousand, with the circumstances still debated. A National Salvation Front, led by former communist officials, took power.

The comparison shows that "1989" comprised different processes: a negotiated transition in Poland, a reform-led transformation in Hungary, mass protest and flight in East Germany, a rapid civic revolution in Czechoslovakia, an elite-led change in Bulgaria, and a violent overthrow in Romania. The common factor was the absence of a credible threat of Soviet intervention, combined with domestic delegitimization, economic stagnation, and the example of neighbors. The Soviet leadership's reaction was largely to accept the changes, though it expressed concern, especially about Germany.

Multiple Perspectives

The Opening of the Berlin Wall

East German protesters and ordinary citizens: For many, the Wall's opening fulfilled demands for freedom of travel and marked a break with a state that had restricted their lives; others sought reform of the GDR, not its end, and the aims of protest shifted within weeks.
Communist leaders: The East German leadership was divided and uncertain. Some tried to preserve party rule through limited concessions, and the Wall's opening, which was intended to relieve pressure, instead accelerated the regime's loss of control.
Soviet officials: Gorbachev and his advisers accepted the change but feared instability. Soviet leaders were concerned about the future of Germany, and military and conservative officials worried about the effects on Soviet security and on the troops stationed in the GDR.
Western governments: Leaders in the United States, West Germany, Britain, and France welcomed the end of the division of Berlin but differed about reunification: West German Chancellor Helmut Kohl pursued it rapidly, while some European leaders were cautious.

The perspectives illustrate that the same event carried different meanings and risks for different groups.

Section 65.35

65.35 — German Reunification, the Transformation of European Security, and the End of the Cold War, 1989–1990

After the Wall opened, German unification moved quickly onto the international agenda. Chancellor Kohl presented a ten-point plan on November 28, 1989, and in March 1990 the first free elections in East Germany produced a government that favored rapid union. The Soviet leadership, concerned about security, the position of the Soviet troops, and the reaction in the USSR, initially resisted, but the pace of events and Gorbachev's principles led to negotiation. Gorbachev and US President George H. W. Bush had met aboard ships off Malta on December 2–3, 1989, in a meeting that signaled cooperation and that both leaders later described as marking the end of the Cold War, though the phrase has been debated.

The settlement of the German question was reached through the "Two Plus Four" negotiations among the two German states and the four wartime Allies (the United States, the Soviet Union, Britain, and France). The key understanding was reached in July 1990 in meetings between Kohl and Gorbachev in Moscow and in the Caucasus, in which Gorbachev accepted that a united Germany could decide its own alliance membership, and thus could remain in NATO, in exchange for German commitments to limits on German forces, the withdrawal of Soviet troops from the former GDR by the end of 1994, and substantial German financial assistance. The treaty was signed in Moscow on September 12, 1990, and Germany was reunified on October 3. Western leaders gave verbal assurances about NATO during the talks, and whether these amounted to commitments regarding later NATO enlargement is a contested question; the written agreements do not contain such a commitment, and scholars disagree about the significance of the oral statements.

Other agreements followed. On November 19, 1990, in Paris, twenty-two states of NATO and the Warsaw Pact signed the Treaty on Conventional Armed Forces in Europe (CFE), setting equal ceilings on major categories of non-nuclear weapons, and on November 21 the CSCE states adopted the Charter of Paris for a New Europe. The Warsaw Pact still existed at the close of 1990, although its military role and future had become increasingly uncertain. Gorbachev was awarded the Nobel Peace Prize in October 1990 for his role in the transformation of East-West relations.

The domestic effects were negative for him. Many Soviet officials, military officers, and ordinary citizens regarded the loss of Eastern Europe and the acceptance of a united Germany in NATO as concessions without sufficient return; the ongoing economic crisis and the prospect of shifting trade with Eastern Europe toward hard-currency settlement made the benefits of the new relationship hard to see. The Soviet public, which had welcomed the end of confrontation, did not credit the leadership with prosperity. Gorbachev's international prestige stood high abroad while his domestic authority was falling.

Historical Debate

Gorbachev's Foreign-Policy Achievements and Their Domestic Costs

The question: What explains the end of the Cold War in Europe and the Soviet acceptance of the loss of its East European empire, and what did these outcomes cost Gorbachev at home?

Interpretation A — Leadership and ideas: Many scholars emphasize Gorbachev's new thinking, his refusal to use force, and his relations with Western leaders, arguing that a different Soviet leader might have chosen differently.
Interpretation B — Structural and economic pressure: Others stress the economic burden of the arms race and the empire, Soviet stagnation, and the effects of Western military and economic competition, arguing that constraints narrowed the choices available to any leader.
Interpretation C — East European agency: A third emphasis highlights the opposition movements, churches, workers, and reformist communists of Eastern Europe, who made the revolutions, and for whom Soviet policy was a permissive condition but not the cause.

Evidence: Summit transcripts and memoranda of conversations, Politburo records, the papers of Eastern European governments and oppositions, memoirs, and public opinion data.

Points of agreement: Most historians agree that the absence of Soviet intervention was a necessary condition for the revolutions of 1989, and that local movements and leaders shaped their course.

Points of disagreement: They disagree about the relative weight of leadership, structure, and Western policy, and about whether Gorbachev anticipated the results.

Section 65.36

65.36 — Chapter Review and Historical Thinking Checkpoint

This chapter has followed the Soviet reform process from the succession crises of 1982–1985 to the end of 1990. The central question, why an attempt to renew Soviet socialism produced economic disruption, political opening, institutional conflict, and the end of the Cold War, can now be answered in terms of interacting processes. Acceleration and discipline failed to produce expected results, and the leadership broadened its program to restructuring of the economy, openness in public life, and then democratization. Each step responded to the failure of the previous one and to resistance from the apparatus; each also released forces that the leadership had not planned for, from the revelations about Stalinism to the strikes and the revival of national and civic movements.

Transformations and continuity. The party-state's constitutional monopoly ended, a competitive legislature and a presidency were established, censorship eroded, and the Soviet position in Europe was dismantled by negotiation and by non-intervention. Continuities were also important: the planning ministries, the security services, the party apparatus, and the union structure persisted into 1991, and many citizens continued to hold socialist and Soviet commitments. The Soviet Union at the end of 1990 was neither a reformed socialist state nor a democracy; it was a hybrid in transition.

Causation and evidence. Students should distinguish long-term conditions (slowing growth, oil dependence, an aging leadership, accumulated social expectations), intermediate developments (Andropov's discipline campaign, the 1986 oil price fall, Chernobyl), and short-term decisions (the enterprise law, the 1988 Conference, the 1989 election rules, non-intervention in Eastern Europe). Evidence includes official documents, statistics of uncertain quality, press and television, memoirs written by participants with reasons to defend themselves, and records from other countries; each needs critical reading.

Competing interpretations. The central historical-thinking question is whether Gorbachev's reforms failed because they went too far, because they did not go far enough, or because the political and economic structures they attempted to change could not be transformed in the sequence he pursued. Supporters of the first view point to the weakening of the party and the loss of control; of the second, to the incomplete market reforms and hesitations of 1990; of the third, to the interaction of political liberalization, economic disruption, and the unresolved federal structure. Each interpretation highlights real evidence, and they are not mutually exclusive. None, however, establishes that the dissolution of December 1991 was predetermined: in 1990, alternatives, from a renewed union to repression, remained possible, and Chapter 66 examines how contingent events and republican politics closed them.

Broader significance. The period reshaped the international system, ended the division of Europe, and opened a new relationship between Russia and its neighbors. It also altered the relationship between state and society within the USSR, creating public political life, civil associations, and a historical debate that continued in the post-Soviet period (Chapters 67–72).

Chapter Summary

This chapter examined the Soviet reform process from the death of Brezhnev in 1982 to the end of 1990. Its central problem was why an attempt to renew and strengthen Soviet socialism produced economic disruption, unprecedented political openness, institutional conflict, and the transformation of the Cold War. The chapter argued that Gorbachev did not begin with a program of multiparty democracy, capitalism, or the end of the Union, and that his aims evolved as measures failed and as society responded.

Yuri Andropov and Konstantin Chernenko represented different responses to institutional difficulty: Andropov's discipline and personnel renewal, Chernenko's restoration of established routines. The deaths of three leaders in under three years, together with the aging of the Politburo, led to the choice of Gorbachev on March 11, 1985. His initial program of acceleration emphasized investment, technology, and discipline within the existing system.

The early campaigns, notably the anti-alcohol policy, revealed the limits of administrative methods, while the Twenty-Seventh Party Congress of 1986 gave official form to a vocabulary of renewal. The Chernobyl disaster of April 26, 1986, resulted from an interaction of operator actions, reactor design flaws, and institutional secrecy. Its human and environmental consequences, which must be described through distinct categories of evidence and attributed estimates, and the handling of information, strengthened arguments for openness without by itself causing glasnost.

Perestroika in the economy, expressed in the 1987 Law on State Enterprise, the 1988 Law on Cooperatives, and agricultural leasing, loosened the planning system without creating prices, markets, or financial discipline. Together with falling oil revenue, lost alcohol revenue, and rising wages, this produced shortages, repressed inflation, and the breakdown of supply links. Debates between Ryzhkov's regulated-market program and the 500 Days plan showed that economic questions had become entangled with questions of union authority.

Glasnost expanded from controlled criticism to open public debate. The reexamination of Stalinism, the publication of suppressed literature, the cultural transformation, the environmental movements, the millennium of 1988, and the growth of informal associations created a public sphere that the leadership had sponsored but could not control. Reopening the past raised questions about the legitimacy of the Soviet system itself.

Democratization proceeded through the Nineteenth Party Conference of 1988, the creation of the Congress of People's Deputies, the partly competitive elections of March 1989, the televised Congress, the miners' strikes, and the revision of Article 6 and creation of the presidency in March 1990. These changes ended the party's constitutional monopoly but left institutions unconsolidated and authority divided among competing centers, with reformers, centrists, and conservatives in conflict.

In foreign policy, new thinking led from Geneva and Reykjavík to the INF Treaty, the withdrawal from Afghanistan, the improvement of relations with China, and the acceptance of non-intervention in Eastern Europe. The revolutions of 1989 in Poland, Hungary, East Germany, Czechoslovakia, Bulgaria, and Romania were distinct processes, shaped by local movements as well as by the permissive condition of Soviet policy. German reunification within NATO and the CFE Treaty marked the end of the Cold War in Europe, at a time when Gorbachev's domestic position was weakening.

Historiographically, the period is a field of open debate about the sources of economic failure, the effects of democratization, and the causes of the end of the Cold War. Some conclusions have broad support, such as the disruptive effects of partial reform and the necessity of Soviet non-intervention for 1989; others, including counterfactual questions of sequencing, remain contested.

The chapter ends at the close of 1990 with the Union intact but its institutions under strain. The sovereignty declarations of the republics, the Union Treaty, the August 1991 coup, and the dissolution of the USSR are examined in Chapter 66, and the post-Soviet transformation in Chapter 67. Nothing in the evidence of 1985–1990 shows that the outcome of December 1991 was predetermined.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because the reforms of 1985–1990 changed the Soviet Union and the world more rapidly than the reformers anticipated. They moved a state from managerial acceleration to open politics in five years, and they ended the Cold War division of Europe through negotiation and non-intervention. They also demonstrated how reform in a highly centralized system can release social, national, and institutional forces that the reformers cannot direct.

What continued afterward was substantial. The economic dislocations, the habits of informal exchange, the cooperative and joint-venture experience, and the networks formed in this period shaped the post-Soviet economy (Chapter 67). The public sphere, the memory debates about Stalinism, and the clash between liberal and nationalist cultural currents carried into the 1990s and later (Chapters 68–72). The question of how to divide power between the center and the republics, only touched on here, became the central problem of 1990–1991.

The chapter also illustrates themes that run through the whole textbook: the relation between state and society, the role of information and memory in political legitimacy, the interaction of domestic reform with international relations, and the importance of regions and non-Russian peoples, from Chernobyl's Ukrainian and Belarusian victims to the miners of Kazakhstan and the movements of the Baltic republics. It shows that Soviet history cannot be reduced to the decisions of one leader or one capital.

Questions remain. Historians continue to debate whether a different sequence of reforms could have stabilized the system, how much weight to give leadership, structure, and external pressure, and how the memory of this period should be understood. Chapter 66 turns to the national revolutions, the August 1991 coup, and the dissolution of the Soviet Union, and asks students to hold on to this chapter's central lesson: that outcomes which appear inevitable in retrospect were, in 1990, still open.
