Part XII — The Russian Federation

The Rise of Vladimir Putin and the Reconstruction of the Russian State, 1999–2008

A History of Russia: From the Eurasian World to the Russian Federation

Chapter Opening

On the last day of 1999, the first president of the Russian Federation announced on television that he was leaving office six months early, asked the country's forgiveness for hopes that had gone unfulfilled, and handed his authority to a prime minister who had been in national politics for only four months.

Boris Yeltsin's resignation on 31 December 1999 was one of the most unusual transfers of power in modern Russian history. It was voluntary, it was announced on the eve of a new millennium, and it was constitutional in form: under the 1993 Constitution, discussed in Chapter 67, the prime minister became acting president and an election had to follow within three months. Yet the circumstances were anything but routine. The departing president was physically weak, politically isolated, and associated in the minds of many citizens with the economic hardship of the 1990s, the default of August 1998, and the first Chechen war. His chosen successor, Vladimir Putin, was a former intelligence officer and city administrator whom few Russians could have described a year earlier. Within three months Putin was elected president in the first round; within eight years he had presided over a transformation of the Russian state that historians still debate.

This chapter studies those eight years. It asks how the Russian Federation moved from the fragmented authority of the Yeltsin period toward a more centralized presidential state, while experiencing sustained economic recovery, rising household incomes, and increasingly constrained political competition. Each part of that question is documented, and each must be kept in view. The state of 2008 collected taxes more reliably, paid pensions on time, controlled its regions more tightly, and commanded greater international attention than the state of 1999. It also had a Federation Council whose members no longer represented independently elected governors, a parliament dominated by a single pro-presidential party, a television landscape in which the main national channels were under state or state-aligned control, and a set of security crises whose official explanations remain disputed by victims' families and independent investigators.

The chapter treats Vladimir Putin as the central political actor but not as the sole historical subject. The reconstruction of the Russian state involved the presidential administration, the security services, regional governors, parliamentary parties, major business owners, journalists, judges, soldiers, and millions of citizens who experienced the period as one of recovery, as one of constraint, or as both at once. It also involved Chechens, Dagestanis, and Ossetians, for whom the decade was dominated by war and terrorism; and it involved the neighboring states of Ukraine, Georgia, and Central Asia, whose political choices were never simply reactions to Moscow's policies.

The chapter's evidence is of a kind that earlier chapters of this textbook rarely enjoyed: contemporary election-observation reports, court judgments, television broadcasts, published statistical series, leaked and declassified diplomatic material, and the testimony of participants who are still alive. Abundance does not remove difficulty. Official statistics, government statements, opposition claims, and journalistic investigations each have characteristic strengths and blind spots, and the period's most politically charged questions, among them the 1999 apartment bombings and the Yukos case, require students to distinguish carefully among documented facts, judicial findings, official positions, and allegation.

The chapter also marks an important boundary within the textbook. Chapter 67 examined the inheritance of the Soviet collapse, market transformation, the constitutional crisis of 1993, the First Chechen War, and the election of 1996. The present chapter begins with the succession politics of 1999 and ends with the institutional situation at the close of Putin's second presidential term in May 2008. The Russo-Georgian War of August 2008, the global financial crisis, the Medvedev presidency and the Putin-Medvedev tandem, and the protests of 2011–2012 belong to Chapter 69. The annexation of Crimea and the later war in Ukraine are examined in Chapters 70 and 71. Reading the period 1999–2008 as though it already contained those later events would be a form of the teleology this textbook has warned against since Chapter 1.

By the end of the chapter, students should be able to explain how Putin came to power; describe the Second Chechen War and its political uses and consequences; analyze the institutional mechanisms of centralization, including federal districts, the reform of the Federation Council, and the appointment of governors; evaluate the changing relationship between the state and big business, with the Yukos affair as a case study; distinguish the contributions of energy prices, post-1998 recovery, and policy to economic growth; assess the changing conditions of elections, media, and civil society; and trace the shift in relations with the United States, NATO, the European Union, and neighboring post-Soviet states.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why Vladimir Putin emerged as Boris Yeltsin's successor in 1999, identifying the interests of the competing political and economic groups involved and avoiding the assumption that his rise was predetermined.
  2. Distinguish the Dagestan incursions of August 1999 from the September apartment bombings, and evaluate the kinds of evidence available for the unresolved questions surrounding the latter.
  3. Trace the course of the Second Chechen War, from the renewed intervention of 1999 through the policy of "Chechenization," and analyze its military, humanitarian, and political dimensions.
  4. Analyze the terrorist attacks at the Dubrovka theater (2002) and Beslan (2004) and the ways in which official responses, court judgments, and public debate treated state responsibility for the protection of hostages.
  5. Describe the institutional mechanisms of the "vertical of power," including the federal districts, the reform of the Federation Council, the appointment of governors, and the role of the security services.
  6. Assess the changing relationship between the Kremlin and major business owners, using the cases of Berezovsky, Gusinsky, and Khodorkovsky and the expansion of state-controlled enterprise in the energy sector.
  7. Differentiate the contributions of post-1998 recovery, energy prices, and economic policy to Russia's growth between 2000 and 2008, and interpret statistical evidence on incomes, poverty, and demography with appropriate care.
  8. Evaluate the evidence for and against the claim that Russian elections in this period were both genuinely popular and increasingly uncompetitive, using documents of the OSCE and other sources.
  9. Explain the transformation of national television and the changing conditions of journalism, civil society, and political opposition.
  10. Contextualize the shift from cooperation to disagreement in relations with the United States, NATO, and neighboring states, from the aftermath of 11 September 2001 to the Bucharest summit of 2008.
  11. Compare the political centralization of the early 2000s with earlier episodes of state-building in Russian history without assuming that similarity has been demonstrated merely because it has been asserted.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did Yeltsin's circle choose Putin as successor in 1999, and what alternatives did it face?
  2. How did security crises, above all the war in Chechnya and terrorist attacks, shape public expectations of the state and the legitimacy of the new president?
  3. What specific legal and administrative changes produced the "vertical of power," and who gained and lost authority as a result?
  4. Was the centralization of the early 2000s a restoration of state capacity, a reduction of political accountability, or both? What evidence supports each claim?
  5. How did the relationship between business owners and the state change between 1999 and 2008, and what does the Yukos case reveal about property rights and political power?
  6. How much of Russia's economic growth after 1999 can be attributed to recovery from the 1998 crisis, to energy prices, and to reforms and policy decisions?
  7. How did ordinary households, in different regions and social positions, experience the decade?
  8. What changed in elections, media, and civil society, and how do historians distinguish documented changes from unsupported assertions?
  9. Why did relations with the United States and NATO shift from early cooperation to open disagreement, and how did neighboring states' own choices affect that shift?

Historical Context

The Russian Federation that Putin inherited in 1999 was a state whose institutional shape had been set in the crises of the previous decade. The Soviet collapse (Chapter 66) had left Russia with the territory, the nuclear arsenal, the permanent United Nations Security Council seat, and much of the bureaucracy of the Soviet center, but with few settled rules for how power should be exercised among Moscow, the regions, and the market. The constitutional crisis of 1993 and the Constitution adopted that December (Chapter 67) created a strong presidency, a bicameral Federal Assembly consisting of a State Duma and a Federation Council, and a Constitutional Court. In practice, however, the presidency's formal powers were often greater than its capacity to implement decisions.

That gap between formal authority and practical capacity was the central problem of the Yeltsin years. Tax collection was erratic; many wages and pensions went unpaid for months; enterprises and local governments relied on barter and offsets; and the central government's fiscal position collapsed in the financial crisis of August 1998, when Russia defaulted on part of its domestic debt and the ruble lost much of its value. Regional governors, many of them directly elected since 1996, negotiated bilateral agreements with the federal government that gave some republics, notably Tatarstan, special fiscal and administrative arrangements, and some regional laws contradicted federal law. Chechnya had, after the Khasavyurt agreement of 1996, become a de facto independent territory whose government under Aslan Maskhadov was unable to prevent kidnapping, criminal activity, and the growth of armed groups, some influenced by militant Islamist ideas imported from abroad.

Economic power was similarly fragmented. The privatization programs of the 1990s, in particular the loans-for-shares auctions of 1995–1996, had concentrated ownership of major resource companies, banks, and media outlets in the hands of a small number of businessmen, often called oligarchs. These individuals exercised influence over the Yeltsin administration, over regional governments, and over television channels, particularly during the 1996 election. At the same time, Russian output had fallen sharply during the 1990s, and by the end of the decade many households relied on small plots, informal trade, and family networks. The default of 1998 devalued the ruble by about two thirds, but it also made imports expensive and domestic production newly competitive, creating the conditions for the recovery that began in 1999.

Internationally, Russia's position was ambivalent. The country had joined the Council of Europe and signed a partnership agreement with the European Union, and it participated in the G8. Yet disagreements over NATO's eastward enlargement, which brought Poland, Hungary, and the Czech Republic into the alliance in March 1999, and over NATO's air campaign against Yugoslavia in the same year, left Russian officials and a considerable part of the public convinced that their country's interests were being disregarded. These grievances, and the competing view held by many in Central Europe and the West that enlargement answered the security needs of formerly dominated states, formed the background to the foreign policy of the new decade.

Students should keep several cautions in mind. First, the Yeltsin period was not simply chaos, nor was the Putin period simply order; both were shaped by structures that overlapped. Several institutions often associated with Putin, such as the primacy of the presidency and the importance of security officials, had roots in the 1990s. Second, personal and institutional explanations should be combined: Putin's choices mattered, but they were made within the constraints of a particular constitutional order, a particular fiscal situation, and a particular political coalition. Third, the period's most vivid episodes, wars, hostage crises, and arrests, were experienced differently by people in different places, and the national political narrative should not be mistaken for the whole of Russian life.

Section 68.1

The Final Crisis of the Yeltsin Presidency, 1999

The Weakening of the Presidency, 1998–1999

By 1999 Boris Yeltsin held the most powerful office in the Russian political system but could use that power only intermittently. His health had deteriorated after heart surgery in 1996, and he was frequently absent from public view. The financial crisis of August 1998 had destroyed the credibility of the economic team of Prime Minister Sergei Kiriyenko, and the political settlement that followed, which brought Yevgeny Primakov to the premiership in September 1998, effectively forced the president to accept a prime minister supported by the State Duma, including its Communist faction. The constitutional order of 1993 gave the president wide latitude to dismiss prime ministers, but it also gave the Duma the ability to refuse confirmation, and a Duma refusal repeated three times would trigger its own dissolution. Both sides had reason to avoid that outcome.

Primakov proved a more successful premier than many expected. A former head of foreign intelligence and foreign minister, he assembled a government that presided over the first phase of post-crisis stabilization and enjoyed high public approval. That popularity was precisely what made him a threat in the eyes of Yeltsin's entourage. On 12 May 1999 Yeltsin dismissed Primakov. The Duma's impeachment debate followed on 13–15 May, and on 15 May none of the five charges, including those concerning the first Chechen war and the dissolution of the Soviet Union, obtained the two-thirds majority (300 of 450 votes) required. The dismissal therefore came in the midst of an escalating institutional confrontation, immediately before the vote in which the same Duma declined to remove the president; historians read the sequence as evidence of how intense the struggle between the presidency and its opponents had become, and of the president's determination to remove a prime minister who enjoyed support in the Duma and the public.

The Rotation of Prime Ministers

Yeltsin replaced Primakov with Sergei Stepashin, a former interior and security minister, whom the Duma confirmed in May 1999. Stepashin's tenure lasted less than three months. On 9 August 1999 Yeltsin dismissed him and nominated Vladimir Putin, then director of the Federal Security Service and secretary of the Security Council, as prime minister, announcing at the same time that he considered Putin a suitable successor. The Duma confirmed the nomination on 16 August by a modest margin. By this point Russia had had five prime ministers in less than eighteen months, a rate of turnover that reflected the president's determination to control the succession and the weakness of any institutionalized process for resolving it.

Historians have offered several explanations for why the choice fell on Putin. One emphasizes personal loyalty: Putin had a reputation, among those who dealt with him in the presidential administration and the FSB, for discretion and reliability, and he had shown no sign of building a rival political base. Another stresses the position he occupied, the leadership of the principal domestic security service, which gave the president a figure able to guarantee the security of the incumbent and his associates after the transfer of power. A third emphasizes the political needs of the moment: a younger, apparently vigorous figure with a background in security and administration might appeal to a public tired of instability. These explanations are not mutually exclusive, and none can be confirmed from the surviving evidence alone, since the decisions were taken within a small circle whose members later offered differing accounts.

Competing Elites and the Contest for the Succession

The succession was contested because it determined the distribution of political and economic influence. Yeltsin's own circle, often referred to as "the Family," included his daughter Tatyana Dyachenko, the head of the presidential administration, Aleksandr Voloshin, and several business figures, among them Boris Berezovsky and Roman Abramovich. Its members had a strong interest in a successor who would not reopen privatization outcomes or pursue prosecutions. Against them stood a rival establishment coalition. In 1999 Moscow Mayor Yuri Luzhkov and former Prime Minister Primakov formed the Fatherland–All Russia bloc, which combined the resources of the capital and several regional governors with Primakov's national popularity, and it was widely regarded as the early favorite to win the Duma elections of December and then the presidency in 2000.

Yeltsin's circle responded by building an alternative. In autumn 1999 a new bloc, officially called Unity (nicknamed "the Bear" after its symbol), was organized with the support of officials and businesspeople and the endorsement of Putin, who said that he would vote for it. Its lead candidate was Sergei Shoigu, the popular minister for emergency situations. National television, notably the channel ORT, in which Berezovsky had a substantial interest, was used for campaigning against Luzhkov and Primakov, whose health and personal associations were attacked in a manner that observers described as unusually harsh. Section 68.3 discusses how the events of August and September 1999 changed the political context in which these contests took place.

The outcome illustrates a recurring feature of the period: the speed with which political preferences shifted. In the summer of 1999 polling showed Putin with single-digit support. By December Unity had won roughly a quarter of the party-list vote, close behind the Communist Party, while Fatherland–All Russia, which had once been the favorite, finished well behind. Historians attribute the transformation to the combined effect of the security crisis, the media campaign, and the shift of regional and bureaucratic elites toward the apparent winner, a phenomenon sometimes called the "bandwagon" effect. The collapse of Fatherland–All Russia as an independent force, and its merger with Unity in 2001 to form United Russia, ended the immediate threat of a rival establishment party.

The institutional setting of the confrontation explains why the dismissal of prime ministers became the president's principal instrument. The 1993 Constitution allowed the president to dismiss the government at will, but a prime minister required Duma confirmation, and three Duma refusals of a nominee permitted the president to dissolve the chamber. In 1998–1999 neither side wished to test that provision, so the president's power over the government was real but costly to use. The spring of 1999 also saw an affair that illustrates how legal institutions were drawn into succession politics: Prosecutor-General Yuri Skuratov, who was investigating allegations concerning the presidential property administration, was suspended after the broadcast of a videotape of a man resembling him with women, and the Federation Council twice declined to remove him. Observers disagreed then, and historians still disagree, about how far the affair was a legitimate inquiry, a political intervention, or both; it is cited here because it shows that prosecutors, security services, and television were all instruments in the contest.

Regional leaders formed a second arena. Many governors, whose own political survival depended on their relationship with the federal government, were uncertain which camp would prevail, and their choices in autumn 1999 helped decide the outcome of the Duma elections: governors who aligned with Unity could deliver administrative support, local media, and votes. The shift of regional elites toward the apparent winner is an example of how formal institutions, including elections, operated in a setting where officeholders had strong incentives to anticipate the result. It also means that the pro-Kremlin victory of December 1999 reflected not only the preferences of voters but the calculations of the officials who organized the campaign.

Public opinion moved quickly but not arbitrarily. Surveys by Russian polling organizations recorded the new prime minister's rating rising from a very low level in August 1999 to a majority approval by the end of the year, and to higher levels in 2000. Historians generally connect this to the combination of the Chechen campaign, the sense of an energetic and decisive contrast with a visibly ailing president, and the early signs of economic recovery after the 1998 devaluation. Opinion data cannot by themselves distinguish genuine preference from the effects of media coverage, which is why the discussion of television in Section 68.29 matters for interpreting the figures.

Section 68.2

Vladimir Putin: From the KGB to the Federal Government

Vladimir Vladimirovich Putin was born in Leningrad on 7 October 1952 into a working-class family; his father had served in the Soviet navy and later worked in a factory, and his mother held a variety of manual jobs. He studied law at Leningrad State University, graduating in 1975, and joined the KGB in the same year. His service included work in the Leningrad directorate and, from 1985 to 1990, a posting in Dresden in the German Democratic Republic, where he worked in the KGB's liaison office. He returned to Leningrad shortly before the collapse of the East German state, and left the KGB in August 1991, at the time of the coup attempt discussed in Chapter 66, with the rank of lieutenant colonel.

His political career began in the city administration. From 1990 he served as an adviser to Anatoly Sobchak, the reformist chairman of the Leningrad city council and then mayor of St. Petersburg, and in the 1990s he became head of the city's committee for foreign economic relations and a deputy mayor. When Sobchak lost the 1996 mayoral election, Putin moved to Moscow, working first in the presidential property management department under Pavel Borodin and, from 1997, as a deputy head of the presidential administration responsible for relations with regional governments. In July 1998 Yeltsin appointed him director of the FSB, and in March 1999 he additionally became secretary of the Security Council. In each of these positions Putin was valued as an administrator who handled assignments efficiently rather than as an independent political figure.

This career should not be read as a predetermined path to the presidency. At several points the outcome could have been different: Yeltsin had dismissed other prime ministers who appeared to be preferred successors, and at the beginning of August 1999 no observer, including most of Putin's colleagues, expected him to hold power for long. The route from regional administrator to head of state was the result of the choices of others, of institutional opportunity, and of events, notably the security crises of that autumn, that Putin did not control in their origins.

Several features of Putin's earlier career are important for the interpretation of his presidency, and each should be described with attention to the quality of the evidence. His years in the KGB's foreign intelligence service in East Germany coincided with the final phase of the Soviet bloc; he later described the collapse of the East German regime as formative, but the details of his intelligence work are known mainly from his own recollections and from accounts by colleagues. In St. Petersburg, as head of the committee for foreign economic relations in the early 1990s, he was responsible for contracts that exchanged raw materials for food imports during a period of shortage. A city council commission examined those contracts in 1992 and raised questions about their implementation; no criminal charges resulted, and the episode is cited by critics and discounted by defenders. Students should treat it as an allegation that was investigated and not proved in court.

The Moscow years placed him inside the administrative core of the presidency. In 1997 he became a deputy head of the presidential administration with responsibility for relations with regional authorities and oversight of the control directorate, an experience that gave him a close view of the problems of enforcing federal decisions on the regions. As director of the FSB from July 1998 he oversaw the reorganization of an agency that had been shrunken and reformed after 1991, and in April 1999 he appeared on television in connection with the Skuratov affair, saying that the man on the videotape resembled the prosecutor. Whatever one concludes about the affair itself, the episode showed the Yeltsin circle that he would act in its interest.

Two further points need emphasis. First, Putin had never been elected to any office before 2000, and had no party; his political capital was derived from the offices he held and from the decisions of others. Second, the characterization of his background as simply "KGB" can mislead: he also spent several years in municipal government in a city that was among the most liberal in Russia, and his early circle in Moscow included both former security colleagues and economists who had worked with Sobchak in St. Petersburg. This mixture of liberal-minded technocrats and security professionals persisted in his governments and is one reason historians avoid a single label for his policy orientation.

Section 68.3

Dagestan, the 1999 Apartment Bombings, and the Security Crisis

In August 1999, the month Putin became prime minister, armed groups led by the Chechen commander Shamil Basayev and the Arab-born fighter known as Khattab entered the mountainous western districts of Dagestan from Chechnya, declaring their intention to establish an Islamic state in the region. Dagestan, the most ethnically varied of the Russian North Caucasus republics, mobilized local militias alongside federal troops, and most Dagestanis did not support the incursion; by the end of September the fighters had been driven out. This episode should be distinguished from the events that followed.

In September 1999 a series of explosions destroyed apartment buildings in several cities: in Buynaksk, Dagestan, on 4 September; in Moscow, on Guryanova Street on 9 September and on Kashirskoye Highway on 13 September; and in Volgodonsk, Rostov region, on 16 September. Roughly three hundred people were killed and many more were injured. The attacks produced widespread fear; residents in cities across Russia organized night watches over their buildings. On 22 September residents of an apartment building in Ryazan reported suspicious sacks in a basement, and local police and bomb-disposal specialists responded. The FSB subsequently stated that the incident was a training exercise; critics of that explanation have questioned its consistency. Russian authorities attributed the bombings to North Caucasian militants and, after investigations and trials, convicted several individuals for some of the attacks. The evidence for and against alternative explanations is examined in the Historical Debate below.

The Dagestan campaign needs its own context. Since 1998 a group of villages in the Buynaksk district, including Karamakhi and Chabanmakhi, had been governed by local adherents of Salafi Islam who rejected the authority of the republican government and the traditional Sufi religious establishment of Dagestan. Their ties to commanders in Chechnya, including Basayev and Khattab, brought them into the plans for the August 1999 incursion. The federal response combined air strikes, ground operations, and the mobilization of local volunteers; Russian forces took the villages in September, and the destruction of Karamakhi and Chabanmakhi displaced thousands of residents. Dagestani society was divided among those who sympathized with the Salafi communities, those who supported the Sufi establishment and the government, and the many who wished simply to avoid war. This internal division is one reason the incursion did not produce the uprising its leaders hoped for.

The apartment bombings had a political effect that can be described without resolving the question of responsibility. Residents in many cities organized volunteer patrols of their buildings; police checked basements and conducted document checks of people from the Caucasus; ethnic tension rose, and Chechens and other North Caucasians reported harassment. In late September Putin used a crude colloquial expression to promise that terrorists would be pursued relentlessly, a remark widely reported at the time and later cited as an indicator of his public style. The combination of fear and a vigorous response contributed to the rapid rise in approval for the prime minister. The government's handling of the Ryazan incident, and the Duma's refusal to establish an investigative commission, are the main points on which critics have questioned the official account.

Courts eventually convicted individuals for particular attacks. A trial in Dagestan concerned the Buynaksk bombing, and in 2004 a Moscow court sentenced two men from Karachay-Cherkessia to life imprisonment in connection with the Moscow and Volgodonsk attacks; the courts named other suspects who were not apprehended. Critics of the official account argued that the trials did not address key evidence, and that a separate public commission, whose members included prominent liberal politicians and human-rights figures, could not obtain access to documents and witnesses. Students should record both facts: convictions were obtained in Russian courts, and credible observers continued to question the thoroughness of the investigation. A historian of the period must also remember that the political effects of the bombings do not depend on any single theory of their origin.

Historical Debate

What Do Historians Know About the 1999 Apartment Bombings?

The question: Who was responsible for the September 1999 apartment bombings, and what role did the attacks play in the political rise of Vladimir Putin?

Official account: Russian prosecutors and courts attributed the Buynaksk bombing and the Moscow and Volgodonsk attacks to militants from the North Caucasus, convicted several defendants in separate trials, and named further suspects who remained at large. In this account the attacks were part of the broader campaign by armed groups based in Chechnya and Dagestan, and the Ryazan incident was an exercise or a misunderstanding.
Critical accounts: Some journalists, former officials, and independent researchers, including some who emigrated, argued that the available evidence is inconsistent with the official account and that elements of the state security apparatus may have been involved, citing the Ryazan incident, the limited disclosure of forensic material, and the closing of requests for an independent parliamentary inquiry. These accounts have not been confirmed by documentary evidence available to historians, and the principal claims have been rejected by the Russian authorities.

Evidence: Historians use trial records, journalistic investigations, testimony from residents and local officials, forensic reports where available, and later memoirs. Many of the most relevant documents remain closed.

Points of agreement: The bombings occurred, killed roughly three hundred civilians, produced fear that benefited demands for strong security policy, and coincided with Putin's rise in public opinion. Several defendants were convicted in Russian courts.

Points of disagreement: Whether the convicted individuals and their organizations were fully responsible, whether the Ryazan incident was an exercise, and whether the state security services played any role remain disputed. Some claims lack documentary support; the official account has also been criticized for gaps in disclosure.

Why it matters: The question concerns the origins of a political order, but a historian can establish the political effects of the attacks without resolving the question of responsibility.

Section 68.4

Yeltsin's Resignation and Putin's Acting Presidency

Yeltsin's announcement on 31 December 1999 came as a surprise to the public, although his entourage had prepared for it. The timing had practical consequences. Under the Constitution an election was required within three months of a presidential vacancy, which meant that the vote would be held in March rather than June, giving rival candidates less time to organize against a prime minister who was now also acting president and could draw on the resources of both offices. On the same day Putin signed a decree guaranteeing the outgoing president immunity from prosecution and certain material privileges, a measure that critics have seen as part of the price of the succession and that supporters viewed as a normal protection for a retiring head of state.

Putin's Programmatic Statements

Putin's first major programmatic statement, the article "Russia at the Turn of the Millennium," had been posted on the government's website on 30 December 1999. It presented the 1990s as a period of economic decline and social dislocation and argued that Russia needed to restore an effective state, while insisting that this was compatible with democratic institutions and a market economy. It spoke of what the author called the "Russian idea," composed of patriotism, the idea of a great power, a strong state, and social solidarity, and it rejected both the copying of foreign models and a return to the Soviet system. The text is a source for how the new leader wished to present his program; it is not a record of how that program would be carried out.

The first months of the acting presidency were marked by activity designed to show continuity with the departing administration and an emerging style of his own. On 1 January 2000 Putin traveled to Chechnya to visit the troops, an early signal of the weight he attached to the war. The decree of 31 December guaranteeing immunity for Yeltsin and his family was a continuation of the arrangement under which the succession had been negotiated. In the new Duma, Unity cooperated with the Communist Party and other groups to elect a speaker and allocate committee chairmanships, a pragmatic arrangement that gave the Kremlin a working majority without a coalition agreement and that lasted only until Unity and its allies had enough seats for independence. The Duma ratified the START II arms-control treaty in April 2000, after years of delay, a sign that the new legislative alignment could deliver on some foreign-policy priorities.

An open letter to voters published in February 2000 elaborated the themes of the millennium article, speaking of the need for a "dictatorship of law" and of equal application of the rules to all participants in political and economic life. The phrase has been interpreted in two ways: as a pledge to end the selective application of law that characterized the 1990s, and as a warning that the state would claim the power to use law as an instrument. Both readings find support in later developments, and the historian's task is to examine specific cases, as the following sections do, rather than to settle the question by the phrase alone. In May 2000, immediately after the inauguration, Putin appointed Mikhail Kasyanov as prime minister, with Aleksei Kudrin remaining at the finance ministry and Gref heading economic planning; the combination of continuity in the Yeltsin-era economic team and the introduction of security-service veterans in the Security Council and the administration set the pattern for the first term.

Section 68.5

The Presidential Election of March 2000

The presidential election of 26 March 2000 was won by Putin in the first round with approximately 53 percent of the vote, against about 29 percent for the Communist leader Gennady Zyuganov and under 6 percent for the liberal politician Grigory Yavlinsky of Yabloko. Turnout was roughly 69 percent. Putin ran as an independent candidate without a detailed platform and conducted a limited campaign, relying on his role as acting president and prime minister and on favorable coverage on the main national television channels. Observers from the OSCE and the Council of Europe concluded that the voting was largely orderly, but they criticized the lack of equal media access and the use of the incumbent's position. The election thus illustrates the pattern analyzed throughout this chapter: a leader with real and substantial public support who also enjoyed institutional advantages that limited competition.

The campaign was unusual in that the leading candidate did not offer a detailed program, declined to take part in televised debates, and relied on the visibility of his offices. Eleven candidates appeared on the ballot, including the Communist leader Gennady Zyuganov, the liberal Grigory Yavlinsky, the nationalist Vladimir Zhirinovsky, and the Kemerovo governor Aman Tuleyev, who ran as a regional figure with a social-democratic profile. Putin's campaign emphasized restoration of order, economic revival, and the war in Chechnya, and the main television channels gave him far more coverage than his rivals. Observers noted that voting in Chechnya took place under conditions that precluded independent verification; the Russian Central Election Commission reported turnout there that independent monitors could not confirm.

The result should be interpreted at two levels. At the level of voter preference, a majority of those who voted preferred Putin, and he enjoyed high approval in public-opinion surveys both before and after the election; this was a real base of support, strengthened by the security crisis and the early recovery. At the level of electoral institutions, the vote was held earlier than scheduled, the incumbent used the resources of the offices he held, and rivals had less access to the main channels of communication. The OSCE's observation mission for the 2000 election identified problems of this kind while concluding that the vote broadly reflected voters' preferences; students should use the report's actual wording, which is available in the OSCE's archive, in preference to summaries. Putin took the oath of office on 7 May 2000.

The composition of the first Putin government gave an early indication of his approach. Kasyanov, a finance official with experience of foreign debt negotiations, was appointed prime minister; Gref, a St. Petersburg economist, led the ministry of economic development; Kudrin remained responsible for finance; and Sergei Ivanov, a former intelligence colleague, headed the Security Council before moving to the defense ministry in 2001. This pairing of reform-minded economists with security officials meant that the administration pursued liberalizing economic measures and centralizing political ones at the same time, a combination that some observers found contradictory and others, following the logic of the millennium article, regarded as complementary. The question of which tendency would prevail in a given area was not resolved in 2000, and students should avoid reading later outcomes back into the first months of the presidency.

Section 68.6

The Second Chechen War: Origins and Renewed Intervention

The Second Chechen War cannot be understood without the aftermath of the first, examined in Chapter 67. The Khasavyurt agreement of August 1996 ended combat and deferred the question of Chechnya's final status, and in January 1997 Aslan Maskhadov, a former Soviet officer who had commanded Chechen forces, was elected president in a vote that international observers considered broadly acceptable. In practice his government could not establish a monopoly of force. Armed groups under field commanders such as Shamil Basayev and Salman Raduyev operated independently; kidnapping for ransom became a major source of income and affected Chechens, Russians, and foreign visitors alike; a Russian-language economy collapsed and much of Grozny remained in ruins; and Islamist networks, including volunteers from abroad such as Khattab, gained influence among part of the armed opposition. Moscow, for its part, provided little reconstruction aid, imposed economic restrictions, and did not conclude a definitive political settlement.

These conditions gave rise to two incompatible narratives that students will encounter in sources. In the Russian government's account, Chechnya had become a base for criminal and terrorist activity that threatened neighboring regions and the Russian state, and a military response was required after the Dagestan incursions and the apartment bombings. In the account of Chechen separatists and many human-rights organizations, Moscow had failed to honor the spirit of the 1996 settlement, and the new campaign was an unnecessary and disproportionate war that punished a civilian population for the actions of armed groups. Historians generally accept that the Dagestan incursion by Basayev and Khattab was a major trigger and that Chechen society was deeply divided, with many opposing both the war and the radicals. Russian forces crossed into northern Chechnya at the beginning of October 1999; Putin, as prime minister, declared Maskhadov's government illegitimate and committed the federal government to what was described as a counterterrorist operation.

Chechnya's political evolution after 1996 deserves attention because the Second Chechen War is sometimes presented as the continuation of the first without regard to the changes in between. The republic's declaration of independence in 1991 under Dzhokhar Dudayev, the war of 1994–1996, and the Khasavyurt agreement are examined in Chapter 67; the memory of the deportation of the Chechens in 1944, examined in Chapter 58, shaped the political culture of the republic and the suspicion with which many Chechens regarded Moscow. After 1996, divisions within Chechen society deepened. Maskhadov, who favored negotiation, had to contend with field commanders who rejected compromise; traditional Sufi brotherhoods, important in Chechen religious life, were challenged by Salafi preachers; and clan and village networks provided the main source of security for many families. Akhmad Kadyrov, then the republic's mufti, broke with Maskhadov and the radicals in 1999 and supported the federal side, an early example of the divisions that Moscow later exploited.

The kidnapping economy deserves precise treatment. Between 1996 and 1999 hundreds of people, including Russian civilians, journalists, aid workers, and foreign visitors, were abducted in Chechnya or neighboring regions, with ransoms and slave labor forming an important source of income for some armed groups. Estimates of the number of victims vary by source. The practice undermined the Chechen government's authority and the sympathy of Russian and foreign opinion. Moscow, for its part, did not provide the economic assistance promised in 1996, and the border regions restricted movement. Historians therefore describe the interwar period as one in which both Moscow and Grozny failed to create a functioning settlement, without assigning identical responsibility to each.

Russian decision-makers in 1999 had several options: containing the Chechen border, supporting Chechen opponents of the radicals, negotiating with Maskhadov, or launching a large-scale invasion. The documentary record of the decision is incomplete, and accounts by participants conflict. What is clear is that the Dagestan incursion and the bombings eliminated the political space for negotiation in Moscow, and that the leadership believed that a military operation, unlike in 1994, would receive public support. That belief proved correct in the short term.

Section 68.7

The Assault on Grozny and the Reconquest of Chechnya

The military campaign of 1999–2000 differed in important respects from that of 1994–1996. Federal commanders, drawing on the lessons of the earlier failure, relied on heavy artillery and air power to reduce the use of infantry in contested areas, advanced methodically through the flat northern districts, and sought to surround Grozny rather than assault it directly at the outset. Russian forces also enjoyed better coordination with the Interior Ministry and a more supportive domestic press and public than in 1994. The bombardment of Grozny and other communities, and an ultimatum in December 1999 reported to threaten that those who remained in the city would be treated as terrorists, drew international criticism for the risks it posed to civilians. Fighting in the city lasted into February 2000, when Chechen commanders withdrew southward, suffering heavy losses in crossing minefields near Alkhan-Kala.

Human-rights organizations documented serious abuses during these months. Reports by Human Rights Watch and by the Russian organization Memorial recorded the killing of civilians in Alkhan-Yurt in December 1999 and in the Grozny district of Novye Aldi in February 2000, as well as the detention and ill-treatment of men in so-called filtration procedures, a system in which individuals were screened for suspected links to fighters, at facilities including the Chernokozovo detention center. The Russian government rejected many of these accounts, and prosecutions of federal personnel were few. Estimates of civilian deaths vary widely and there is no authoritative total; sources differ on the numbers of fatalities and of people displaced, and students should treat any single figure as an estimate tied to a particular source and methodology. It is established that hundreds of thousands of people left their homes, many to neighboring Ingushetia, and that Grozny suffered extensive destruction.

The campaign unfolded in stages that can be traced on a map. In October and November 1999 federal forces occupied the northern plains up to the Terek and Sunzha rivers and advanced toward Grozny and Gudermes; the second phase, the encirclement and assault on Grozny from December, was followed in February 2000 by the movement of fighters into the southern mountains. The commanders used lessons from 1994–1996: they relied more on firepower than on infantry, tried to avoid entering the city without prior bombardment, and imposed tight restrictions on journalists. Reporters' access to Chechnya was limited, and episodes such as the detention in January 2000 of the Radio Liberty correspondent Andrei Babitsky, who was later transferred to Chechen fighters in an exchange arranged by the authorities, drew protest from Russian and international press organizations. For historians, the restriction of information is itself a limitation: many details of the campaign rest on military statements, accounts by the participants, and the later work of human-rights investigators.

The humanitarian effects were extensive. The United Nations and aid agencies estimated that at the peak about 200,000 people from Chechnya were sheltered in Ingushetia, in camps, railway cars, and private homes, with others displaced within Chechnya or to other regions of Russia. Medical care, water supply, and housing were severely damaged; Grozny, which had been heavily damaged in 1994–1996, was by 2000 described by the United Nations as one of the most destroyed cities in the world. Federal officials emphasized that safe corridors were offered and that the operation targeted armed groups; humanitarian organizations questioned the adequacy of the arrangements. In June 2000 Putin issued a decree establishing direct presidential rule in Chechnya and appointed Akhmad Kadyrov to head the administration, marking the transition from conventional war to the political phase discussed below.

Section 68.8

Insurgency, Counterinsurgency, and Civilian Life

The fall of Grozny did not end the war. Chechen fighters shifted to guerrilla methods: ambushes, mines, attacks on checkpoints, and assassinations of officials seen as collaborators. Some groups also turned to terrorism outside Chechnya, including suicide bombings, a tactic previously rare in the region. Federal forces responded with "sweep" operations (zachistki) in villages, checkpoints, and detentions. Human-rights organizations and, later, the European Court of Human Rights recorded repeated cases of enforced disappearances, torture, and extrajudicial killing, and of failures to investigate. In a series of judgments beginning in 2005 the Strasbourg court held Russia responsible for violations of the right to life and the prohibition of torture in Chechnya. Russian officials, for their part, pointed to attacks on civilians by militants and to the protection of the population from armed groups.

Civilian life in Chechnya during these years was shaped by insecurity, unemployment, and damaged infrastructure. Families lived with the risk of detention, the uncertainty of disappearances, and the presence of mines; women and children carried a disproportionate burden of displacement and loss of male relatives. Outside the republic, many Chechens in Russian cities faced suspicion and discrimination. Some historians stress that the war involved cycles of violence in which each side's tactics generated further recruitment; others emphasize the role of ideology and external funding in the insurgency. The evidence does not allow either account to be treated as complete.

The character of the insurgency changed over time. In the first phase, from 2000, it consisted of small units under commanders such as Basayev, Khattab (killed in 2002), and Gelayev, using the forests and mountains of the south, and it benefited from local support and from the grievances created by federal operations. A turning point came in June 2000, when the first suicide truck bombing of the conflict struck a federal base; later attacks included the destruction of the government building in Grozny in December 2002, an attack that killed dozens of people, and the downing of a military helicopter in August 2002 with heavy loss of life. Beyond Chechnya, a series of attacks in Moscow and other cities, including the bombing of a rock festival in July 2003, were attributed by the authorities to Chechen militants. These attacks, and the participation of women suicide bombers, influenced Russian public perceptions of the conflict and fed the policy of toughening security measures.

Human-rights organizations documented the system of detentions and disappearances: thousands of persons were reported missing in the course of the conflict, according to Memorial and other groups, though totals depend on definitions and on the willingness of families to report cases. The European Court of Human Rights began to issue judgments in 2005, including cases on the bombardment of civilians and on disappearances, in which it held that Russia had violated the right to life and failed to investigate. These judgments are retrospective sources: they were not available to participants at the time, but they provide detailed evidence drawn from testimony and documents. Russian authorities maintained that violations were individual crimes that were being prosecuted, and some servicemen were convicted, including a colonel convicted in 2003 for the murder of a young Chechen woman. Students should note the contrast between a small number of prosecutions and the large number of documented complaints.

Multiple Perspectives

Experiences of the War

Chechen civilians: For residents of Grozny and the villages, the war meant bombardment, displacement, filtration and sweep operations, and the loss of relatives. Many opposed both the radical fighters and the federal forces, and survival depended on local networks, humanitarian aid, and, for those who left, refugee camps in Ingushetia.
Russian conscripts and officers: Soldiers and Interior Ministry personnel served in an environment of mines, ambushes, and low morale in some units, while others, including volunteers and contract soldiers, treated service as career or income. Their experience is documented in memoirs, soldiers' mothers' organizations, and military press, and was often shaped by poor equipment and unclear command.
Russian citizens outside the Caucasus: After the apartment bombings and later attacks, many supported a firm response; others, particularly in human-rights circles, criticized the conduct of the war. Public opinion surveys show shifting attitudes, with support high in 1999–2000 and growing fatigue later, though polling in conditions of limited media pluralism has its own limits.
Dagestanis, Ingush, and Ossetians: Neighboring populations hosted refugees, saw armed groups operate on their territory, and became targets of attacks. Their experiences differed according to local political relations and historical memory, including the memory of the deportations of 1944 (Chapter 58).

These viewpoints show that the war was neither a single national experience nor a conflict between two homogeneous sides.

Section 68.9

Chechenization and the Rise of the Kadyrov Political Order

From 2000 Moscow adopted a policy, sometimes called Chechenization, of transferring day-to-day security and administration to Chechen officials loyal to the federal government. The first major figure was Akhmad Kadyrov, a religious leader who had fought against Russia in 1994–1996 and who was appointed head of the republic's administration in June 2000. A constitution was approved by a referendum in March 2003 and Kadyrov was elected president in October 2003; independent observers criticized both votes for the limits on competition and for the security conditions in which they were held, while Moscow presented them as steps toward normalization. On 9 May 2004 Kadyrov was killed in a bomb attack at the Grozny stadium during a Victory Day ceremony. Alu Alkhanov, a former interior-ministry official, was elected president in August 2004.

The most consequential long-term development was the rise of Akhmad Kadyrov's son Ramzan, who commanded the presidential security service, became prime minister in 2006, and was appointed president in 2007 with Putin's support. Armed formations loyal to him, sometimes called kadyrovtsy, took part in operations against insurgents and acquired a reputation for harsh methods, according to human-rights organizations. Meanwhile, the separatist movement lost its leaders: Maskhadov was killed in March 2005 and Basayev in July 2006. The insurgency, increasingly Islamist in orientation, spread into Ingushetia, Dagestan, and Kabardino-Balkaria, as in the armed raid on Nalchik in October 2005. Historians therefore distinguish administrative stabilization in Chechnya, supported by large federal transfers and reconstruction, from reconciliation, and note that the arrangement rested on the personal loyalty of a regional leader to the president. Its long-term implications for the relationship between federal and regional authority lie beyond this chapter.

The political process of 2003 illustrates both the aims and the limits of Chechenization. The constitutional referendum of March 2003 was reported to have been approved by a large majority on a very high turnout; independent observers and human-rights groups said that conditions of insecurity, the presence of the military, and restrictions on campaigning made it impossible to judge the real level of support. The presidential election of October 2003, in which Kadyrov was reported to have won a large majority, was preceded by the withdrawal or disqualification of several rival candidates; international organizations, including the OSCE, declined to send observers, citing security concerns, and criticized the process. An amnesty was offered to fighters, and a number of former insurgents joined pro-Moscow security formations. Moscow supported the process with large financial transfers and a reconstruction program, with the result that the visible rebuilding of Grozny and other towns accompanied the consolidation of a political order centered on the Kadyrov family.

The transition to Ramzan Kadyrov was managed in stages: he was named prime minister in March 2006, and in February 2007 Putin proposed him as president, a nomination approved by the Chechen parliament in March. The state recognized his role with the award of the title Hero of the Russian Federation in 2004 to his father and later honors to him. The model that emerged has been analyzed in two ways: as an example of the Russian state's delegation of authority to a regional leader who guarantees stability in exchange for resources and autonomy in internal affairs, and as a case in which the rule of law and the protection of rights were subordinated to personal loyalty. Both assessments rely on evidence of financial flows, human-rights reports, and the decline in the scale of violence in Chechnya itself, which is among the facts that supporters cite. The longer-term consequences are beyond this chapter's scope.

Section 68.10

The Moscow Theater Hostage Crisis of 2002

On 23 October 2002 a group of about forty armed men and women led by Movsar Barayev took hostage an audience of more than eight hundred at the Dubrovka theater in Moscow during a performance of the musical Nord-Ost, demanding the end of the war in Chechnya. After negotiations that included several public figures and journalists, the security services early on 26 October pumped an incapacitating gas into the building and stormed it. All the attackers were killed; approximately 130 hostages died, the great majority from the effects of the gas combined with inadequate medical preparation, delays in evacuation, and the authorities' reluctance to disclose the agent's composition to doctors. Russian officials later said the agent was based on derivatives of fentanyl.

The crisis raised questions that remain relevant for students of state responsibility. Supporters of the operation argued that the alternative was the detonation of explosives the attackers had placed in the hall and that the decision saved the majority of hostages. Critics argued that the casualties were greatly increased by poor planning. In 2011 the European Court of Human Rights, in Finogenov and Others v. Russia, found that the decision to use gas was not itself a violation of the right to life in the circumstances, but that the authorities had failed to plan and carry out the rescue and evacuation adequately and had failed to conduct an effective investigation.

The negotiations during the three days of the siege involved a changing set of intermediaries. The attackers, who described themselves as suicide bombers and wore explosive belts, released some hostages, including children and several foreign nationals; the authorities allowed a small number of public figures, doctors, and journalists to enter, among them the physician Leonid Roshal and the journalist Anna Politkovskaya. The official negotiating position was that the government would not agree to withdrawal from Chechnya, but that the hostages' lives would be protected. On the third night the attackers shot several hostages, which the authorities cited as the reason for the assault; critics noted that the planning for gas had begun before then. The gas was pumped through the ventilation system and the special forces entered in the early hours of 26 October; most of the attackers were shot while unconscious.

The medical response is central to the controversy. Hostages were carried out by rescuers and left lying on the ground or transported by city buses; doctors at the receiving hospitals were not told what substance had been used, and the antidote, naloxone, was in short supply. The authorities later said that the agent was based on derivatives of opioids, and did not publish its exact composition. At least 130 hostages died, nearly all of them of the effects of the gas. Subsequent coverage in the media was subject to government pressure: the main television channels were warned over their coverage of the siege, and legislation restricting the reporting of counterterrorist operations was passed and then revised after protests by journalists. Survivors' groups pressed for investigation and compensation for years, which is one of the sources of the evidence later reviewed by the Strasbourg court.

Section 68.11

Beslan, September 2004, and the Transformation of Counterterrorism

The Beslan attack was preceded by a summer of violence. In June 2004 insurgents raided police posts and government buildings in Ingushetia, killing dozens of officials and security personnel; in August two passenger aircraft crashed almost simultaneously after bomb explosions, and a bombing outside a Moscow metro station killed about ten people on 31 August. The hostage-taking therefore occurred in a climate of anxiety and in a security environment in which warnings of possible attacks existed; the Strasbourg court later examined whether the authorities had adequate information to prepare. On 1 September about thirty armed attackers drove into the school grounds during the opening ceremony and herded children, parents, and teachers into the gymnasium, which they wired with explosives. Water and food were denied for much of the siege, and early official statements put the number of hostages at about 350, far below the real figure of more than 1,100, a discrepancy that damaged public trust in official information.

Negotiations were minimal. Ruslan Aushev, the former president of Ingushetia, was allowed to enter on 2 September and secured the release of a small number of infants and mothers; the attackers' demands, which included the withdrawal of Russian forces from Chechnya and the release of detainees from the June raid, were not met. On 3 September explosions occurred in the gymnasium, the circumstances of which remain disputed; the authorities described them as detonations by the attackers, while some survivors and independent analysts have suggested that an external projectile might have triggered the first blast, a point the Strasbourg court addressed in assessing the evidence. A fire spread through the building and the roof collapsed. The official death toll was 334 people, including 186 children, and several hundred others were injured. Of the attackers, one, Nur-Pashi Kulayev, was captured alive and was convicted in 2006. Shamil Basayev claimed responsibility for the attack.

The sequence of events inside the school and the role of the security forces' weapons became the subject of prolonged controversy. A Russian parliamentary commission and the investigations of the prosecutor's office produced accounts that were criticized by a separate commission in North Ossetia and by organizations of victims' relatives, who maintained that tank and flamethrower fire had contributed to deaths. In 2017 the European Court of Human Rights, in Tagayeva and Others v. Russia, held that Russia had violated the right to life of hundreds of applicants through failures to take preventive measures despite available intelligence, inadequate planning and command, the use of indiscriminate lethal force, and the lack of an effective investigation. The Russian government rejected important parts of that judgment. Students should note that the court's findings concern state responsibility and do not diminish the responsibility of the attackers.

The use of an atrocity to justify institutional change is a theme historians continue to evaluate, and students should assess the connection by comparing the stated rationale with the actual content of the reforms.

Regional Context

The North Caucasus Beyond Chechnya

The North Caucasus is among the most linguistically and religiously varied regions of the Russian Federation, composed of republics with distinct histories, including Dagestan, Ingushetia, North Ossetia, Kabardino-Balkaria, and Karachay-Cherkessia. Their experience of the 2000s cannot be reduced to Chechnya. North Ossetia, with a largely Orthodox Christian population, was the site of Beslan and of earlier disputes with Ingushetia over the Prigorodny district after the deportations of 1944. Dagestan, with dozens of ethnic groups, experienced violence from both insurgent groups and security forces, and its political life depended on balances among its communities. Federal subsidies formed a large part of regional budgets throughout the region.

From the perspective of these republics, the Kremlin's policies of the period combined financial support, security operations, and the appointment of loyal leaders. The region's unemployment, clan networks, and the reputation of local security forces produced grievances that insurgent groups could exploit. This regional history is a reminder that the conflict spread beyond the borders of Chechnya and that explanations based only on events in Moscow are incomplete.

Division III — Reconstructing the State: The Vertical of Power

Section 68.12

The Meaning and Construction of the "Vertical of Power"

The phrase vertikal vlasti, "vertical of power," came into common use in Russian political discussion in these years to describe a chain of command running from the president through the federal government and the presidential administration to regional and local authorities. Putin used the expression of a "dictatorship of law" in early 2000 to describe the goal of making laws binding on all, a phrase that critics read as signaling a willingness to use law selectively and that supporters understood as a commitment to equal enforcement. Whatever the intention, the program required concrete institutional changes, examined here and in Sections 68.13–68.15.

Historical Vocabulary
Vertical of Power

Definition: A political term for a hierarchical structure of authority in which directives from the president are transmitted downward through subordinate officials, with each level accountable to the one above.

Historical context: Used from the early 2000s, both by supporters, who saw it as a remedy for the fragmentation of the 1990s, and by critics, who saw it as a replacement of accountability to voters with accountability to superiors. It describes an aspiration and a set of reforms, not a literal description of how the state always functioned.

Why it matters: Students should not assume that a formal chain of command guaranteed practical control; regional officials, bureaucratic agencies, and private actors retained ways of resisting, delaying, or reinterpreting orders.

Putin brought into his administration people from several backgrounds. Some, such as economic officials associated with German Gref and Aleksei Kudrin, were liberal-minded technocrats focused on fiscal and market reforms; others had careers in the security services, and some, such as Dmitry Medvedev, a St. Petersburg lawyer, came from the city administration. The Presidential Administration, headed successively by Voloshin, Medvedev, and Sergei Sobyanin, expanded as a coordinating body, and Vladislav Surkov, a deputy head, became the principal manager of domestic politics. Mikhail Kasyanov, who had been finance minister, served as prime minister until February 2004, when Putin dismissed him shortly before the presidential election and replaced him with Mikhail Fradkov.

The conceptual background of the term needs historical grounding. After the 1990–1991 "parade of sovereignties," when Yeltsin urged regional leaders to take as much autonomy as they could manage, the Russian Federation had to establish the relationship between the center and eighty-nine subjects without a Soviet-era party apparatus to coordinate them. The Constitution of 1993 asserted the supremacy of federal law but left the practice to political bargaining. The "vertical" emerged as a response to this problem and used the instruments the president controlled: appointments, the presidential administration, the Security Council, the prosecutor's office, and the federal budget. Scholars have emphasized that it also relied on informal networks of loyalty, and that the vertical's reach differed across sectors and regions.

Interpretations of the concept differ. Institutionalist accounts treat it as a program of state reconstruction in which formal rules and agencies were reorganized to improve coordination. Others, drawing on the study of patronage politics, argue that personal ties and the exchange of benefits for loyalty were the actual mechanism, and that formal institutions played a secondary role. The evidence of this period supports aspects of both: formal changes were real and measurable, and informal practices shaped how they worked. A third caution comes from the study of bureaucratic behavior: orders flowing downward could be reinterpreted, delayed, or ignored, and the central government lacked complete information, so the term describes an ambition more than an achieved condition.

The Federal Inheritance

The Russian Federation of 1999 consisted of eighty-nine "subjects of the federation": republics, territories (krai), provinces (oblast), two federal cities (Moscow and St. Petersburg), an autonomous oblast, and autonomous districts (okrug). The Federation Treaty of 1992 and the Constitution of 1993 gave these units formal equality, but in practice republics such as Tatarstan and Bashkortostan had negotiated special arrangements, and from 1994 the federal government had concluded dozens of bilateral power-sharing treaties with individual regions. Governors, directly elected since 1996 in most regions, acted as political entrepreneurs: some reached favorable fiscal agreements with Moscow, some withheld tax revenue, and some enacted laws at variance with federal legislation. Federal officials in 1999–2000 counted thousands of such regional acts.

This was not simply disorder. Strong governors sometimes provided stability and public services, and regional variety reflected real differences of economy and ethnic composition. But the arrangement meant that citizens in different regions could enjoy different rights and fiscal treatment, and that federal policy could be blocked by local resistance. The changes of 2000–2005 were intended to address that problem and substantially redistributed power.

Section 68.13

The Seven Federal Districts and Presidential Plenipotentiaries

A decree of 13 May 2000 created seven federal districts, each headed by a plenipotentiary representative of the president: Central, Northwest, South, Volga, Urals, Siberia, and Far East. The districts grouped the regions and did not themselves have elected governments; the envoys' formal tasks were to coordinate federal agencies, monitor the implementation of presidential decisions, and ensure the conformity of regional law with federal law. Several of the first envoys had backgrounds in the security services or the military, though others, such as Sergei Kiriyenko, a former prime minister, were political figures.

In practice the plenipotentiaries performed a range of functions. They convened meetings of federal officials in each district, which had not previously had a common coordinating body, and they reviewed regional legislation against federal law; the Ministry of Justice and the prosecutors reported that thousands of regional acts needed to be amended, and by the middle of the decade the great majority had been brought into line. They also took part in the selection of regional prosecutors and the supervision of federal personnel, and the reorganization of federal agencies, such as the Interior Ministry and the courts, tended to follow the district structure. Their influence varied with the person: Kirienko in the Volga district concentrated on economic coordination and the revival of regional industry, while envoys with military or security backgrounds emphasized law enforcement. In 2004 Dmitry Kozak became the envoy to the South, in charge of a region marked by violence, and later in the decade the district system continued to expand.

Regional leaders responded in different ways. Some governors complained that the envoys duplicated their role and diminished their standing; others used the envoys as intermediaries with the Kremlin. By the end of the decade the plenipotentiaries' role as monitors of governors had become less significant as the appointment procedure of 2004 gave the president more direct leverage. This pattern illustrates how an institution created for one purpose can acquire a different function when the surrounding institutions change.

The district system also had a security dimension that should be distinguished from its economic and administrative functions. The boundaries of the new districts coincided in most cases with those of the military districts and the regional organization of the Interior Ministry, which made it easier to coordinate the work of these agencies, and the South district was created with the situation in the Caucasus in mind. The first envoys were charged with reporting on the political situation in the regions, and their reports to the presidential administration were one of the channels through which information about regional conflicts reached Moscow. The question for historians is whether these channels increased the center's real knowledge or mainly its capacity to apply pressure; surviving documents are limited, and the answer is likely to have varied by region and by envoy.

The change also had a symbolic aspect. By placing an additional level between the president and the governors, the reform signaled that the relationship of governors to the center would no longer be one of negotiation between near equals. The governors' public responses were mostly cautious, since the president's popularity was high and the reform did not alter their formal powers at once. Over time, as the changes of 2000–2005 accumulated, the combined effect was larger than the effect of any single measure, which is a useful reminder that institutional change may be incremental and cumulative.

Section 68.14

Reforming the Federation Council and Federal Institutions

Under the 1993 Constitution, the Federation Council consisted of two representatives from each subject, and in practice from 1996 these were the governor and the chair of the regional legislature, who held seats ex officio. This gave governors a place in national lawmaking, parliamentary immunity, and a collective voice. In July 2000 laws were passed under which the chamber would be composed of representatives appointed by the governor and by the regional legislature, who would serve full time. The new rules took effect in stages through 2002. The same package gave the president the power, after a court finding, to remove a governor for violating federal law and to dissolve a regional legislature. A new State Council, an advisory body of governors chaired by the president, was created as compensation.

The immediate effect was to separate governors from the upper house, to end their immunity, and to turn the chamber into a body whose members depended on, and were often chosen for, their relationship with the Kremlin. In 2001–2003 a commission under Dmitry Kozak reviewed the division of powers between the federal and regional governments, and the bilateral treaties were allowed to lapse; Tatarstan's treaty was replaced by a narrower agreement in 2007. Federal revenue also became more centralized as more taxes were assigned to the federal budget, and regions grew more dependent on transfers.

The Federation Council's transformation was among the most consequential and least visible of the reforms. Under the old arrangement the chamber included about 178 members, among them eighty-nine governors and the speakers of eighty-nine regional legislatures, who attended sessions intermittently and who were protected by immunity. Under legislation signed in the summer of 2000, each region was represented by one person chosen by the governor and one chosen by the regional legislature, who would serve full time in Moscow; the change was phased in during 2001 and 2002. Supporters said it would professionalize the chamber and end the practice by which governors neglected either their regional duties or their legislative role; critics said it would turn the chamber into a body of appointees, many of them wealthy businessmen or former officials who bought or received seats.

Evidence from the next years bears out some of both claims. The chamber became more consistently cooperative with the executive; in 2001 Sergei Mironov, a St. Petersburg politician close to the president, became its chair, and the chamber seldom rejected legislation passed by the Duma. At the same time, the institution retained a role as a forum in which regional interests were represented through the appointing governors, and in some cases it did amend or delay legislation. The reform of fiscal federalism also mattered: the Budget Code and tax legislation adopted in 2000–2001 shifted the division of tax revenue toward the federal budget, so that regions' share of consolidated revenue fell and their dependence on federal transfers rose. The governors, who had lost both their national platform and some of their fiscal independence, were compensated with the State Council, an advisory body, and with federal projects in their regions.

Section 68.15

Regional Governors, Elections, and the Reform of 2004

On 13 September 2004, ten days after Beslan, Putin proposed that regional heads be nominated by the president and approved by regional legislatures, with the president empowered to dissolve a legislature that twice rejected his nominee. The law was signed on 11 December 2004 and applied from the beginning of 2005, when the first nominations were made. Officials argued that the change would improve coordination in the fight against terrorism and curb corruption and the influence of local business and criminal groups in gubernatorial campaigns. Critics, including some within the establishment, argued that the link between terrorism and the system of election was unclear, that the reform removed an element of direct accountability, and that it was in tension with the constitutional principle that the organization of regional government is a matter for the subjects of the federation within the framework of the Constitution. The Constitutional Court did not block the measure. During these years the federal government also encouraged the merging of some regions with autonomous districts, reducing the number of subjects from 89 to 83 by 2008.

The application of the new system can be traced in the personnel decisions that followed. The president's nominations for governorships were often incumbents, in line with the principle that experienced regional leaders were valuable, but they also included replacements, some of them from the federal bureaucracy or the security services. Long-serving regional leaders, among them Mintimer Shaimiev of Tatarstan, were reappointed, which suggests that the center negotiated with powerful regional figures. By 2008 the number of governors who had originally been elected and then reappointed was large; the system allowed the president to remove governors by declining to renominate them, and the pressure of such a possibility was an important instrument. Students should recognize that the same measure could produce stability in regions where elections had been polarizing and a loss of local accountability elsewhere.

Alongside appointments, the federal government supported the consolidation of regions. Referendums in the period merged Perm oblast with the Komi-Permyak autonomous district in 2005, Krasnoyarsk krai with the Taymyr and Evenk districts in 2007, the Kamchatka oblast with the Koryak district in 2007, the Irkutsk oblast with the Ust-Orda Buryat district in 2008, and the Chita oblast with the Aga Buryat district in 2008, reducing the number of federal subjects from eighty-nine to eighty-three. Supporters argued that the mergers rationalized administration and improved the finances of small districts; opponents among the affected indigenous communities, notably Buryats and Evenks, were concerned about the loss of institutional representation. Elections to the merged units were held and the results were reported as favorable, but the ethnic dimension was one of the issues raised.

Multiple Perspectives

The End of Gubernatorial Elections

The presidential administration: Officials presented the reform as a response to a national security emergency and to problems of coordination, arguing that popular elections had produced local fiefdoms and that regional heads should be part of a single chain of responsibility.
Regional governors: Responses varied. Some, seeking reappointment, welcomed a procedure that depended on federal favor; others valued the independence that an electoral mandate provided. Many had themselves arrived through elections and understood the change as a loss of leverage in dealing with Moscow.
Voters and opposition parties: Surveys at the time showed mixed attitudes; a number of citizens were skeptical of the quality of their governors, and the reform was not a leading public concern. Opposition parties and independent commentators argued that voters had lost a right and that nominations would depend on private negotiation.

The groups assessed the reform by differing criteria: effectiveness, security, and accountability.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Federalism, 1993–2008

What Changed

  • Governors lost their seats in the upper house, and from 2005 were no longer elected by voters.
  • Bilateral treaties lapsed; federal districts and presidential envoys appeared; fiscal resources were centralized.

What Remained

  • The constitutional framework of a federation of equal subjects, with republics retaining their own constitutions and, in form, their ethnic designation.
  • Real regional variation in economic structure and political practice, and the use of informal arrangements with leaders such as those of Tatarstan and Chechnya.

Why the pattern matters: The shift occurred rapidly, mostly through ordinary law and decree, not a constitutional amendment, which illustrates how institutions can change substantially within an unchanged formal framework.

Regional Context

Tatarstan and Kazan

Tatarstan, on the middle Volga (see Chapter 23 on the conquest of Kazan), signed the first bilateral treaty with Moscow in 1994 and under President Mintimer Shaimiev negotiated wide control over local resources and taxes. In the 2000s it was among the regions most affected by the harmonization of laws. Federal pressure led to changes in regional legislation, yet Tatarstan remained a case in which a regional leader with strong local support retained considerable influence and in which questions of language policy, including the status of Tatar and the choice of alphabet, remained contested. The republic shows that centralization was negotiated with regional elites rather than applied uniformly.

Section 68.16

Security Officials, Bureaucracy, Courts, and State Capacity

Security Officials and the Siloviki

Historical Vocabulary
Siloviki

Definition: From the Russian silovye struktury, "power structures"; a collective term for officials in the security services, the armed forces, the prosecutor's office, and law-enforcement bodies.

Historical context: The term gained political significance in the 2000s as former intelligence and security officers took senior positions in government and state-owned companies. Its use as a single category conceals divisions and rivalries among agencies and individuals.

Why it matters: The influence of security officials is a central claim of many interpretations of the period, but it should be demonstrated through specific appointments and decisions, not inferred from the shared background of individuals.

The appointment of former colleagues to senior positions was notable. Sergei Ivanov, an intelligence veteran, became defense minister in 2001; Nikolai Patrushev succeeded Putin as head of the FSB in 1999; Igor Sechin, a longtime associate, held senior posts in the administration and later chaired Rosneft. Studies by sociologists of elites found that the proportion of senior officials with a security or military background increased significantly in the first decade of the century, although estimates vary according to definitions. Scholars disagree about whether this represented the capture of the state by a security elite, as some critics argue, or the recruitment of an experienced and loyal cadre at a time when other state structures had weakened, as others suggest. The evidence suggests that it combined both elements and that the group was not unified.

Courts, Law Enforcement, and Bureaucracy

The period saw significant reforms in the legal system. New codes of criminal procedure, civil procedure, and arbitration procedure were enacted in 2001–2002, judicial salaries were raised, the principle that arrests required judicial approval took effect in 2002, while jury trials, introduced in a small number of regions in the 1990s, remained geographically limited. Commercial courts began to resolve a larger number of business disputes. At the same time, Russian and foreign observers noted that outcomes in politically sensitive cases often appeared to depend on the executive, that prosecutors retained great influence, and that acquittals remained rare in criminal trials. The tax administration and customs service became more effective, but the enforcement of rules could also be used selectively.

Reforms of the bureaucracy in 2003–2004 reduced and reorganized the federal ministries in an effort to define clearer lines of responsibility. These measures did not eliminate corruption, which surveys by Russian and international organizations continued to identify as a serious problem, and the growth in the number of officials and in their discretionary powers created new opportunities for it. A historian assessing state capacity must therefore distinguish different dimensions: the ability to collect taxes and pay salaries clearly improved, while impartial law enforcement and control of corruption showed little consistent improvement.

The Kursk Disaster and Military Reform

On 12 August 2000 the nuclear-powered submarine Kursk sank in the Barents Sea after explosions in its torpedo compartment, which investigators attributed to the failure of a training torpedo; all 118 crew members died. The Russian Navy's rescue efforts were unsuccessful, and the government delayed several days before accepting help from Norway and Britain. Putin, who remained at a vacation residence for some days, was criticized in the Russian and international press, and in later conversations with the families of the crew he faced anger. He later described the loss as a source of personal grief, and relations with the independent television channels, which had broadcast sharp commentary, worsened. The disaster exposed the decline of the armed forces' readiness, secrecy in official communication, and the weakness of civil-military accountability.

Military policy in these years combined rising budgets with partial reform. A new defense minister, Sergei Ivanov, announced plans to increase the share of professional contract soldiers, to reduce the term of conscription, which was shortened from two years to eighteen months in 2007 and one year in 2008, and to reorganize the command structure. Large-scale reform of the armed forces, including the reduction of the officer corps, came after the Russo-Georgian War and is discussed in Chapter 69. Throughout the period the military remained a politically important institution whose reform met resistance from senior officers.

The reorganization of security and law-enforcement agencies in 2003–2004 expanded the FSB's remit: the Federal Border Service and the Federal Agency for Government Communications and Information were folded into it, and the Federal Tax Police was abolished with part of its functions going to other bodies. The prosecutor's office, led by Vladimir Ustinov from 2000 to 2006, retained broad powers of oversight and investigation; in 2007 a new Investigative Committee was created within it, a change that was a step toward a separate investigative agency. Observers pointed out that overlapping jurisdictions and competition among agencies encouraged selective cases and, at times, conflicts among them, so that the security sector was not a single unit. The Russian elite studies of the decade, by sociologists such as Olga Kryshtanovskaya, claimed a large increase in the share of officials with security backgrounds in the highest posts; other scholars questioned the measures used and argued that the group was divided and that its policy preferences were not uniform.

The investigation of the Kursk disaster concluded in 2002 that a leak of hydrogen peroxide fuel in a practice torpedo triggered the explosion that sank the vessel, and that a second, larger explosion followed. Norwegian divers opened a hatch on 21 August 2000 and found that the surviving compartments had been flooded, so that there were no survivors; a note written by a crew member, found later, showed that some had lived for hours. When Putin met the relatives in Vidyayevo on 22 August, many were angry about the delay in accepting foreign assistance and the contradictory official statements. In subsequent years he changed the naval leadership, and the episode is generally regarded as an early test of his handling of a crisis. It also affected the relationship of the state with the independent television channels, which had criticized the response, and fed the argument of officials that the media had been used to undermine the state.

Judicial change must be assessed in terms both of the reforms and of the practice. Judges' salaries were raised significantly, the commercial (arbitration) courts developed a reputation for competence in business disputes, and the new codes expanded procedural protections. At the same time, the very low rate of acquittals in criminal cases, the influence of prosecutors, and the handling of politically sensitive cases led Russian and foreign legal experts to doubt the independence of the courts in matters involving the state. The pattern is one of improved procedures and uneven independence, a combination that complicates any single judgment about the rule of law.

The question: How should historians evaluate the centralizing reforms of 2000–2005?

Interpretation A — state capacity: Supporters and some scholars argue that the Russian state of the 1990s could not enforce its own laws, protect property, or collect taxes, and that centralization was a necessary response that made recovery possible. They point to improved tax collection, the ending of regional laws that conflicted with the constitution, and public support.
Interpretation B — reduced accountability: Critics argue that the same measures removed mechanisms through which citizens and regions could hold officials to account, replaced competition with appointment, and made institutional performance depend on personal loyalty, with long-term costs for adaptability and for the rule of law.

Division IV — Oligarchs, Property, and the Political Economy of Centralization

Section 68.17

Putin's Settlement With the Oligarchs

The business elite of 1999 had been shaped by the privatization of the 1990s, including the loans-for-shares auctions examined in Chapter 67, and by direct involvement in politics. The most prominent businessmen owned banks, resource companies, and media, and a few were prominent in the Yeltsin entourage. Putin's approach to this group combined reassurance and warning. At a meeting with about two dozen leading businessmen on 28 July 2000, he reportedly indicated that the state would not reopen the privatizations of the 1990s on a wholesale basis, provided that business owners stayed out of politics and observed the law. This understanding, sometimes called a bargain, was never written down, and its content is known from participants' accounts. Historians stress that it was not applied uniformly and that its terms depended on the continuing judgment of the president.

Historical Vocabulary
Oligarch

Definition: In Russian usage of the 1990s and 2000s, a very wealthy business owner assumed to have unusual influence over politics. The Greek-derived term, meaning "rule by the few," was applied to businessmen who acquired large assets in privatization.

Historical context: The label was used in political debate and journalism and has no legal definition. Its application to individuals varied, and it can obscure differences between owners of resource companies, bankers, and media proprietors.

Why it matters: Students should ask what kind of influence is meant, and whether the term describes a stable group, since the wealth and influence of individuals changed dramatically in this period.

The terms of the settlement should be seen against the experience of the 1990s. The 1995 loans-for-shares auctions, in which a group of banks lent to the government in exchange for shares in major enterprises that they then acquired at low prices, produced fortunes that were later associated with the 1996 re-election campaign; the 1998 default, which devastated many banks, reshuffled the group, and by 1999 the leading owners were primarily in oil, metals, and media. The oligarchs of the Yeltsin period had direct access to power and, in some cases, to government posts, but by 1999 their relations among themselves were also unstable and their interests divided over the succession.

At the meeting of July 2000 Putin did not repudiate the privatizations, which would have created uncertainty, and he did not give business owners a privileged political role. Participants, including heads of large companies and banks, later gave accounts in which the central message was that businesses should not use their wealth to pursue independent political aims. In the following years, the leading owners joined an association, the Russian Union of Industrialists and Entrepreneurs, which became a channel for discussion with the government. Some businesspeople, such as Roman Abramovich, retained access and wealth; others, including those who financed opposition parties, did not. Historians draw two conclusions. First, the class of very wealthy owners did not disappear: their number and wealth increased during the commodity boom. Second, their relationship with the state changed from one in which some owners influenced policy to one in which the state determined the boundaries of acceptable behavior, and in which proximity to the president became a source of wealth in its own right.

Section 68.18

Berezovsky, Gusinsky, and the Restructuring of Business Influence

The first conflicts were with owners of media assets. Vladimir Gusinsky, whose Media-Most holding included the independent channel NTV, was arrested in June 2000 on fraud charges and released after three days; he subsequently reached an agreement with the state gas monopoly, a major creditor, that critics said exchanged the sale of his media for release from prosecution, and he left Russia. In April 2001 Gazprom took management control of NTV, and the editorial staff departed or were replaced. Boris Berezovsky, who had been instrumental in the rise of the Yeltsin circle and who owned a large stake in the channel ORT, initially supported Putin's election but opposed the new centralization, left Russia late in 2000, and later obtained political asylum in Britain. His stake in ORT was sold. The channel TV-6, where he held shares and which employed former NTV journalists, was closed by court order in 2002.

These episodes are examined further in Section 68.29; here the focus is the relationship between business and political power. The owners involved had pursued political influence openly, which created a clear boundary. Each case was also presented by the government as a legal matter (debts, shareholders' rights, financial violations), while critics saw an effort to remove independent voices. Court records supply some evidence, but the courts themselves were not seen as independent by many observers, and the sequence suggests that business power and political independence were linked.

The sequence of events in the Gusinsky case is documented in court records and press accounts, though its interpretation is contested. In May 2000 armed and masked men searched the offices of Media-Most, and in June Gusinsky was jailed in the Butyrka prison before being released on 16 June. In July he signed an agreement with the press minister, Mikhail Lesin, in which, according to the document (later known as Protocol No. 6), the criminal case would be dropped in exchange for the sale of his media assets to Gazprom, then a major creditor of Media-Most. Gusinsky later said he had signed under duress, and the authorities said the matter was a private commercial agreement. He left Russia, and after a dispute over the company's debts, Gazprom-Media took control of NTV in April 2001; the journalists who had made the channel's reputation moved to TV-6, which was in turn closed in January 2002 following a lawsuit by a minority shareholder, and a successor, TVS, was shut down in 2003.

The case of Berezovsky was different, because he had been among the sponsors of Putin's rise. After the election he resigned from the Duma in July 2000, criticizing the president's centralizing reforms, and in late 2000 left for abroad; he was later charged in absentia with fraud connected to Aeroflot and other matters, and in 2003 British authorities refused to extradite him and granted him asylum. His shares in ORT were sold, and the channel came under the management of the state-aligned owners. Historians read the two cases as marking the end of the era in which owners could use media outlets for direct political purposes, while differing over whether the legal pretexts were genuine or instrumental. What cannot be disputed is the effect: the independent national television that had existed in the 1990s ceased to exist as an independent political voice by 2003.

Section 68.19

The Yukos Affair and Mikhail Khodorkovsky

The most significant case concerned Mikhail Khodorkovsky, the chief owner of Yukos, then Russia's largest private oil company. Khodorkovsky had built a company that adopted Western accounting standards and corporate governance, and from 2001 he funded civic and educational projects and political parties across the spectrum. In February 2003, at a televised meeting with Putin, he raised the problem of corruption in the state, in an exchange that has often been cited as a turning point. In July 2003 his business partner Platon Lebedev was arrested; Khodorkovsky himself was arrested at an airport in Novosibirsk on 25 October 2003 and charged with fraud and tax evasion.

The legal campaign against Yukos proceeded with tax claims totaling many billions of dollars, freezes of assets, and in December 2004 the forced auction of its main production subsidiary, Yuganskneftegaz, to a previously unknown company that was shortly afterward acquired by the state-controlled Rosneft. Khodorkovsky was convicted in May 2005 and sentenced to nine years, later reduced on appeal to eight, and served in Siberia. Yukos was declared bankrupt in 2006 and its remaining assets were sold, most to Rosneft. Subsequent proceedings, including a second trial and international arbitration, extend beyond the scope of this chapter.

The company's trajectory before 2003 is relevant to the case. Khodorkovsky had acquired Yukos in the 1995 loans-for-shares auction, and in the 1990s the company was accused of practices harmful to minority shareholders; after 1999 it reorganized, improved transparency, increased output rapidly, and became one of the most valuable Russian firms, producing roughly a fifth of Russia's oil. In 2003 it announced a merger with Sibneft, which would have created one of the world's largest oil companies, and held talks with American oil firms about the sale of a stake. Khodorkovsky also supported civic programs, including the Open Russia foundation, and financed several political parties across the ideological spectrum, which was one of the activities that, according to observers, could be seen as crossing the boundary indicated at the 2000 meeting. At a televised meeting in February 2003 he criticized corruption in a state purchase of an oil asset, in an exchange that Putin answered sharply.

The legal process proceeded on several tracks. Tax authorities issued assessments for the years 2000 to 2003 that eventually reached many billions of dollars, including penalties, exceeding what the company could pay; courts froze its accounts and shares; the main production subsidiary was auctioned in December 2004. The criminal case against Khodorkovsky and Lebedev resulted in conviction at the Meshchansky court in May 2005 for fraud and tax evasion, and the sentence was reduced on appeal to eight years. Other Yukos managers left the country, and the effects on the investment climate included a fall in the stock market and increased capital outflow in late 2003. The affair is, therefore, evidence both about law and about power: it demonstrated that a large private company could be dismantled through the law, while also showing the capacity of the state to enforce tax obligations on one of its largest taxpayers.

Historical Debate

What Was the Yukos Affair?

The question: Did the prosecution of Yukos and Khodorkovsky primarily enforce tax and criminal law, or was it chiefly political, intended to remove a political challenger and transfer assets to the state?

Interpretation A — law enforcement: Russian officials argued that Yukos had used aggressive tax-minimization schemes, including transactions through low-tax regions, that violated the law, and that the case showed the principle that no one stood above it. Some commentators held that the privatization of the 1990s had left a legacy of unlawful practices that required correction.
Interpretation B — political and economic motives: Critics noted that other companies used similar schemes and were not pursued as intensively, that Khodorkovsky had financed opposition parties, that the pace and scale of the tax claims and the auction were unusual, and that the main beneficiary was a state company headed by an official of the presidential circle.

Evidence: Court records, tax audits, company documents, journalistic investigation, parliamentary statements, and judgments of international courts. The European Court of Human Rights later found violations of certain procedural rights in the case, but did not accept the claim that the prosecution was politically motivated as proved, and it found other irregularities in the tax proceedings against the company.

Points of agreement: Yukos did use tax-minimizing arrangements; the case ended the company's independence; its assets passed largely to state-controlled firms; and the case altered perceptions of property security.

Points of disagreement: The relative weight of legal and political motives, and whether the proceedings met standards of fairness.

Student task: Separate what the courts decided from what observers inferred about motives, and explain what further evidence would be needed to establish motive.

Section 68.20

Gazprom, Rosneft, and the Expansion of State-Controlled Enterprise

The Yukos case coincided with the growth of state-controlled companies in the energy sector. Gazprom, the gas monopoly that controls the unified gas transmission system, came under new management in 2001 under Aleksei Miller, a St. Petersburg colleague of Putin, and Dmitry Medvedev chaired its board in the early 2000s. In 2005 the state raised its stake above fifty percent, and the company acquired the oil company Sibneft from Roman Abramovich. Rosneft, which was a minor producer in the 1990s, became a leading oil company through the acquisition of Yukos assets and a public share offering in 2006 that left the state as the majority owner. Pressure on foreign investors in 2006 over the Sakhalin-2 project led to the sale of a controlling stake to Gazprom.

Students should distinguish categories that are sometimes merged: state-owned companies, where the state holds a majority; companies influenced by the state through regulation, licensing, or informal relations; and privately owned companies. By 2008 the state held much greater direct control of strategic sectors than in 1999, while large private firms such as Lukoil remained important. Executives of state firms were often officials or former officials, which blurred the line between political and corporate authority.

The development of Gazprom shows the interaction of state ownership and personal networks. In the 1990s the company, which was partly privatized, was managed by Rem Vyakhirev, who was replaced in May 2001 by Aleksei Miller; in the early 2000s Dmitry Medvedev chaired its board. The state increased its holding above fifty percent in 2005 through the transfer of shares, and the company's acquisitions, including that of Sibneft, enlarged its role in oil as well as gas. Gazprom also acquired banks, insurance companies, and media holdings, so that the line between a commercial firm and an instrument of state policy was indistinct. The company's export monopoly on pipeline gas and its responsibility for supplying domestic customers at regulated prices meant that it was both a commercial enterprise and a means of social and foreign policy.

Rosneft's growth followed a different path: the acquisition of Yuganskneftegaz in 2004 through a temporary buyer, the purchase of other Yukos assets in 2006–2007, and a public offering in London in July 2006, which raised large sums while leaving the state with a majority stake. Igor Sechin, a deputy head of the presidential administration, chaired its board from 2004, an example of a senior official holding a corporate role. Foreign investors faced new conditions: in 2006 Shell and its Japanese partners agreed to give Gazprom a controlling stake in the Sakhalin-2 project after Russian regulators raised environmental objections, and the BP-Russian partnership TNK-BP, established in 2003, continued but encountered disputes. Not all the changes pointed toward state control: private firms such as Lukoil, and companies in metals, retail, and other sectors, remained influential, and the sector's performance depended on foreign technology and capital.

Section 68.21

Business Regulation, Taxation, and the Changing Rules of Ownership

The same period saw a significant program of economic legislation. From 2001 the personal income tax was replaced by a flat rate of 13 percent, and in 2002 the profit tax was reduced, accompanied by the simplification of other levies. Revenues from the personal tax rose in the following years, which supporters attributed to greater compliance and which skeptics attributed partly to rising incomes and stronger enforcement. Further measures included a law permitting the sale of non-agricultural land, a new labor code, pension reform, and a law intended to reduce licensing and inspections of small business. The reformers in the government, notably Gref, sought to create predictable rules, though implementation was uneven.

Business surveys nevertheless identified administrative pressure, corruption, and uncertainty over property rights as persistent obstacles. The Yukos case showed that ownership could depend on political standing. Foreign direct investment increased, particularly in the later 2000s, but the largest strategic assets were increasingly controlled by the state or by individuals with close ties to it, and many businesses sought protection through personal connections. The question of how far the reforms created lasting market institutions, and how far they were overshadowed by selective enforcement, is considered in the assessments at the end of this chapter.

The economic reform program of 2000–2002 drew on a strategy prepared by Gref's ministry and associated think tanks and aimed at simplifying the tax system and reducing administrative barriers. Besides the 13 percent flat income tax and the reduced profit tax of 24 percent, the government introduced a unified social tax, lowered the value-added tax rate in 2004, and used mineral-extraction taxes and oil-export duties to capture a share of resource revenue for the budget; the export duty was made progressive with the oil price from 2004. Other measures included a land code allowing the sale of urban and commercial land (2001), a new labor code that replaced the Soviet-era version, a law reducing licensing and inspection requirements, and a pension reform in 2002 introducing a funded component. Not all of these were fully implemented; the agricultural land question was left for later legislation.

Measuring the effect on the business environment requires care. Business surveys and the World Bank's governance and business indicators showed improvement in some areas, notably tax administration, and persistent problems in others, including corruption, courts, and the protection of minority shareholders. A Russian research foundation estimated the annual scale of bribery at very large sums, a figure contested by other analysts, who warned against extrapolating from survey data. Taken together, the sources indicate that the state became more effective at collecting revenue and setting rules while selective enforcement and administrative discretion continued, so that predictability depended partly on a company's political position.

Division V — Economic Recovery and the Transformation of Everyday Life

Section 68.22

Recovery After 1998 and the Foundations of Growth

The Russian economy began to grow in 1999, before Putin took office as president. The depreciation of the ruble after the August 1998 crisis raised the cost of imports and encouraged domestic production of food, consumer goods, and machinery; many enterprises had idle capacity that could be put to use with little new investment. Demand returned as wages were paid more regularly, and the shift of production away from barter reduced inefficiencies. World Bank analyses of the period describe annual growth averaging around 7 percent between 2000 and 2008, accompanied by substantial declines in poverty. The recovery was therefore partly a rebound from a deep contraction, and partly a response to rising external prices.

The mechanisms of the post-crisis recovery can be set out in sequence. The August 1998 default on domestic treasury bills and the abandonment of the ruble's managed exchange rate led the currency to lose about two thirds of its value against the dollar within months; inflation spiked in 1999 and banks collapsed. The immediate consequences were severe, but by the middle of 1999 imports had become expensive and domestic substitutes were competitive; food processing, light industry, and machinery recovered in many regions, and the economy grew by roughly 6 percent in 1999 and 10 percent in 2000, according to official statistics. The share of barter and payment arrears fell as firms moved to cash settlements, tax collection improved because the federal budget was reorganized and because the weakened ruble raised the ruble value of dollar-denominated export earnings.

Industrial capacity inherited from the Soviet period played an important role: factories that had operated at low utilization in the 1990s could increase output without large new investment, and skilled workers were available. This also means that the early growth rates overstated the long-term capacity of the economy, because the easy gains from idle resources would eventually be exhausted. Economists who stress this point argue that the later growth required investment and productivity improvements that were more difficult, and that the ending of the recovery phase coincided with the rise in oil prices, which masked the transition. The policy contributions in these years were the maintenance of a primary budget surplus, the avoidance of renewed devaluation, and the reform measures of 2000–2002.

Regional differences appeared early. Regions with food processing, light industry, or consumer manufacturing benefited from import substitution, while those dependent on defense production or heavy machinery saw a more uneven revival, and resource-exporting regions gained from the combination of a weaker ruble and higher export revenue. The banking system, which collapsed in 1998, recovered slowly: public confidence in banks was low, the central bank tightened regulation, and deposit insurance was introduced in 2003–2004 after a period of experience with bank failures. The recovery of household savings and credit therefore lagged behind the recovery of output, a pattern that affected how households experienced the early years of growth.

Section 68.23

Oil, Natural Gas, Public Revenue, and Macroeconomic Stability

The price of oil, which had been below twenty dollars a barrel in 1999, rose sharply in the early 2000s and reached very high levels in 2008. Russia was among the world's largest exporters of oil and gas, and energy accounted for a major share of export earnings and federal revenue. The government used part of the windfall to repay debt, including the early repayment of obligations to the Paris Club in 2006, to accumulate foreign-currency reserves that rose from a very small amount in 1999 to several hundred billion dollars by 2008, and to run federal budget surpluses. In 2004 it established the Stabilization Fund to hold revenues above a reference oil price, a policy reflecting the advice of Kudrin, the finance minister, to insulate the budget from commodity cycles.

The scale of the energy sector explains its importance for the budget. Oil output, which had fallen in the 1990s, rose by roughly two thirds between 1999 and 2007, as companies including Yukos introduced Western drilling techniques and brought idle wells into operation; the rise in output and prices together multiplied export revenues. Oil and gas accounted for a large part of export earnings and, through taxes and duties, for a large part of federal revenue. The finance ministry's approach was to set the budget on a conservative oil price assumption and to save the excess, which is the logic of the Stabilization Fund; it also used surpluses to repay foreign debt, including a prepayment to the Paris Club of about twenty-two billion dollars in 2006, so that the ratio of public debt to national income, which had been high in 1999, fell to a small fraction.

The strategy had costs and limits. A large inflow of foreign currency tended to strengthen the real exchange rate, making non-energy exports and import-competing industries less competitive, a phenomenon economists call Dutch disease. Government spending increased substantially in the later years, including on defense and social programs, so that the budget's dependence on high oil prices grew, even though the fund provided a buffer. Commentators within and outside the government warned that diversification was lagging, and that the reform momentum of the early 2000s had slowed. Whether the criticism was justified depends partly on how one assesses what other policies were feasible; the evidence shows that Russia did not use the boom years to change the composition of its output greatly.

Section 68.24

Wages, Pensions, Poverty Reduction, and Consumer Society

For many households the 2000s were a period of tangible improvement. Wages and pensions were paid on time, employment rose, and real incomes increased sharply. Consumption of durable goods grew: washing machines, televisions, and mobile telephones spread widely; imported and domestic cars became affordable to more families; supermarkets and shopping centers appeared in large cities and gradually in smaller ones; and mortgage and consumer credit, almost nonexistent in the 1990s, began to expand. Holidays abroad became more common among the urban middle class. This growth in consumer choice made the contrast with the shortages of the Soviet decades and the hardships of the 1990s especially vivid for older generations. The experience helps explain the high levels of public approval recorded for the president in these years, although approval had many sources, including security, national pride, and the weakness of alternatives.

Quantitative evidence supports the claim that incomes rose strongly. Real wages grew by double-digit percentages in several early years, unemployment fell from about twelve percent at the end of the 1990s to about six percent in 2007, and pensions rose in real terms, although from a very low level. Income inequality, however, remained high and increased, and household surveys showed that the upper income groups gained more than others. The adoption of consumer credit created new opportunities and new vulnerabilities; the mortgage market began to develop only in the middle of the decade, and in most families housing was still determined by the Soviet-era allocation or by inheritance. Mobile telephone use expanded dramatically, and the number of internet users grew from a small minority to a large share of urban households.

The meaning of these changes for ordinary people depended on place and age. Younger and better-educated residents of large cities benefited from new jobs in services, finance, and retail; the elderly in small towns lived on pensions that rose but remained modest, and often relied on family help and subsistence plots. The expansion of a consumer society was accompanied by new patterns of work, including long hours and informal employment, and by a mixture of optimism and anxiety documented in sociological surveys. The experience was therefore broadly positive in comparison with the 1990s, but not uniform, which is reflected in surveys that show high satisfaction with personal circumstances alongside concern about inflation, corruption, and the quality of public services.

Pensions deserve separate attention because they were the main income of a large share of the population. The 2002 reform introduced a funded element for younger workers alongside the basic and insurance parts, and pensions were indexed several times, but replacement rates relative to wages remained low, and the monetization of benefits in 2005 showed how sensitive pensioners were to changes in the system. Retail trade provides another view: chain stores and shopping centers, many with foreign participation, expanded rapidly, bringing standardized goods and prices to cities and altering shopping habits. These changes were not equally visible everywhere, and in rural areas the village store, the market, and the household plot remained central to daily provisioning.

Everyday Life and Social History

Household Life in the Early 2000s

A family in a Volga industrial city in 2000 might have lived in a Soviet-era apartment that the family had privatized, supplemented its income with a garden plot at a dacha, and relied on a mixture of wages, pensions, and informal work. By 2007 the same household might have had higher wages, a cell phone for each adult, a bank account, and an installment loan for appliances or a car. Retired people, whose pensions rose but often lagged behind prices, and rural families, whose employment depended on struggling collective farm successors, experienced the changes less favorably. In the cities, children's education and health care increasingly involved informal payments or private services, whose cost fell heavily on lower-income families.

Women remained responsible for much of unpaid domestic labor while also working in large numbers in education, health care, trade, and services, and many households relied on grandmothers for child care. The evidence for these patterns comes from household surveys and sociological studies; it describes tendencies and not the experience of every household.

Multiple Perspectives

Experiencing Recovery

A young professional in Moscow: Rising salaries in finance, consulting, and retail, access to credit, and foreign travel defined the experience, and surveys of this group show relatively high support for the economic course alongside criticism of corruption and political constraints.
A pensioner in a small industrial town: Regular payment of pensions was a real improvement over the arrears of the 1990s, but inflation, the monetization of benefits in 2005, and the cost of medicines and utilities eroded the gain; family support and garden plots remained important.

Section 68.25

Demography, Public Health, Education, and Social Policy

Not all social indicators moved in the same direction. Russia's population, which had begun to decline in the early 1990s as deaths exceeded births, continued to fall during the 2000s, from about 146 million at the start of the decade to about 142 million by 2008, with immigration partly offsetting natural decrease. Male life expectancy, among the lowest in industrialized countries, was around 59 in the early 2000s and rose gradually afterward, although it remained far below that of Western Europe; deaths from cardiovascular disease, accidents, violence, and alcohol-related causes contributed strongly to the gap. Fertility, which had fallen to a very low level in the late 1990s, rose modestly, a change that officials linked to the maternity capital program introduced in 2007 and that demographers attributed also to the age structure and to improved incomes.

In 2005 the government announced four priority national projects in health, education, housing, and agriculture, intended to increase investment in the social sphere and to raise salaries for doctors and teachers. In the same year the "monetization" of benefits, which replaced in-kind privileges for categories such as pensioners with cash payments, provoked protests in many cities in January 2005 and was partly modified, an illustration of the limits on the government's control of public opinion. Critics of the national projects argued that they were small in comparison with needs and unevenly implemented; defenders pointed to improvements in equipment and salaries. Systematic evaluation requires comparing outcomes across regions and over time.

Demographic change was among the period's most serious problems, and its causes were multiple. The 2002 census counted about 145 million inhabitants; by 2008 the figure was about 142 million, as deaths exceeded births each year, partly offset by immigration. Male life expectancy fell to a low point in the mid-1990s, was around 59 years in 2000, and rose to roughly 62 by 2008, while female life expectancy was in the low to mid seventies, producing a gap of about twelve years that was among the largest in the world. Demographers identify excess mortality from cardiovascular disease, accidents, violence, and alcohol-related causes among working-age men, along with the spread of tuberculosis and HIV infection in the 1990s and 2000s. The total fertility rate rose from about 1.2 in 1999 to about 1.5 in 2007, a recovery connected to the age structure, to rising incomes, and, from 2007, to maternity capital payments, though demographers disagree on how much was caused by policy.

Education and health policy moved in uneven ways. Universities expanded enrollment, including through fee-paying places, and the country joined the Bologna process for European higher education in 2003; a unified state examination for school-leavers was being tested and was extended in stages. Health spending increased, and the 2005 national projects raised pay for primary-care doctors and funded equipment and housing programs, but the system continued to depend on informal payments and showed significant regional differences. An evaluation of these programs has to compare their cost with outcomes, such as mortality from specific causes, which changed gradually and in several regions only slightly.

Section 68.26

Regional Inequality, Migration, and Uneven Development

The geography of recovery can be described by contrasts. Moscow, with a small share of the population, generated a large share of national income and housed most corporate headquarters, so that wage levels and property values there were far above the national average. Oil- and gas-producing regions such as the Khanty-Mansi and Yamal-Nenets districts of western Siberia had high per capita output, though their prosperity depended on federal and corporate decisions. Hundreds of single-industry towns created in the Soviet period faced the closure or downsizing of their main employer and had few alternatives. In the Far East, population loss continued as people moved to the west, and the North Caucasus, as noted, had high unemployment and dependence on subsidy.

Migration was a prominent feature. Internal migration moved people from villages and small towns to regional centers and Moscow; international labor migration, mainly from Central Asia, Ukraine, Moldova, and the Caucasus, supplied workers for construction, trade, and services, and estimates of the total number of migrants varied widely because many were unregistered. New migration rules adopted in 2007 simplified registration for citizens of some neighboring states while introducing quotas for work permits, and ethnic tensions and xenophobic attacks accompanied the migration in some cities. Tracing these movements illustrates how the economic recovery was connected to a larger regional labor market within the former Soviet space.

Budget data illustrate the differences. Regions with large resource revenues, or with a large share of the federal capital's economy, had budgets that allowed substantial local spending, while most regions relied on equalization transfers from the federal budget; the federal government also used targeted programs for the Far East, Kaliningrad, and the North Caucasus. Housing and transport infrastructure reflected these differences: new construction, shopping centers, and urban transit improvements were concentrated in the largest cities, while many small-town roads, heating systems, and water networks continued to deteriorate. Historians who use regional statistics must note that official series on incomes and prices differ in method across years, and that informal and migrant labor was imperfectly recorded.

The experience of the Arctic and Far North deserves separate mention. Settlements created in the Soviet period around mining and military installations lost population after 1991 as subsidies ended, and in the 2000s the government supported relocation programs for some residents, even as new oil and gas projects drew workers to others. Indigenous communities of the North, discussed in earlier chapters, were affected by both development and decline. These patterns show that the national average conceals experiences ranging from rapid growth to depopulation within the same federation.

Regional Context

Uneven Recovery Across the Federation

The benefits of growth were unevenly distributed. Moscow, where much of the financial activity and the headquarters of the major energy firms were concentrated, had much higher incomes and consumption than the average, and St. Petersburg and some resource-producing regions such as Tyumen oblast in western Siberia also did well. Smaller industrial towns, particularly single-industry towns dependent on a failing plant, and many rural areas, lagged. The North Caucasus remained heavily reliant on federal transfers and had high unemployment. Millions moved internally from smaller towns to larger cities, and millions of labor migrants, mostly from Central Asia and the South Caucasus, worked in construction, trade, and services, often in informal conditions; in 2007 new rules on migrant registration and quotas were introduced. The same economy thus produced prosperity in some places and stagnation in others, and a history of the period must include both.

Division VI — Elections, Media, Opposition, and Political Legitimacy

Section 68.27

United Russia, Political Parties, and the State Duma

The formal institutions of electoral democracy continued to operate throughout the period: elections were held on schedule, multiple candidates appeared on ballots, and turnout was substantial. What changed was the conditions of competition. After the December 1999 elections, in which Unity and the Communist Party each won roughly a quarter of the party-list vote, the pro-presidential camp consolidated. Unity merged with Fatherland–All Russia in 2001 to form United Russia, which in the December 2003 Duma elections won about 38 percent of the party-list vote and, together with single-member-district victories and the adhesion of independent deputies, a majority of seats sufficient to pass ordinary legislation. The Communist Party fell to about 13 percent, the nationalist Liberal Democratic Party and the new Rodina bloc each took roughly a tenth, and the liberal parties Yabloko and the Union of Right Forces failed to pass the five-percent threshold.

In the following years the rules were changed. A 2001 law on political parties set high requirements for membership and regional branches; in 2005 the Duma abolished single-member districts, so that all 450 deputies would be elected by party list; the threshold was raised to seven percent; electoral blocs and the "against all" option on ballots were abolished. In the December 2007 election, held under the new rules, United Russia won about 64 percent of the vote and a constitutional majority of seats, with only the Communists, the Liberal Democrats, and A Just Russia, a Kremlin-supported party, also entering the chamber. The OSCE did not send an observation mission to the 2007 election after disagreements about restrictions on its work. Supporters of these changes argued that they would create stronger parties and a more stable parliament; critics argued that they reduced pluralism and the representation of regions and independent figures.

The party system's development can be traced through the sequence of reforms. The 2001 law on political parties required parties to have large memberships and regional branches in a majority of regions, which led to the disappearance of many small parties and to the consolidation of the field. United Russia, formed in 2001, benefited from the support of governors and officials and from the claim to represent the president's policies; it did not formally nominate Putin in 2004, when he ran as an independent. In the 2003 Duma election the Kremlin also assisted the creation of the Rodina bloc, which drew nationalist and left-leaning voters away from the Communists, according to widely reported accounts. The OSCE's observation of the 2003 election concluded that it did not adequately meet many commitments for democratic elections, citing the use of state resources and unbalanced media coverage.

The 2005–2006 laws changed the structure of competition. Federal Law No. 51-FZ of 18 May 2005 provided for the election of all 450 deputies by proportional representation from party lists, with a seven percent threshold, to apply from the 2007 election; the option on ballots to vote "against all" candidates was abolished in 2006, and rules on electoral blocs were also changed. The new rules favored large parties with administrative support and reduced the representation of independent candidates and small parties. In 2006 the Kremlin supported the formation of A Just Russia as a second pro-government party of the center-left. In the December 2007 elections, United Russia won roughly 64 percent and 315 seats, giving it a majority large enough to amend the Constitution when combined with its allies, a power that was not used in this period.

Section 68.28

The 2004 Presidential Election and Electoral Competition

Putin was re-elected on 14 March 2004 with about 71 percent of the vote, on a turnout of roughly 64 percent. His principal opponents were the Communist Nikolai Kharitonov, who took under 14 percent, and Sergei Glazyev, Irina Khakamada, and others who received small shares; several prominent politicians, including the leaders of the Communist Party and Yabloko, did not run. Surveys at the time consistently showed very high approval for the president, and the result was not seriously doubted as a reflection of his popularity. The documentary question is the quality of the contest. The OSCE's Office for Democratic Institutions and Human Rights concluded that the election was generally well administered and reflected the incumbent's consistently high approval but lacked elements of a genuine democratic contest, citing unequal access to media and the use of official positions to support the campaign.

The conditions of the 2004 campaign are more significant than the result. The major opposition parties declined to nominate their top leaders: the Communists put forward Nikolai Kharitonov, the Liberal Democrats Oleg Malyshkin, a bodyguard of their leader, and Yabloko and the Union of Right Forces did not field their chairmen; the independent candidates were Sergei Glazyev and Irina Khakamada. Former prime minister Ivan Rybkin's disappearance for several days in February, and reappearance in Ukraine with a contested explanation, attracted attention. Putin dismissed the government of Kasyanov on 24 February, which appeared to be a signal about the independence of his team, and refused to participate in debates, appearing instead in official roles.

For the historian the question is not whether Putin had majority support, which polls and results agree he did, but how much of the result reflected a competitive process. Political scientists have proposed concepts such as managed democracy, competitive or electoral authoritarianism, and hybrid regime to describe systems in which elections are held and carry meaning but in which the playing field is tilted. These labels are analytical tools, not neutral descriptions, and their use depends on criteria, such as the real possibility of alternation in power, that the evidence of a single election cannot settle. The OSCE's findings give a documented basis for assessing the conditions; the other basis is the later record of competition, which the following chapters examine.

Official figures should be read with this distinction in mind. The Central Election Commission reported a turnout of about 64 percent and a result of about 71 percent for Putin, and observers noted few reports of ballot-box fraud at the national level, although some regional anomalies were reported and the quality of the voter registration lists was questioned. The more consequential limits were structural: the unequal media environment, the use of administrative resources, the lack of strong challengers, and the effect of the Yukos case and other developments on the willingness of financial backers to support opposition candidates. A student writing about the election should avoid two errors: asserting that the result was falsified, which the evidence does not establish, and asserting that the contest was fully competitive, which the OSCE and others did not find.

Section 68.29

Television, Journalism, and the Restructuring of the Media

Television was the most important source of news for most Russians, and the national channels, ORT (later Channel One), RTR, and NTV, reached nearly the whole population. In the 1990s, private owners had used channels to advance political and business interests, and in 1999 ORT's campaign against Fatherland–All Russia had illustrated the political uses of broadcasting. Under Putin the structure of ownership changed. The seizure of NTV by Gazprom-Media in 2001, the closing of TV-6 in 2002 and of TVS in 2003, and the sale of Berezovsky's stake in ORT left the main national channels under state or state-aligned ownership. News programs gave extensive coverage to the president and government and limited airtime to opposition figures. The OSCE's media monitoring of 2003 and 2004 documented the imbalance.

The print and online media were more varied. Newspapers such as Novaya Gazeta and the radio station Ekho Moskvy continued to publish critical material, and the internet expanded rapidly, but their audiences were much smaller than television's. Regional media depended on local authorities' advertising and ownership. Journalists faced risks that included lawsuits, pressure from owners, and violence. Organizations that monitor the safety of journalists recorded the killing of a number of reporters in these years, and in many cases those who ordered the attacks were not identified. The murder of Anna Politkovskaya, a correspondent known for her reporting on Chechnya, in Moscow on 7 October 2006 attracted international attention. Several men were later tried in connection with it, but the question of who commissioned the killing remained unresolved at the time of this chapter's cutoff; students should note the distinction between convictions for execution of a crime and the identification of those who ordered it.

The structure of the media market explains the importance of television. In the early 2000s national channels reached nearly the entire population, and surveys showed that television was the main source of news for the large majority of Russians, while newspapers had much smaller circulation. Channel One was majority state-owned and RTR was fully state-owned; NTV, after 2001, was under Gazprom-Media. Programs of political analysis on the main channels were reorganized or ended, and the number of guests who criticized the government decreased. The state also owned or influenced regional channels through local authorities, and the law and licensing system gave the executive tools of control. The OSCE's monitoring of the 2003 and 2004 campaigns documented the imbalance in coverage.

The press and the internet represented a more varied but less influential sphere. The newspaper Kommersant and the news agency network were owned by changing owners with political ties; Novaya Gazeta, partly owned by its journalists and by Mikhail Gorbachev and a businessman, continued to publish investigations, as did the radio station Ekho Moskvy, which was majority-owned by Gazprom-Media but retained editorial independence for years. The killing of journalists, including the editor of the Russian edition of Forbes, Paul Klebnikov, in July 2004, and the unsolved or only partly solved cases of other reporters, formed the background to professional decisions. Self-censorship, concern about lawsuits, and economic dependence on state advertising or owners were probably more common means of restraint than direct prohibition, a point made by journalists themselves.

Everyday Life and Social History

Watching the News

For most households the evening news on the main channels was a daily ritual, often watched as a family in the main room of an apartment. Viewers encountered a combination of reports of economic improvement, ceremonial coverage of the president, and stories of crises, including the hostage-takings, presented in sober or emotional tones. Viewers also had access to entertainment programs, serials, and, in the large cities, new commercial channels and cable. Surveys indicate that trust in television was high but not uniform, with younger and urban viewers more likely to seek alternatives such as the internet, whose use was growing but remained a minority practice outside the largest cities.

Historians use ratings data, surveys, and archived broadcasts to study these patterns. They cannot show with certainty how far broadcasts shaped opinions, since people interpret what they see in light of their experience, a limit that applies to all studies of media influence.

Section 68.30

Political Opposition, Civil Society, and Human Rights

Opposition in this period took several forms. In parliament, the Communist Party and the Liberal Democratic Party generally cooperated with the government on major legislation while criticizing it on social questions. Liberal parties lost parliamentary representation after 2003. Outside parliament, civic groups, trade unions, human-rights organizations such as Memorial, environmental groups, and soldiers' mothers' committees worked on specific issues, and some drew on foreign funding. In 2005 the government created a Public Chamber of appointed civic representatives as a consultative body, and a law of January 2006 (Federal Law No. 18-FZ, signed on 10 January and in force from 18 April 2006) amended the regulation of non-governmental organizations, increasing registration requirements and state oversight, particularly for those receiving foreign money. Domestic and international human-rights groups criticized the law as a constraint on civic activity; officials said it was intended to ensure transparency and prevent foreign interference. Youth movements supported by the authorities, notably Nashi, appeared in 2005 and mobilized in support of the government, while opposition coalitions, such as The Other Russia led by Garry Kasparov and others, organized marches in 2006–2007 that were often restricted or dispersed by police.

Students should be careful here about evidence. Documented facts include changes in law, the denial of permission for certain demonstrations, and the cases of specific individuals. Claims that particular acts of violence were ordered by officials, or that all opposition was suppressed, need to be supported by specific evidence; so do claims that opposition was marginal because of lack of popular support alone. The most defensible conclusion is that independent political activity continued but within a narrowing space whose boundaries were set largely by the executive.

The legal framework for civil society changed substantially. A law of 2002 on countering extremism defined the concept broadly, and its application to organizations and publications drew criticism from human-rights groups. The Public Chamber, established by a law of April 2005 and first convened in January 2006, brought together representatives chosen partly by the president and partly by civil-society groups to review legislation and monitor government; supporters described it as a channel of dialogue, and critics as a way to co-opt civic activity. Federal Law No. 18-FZ, signed on 10 January 2006 and entering into force on 18 April 2006, gave officials broad powers over the registration and activities of non-commercial organizations, including requirements to report on funds and activities; foreign organizations' branches were also subject to restrictions. Many organizations re-registered successfully, but some groups with foreign funding encountered administrative difficulties.

Opposition activity was constrained but not absent. The Other Russia coalition, whose participants ranged from liberals to nationalists to leftists, held "Marches of Dissent" in 2006 and 2007 in Moscow, St. Petersburg, and other cities, and police used force and detentions in some of them. The authorities also supported mass pro-government movements such as Nashi. The poisoning and death in London of the former FSB officer Aleksandr Litvinenko in November 2006 became a major dispute in Russian-British relations; the question of responsibility has been examined by British authorities and rejected by the Russian side, a clear instance in which an allegation must be attributed to its source. Attention to the sources of evidence is essential: some claims about intimidation rest on documented court and police records, others on the testimony of participants or on inference.

Section 68.31

National Identity, Historical Memory, Religion, and Political Legitimacy

The government's use of symbols and history was another dimension of legitimacy. In December 2000 the Soviet-era melody was restored for the national anthem with new words by Sergei Mikhalkov, who had written the Soviet text, while the tricolor flag and the double-headed eagle of the pre-Soviet period were retained, a combination that was intended to draw on several periods of the past and that divided opinion. Commemoration of the victory over Nazi Germany in the Great Patriotic War, discussed in Chapters 59 and 60, became increasingly prominent, and the sixtieth anniversary celebrations in May 2005 drew many foreign leaders to Moscow. Alongside patriotic education programs and new history textbooks, these events reinforced a narrative of national achievement and of the state as the guardian of continuity. The narrative coexisted with differing views among historians and in society about the Soviet past, including the repression of the Stalin years, a subject on which Memorial and other organizations continued their work.

The Russian Orthodox Church, led by Patriarch Alexy II, enjoyed rising public visibility and close relations with the state; in 2007 the Moscow Patriarchate and the Russian Orthodox Church Outside Russia, divided since the revolution, signed an act of canonical communion. Islam, Judaism, and Buddhism were recognized as traditional religions, and relations with Muslim communities were affected by the war in the Caucasus and by the growth of Islamist movements abroad. Nationalism was a varied phenomenon: state-sponsored patriotism, ethnic Russian nationalist movements, and xenophobic violence against migrants and minorities all existed and should not be conflated. Public opinion was diverse; surveys indicate widespread pride in the state's recovery, concern about corruption and inequality, and divisions over the memory of the Soviet period. The phrase "sovereign democracy," used by Surkov in 2006 to describe the political system, is a source for official self-understanding and has been criticized by others as a justification for limiting external scrutiny.

The state's use of symbols was deliberate and contested. The law of December 2000 on state symbols kept the tricolor flag and the double-headed eagle, which had been adopted in the 1990s, and returned the Soviet-era melody of the anthem with new words; the combination was presented as reconciling different periods of the national past, while some critics saw it as ambiguous about the Soviet legacy and others as insufficiently respectful of it. In 2005 a new holiday, the Day of National Unity on 4 November, commemorating the end of the Polish occupation of Moscow in 1612 (Chapter 25), replaced the commemoration of the 1917 October Revolution on 7 November, a change that illustrates how the calendar of public memory was revised. The sixtieth anniversary of the victory in 1945 was celebrated with extensive ceremonies, and the St. George ribbon became a popular symbol of commemoration.

Debates about the teaching of history accompanied these changes. Textbooks and teacher manuals published in the mid-2000s presented the Soviet period in different ways, and a 2007 manual for teachers drew criticism from historians for its treatment of the Stalin period and its use of the phrase "sovereign democracy." Historians such as those working with Memorial continued to publish on political repression, and the opening of some archives made research possible, although access in practice varied and later declined. Religious institutions also appeared in public life: the Russian Orthodox Church enjoyed rising prestige and the Moscow Patriarchate and the Russian Orthodox Church Outside Russia restored communion in May 2007, a result of negotiations begun earlier. Nationalist organizations, including those that organized an annual "Russian March" from 2005, expressed ethnic Russian nationalism that was distinct from the state's civic and imperial patriotism and sometimes critical of it, and surveys recorded rising hostility to migrants from the Caucasus and Central Asia. These facts remind students that public identity was contested and that the state's narrative was only one of several.

Division VII — Russia and the Changing International Order

Section 68.32

Early Cooperation With the United States and the West

Putin's early foreign policy aimed at restoring Russia's international standing while securing economic ties. After the attacks of 11 September 2001 on the United States, he was among the first foreign leaders to telephone President George W. Bush and he supported the American campaign in Afghanistan: Russia shared intelligence, did not object to the temporary use of bases in Central Asia by American forces, and supported the Northern Alliance, with which it had long-standing contacts. Officials presented this as consistent with Russia's own struggle against terrorism, and some hoped for reciprocal recognition of Russian concerns. In May 2002 the NATO-Russia Council was created in Rome as a forum for consultation, and Russia participated in the G8, whose presidency it held in 2006, hosting the summit in St. Petersburg.

The scope of cooperation was wider than is often remembered. Putin's response on 24 September 2001 offered intelligence sharing, access to Russian airspace for humanitarian flights, participation in search-and-rescue, and an agreement not to object to the use of Central Asian bases, and Russia increased supplies to the Northern Alliance. In October 2001 Russia announced the closure of its signals-intelligence facility at Lourdes in Cuba and its naval base at Cam Ranh Bay in Vietnam, decisions interpreted as a sign of its priorities. In May 2002 Presidents Bush and Putin signed the Treaty of Moscow on strategic offensive reductions, and the creation of the NATO-Russia Council brought Russia into a consultative framework with the alliance on terrorism, nonproliferation, and other issues. The summit of the G8 and the celebration of St. Petersburg's three hundredth anniversary in 2003 provided further settings.

The limits were also visible. Russia regarded the American presence in Central Asia as temporary and was disappointed that cooperation did not produce revisions of American policy on missile defense; the United States continued to raise concerns about human rights, and the status of trade measures such as the Jackson-Vanik amendment remained an irritant. Differences over Georgia's Pankisi Gorge, where Russia alleged that Chechen fighters had bases, and over relations with Iran added to the friction. Scholars therefore describe the period as one of tactical alignment around a shared concern with terrorism, within which more fundamental differences about the international order remained.

Cooperation also had effects within Russia. The government's public embrace of partnership with the United States was criticized by some officers and nationalists, who argued that Russia was making concessions without reciprocal benefits, and was welcomed by liberals and many business leaders who saw an opportunity for investment and integration. Public opinion data show that favorable views of the United States among Russians rose in late 2001 and declined in the following years, with a marked fall after the Iraq War. This pattern shows that the public mood was responsive to events and that the relationship was not fixed in a pattern of hostility from the beginning of the decade.

Russia and the World

Counterterrorism Cooperation and Its Limits

The convergence after 2001 rested on a shared opponent but different expectations. Washington sought logistical help and intelligence; Moscow sought acceptance of its description of the war in Chechnya as part of a global campaign against terrorism, and a greater voice in European security. American officials continued to criticize human-rights violations in Chechnya, and Russian officials saw the stationing of American personnel in Central Asia, in a region it regarded as within its sphere of interest, with ambivalence. Neither side simply adopted the other's model; both adapted to a limited convergence of interests. Studies of the period show that cooperation was real, that it produced practical results, and that the expectations behind it were never identical.

Section 68.33

NATO Enlargement, Arms Control, and Strategic Disagreement

Disagreements accumulated. In December 2001 the United States announced its withdrawal from the Anti-Ballistic Missile Treaty of 1972, which took effect in 2002; Putin called the decision a mistake but did not break off cooperation. In March 2004 NATO admitted seven new members, including Estonia, Latvia, and Lithuania, former Soviet republics, which Russian officials criticized as an advance of military infrastructure toward Russian borders and which the new members described as a response to their own security needs and sovereign choice. The Iraq War of 2003 was opposed by Russia, France, and Germany, and the break among the Western allies temporarily brought Moscow closer to some European capitals. Later plans for American missile-defense installations in Poland and the Czech Republic became another subject of dispute, and in December 2007 Russia suspended its participation in the Treaty on Conventional Armed Forces in Europe. Interpretations of these developments differ: some historians emphasize Western disregard of Russian security concerns, others the right of states in Central and Eastern Europe to choose their alliances and the pattern of Russian pressure, and many emphasize the interaction of both.

The question of NATO's enlargement is one of the most debated in the historiography of the post-Cold War period. Russian officials argued that enlargement violated the spirit of understandings reached during German unification in 1990, a claim that historians have examined at length: some find evidence that Western leaders gave informal assurances about the alliance's non-expansion that were not codified, others conclude that the discussions concerned the territory of the former East Germany and that no binding commitment about further enlargement was made. Western governments pointed to the 1997 NATO-Russia Founding Act, which committed the alliance to restraint in the deployment of forces and established consultation, and to the sovereign right of states to seek membership. The accession of seven states in March 2004 intensified Russian objections, particularly concerning the Baltic states, whose territory directly bordered Russia and where disputes over the citizenship and language rights of Russian-speaking residents were a source of tension.

Arms control added to the disagreements. After the American withdrawal from the ABM Treaty, discussions about missile-defense installations in Poland and the Czech Republic, announced in 2007, were viewed in Moscow as undermining the strategic balance, while Washington described the system as directed against potential threats from the Middle East. The Treaty on Conventional Armed Forces in Europe had been adapted in 1999, but NATO states did not ratify the adapted text until Russia fulfilled commitments to withdraw forces from Georgia and Moldova; Russia ratified in 2004, and suspended its participation in December 2007. The Iraq War of 2003, which Russia opposed along with France and Germany, and the Kosovo declaration of independence in February 2008, which Russia refused to recognize, rounded out the list of grievances. Each issue involved different legal and political arguments and should be analyzed on its own evidence.

Section 68.34

Ukraine, Georgia, and the Politics of the Post-Soviet Region

The politics of the post-Soviet region became an important arena. In November 2003 mass demonstrations in Georgia, the Rose Revolution, forced President Eduard Shevardnadze to resign after disputed parliamentary elections and brought Mikheil Saakashvili to power. In Ukraine, the presidential election of late 2004 was contested between Viktor Yanukovych, supported by the outgoing president and by Moscow, and Viktor Yushchenko; after the runoff was annulled by the Supreme Court because of fraud, a repeat vote in December gave victory to Yushchenko in what is called the Orange Revolution. Russian officials, who had openly supported Yanukovych, interpreted these events as externally orchestrated "color revolutions" and as a threat to Russian influence; Western governments and many in the region regarded them as popular movements reacting to electoral manipulation. Historians emphasize that Georgian and Ukrainian citizens had their own grievances and political actors, and that foreign support, though present, did not by itself create the mobilizations. These events fed Russian official concerns about domestic protest, which helped shape the NGO and youth policies described in Section 68.30. The Russo-Georgian war of 2008 is outside the scope of this chapter and is examined in Chapter 69.

The politics of Georgia and Ukraine had domestic roots that preceded Russian involvement. Georgia in the early 2000s suffered from corruption, weak central authority, and unresolved conflicts in Abkhazia and South Ossetia, where Russian peacekeepers were stationed and where Russia later issued passports to many residents. The Rose Revolution followed the falsification of the November 2003 parliamentary results and brought to power a reformist government under Saakashvili, which sought NATO membership and the removal of Russian bases; by 2007 Russia had withdrawn its military bases from Batumi and Akhalkalaki under the 1999 Istanbul agreement. In Ukraine, the background was the political crisis around President Leonid Kuchma, the disputed election of 2004, the poisoning of Yushchenko in September 2004 (the cause of which has been the subject of investigation and dispute), and mass demonstrations in Kyiv's Independence Square. Constitutional amendments adopted during the crisis shifted some powers from the president to parliament.

Russian policy in the region combined economic leverage, political support for favored candidates, and, in Moldova, involvement in the settlement of the Transnistria conflict, where a proposal in 2003 (the Kozak memorandum) failed. Elsewhere, the Tulip Revolution in Kyrgyzstan in March 2005 and the government's suppression of unrest at Andijan, Uzbekistan, in May 2005, which the West criticized and Russia did not, showed the varied responses. After the Orange Revolution, the Ukrainian government's political coalition collapsed in 2005, and Yanukovych returned as prime minister in 2006. Surveys in Ukraine showed deep regional differences in attitudes to NATO and to Russia, and no consistent majority in favor of membership. These facts indicate that Ukrainians and Georgians were active participants with differing preferences, not simply objects of competition between Moscow and the West.

Section 68.35

Energy Diplomacy, Europe, China, and Central Asia

Energy became an instrument of both commerce and politics. Europe was Russia's largest market for gas and oil, and long-term contracts tied Russian suppliers and European buyers together. Disputes over prices and transit with Ukraine and Belarus, notably the interruption of gas supplies to Ukraine in January 2006, led to reductions in deliveries to some European customers and raised questions in Europe about the reliability of supply; Russian officials said they were moving to market pricing after years of subsidized rates, while critics, including the governments of several neighbors, described the moves as political pressure. A new pipeline under the Baltic Sea, agreed in 2005 between Gazprom and German companies, was supported by some European governments and opposed by others. Russia also developed relations with China, signing a treaty of friendship in 2001 and participating in the Shanghai Cooperation Organisation, founded in 2001 with China and four Central Asian states, which addressed border security, counterterrorism, and regional cooperation. Energy and arms sales and diplomatic coordination at the United Nations were key elements. These partnerships show that Russian policy was not simply a response to the West.

The gas disputes illustrate the combination of commercial and political factors. Ukraine had paid prices far below European levels, and transit disputes arose over unauthorized withdrawals of gas intended for Europe; in January 2006 Russia cut supplies after Ukraine refused a large price rise, and the reduction in flows to Europe led to concern in several countries. The settlement set higher prices through an intermediary company whose ownership was controversial. In 2007 a dispute over oil transit with Belarus disrupted flows in the Druzhba pipeline. Russian officials described the moves as a transition to market prices; critics, including officials in Kyiv and Warsaw, described them as the use of energy as a political instrument. Comparing contract prices over time and across countries provides the evidence for the debate and shows that neighbors that were politically closer to Moscow received more favorable terms in some periods.

Russia's diversification of partners complemented its European relations. The 2001 treaty with China, the 2004 agreement settling the remaining border questions, arms sales, and the planning of the Eastern Siberia–Pacific Ocean oil pipeline linked the two economies; the Shanghai Cooperation Organisation addressed security cooperation with Central Asian states and became a forum for expressing reservations about American policy in the region. The Nord Stream agreement of 2005 and the subsequent appointment of former German Chancellor Gerhard Schröder to a supervisory role in the project drew criticism in Europe, while the competing pipelines supported by European and American governments, including the Baku-Tbilisi-Ceyhan oil pipeline completed in 2005–2006, bypassed Russian territory. The European Union and Russia negotiated four "common spaces," but talks for a successor to the 1994 Partnership and Cooperation Agreement were blocked in 2006–2007 by disputes involving Poland and Lithuania. The result was an interdependence that was substantial and politically contested.

Europe's reliance on Russian gas and Russia's reliance on European revenue meant that neither side could easily break the relationship, and each tried to limit its vulnerability: the European Union by diversifying suppliers and liberalizing its market, Russia by building routes to other consumers and by seeking ownership in European distribution. Section 68.36

The Munich Speech of 2007 and the Security Debate of 2008

On 10 February 2007, speaking at the Munich Conference on Security Policy, Putin offered a sustained critique of the post-Cold War international order. He argued that the concentration of power around the United States, which he described as a unipolar model, encouraged unilateral military action and weakened international law. He criticized NATO enlargement, questioned assurances previously given to Moscow, and objected to proposed American missile-defense installations in Central Europe. He also argued that the United Nations, rather than individual states or military alliances acting alone, should authorize the use of military force. These were political and legal claims made by Russia's president, not uncontested descriptions of the international system. NATO members and Central and Eastern European governments offered different accounts: they regarded enlargement as a decision by sovereign states seeking security after the Soviet period, and the United States presented missile defense as a response to threats from outside Europe. The speech brought disagreements already evident in policy into an unusually public confrontation. Its importance lies in its combination of strategic arguments, political messaging, and international reaction, rather than in the assumption that it inaugurated a wholly new foreign policy.

The immediate responses to Munich are themselves historical evidence. The American defense secretary, Robert Gates, responded in a lighter tone, saying that one cold war was enough for him, and German and other officials criticized parts of the speech while noting the continuing need for dialogue; Russian commentators described it as an expression of long-standing positions. Within the Russian government, the speech was presented as candor in response to what it regarded as the ignoring of Russian interests. It occurred in a context of increased Russian confidence, higher energy revenues, and anticipation of the end of the president's term, and many historians treat it as a marker, not the cause, of the deterioration.

At Bucharest, the debate among allies is documented in press reports and later memoirs. The United States supported giving both countries a Membership Action Plan; Germany and France, among others, argued that Ukraine and Georgia were not ready, that public support in Ukraine was insufficient, and that the step would provoke Russia. The compromise was the formula in the declaration, accompanied by an instruction to foreign ministers to review the question in December 2008. Putin attended a session of the NATO-Russia Council on 4 April and made clear his opposition. The outcome has been read in two ways: as a reasonable postponement that kept options open and as an unstable ambiguity that offered a prospect of membership without protection. Historians should keep to what the document says and the decisions the allies actually took.

The decisions of April 2008 should also be placed beside developments within Ukraine and Georgia. In January 2008 the Ukrainian president, prime minister, and parliamentary speaker had signed a letter requesting a Membership Action Plan, but the Ukrainian political class was divided, and the Party of Regions, the largest opposition party, opposed the step. In Georgia, the government had held a plebiscite on NATO membership in January 2008 in which a large majority of those voting expressed support, though opposition parties disputed the conditions of the vote. These facts show that the question was contested within the countries concerned and that the alliance's decision was a response to these politics as well as to relations with Moscow.

Section 68.37

Reconstructed State, Stronger Presidency, and Institutional Trade-offs

Between 1999 and 2008 the Russian state changed in documented ways. Federal authority over the regions increased through districts, the reform of the Federation Council, and the appointment of governors. Fiscal capacity improved as tax reforms, enforcement, and energy revenue combined to produce surpluses and reserves. The presidency concentrated authority over personnel, with a pro-presidential party dominating the Duma, and major energy assets moved under state control. At the same time, independent television disappeared from the national scene, opposition parties lost parliamentary representation, the NGO sector was subjected to new regulation, and prominent crimes against journalists remained unresolved. The central historical question is how to weigh the genuine increase in administrative capacity, and in the security and income that many citizens experienced, against the decline of mechanisms of accountability. The evidence does not support the view that stronger institutions inevitably produced better governance, nor the view that the changes were merely the outcome of a leader's preferences.

Historians have offered several interpretations of the political order that emerged. Some, using the concept of managed or sovereign democracy, describe a system in which elections and institutions were preserved but the outcomes were tightly controlled by the executive. Others emphasize a continuity of informal, patronage-based practices from the 1990s and the Soviet period and argue that the Putin years consolidated a pattern already present. Still others stress the role of popular consent and of the economic recovery, noting that in surveys of the period majorities supported the president's course, and caution against explanations that treat the public as passive. A fourth view emphasizes bureaucratic politics and the competition among groups within the elite. These perspectives are not mutually exclusive, and each depends on the criteria used, which students should identify.

The trade-offs identified in the evidence can be summarized. The state increased its capacity to collect taxes, pay salaries, and implement policy in the regions; it decreased the independence of regional governments, national television, and the party system; it improved procedures in courts while political cases remained open to influence; it increased the role of the state in strategic industries while permitting rapid growth in private consumption. None of these is simply a gain or a loss, and the distribution of benefits and costs differed across groups. A responsible assessment states which claims rest on documents, which on surveys and statistics, and which on interpretation.

Comparisons with other periods require care. Some commentators have compared the centralization of the 2000s with earlier episodes of state-building in Russian history, including the reforms of Peter the Great (Chapter 32) or the post-revolutionary consolidation of the 1920s; the comparison is useful where it identifies specific institutional parallels, such as the use of appointed officials to supervise regions, and misleading where it assumes continuity of character or purpose. The state of 2008 operated in a market economy integrated into global trade, a society with high levels of education and urbanization, and an international environment very different from any earlier period. Similarity must therefore be demonstrated case by case, a principle this textbook has applied throughout.

Historical Debate

How Should Historians Characterize the Political Order of 2000–2008?

The question: Was the system a form of managed or electoral authoritarianism, a weakened and incomplete democracy, a state-building project that strengthened necessary capacities, or a mixture?

Interpretation A — authoritarian consolidation: Emphasizes the control of television, the tilt of electoral conditions, the dominance of one party, the dismantling of Yukos, and the weakening of federalism as evidence of a system in which competition was constrained and outcomes largely determined in advance.
Interpretation B — state-building with popular support: Emphasizes the restoration of fiscal and administrative capacity, the decline of violence and of regional fragmentation, rising incomes, and sustained public approval, and argues that the system reflected a broad preference for order after the 1990s.
Interpretation C — continuity of informal institutions: Emphasizes patronage networks and personalized relations that predate 1999 and shaped how formal reforms worked.

Evidence: Election reports, laws, media monitoring, surveys, court records, and case studies of regions and companies.

Points of agreement: Power was more concentrated in 2008 than in 1999; the president was popular; elections were held without a realistic chance of defeat for the president's party.

Points of disagreement: The weight of popular preference versus institutional constraint, and whether the changes were reversible.

Student task: Select three pieces of evidence and explain which interpretation each most strongly supports and which it fits poorly.

Section 68.38

Russia at the End of Putin's Second Presidential Term, 2008

Under the Constitution of 1993, as then in force, a president could serve no more than two consecutive terms, and Putin's second term ended on 7 May 2008. In autumn 2007 he led United Russia's list in the Duma elections and in December indicated his preference for Dmitry Medvedev as the candidate for president. The election of Medvedev in March 2008, the arrangement by which Putin became prime minister, and the question of how power was shared are examined in Chapter 69. At the end of his second term Russia had large reserves, a rapidly growing consumer economy, and a government with high public approval; it also depended heavily on energy exports, faced demographic decline and corruption, had an insurgency in the North Caucasus, and was in open disagreement with the United States and NATO. Seeing 2008 as a point at which later developments were already determined would be a mistake: the Georgian war, the global financial crisis, and the later choices of the leadership were still to come.

The question of the president's succession occupied the political class during 2007. Constitutional provisions limited the president to two consecutive terms, and proposals from some political figures to amend the Constitution or to extend terms were discussed in the press; Putin stated that he would not seek a change of the Constitution. In December 2007, after United Russia's victory in the Duma elections, he endorsed Dmitry Medvedev, then first deputy prime minister, as a candidate, and the leadership of several parties nominated him. The election of March 2008 and the arrangement whereby Putin became prime minister after leaving the presidency are examined in Chapter 69.

The state of the country in early 2008 can be summarized from the evidence of the preceding sections. The economy had grown for nearly a decade; reserves were large; inflation was a concern as it rose again in 2007; the population was declining but at a slower pace; the insurgency in the North Caucasus continued at a lower level than in the early 2000s but with violence spreading to other republics; relations with NATO and the United States were strained; and public approval of the president was high in national surveys. In February 2008 Putin gave a speech to the State Council on development to 2020, which set out an agenda for diversification and investment. Whether these aims could be realized depended on circumstances that no one had yet seen, including the global financial crisis that began later in the year.

Chapter Review and Historical Thinking Checkpoint

The checkpoint asks students to combine the sections into an integrated explanation. The central relationships can be stated as hypotheses to be tested. First, the security crises of 1999–2004 increased public demand for order and gave the leadership arguments for centralization, but the specific institutional measures also reflected earlier preferences and bureaucratic interests. Second, energy revenues and the post-crisis recovery supplied the fiscal means for stabilization and for expansion of state activity, and also reduced the pressure for institutional reform. Third, the interaction of economic recovery and constrained competition helped to sustain high public support while narrowing the means through which dissent could be expressed. Fourth, the foreign-policy trajectory resulted from the interaction of Russian confidence, Western decisions, and the independent choices of neighboring states. Each hypothesis can be examined through the primary and secondary sources in this chapter.

The checkpoint also asks students to consider the limits of the evidence. For the period examined here, scholars have access to laws, court judgments, election reports, statistics, memoirs, journalism, and a growing literature, but important documents remain closed, notably those on security-service decision-making and on the negotiations within the elite, and many sources, including official statistics and public-opinion surveys, were produced in an environment of constrained information. A historian must therefore state conclusions with a degree of confidence proportionate to the evidence, distinguish the documented from the inferred, and avoid explanations that treat later developments as the necessary outcome of the period. Students who can do so have mastered the central skill this chapter is designed to teach.

Finally, students should consider what alternative developments were conceivable and which conditions made them more or less likely. A different succession in 1999, a different outcome in the Duma elections, a more negotiated settlement in Chechnya, a different use of the energy windfall, or a different response to the color revolutions were all possible in principle. The purpose of asking such questions is not to speculate about unrealized outcomes but to recognize the choices and constraints faced by historical actors, which is the basis of historical explanation.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the transition from the late Yeltsin presidency to the end of Vladimir Putin's second term, a period in which the Russian Federation became a more centralized presidential state while experiencing rapid economic recovery. The central problem was the relationship between the reconstruction of state capacity and the narrowing of political competition.

The succession of 1999 was the product of the weakness of the Yeltsin presidency, the rotation of prime ministers, the rivalry between the president's circle and the Fatherland–All Russia bloc, and the security crises of that autumn. Putin, a former intelligence officer and city administrator with no electoral experience, rose from obscurity to the presidency in under a year. His rise was neither predetermined nor merely accidental; it reflected choices by others, public fatigue with instability, and events he did not originate.

The Second Chechen War, beginning in 1999, was a military campaign, a humanitarian crisis, and a political resource. The fall of Grozny was followed by an insurgency, large-scale displacement, and documented violations by federal forces and by militants. Chechenization transferred local authority to Moscow-aligned Chechens under Akhmad and Ramzan Kadyrov and produced administrative stabilization but not reconciliation. The Dubrovka and Beslan crises raised enduring questions about state responsibility for protecting hostages, which later international court judgments addressed.

The "vertical of power" was built through specific measures: federal districts, a reformed Federation Council, the removal of governors' direct election, the expansion of the presidential administration, and the greater role of security officials. These measures responded to real problems of the 1990s and were supported by many citizens, and they also reduced channels of accountability. Courts and bureaucracy were reformed in some respects, but selective enforcement remained a concern.

The relationship between business and the state changed after 1999. The government accepted the outcomes of privatization on the condition that owners avoided independent political activity, and the cases of Gusinsky, Berezovsky, and Khodorkovsky showed what could follow from a breach. The Yukos affair, interpreted by some as law enforcement and by others as political, ended in the transfer of assets to state-controlled enterprises, whose expansion in the energy sector became a hallmark of the period.

The economy grew rapidly. Recovery from the 1998 crisis, rising oil prices, and reforms of taxation and regulation combined to produce higher incomes, falling poverty, balanced budgets, and large reserves. Historians disagree about the relative weights of these causes. The improvement in living standards was real and uneven: Moscow and resource regions did much better than small industrial towns and rural districts, while demographic decline and health problems continued to be serious.

In politics, elections continued but the conditions of competition changed. United Russia became dominant, electoral rules favored large parties, national television came under state-aligned control, and the legal environment for civil society tightened. OSCE reports distinguished between the administration of elections and the presence of a genuine contest, and the distinction is central to evaluating the period. High public approval of the president was a fact, as were constraints on competition.

In foreign relations, early cooperation after 11 September 2001 gave way to disagreements over missile defense, NATO enlargement, Iraq, the color revolutions, and energy. The Munich speech and the Bucharest summit marked a hardening of positions. Relations with China, Central Asia, and Europe also developed, and the policies of neighboring states, such as Ukraine and Georgia, had their own dynamics.

Taken together, the period shows that the same measures can serve several purposes and be assessed by different standards, and that evidence about intentions, institutions, and outcomes must be kept distinct. Chapter 69 examines the transition to the Medvedev presidency, the war with Georgia, and the global financial crisis; Chapter 70 follows the transformations after 2012; Chapter 71 considers the war in Ukraine; and Chapter 72 offers a synthesis of the structures of the contemporary Russian Federation.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because the institutional arrangements of 1999–2008 shaped the political and economic framework within which the Russian Federation operated in later years. It changed the relationship between president, regions, business, media, and parliament, and it established expectations among citizens about stability, income, and security.

What continued afterward was the presidency's central role, the importance of energy, and a security environment shaped by the Caucasus. What changed was the balance between federal and regional authority, the structure of the party system, and Russia's place in the international order. These changes are examined in Chapters 69 through 72, but they should not be treated as the necessary outcome of the period studied here.

The chapter also demonstrates the textbook's larger themes: the relationship of state and society, the interaction of center and periphery, and the way different groups experience the same developments differently. Questions remain unresolved, including the responsibility for some of the period's most serious events and the balance of causes of economic growth. Historians will continue to revise their assessments as sources become available.
