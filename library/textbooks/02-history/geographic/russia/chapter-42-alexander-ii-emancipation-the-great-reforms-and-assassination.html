Part VII — Autocracy, Empire, and Reform

Alexander II: Emancipation, the Great Reforms, and Assassination

A History of Russia: From the Eurasian World to the Russian Federation

Chapter Opening

A frequently repeated formulation, attributed to Alexander II in a speech to Moscow nobles in March 1856, held that it was better to abolish serfdom from above than to wait until it began to abolish itself from below. The wording survives in several reported versions and should be read as a paraphrase of a policy argument, not a verbatim record.

On Sunday, 1 March 1881 (Old Style; 13 March in the Gregorian calendar), Alexander II left the Winter Palace in St. Petersburg for his customary Sunday military review at the Mikhailovsky Manege. After the review he made a brief visit to Grand Duchess Catherine Mikhailovna at the Mikhailovsky Palace, and he was returning to the Winter Palace along the embankment of the Catherine Canal. Earlier that morning he had approved, in principle, a report from his minister of the interior, Mikhail Loris-Melikov, proposing that elected representatives of local government take a limited advisory part in preparing legislation. The report was to go before the Council of Ministers three days later. A member of the revolutionary organization People's Will threw a bomb that damaged the imperial carriage but did not kill the emperor. Alexander stepped out to speak with the wounded, and a second bomb thrown at close range mortally wounded him. He died within hours, aged sixty-two, having reigned for twenty-six years.

The assassination ended the reign most closely associated in Russian memory with a program that contemporaries called the Great Reforms. Between 1861 and 1874 the government abolished the legal institution of serfdom, created elected organs of local self-government, rebuilt the court system on principles of public trial and professional independence, replaced the old recruit levy with a system of universal military obligation, relaxed some controls on universities and the press, and presided over a rapid expansion of railways, banks, and commerce. These measures altered the legal status of tens of millions of people and reshaped the working institutions of the empire. Yet the same government never relinquished the principle that supreme authority belonged to the emperor alone, and it responded to growing criticism and to revolutionary violence with a mixture of concession, surveillance, and repression.

This chapter asks whether the Russian Empire could modernize its institutions and society while leaving unlimited autocratic sovereignty intact. It does not treat the Great Reforms as either a failed liberal revolution or a simple continuation of autocracy by other means. It argues that they were a major state-directed attempt to repair the weaknesses exposed by the Crimean War (Chapter 41) while preserving imperial authority, and that they transformed Russian society profoundly while also producing new inequalities, frustrated expectations, semi-autonomous institutions, educated publics, and revolutionary movements that the autocratic state was increasingly unwilling or unable to accommodate.

The people who experienced these changes were extraordinarily varied. The roughly twenty-two to twenty-three million peasants who had belonged to private landlords received legal freedom on terms negotiated by officials and landowners, not by the peasants themselves. Landowners lost the power to buy, sell, and command people but gained compensation and, in many regions, the strategic advantage of controlling how land was divided. Townspeople and provincial gentry sat together in new elected assemblies. Lawyers, doctors, teachers, and statisticians found a new profession in local public service. Young men and women from noble, clerical, and commoner families attended universities and women's courses, and a significant minority turned to populism and, eventually, to terror. The borderlands of the empire, from the Baltic provinces to Poland, the Caucasus, and Turkestan, experienced the reform era in distinct and often unequal ways.

The evidence for this period is unusually rich and unusually contested. It includes the legislative texts and drafting records of the Editing Commissions, the reports of provincial noble committees, the proceedings of zemstvo assemblies, trial transcripts, police files, memoirs, newspapers, and the periodical press of the exiled Alexander Herzen and of domestic journals. Peasant voices appear indirectly, through petitions, village disturbances recorded by officials, ethnographic surveys, and later reminiscence. Each type of source reflects the perspective and purposes of its creator, and historians have long disagreed about how to weigh them. The debate over the outcomes of emancipation, examined in this chapter, is among the most sustained in Russian historiography.

The chapter also connects Russia to wider nineteenth-century processes. The 1860s were a decade of emancipation, state-building, and military and legal reform across much of the world: slavery was abolished in the United States, the Habsburg and Ottoman states pursued administrative modernization, Prussia's war with Austria and later France showed the power of reformed armies and railways, and Japan began its own state-led transformation. Russian officials studied and borrowed from these experiences while shaping reforms to the empire's own estate society, fiscal structure, and imperial diversity. Russia was also an active imperial power during these years, completing the conquest of the western Caucasus in 1864, advancing into Central Asia, and selling Russian America to the United States in 1867.

By the end of the chapter, students should be able to explain why reform became possible and necessary after 1856, distinguish what emancipation changed legally from the economic terms on which it took place, assess the institutional character of the zemstvos and courts, connect military and educational change to the problems exposed in the Crimean War, understand how populism differed from terrorism, and evaluate the interpretive problems posed by the assassination of 1881. Chapter 43 picks up at the moment of shock that followed.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why reform became politically possible and increasingly necessary after the Crimean War, and why earlier reform thinking under Nicholas I mattered.
  2. Trace the process of drafting and issuing the Emancipation Statutes of 1861, identifying the principal participants and conflicts.
  3. Distinguish the legal changes brought by emancipation from the economic terms of the land settlement.
  4. Analyze allotments, temporary obligation, redemption payments, and the peasant commune as parts of a compromise among peasant, landlord, and state interests.
  5. Evaluate the zemstvo and municipal reforms as limited representative institutions within an autocratic framework.
  6. Assess the Judicial Statutes of 1864, including their innovations, their exceptions, and the later restrictions imposed on them.
  7. Connect military and educational reform to the weaknesses revealed in the Crimean War and to the problem of politically autonomous educated publics.
  8. Describe the economic and social changes of the reform era without confusing them with the later industrialization examined in Chapter 44.
  9. Differentiate populism broadly understood from the terrorist strategy of People's Will, and explain why radicalization was not inevitable.
  10. Interpret the assassination of Alexander II and the unresolved question of the Loris-Melikov proposals without exaggerating either as a constitutional turning point.
  11. Evaluate competing historiographical judgments on the success of emancipation and the Great Reforms.
  12. Contextualize the Russian reforms within the wider nineteenth-century age of emancipation, military modernization, and legal reform.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Could the Russian Empire modernize its institutions and society while leaving unlimited autocratic sovereignty intact?
  2. What changed after 1856 that made reforms possible which had been discussed but not carried out under Nicholas I?
  3. What was the difference between becoming legally free and becoming economically independent for a former serf?
  4. Why did the government create elected local institutions and independent courts while refusing a national representative body?
  5. How did military reform answer the failures of the Crimean War, and how did it change the relationship between estates and the state?
  6. How did reform expand the educated public, and why did the government find that public both necessary and dangerous?
  7. Why did populism emerge, and why did only a part of the movement turn toward terror?
  8. How should historians interpret the assassination of 1881 and the Loris-Melikov proposals, given the risk of reading later events backward into earlier ones?
  9. How did the reform era look different from the Baltic provinces, Poland, the Caucasus, Ukraine, and the Russian village?

Historical Context

Chapter 38 examined the system of Nicholas I, an autocracy that relied on a personal chancery, the political police, a codified law, and a bureaucracy that was often undermanned and dependent on landowners to govern the countryside. Serfdom, the legal institution that bound peasants to landlords and obligated them to labor or pay rent, remained the foundation of that system, as Chapters 27 and 35 showed. Estates (sosloviia), the legal categories of nobility, clergy, townspeople, and peasantry, determined taxation, service obligations, and legal rights. Russia's economy remained overwhelmingly agrarian, its transport networks thin, and its fiscal capacity limited by a tax base concentrated in a peasantry that was both taxed and tied to the land.

Chapter 41 showed how defeat in the Crimean War exposed the practical consequences of those limits. The army lacked modern rifles and reliable logistics, railways did not reach the theater of war, the treasury was strained, and the recruit levy imposed extremely long nominal service obligations, although the indefinite-leave system introduced in 1834 had created a limited trained reserve that proved inadequate for the demands of the war. The Treaty of Paris of 1856 deprived Russia of its Black Sea fleet and of its established claim to special protective rights over the Ottoman Empire's Orthodox subjects. Officials and educated observers concluded that Russia's position as a great power depended on institutional change. They did not agree on what change, or how far it should go.

Reform thinking was older than the war. Nicholas I had convened a series of secret committees on the peasant question, and his government had carried out one major agrarian experiment, the reform of state-peasant administration directed by Pavel Kiselyov between 1837 and 1841. Educated Russians, including Slavophiles and Westernizers who disagreed on many other things (Chapter 40), criticized serfdom as morally corrosive and economically inefficient. Some landowners, particularly in the grain-producing southern provinces and in the Baltic provinces where serfdom had been abolished without land between 1816 and 1819, were already experimenting with hired labor. The war did not invent the problem of serfdom. It changed the balance within the governing elite by making change appear a requirement of state survival.

This context also shaped the terms of reform. Officials such as Nikolai Milyutin and Yakov Rostovtsev, and supporters in the imperial family such as Grand Duke Konstantin Nikolaevich and Grand Duchess Elena Pavlovna, saw emancipation as necessary but wanted to prevent economic dislocation and political disorder. Landowners, who dominated the provincial committees that were asked to propose terms, were divided: some accepted emancipation with land and compensation, some preferred to free peasants without land, and some resisted any change. Peasants were not formally consulted, though their expectations influenced officials' calculations, since rumors of imminent freedom had long circulated in villages and had occasionally produced disturbances.

Finally, students should recognize that the Great Reforms unfolded across an empire, not a single nation. The Baltic provinces, Poland, Finland, the Caucasus, Siberia, and Central Asia had distinct legal orders and social structures, and the reforms reached them at different times, in different forms, or not at all. The Polish uprising of 1863 and the empire's continuing expansion shaped the political climate in which reform was carried out. Section 42.1

Alexander II and the Reform Imperative

Alexander Nikolaevich was thirty-six when he became emperor in February 1855, in the middle of a losing war. He had been educated by a mixture of tutors, including the poet Vasily Zhukovsky, and trained for rule by his father, Nicholas I, who had involved him in committee work and taken him on tours of the empire. He was not a theorist of liberty. His consistent concern was to govern effectively and to hand on an intact dynasty and empire. Historians have long rejected both the image of a heroic emancipator who simply wished to free the serfs and the opposite image of a cynic whose reforms were empty gestures. The better view is that he was a cautious autocrat who came to believe, under pressure of defeat and with the advice of reform-minded officials, that some changes had become unavoidable, and who remained sensitive to the interests of the nobility and to the dangers of disorder.

The reform imperative had several components. The first was military and fiscal. The war had shown that a state which relied on conscripted serfs, on a small number of railways, and on a limited fiscal base could not sustain a prolonged conflict against industrial opponents. The second was economic. Many officials and landowners argued that unfree labor produced low productivity and discouraged initiative, though historians disagree about how far serfdom was economically exhausted in 1855; in some regions, serf labor remained profitable for landlords. The third was moral and political. The educated public, relieved by the relaxation of censorship at the start of the reign, openly debated serfdom, and the government feared that the issue would be taken up outside its control. The fourth was fear of rural violence. Peasant disturbances were frequent in the 1850s, and rumors that the tsar intended to free the serfs and that landlords were concealing the decree were common.

What changed after 1856 was therefore not the existence of a reform program but the political balance within the governing elite. Nicholas's committees had produced studies, limited decrees, and the Kiselyov reform of state peasants (Chapter 38), but no breach with the principle that landlords' rights over their serfs should not be disturbed in a way the nobility would resist. Under Alexander, the argument that reform was needed to preserve Russia's strength gave reformers an advantage they had lacked. Alexander's brother, Grand Duke Konstantin Nikolaevich, and his aunt, Grand Duchess Elena Pavlovna, gave dynastic support. Officials in the Ministry of the Interior and in the Naval and Finance Ministries, many of them educated, professionally trained, and acquainted with European administration, formed what historians sometimes call the "enlightened bureaucracy."

In March 1856, speaking to the marshals of the Moscow nobility, Alexander reportedly warned that it was better to begin abolishing serfdom from above than to wait for it to be abolished from below. The words circulated in several versions, and what survives is a reported summary, not an official transcript, as Chapter 41 noted when discussing similar formulations. The speech nevertheless captures a central logic: the government wished to act before the peasantry did, and to keep control of the process. In January 1857 the emperor established a Secret Committee on Peasant Affairs, and in November 1857 he issued a rescript to the governor-general of Vilna, Vladimir Nazimov, authorizing the formation of noble committees to propose terms for emancipation in the western provinces. The rescript was published and extended to other provinces, turning a private deliberation into a public one.

The reform program of the 1860s was never a single coherent plan. Emancipation came first and shaped the rest. The creation of zemstvos and new courts followed in part because emancipation removed the landlord's role as local administrator and judge over his serfs, leaving a gap that the state had to fill. Military reform followed the logic of the Crimean defeat. Educational and press measures reflected both the need for trained personnel and the hope that a loyal educated public could be cultivated. Reformers such as the Milyutin brothers, Reutern, and Dmitry Zamyatin, minister of justice from 1862, were interested in rationalizing the state, not in limiting the emperor. That tension, between modernization and unlimited sovereignty, runs through the whole chapter.

The reforms also coincided with a changing international position. Russia, diplomatically isolated after 1856, pursued a policy of recueillement, or "gathering of strength," associated with the foreign minister Alexander Gorchakov. At the same time the empire continued to expand: the Caucasian War reached its conclusion in 1864 (Chapter 39), and advances in Central Asia brought Tashkent (1865) and Samarkand (1868) under Russian control. The sale of Russian America to the United States in 1867 reflected a judgment that a distant and costly colony could not be defended. Reform, retrenchment, and expansion were therefore part of a single effort to strengthen the empire's position, and they should not be separated in the student's mind.

Section 42.2

The Emancipation of the Serfs

The central question of this section is what emancipation changed legally. The preparation of the legislation took four years and involved a complicated interaction between the tsar, the Secret Committee (renamed the Main Committee in 1858), provincial noble committees, the Editing Commissions of 1859–1860, and the State Council. The provincial committees, composed of landowners, submitted proposals that reflected local conditions and the interests of their authors. The Editing Commissions, led by Yakov Rostovtsev until his death in early 1860 and then by Count Viktor Panin, with Nikolai Milyutin as a leading intellectual force, reconciled these into general legislation. The final text was debated in the Main Committee and the State Council, where conservative members argued for greater concessions to landowners. Alexander personally intervened to secure passage.

The Manifesto and the Statutes were signed on 19 February 1861 (3 March N.S.), the sixth anniversary of the emperor's accession, and published in the first days of March after delays designed to prepare troops and officials. The Manifesto itself, drafted by the Metropolitan Filaret of Moscow in language suited to a religious audience, was read in churches. It was accompanied by a large body of Statutes: a General Regulation setting out the principles of the settlement and local regulations for different regions of the empire. The scale of the legislation is itself a clue to the complexity of what was attempted. The legislation covered the approximately twenty-two to twenty-three million peasants of private landowners, along with a smaller number of household serfs (dvorovye), who were employed in domestic service and, in many cases, received freedom without allotments of land. State peasants were dealt with separately in 1866, and appanage peasants (those on lands of the imperial family) in 1863.

The legal changes were substantial and should not be minimized because the economic settlement disappointed many peasants. Former serfs acquired the status of "free rural inhabitants," with personal rights that had been controlled by landlords. They could marry without a landlord's consent, own property, make contracts, bring suits in court, engage in trade and craft, and enter other estates or educational institutions under the conditions that applied to them. Landlords lost the legal power to buy and sell peasants, to compel them to marry, to punish them, and to command their labor except under the terms of the settlement. In short, landlords lost ownership of peasants' persons and their former broad patrimonial authority, although during temporary obligation they retained limited supervisory and police powers connected to estate order and the peasants' continuing obligations, which faded as peasants moved to proprietor status. For the first time, a very large part of the Russian population was no longer legally the property or dependent of an individual master. This was among the largest legal changes of status in the nineteenth century anywhere.

The legislation also created new institutions of peasant administration. Peasants were organized into village societies and volosts, administrative districts of several villages governed by elected officials and assemblies and served by volost courts that judged peasants' minor cases according to local customary law. The village assembly (skhod), composed of heads of households, handled communal affairs. These bodies gave peasants more self-administration than before, but they also confirmed the peasantry as a separate legal estate. Peasants remained subject to the poll tax until the 1880s, were liable to corporal punishment by volost courts until 1903, and required permits and passports to leave their villages for extended periods. Freedom, in the legal sense, was therefore real but bounded: peasants were free from landlords, not equal citizens.

The settlement's immediate reception demonstrated how much it fell short of peasant expectations. Many peasants had understood "freedom" (volia) to mean full personal liberty and ownership of the land they cultivated. When the Statutes imposed a temporary period of obligation to the landlord and required redemption payments for land, many concluded that the real emancipation had been hidden by landlords and officials. Disturbances spread through the provinces in the spring of 1861. The most notorious occurred in the village of Bezdna in Kazan province, where a peasant named Anton Petrov preached that the Manifesto had been misinterpreted and that true freedom would be granted; troops fired on a large crowd, and dozens were killed, with reports of casualties varying by source. Petrov was executed. Peasant unrest declined after the first year, but it taught officials and peasants alike that the Statutes were contested terrain.

Implementation depended heavily on the peace mediators, local noblemen appointed to supervise the drawing up of "statutory charters" (ustavnye gramoty) that specified each estate's land allotments and obligations. The process was slow and uneven, and the mediators' loyalties were divided between their class and their office. Some mediators sympathized with peasants and were removed or transferred; others favored landlords. The system gave the nobility significant authority over the way emancipation was carried out, which is one reason the settlement could appear to peasants as an act of gentry self-interest even though the state had insisted on it.

Primary Source Window

Manifesto of 19 February 1861 on the Abolition of Serfdom

Creator: Drafted by the Metropolitan Filaret of Moscow for Alexander II · Date: signed 19 February 1861 O.S. (3 March N.S.), publicized in the first days of March · Type: Imperial manifesto, accompanying the Statutes

Context: The Manifesto was the public face of a massive body of legislation. It was addressed to the population as a whole and read from church pulpits. Filaret, an influential cleric, wrote it in a style intended to be intelligible to peasants and to present emancipation as an act of the tsar's care. The text was not a summary of the actual economic terms, which were set out in the Statutes, and its tone reflects the government's wish to present reform as a gift from above while discouraging disorder.

Source type: Official proclamation.

Brief paraphrase (not a verbatim translated quotation):

The Manifesto recalls that serfdom had developed over time and that the tsar, with the cooperation of the nobility, has decided to grant the serfs the full rights of free rural inhabitants. It affirms that landowners keep their property rights in the land while peasants receive their homesteads and a field allotment for use in return for specified obligations. It calls on the peasants to understand that freedom depends on work, order, and obedience to law and authority, and invites the Orthodox people to make the sign of the cross and ask God's blessing on their free labor.

Analysis: The Manifesto shows how the state framed emancipation: as a paternal act that maintained property, order, and religious legitimacy. It is evidence of official intentions and of the political rhetoric used to reassure both landowners and peasants. It cannot establish how peasants experienced the settlement, what they believed the text meant, or how the Statutes worked in practice. Peasant responses, including the rumors and disturbances of 1861, show that many did not accept the official reading. The voices of peasants appear in the source only as an addressee and as an implied threat. Historians therefore pair the Manifesto with the Statutes, statutory charters, officials' reports on unrest, petitions, and later memoirs.

Questions for students:

  1. What does the Manifesto say about freedom, property, and obligation, and what does it leave to the Statutes?
  2. Who created it, and why might a churchman have drafted it for the emperor?
  3. What can historians learn from it about official goals, and what can they not learn about peasant understanding?
  4. What additional evidence would help assess the settlement's actual effects?
Section 42.3

Land Settlements and Redemption Payments

If Section 42.2 asked what emancipation changed legally, this section asks on what economic terms freedom occurred. Three interests collided. Peasants wanted land, since in an agrarian society a household without adequate land could not support itself and remained dependent on others. Landlords wanted preservation of their estates, a labor supply, and compensation for what they regarded as property. The state wanted emancipation to proceed without economic collapse or political disorder and without placing intolerable fiscal burdens on the treasury. The resulting settlement was a compromise among these interests, and it favored none completely.

The Statutes recognized the peasant's right to a homestead plot and a field allotment. Allotments were determined by regional norms that distinguished three broad zones: a non-black-earth northern zone with poor soil, where landlords relied more heavily on peasants' quitrent (obrok) and non-agricultural earnings; a fertile black-earth zone in the center and south, where landlords relied on corvée labor (barshchina) and where land was most valuable; and a steppe zone with thinly settled lands. Within each, the legislation set upper and lower limits on the size of allotments. If peasants had used more land than the maximum, landlords could claim the surplus. These "cutoffs" (otrezki) were most significant in the black-earth provinces, where landlords had the greatest incentive to retain land, and in some provinces peasants lost a significant share of the land they had previously cultivated. If peasants had used less than the minimum, the landlord was supposed to provide additional land. Landlords also frequently managed the placement of boundaries to retain meadows, forests, watering places, and access roads, giving them leverage over peasants who needed those resources.

For a transitional period, called temporary obligation, peasants were required to continue to provide labor or pay quitrent to the landlord in return for the use of their allotments. The system was intended to preserve estates' economic functioning while allowing a gradual transition. Peasants could initiate redemption of their land, with the landlord's consent, or the landlord could force redemption by demanding it. The redemption operation was financed by the state, which paid landlords most of the capitalized value of the land in interest-bearing securities and then collected repayment from the peasants over a period of forty-nine years with interest. In 1881 the government decreed that temporary obligation should end and redemption become compulsory beginning in 1883, and it reduced redemption payments. The payments were reduced further after the revolution of 1905 and ended in 1907 (Chapter 46).

It is easy to oversimplify this system. Students should avoid the claim that peasants "received free land and then were taxed for it." The legal mechanism was more complex: the state effectively compensated landlords and then recovered the cost from peasants through long-term redemption payments, which were calculated by capitalizing the income from the peasants' former dues, not by the market value of the land. In some regions, notably in less fertile areas, the redemption price was said to exceed what land could produce, and peasants were in effect paying for their liberty as well as for the soil. In other regions, redemption prices were closer to or below market values. Scholars debate how burdensome the payments were in practice. What is clear is that the burden fell on households with limited cash resources, and that arrears accumulated in many districts in the 1880s and 1890s, when falling grain prices and growing population reduced household incomes.

The land settlement also reinforced the position of the peasant commune. In most Russian provinces, allotments were granted to the village community, not to individual households. The commune bore collective responsibility (krugovaia poruka) for taxes and redemption payments, meaning that the community as a whole was liable for the arrears of its members. Many communes periodically redistributed land among households according to the number of working hands or mouths, a practice that evened out holdings but could discourage individual improvement. It also helped the state collect revenue and maintain order, and it tied peasants to the village by making departure complicated. Peasants could leave the commune only under conditions that included settling payments. The institution should not be treated as uniform across the empire. Repartitional tenure was dominant in the central Russian provinces; in the western and right-bank Ukrainian provinces and parts of the south, hereditary household tenure was common; and practices varied within regions according to local custom, soil, and landlord policy.

The settlement therefore produced a society of legally free peasants who often held less land than they wished, faced payments that were burdensome in many localities, and remained embedded in a separate legal and fiscal order. The longer-term demographic effects aggravated the problem. As the peasant population grew in the decades after 1861, the average allotment per capita shrank, and land hunger became a central feature of rural politics. Historians disagree about the extent to which these developments reflect the settlement itself or broader demographic and market conditions, but all agree that the question of land remained unresolved and politically explosive (Chapters 44, 47).

Historical Vocabulary
Redemption Payments

Definition: Annual payments made by former serfs, through their communes, to the state as repayment, with interest, of the sum advanced by the treasury to compensate landlords for land transferred to peasants under the settlement of 1861.

Historical context: The state paid landlords most of the redemption value, calculated by capitalizing the peasants' former dues, and recovered it from peasants over forty-nine years. The system was made compulsory in 1883, reduced in 1881 and again in 1906, and abolished in 1907.

Why it matters: The term is easily misunderstood. Redemption payments were not a simple tax on free land; they were a state-financed purchase scheme. They also explain why legal liberty and economic autonomy diverged, and why many peasants felt that the "real" freedom had been withheld.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Peasant Household After 1861

For a peasant household in a central black-earth village, the change in 1861 was both dramatic and incomplete. A serf who had needed a landlord's permission to marry could now marry freely, though families and village elders still shaped marriages in practice. A man could bring a complaint to the volost court, but the court judged according to custom and could still order corporal punishment. A landlord no longer commanded the household's labor at will, but the family continued, during temporary obligation, to work the lord's fields for a fixed number of days or to pay quitrent. The village assembly allocated tax shares, mediated disputes, and decided, in repartitional communes, who would hold which strips of land.

The household was typically an extended patriarchal unit, headed by a senior man (bol'shak), whose authority covered the allocation of labor and the family's dealings with the commune. Historians debate whether emancipation accelerated household partitions, in which younger married sons separated from the parent household, and the evidence varies by region. Women carried out much of the work of food preparation, textile production, child care, and field labor, and in households from which men migrated to work in towns or on estates they bore even heavier burdens. Men's seasonal labor migration (otkhodnichestvo), already well established in the non-black-earth north, grew as railways and markets expanded, and wages sent home became part of the household's income.

The experience differed by region, by the former status of the household, and by the landlord. Household serfs who left estates without allotments faced a more precarious start than peasants with land. Households in provinces with large cutoffs faced land shortage earlier. Literacy remained low, though schools supported by the zemstvos and the church slowly increased access. This evidence must be used carefully: much of it comes from officials, ethnographers, and educated observers outside the village, and peasants' own accounts survive mainly in petitions, court cases, and later memoirs.

Historical Debate

How Successful Was the Emancipation of 1861?

The question: Did emancipation represent a historic liberation of the peasantry, or a settlement so favorable to landowners and the state that it left peasants impoverished and dependent?

Interpretation A — Liberation with limits: Many historians, including scholars working on the drafting process such as Daniel Field and Terence Emmons in the Western tradition, emphasize that the reform ended a legal order that had existed for centuries, that it was driven by officials and sections of the nobility who sought to make the transition workable, and that the compromises reflected real political constraints on an autocracy that depended on landowners. On this view, the settlement was a serious, if imperfect, attempt to avoid social collapse.
Interpretation B — Landlord advantage and peasant burden: Soviet historiography, drawing on Lenin's characterization of 1861 as a "bourgeois" reform carried out by feudal landowners, emphasized cutoffs, redemption burdens, and persistent dependence, and argued that the settlement was designed to protect the nobility's interests and to extract resources from the peasantry. Other scholars, in Russia and abroad, have argued that arrears, land shortage, and communal constraints depressed peasant incomes and fed later unrest.
Interpretation C — Revisionist economic assessments: Some economic historians, using regional data on consumption, mortality, and arrears, have questioned the thesis of general peasant immiseration, arguing that peasant welfare varied and that arrears do not by themselves prove falling living standards.

Evidence: Historians use the Statutes and drafting records, statutory charters, redemption accounts, tax and arrears data, zemstvo statistical surveys, police and gendarme reports on unrest, petitions, and memoir and ethnographic literature.

Points of agreement: Nearly all scholars agree that emancipation was a major legal transformation, that land and fiscal terms were more favorable to landowners and the state than to peasants in many regions, that outcomes varied significantly across regions, and that the question of land remained politically volatile.

Points of disagreement: Historians differ on how burdensome redemption payments were, how much of the arrears problem reflects the settlement and how much reflects demography and prices, and how far the reform was a defeat for peasants as opposed to a limited gain.

Why it matters: The judgment shapes interpretations of the peasant role in 1905 and 1917 (Chapters 46, 50) and the debate over whether Russia's agrarian crisis was a result of the settlement, of population pressure, or of later policy.

Student task: Select two kinds of evidence and explain how each might support or complicate a verdict of success or failure.

Section 42.4

Zemstvo Reform

The Statute of 1 January 1864 created zemstvo institutions in the provinces and districts of those parts of European Russia that the government judged suitable. The motivation was practical. Once landlords no longer governed their serfs, and once the state recognized that its own provincial bureaucracy was too small and underfunded to provide local services, it needed institutions that could build and maintain roads, run primary schools, organize medical care, support agriculture, collect local statistics, and administer local welfare. The zemstvo was designed to do these things through elected bodies financed by local taxation, under the supervision of the governor.

Each district and province had an elected assembly and an executive board. Voters were divided into three curiae: private landowners, townspeople, and peasant communes. The system was indirect for peasants, who elected delegates through village and volost stages, and it favored property: the property qualification meant that landowners, who were mostly nobles, dominated district assemblies and held a still larger share of seats at the provincial level. The marshal of the nobility presided over assemblies. The zemstvos could levy local taxes, but their financial powers were limited, their scope of action was restricted to local economic and welfare matters, and governors could protest or suspend decisions that violated the law or the "general interests of the state." Zemstvo institutions were extended to thirty-four of the fifty provinces of European Russia but not to Siberia, the Caucasus, Central Asia, Poland, or at first the western and Baltic provinces. A parallel Municipal Statute of 1870 established elected town councils (dumas) with property-based franchise and similar limits.

The zemstvos should be seen as a major institutional innovation. For the first time, nobles, townspeople, and peasants participated in elected assemblies that managed public resources at the provincial and district level. In time the zemstvos built thousands of schools, hired physicians and teachers, established hospitals, funded agronomic services, and organized statistical research that became a major source for the study of rural Russia. The educated employees of the zemstvos, often called the "third element" alongside the elected members and the appointed officials, formed a professional corps of doctors, teachers, statisticians, and agronomists, many of whom were sympathetic to reform and some to radicalism. They helped create a public sphere of local expertise and debate.

Yet the zemstvos were not parliaments, and Russia did not become a constitutional monarchy. Representation was weighted toward landowners, governors held supervisory powers, the central government remained the sole source of sovereign authority, and there was no national body to coordinate or represent the zemstvos. The government soon showed its reluctance to permit them to develop political roles. It restricted their taxing powers in the 1860s, limited publicity of their proceedings, and restrained efforts at coordination between provinces. Liberal zemstvo leaders in the late 1870s began to petition for broader participation in government, and a minority within the zemstvo movement came to regard the lack of a national representative body as the central political problem. In 1890, under Alexander III, the government revised the statute to increase noble control and supervision (Chapter 43).

The central contradiction of the Great Reforms appears clearly here. The state created organized, educated, locally rooted public life because it needed better administration, while refusing to allow that public life a share in national political sovereignty. That arrangement could work only so long as the zemstvos remained limited to administration and so long as educated society accepted that limit. The tension led directly toward later Russian liberalism (Chapter 46).

Historical Vocabulary
Zemstvo

Definition: An elected assembly, with an executive board, responsible for local economic and welfare affairs in a district or province of the Russian Empire, created by the statute of 1864.

Historical context: Zemstvos existed in thirty-four provinces of European Russia, with voting by three curiae (landowners, townspeople, peasants) and a property qualification that gave landowners the largest share of seats. They financed schools, medicine, roads, and agricultural services under the supervision of the governor.

Why it matters: The word is sometimes translated as "local self-government," but the zemstvos were neither parliaments nor independent of the state. They combined real administrative responsibility with limited political power, and that combination made them both useful to the regime and a seedbed of liberal opposition.

Section 42.5

Judicial Reform

The Judicial Statutes of 20 November 1864 (2 December N.S.) were among the most institutionally radical measures of the reign. The previous system had combined judicial and administrative functions, relied on written procedure and secret hearings, and varied by estate; it was notorious for delay, formalism, and corruption. Contemporary observers, including officials, regarded it as a serious obstacle to both justice and economic development. The reform was drafted by a commission of jurists and officials, among them Sergei Zarudny and Nikolai Stoyanovsky, working under the minister of justice Dmitry Zamyatin, who drew on continental European, especially French and German, legal models while adapting them to Russian conditions.

The new system introduced several principles. Judicial power was separated from administration. Judges received security of tenure, meaning that they could not be dismissed at will. Trials were held in public, with oral proceedings and adversarial argument. Professional prosecutors represented the state, and a new legal profession of sworn attorneys (prisiazhnye poverennye) defended and represented litigants. Serious criminal cases were tried before juries, whose members were chosen from property-qualified residents of the district, including peasants. For minor civil and criminal cases, elected justices of the peace, initially chosen by zemstvo assemblies and town councils, provided rapid and accessible justice. The Senate served as a court of cassation. The press was allowed to report on trials, which made the courtroom a site of public debate.

The reform's consequences were significant. A legal profession emerged, producing prominent advocates and creating a milieu of educated professionals with an interest in legality. Jury trials produced public discussion of social problems, from the treatment of peasants to the conduct of officials. The courts also confirmed, in some respects, a principle that law could constrain officials. Historians of Russian law have argued that the reform helped create a rudimentary "legal consciousness" among educated Russians, though they disagree about its depth and reach.

But the reform also had limits. The new courts were introduced gradually, beginning in the St. Petersburg and Moscow judicial districts in 1866, and they reached many provinces only after years of delay. They were not extended to much of the empire's periphery, including large parts of Siberia, the Caucasus, and Central Asia, for a long time, or they were introduced in modified form. Peasants retained their own volost courts, applying customary law and capable of imposing corporal punishment, so the principle of equality before the law was incomplete. Church, military, and other special courts remained, and the empire's estate, religious, and regional distinctions meant that law was applied unequally. Administrative authorities also retained the right to deal with certain categories of cases through extrajudicial means.

The strains between the new courts and the political order showed most clearly in cases involving political crimes. After the attempt on the emperor by Dmitry Karakozov in 1866, the government increased police powers. In 1872 it established a Special Presence of the Senate to try state crimes. In 1878 the trial of Vera Zasulich, a radical who had shot and wounded the governor of St. Petersburg, Fyodor Trepov, ended in her acquittal by a jury, to the astonishment of the government. Anatoly Koni presided, and the verdict was widely understood as a statement of public sympathy. Within months the government removed cases of violence against officials from juries and transferred many of them to military courts. The episode illustrates the question posed by the reform: what happens when an autocratic state creates courts with some institutional independence from administrative officials? The answer, in the late 1870s and after, was that the state tended to restrict that independence whenever it clashed with perceived political necessity.

Regional Context

The Borderlands and the Great Reforms

The reforms looked different from outside the central Russian provinces. In the Baltic provinces of Estland, Livland, and Courland, peasants had been freed without land between 1816 and 1819 under the rule of the Baltic German nobility, which retained control of estates and provincial institutions; the Great Reforms interacted with a distinct local legal and political order, and elements such as the zemstvo were not extended to them at first. In Congress Poland, the failed January Uprising of 1863–1864 led the government to issue a decree of 1864 that gave peasants more favorable terms than in Russia, partly in order to win their loyalty and to weaken the Polish nobility, and the western provinces saw revised terms and reductions in payments after the uprising.

In the Caucasus, emancipation of landlord peasants in Georgia and other regions occurred in the 1860s and 1870s under separate statutes that generally favored the nobility. In right-bank Ukraine and Belarus, the uprising of 1863 produced a government drive to reduce the influence of the Polish landowners and to promote Russian officialdom and the Orthodox Church; the Valuev Circular of 1863 restricted publications in Ukrainian, and the Ems Ukaz of 1876 extended those restrictions (Chapter 45). In Siberia, where serfdom had not existed in the same form, zemstvos were not introduced, and in Turkestan, which was in the process of conquest and military administration, none of the reforms applied as they did in European Russia. Students should therefore treat the reform era as a set of differently applied policies within a multiethnic empire (Chapter 39), not as a single national process.

Section 42.6

Military and Educational Reform

This section is the institutional answer to the failures identified in Chapter 41. The principal architect of military reform was Dmitry Milyutin, an officer and military scholar who became minister of war in November 1861 and held the post until 1881. Milyutin worked within severe fiscal constraints and in the face of resistance from officers who preferred the traditional system, and his reforms were carried out in stages over two decades.

The first stage reorganized command and administration. In 1862–1864 the empire was divided into military districts, each with a commander responsible for troops, supplies, and mobilization in his region, replacing a more centralized and cumbersome system. A professional system of military education was expanded: cadet corps were reformed into military gymnasia and junker schools, and the military academies were strengthened to produce better-trained officers and staff. Soldiers' living conditions improved, corporal punishment was substantially restricted in the 1860s, and a system of literacy instruction in the army was encouraged. The army was rearmed with breech-loading rifles, and the artillery and logistical services were modernized. Railways were treated as strategic assets in mobilization planning.

The most significant change was the Universal Military Service Statute of 1 January 1874 (13 January N.S.). Under the old recruit system, villages and towns in the taxed estates supplied a quota of men chosen by their communities or landlords to serve nominal terms that had once been as long as twenty-five years and that, for many recruits, meant lasting separation from their families. Since 1834 an indefinite-leave system had allowed many soldiers to pass out of continuous active service after about fifteen years while remaining liable to recall, and most of these men were called up during the Crimean War. The nobility and many other groups were exempt. After the experience of the Franco-Prussian War of 1870–1871, in which a reserve-based mass army of conscripts had defeated the professional French army, Milyutin and his supporters argued that the existing indefinite-leave reserve was too limited and inflexible for modern mass warfare. The Prussian victories of 1866 and 1870–1871 reinforced their case for shorter active service combined with a far larger, systematically organized trained reserve and a broader social basis for military obligation. The Statute made all men liable for service at the age of twenty without regard to estate. Those chosen by lot served in the active army for a period of about six years and then passed into the reserve for about nine more. Exemptions and reductions were granted for family circumstances and for education: a university graduate could serve a much shorter term than an unschooled peasant. Clergy and certain other categories were exempt, and many non-Russian populations, particularly in Central Asia, the Caucasus, and Siberia, were exempted from service and often subjected to special taxes in lieu.

The larger analytical point is that universal service weakened one of the old estate distinctions. Noble privilege no longer automatically exempted the social elite from military obligation as it had under the older system, and a shared principle of obligation was extended to all male subjects. Yet universality should not be exaggerated: since the army needed fewer men than were available, most of those eligible did not serve, selection by lot continued, and exemptions were extensive. For peasant conscripts, army service could be a significant experience, offering literacy instruction and exposure to wider society, though conditions remained hard and discipline harsh. The reformed army was tested in the Russo-Turkish War of 1877–1878, which ended in a Russian victory achieved at heavy cost and in a diplomatic settlement at the Congress of Berlin that disappointed Russian expectations. Historians assess the reformed army as significantly more capable than that of 1854 but still limited by logistics, finance, and command.

Education was integrated into the reform program because a modernizing state needed engineers, officers, physicians, teachers, and administrators. The University Statute of 1863 gave universities a degree of autonomy, with councils electing rectors and deans and with greater control over curricula. The Elementary Schools Statute of 1864 allowed zemstvos, the church, and other bodies to establish primary schools under the oversight of school boards, and the Gymnasium Statute of 1864 provided for secondary schools of classical and "real" (science-oriented) types. In 1865 provisional rules relaxed preliminary censorship for larger books and for periodicals in the capitals, replacing it in some cases with post-publication penalties. Opportunities for women expanded in a limited way: higher courses for women appeared in St. Petersburg and Moscow in the 1860s and 1870s, including the Guerrier Courses in Moscow (1872) and the Bestuzhev Courses in St. Petersburg (1878), and women's secondary schools multiplied. Literacy remained low: the census of 1897 recorded that roughly one in five of the empire's inhabitants could read.

The educational reforms illustrate the paradox central to the chapter: modernization required education, but education produced increasingly autonomous publics that the government found politically dangerous. Student unrest, beginning in 1861, led to university closures and expulsions. After Karakozov's attempt in 1866, Dmitry Tolstoy became minister of education and pursued a more conservative course, increasing the classical emphasis of the gymnasium in 1871 in the hope of directing students toward languages and away from natural science and social theory, which officials associated with radicalism. Women's higher courses were repeatedly restricted. The university autonomy of 1863 was reversed in 1884 (Chapter 43). A system created to produce loyal, trained subjects had also produced a large number of educated people with grievances and ideas that the state could not easily contain.

Russia and the World

An Age of Emancipations and State Reform

The Great Reforms occurred amid a wave of state-led transformation. In the United States, the Civil War ended slavery by 1865; Brazil abolished slavery in 1888; the Habsburg monarchy had ended robot labor obligations in 1848; and Prussia had earlier dismantled serfdom in the reforms of the early nineteenth century. The Ottoman Empire pursued the Tanzimat reforms, and in 1868 Japan began the Meiji transformation. Russian officials studied foreign models in law, military organization, finance, and local government, borrowing, for example, from French and German legal procedure and from the Prussian reserve system, while adapting them to a vast agrarian empire of estates.

Comparison must be careful. Russian serfdom and American chattel slavery differed in legal definition, in the proportion of the population affected, in the racial basis of American slavery, and in the manner of abolition: Russian emancipation was carried out by an autocratic state, with compensation to owners and land allotments to peasants, while American emancipation resulted from war. The scholar Peter Kolchin has compared the two systems in detail. What the comparison reveals is that large-scale abolition of unfree labor was a global phenomenon, shaped in each case by local social structures and political institutions, and that states often pursued such measures when pressed by war, fiscal needs, and shifting international norms. The Russian reforms were also part of an international competition: the military victories of Prussia in 1866 and 1870–1871 reinforced the lesson of the Crimean War that reformed armies and railways mattered.

Section 42.7

Industrial and Social Change

This section examines the reform-era beginnings and consequences of economic and social change. It does not treat the mature state-directed industrialization of the 1890s, associated with Sergei Witte, which is the subject of Chapter 44. Students should distinguish between the expansion of markets, transportation, and commerce that accompanied the Great Reforms and the later, much more rapid growth of heavy industry and the industrial working class.

Railway construction was the most visible economic change. Russia had perhaps a thousand kilometers of track in 1855; by the early 1880s it had more than twenty thousand. Lines were built largely by private companies with state guarantees and foreign capital, and they linked grain-producing regions to ports and to the capitals. The expansion fostered the grain export trade, particularly through Black Sea ports such as Odessa, and encouraged specialization in agriculture. Finance minister Mikhail Reutern reorganized state finances, introduced greater budget transparency, supported the new State Bank, founded in 1860, and encouraged joint-stock and commercial banks. The state replaced the tax-farming system for alcohol with an excise system in 1863 and in the late 1860s moved toward a more liberal tariff. Cotton textiles, sugar refining, and some metallurgical industries grew, and individual foreign entrepreneurs began to establish enterprises, for example the Welsh engineer John Hughes, who founded an ironworks in the Donets basin around 1869–1870 that later became the city of Yuzovka.

Emancipation changed rural social relations. The removal of the landlord's legal authority and the need to pay redemption and taxes in cash encouraged peasants to engage with markets, to sell grain, and to work for wages. Seasonal labor migration to towns, estates, and construction sites expanded. Many noble landowners, deprived of serf labor and burdened by debts, struggled to adapt: some shifted to hired labor and commercial farming, some rented out land to peasants or sold parts of their estates, and a significant number mortgaged and ultimately lost their properties. Historians debate the extent of the "decline" of the gentry, since some nobles adapted successfully and others moved into state service and the professions. Merchants and some peasants acquired land, and the social composition of the landowning class gradually shifted.

The reforms also created new social groups. The legal profession, zemstvo employees, physicians, teachers, engineers, journalists, and other professionals formed an expanding educated stratum, drawn from nobles, clergy, townspeople, and the broad category of raznochintsy, "people of various ranks" who did not fit neatly into a single estate. Towns grew, and urban life began to change with the development of municipal institutions, commercial press, and educational and cultural associations. The reforms allowed some categories of Jewish residents, including certain merchants, craftsmen, and university graduates, to live outside the Pale of Settlement, though the Pale itself remained and restrictions continued (Chapters 43, 45). The estate system remained legally in force even as economic and educational change eroded its practical boundaries.

These changes were gradual and unevenly distributed. Russia remained overwhelmingly agrarian: most of the population was rural, industrial workers were a small minority, and many regions experienced little structural change. The period's significance lies less in a sudden industrial take-off than in the institutional and social conditions it created: a legal framework for free labor, a transport network, an expanding market, a growing professional class, and a peasantry that was legally free but economically constrained. These conditions became the starting point for the more rapid transformations examined in Chapter 44.

Multiple Perspectives

The Reform Era from Different Positions

Former serfs: For peasants, emancipation meant freedom from the landlord's personal authority and the end of the legal risk of sale, but also the continuation of obligations, the burden of redemption, communal responsibility, and, in many places, less land than they had hoped for. Evidence for their views appears in the disturbances of 1861, petitions, and rumors of a "real" freedom, which suggest that many regarded the settlement as incomplete or deceptive.
Noble landowners: Nobles were divided. Some embraced emancipation as necessary, some sought maximum compensation and control over land, and some resented the loss of status and authority. Many gained significant financial benefits from redemption, but they also lost secure labor and, for some, their position as local rulers. Participation in zemstvos and as peace mediators allowed many to retain local influence.
Zemstvo professionals and officials: Doctors, teachers, statisticians, and reformist officials found in the new institutions a chance to apply expertise and to serve the public. They also encountered financial limits and official supervision, and some of them developed critical views of the state and of rural poverty.
Radical intellectuals and students: For many young radicals, the reforms were too limited, left autocracy intact, and left the peasantry in poverty. Their perspective, especially in the 1860s and 1870s, was shaped by Herzen, Chernyshevsky, and a belief that Russia could take a socialist path distinct from Western capitalism.

These perspectives show that the same reform could be experienced as liberation, loss, opportunity, or betrayal, depending on social position and expectation.

Section 42.8

Populism and Revolutionary Movements

This section should be read as an account of radicalization, not a catalogue of revolutionaries. Chapter 40 introduced the intelligentsia, Alexander Herzen and his idea of Russian socialism, Vissarion Belinsky, and the radical circles of the 1840s. The generation that came of age in the 1860s confronted a Russia that had been changed dramatically but not in the direction the radicals desired. The peasants were free but poor, the state remained autocratic, and the educated public had a growing voice but no political power.

In the late 1850s and early 1860s, the periodical press played a central role. Herzen's The Bell, published in London and smuggled into Russia, urged emancipation with land, and the journal The Contemporary, in which Nikolai Chernyshevsky was a leading voice, advanced radical social and political ideas. After the settlement of 1861, which Chernyshevsky and others considered a deception, radical groups appeared, including a first organization called Land and Liberty (1861–1864). Chernyshevsky was arrested in 1862 and exiled; while imprisoned he wrote the novel What Is to Be Done? (1863), which portrayed a new type of rational, cooperative, and self-sacrificing young people and became a guide for a generation. Ivan Turgenev's novel Fathers and Sons (1862) introduced the figure of the "nihilist" to a wide readership, and the label came to be applied to young radicals who rejected inherited authority and valued science and utility. Not all radical youth were nihilists, and the term often describes a cultural attitude more than an organized political movement.

After the Polish uprising and the attempt on the emperor by Karakozov in 1866, repression intensified, but radical ideas continued to develop. Peter Lavrov's Historical Letters (1868–1869) argued that educated people owed a debt to the peasantry and should serve it, while Mikhail Bakunin advocated anarchism and spontaneous peasant revolt. Sergei Nechaev's conspiratorial circle of 1869, which committed a murder of a member and promoted an extreme doctrine of revolutionary secrecy and ruthlessness, scandalized many radicals and alienated much of the movement from similar methods for a time. In the early 1870s, groups such as the Chaikovsky Circle organized study and propaganda among workers and students.

Populism (narodnichestvo) is the name given to the broader movement that emerged. Populists believed that Russia could move toward socialism through the peasant commune, bypassing capitalist development as understood in Western Europe, and that educated people should work with and for the people. In 1873–1874 several thousand young men and women, many of them students, undertook the movement known as "Going to the People," living in villages and attempting to educate and inspire peasants. The results were disappointing: peasants were often suspicious of outsiders, uninterested in socialist ideas, or hostile, and some reported the activists to the authorities. Mass arrests followed, with hundreds of activists detained, and in 1877–1878 the government held the Trial of the 193, in which many defendants received light sentences or were acquitted, an outcome that embarrassed the authorities.

A more disciplined organization emerged in 1876, a new Land and Liberty, which combined work among peasants and workers with a degree of conspiratorial organization. Its members debated tactics. Some believed that long-term propaganda and organization in the villages must continue; others, frustrated by repression and the apparent failure of propaganda, argued for direct action against the state, including terrorism against officials. The Zasulich shooting of 1878 and its acquittal seemed to some to show both the need and the opportunity for such action. In the summer of 1879, meetings at Lipetsk and Voronezh produced a split. One group, Black Repartition (Chernyi Peredel), led by Georgy Plekhanov, remained committed to peasant propaganda and the redistribution of land; some of its members later turned toward Marxism. The other, People's Will (Narodnaya Volia), adopted political terrorism as its central method.

The crucial distinction is that populism was broader than terrorism. Most populists were not terrorists, and many who took part in "Going to the People" or in Land and Liberty never participated in political violence. It would be a mistake to write backward from the assassination and to make every radical an inevitable terrorist. It would be equally mistaken to treat these movements as proto-Bolshevik. Their emphasis on the peasant commune, moral duty, decentralized socialism, and agrarian transformation differed sharply from the later Marxist and Leninist emphasis on the industrial working class and disciplined party organization (Chapter 51), though some individuals and ideas linked the traditions. Women played a prominent role: Vera Figner, Vera Zasulich, and Sofia Perovskaya were among the most visible activists, a sign of the opening of education and public life to women even as they faced social and legal constraints.

Why did radicals turn toward violence? Historians point to several factors: the frustrating experience in the villages; the severity of government responses, including long prison terms and exile for non-violent activity; the perceived closure of legal channels after the acquittal of Zasulich and the removal of political cases from juries; the example of European revolutionary movements; and the belief that the autocracy was weaker than it appeared and could be forced to grant political freedom through terror. None of these factors made terrorism inevitable, and the choice remained contested within the movement, but together they help explain why a minority concluded that an attack on the state and the emperor was a viable political strategy.

Section 42.9

People's Will and the Assassination of Alexander II

People's Will was a small, secretive, highly disciplined organization. Its Executive Committee, which numbered a few dozen active members at most, included Andrei Zhelyabov, Sofia Perovskaya, Aleksandr Mikhailov, Vera Figner, Lev Tikhomirov, and the technical expert Nikolai Kibalchich, who designed explosives. The group argued that the autocracy was the main obstacle to social and political transformation and that terror against its leading representatives, and above all the emperor, could shake the regime and force political concessions or inspire popular uprising. In August 1879 its Executive Committee pronounced a sentence of death on Alexander II. Its political program called for a representative assembly elected by universal suffrage and for broad civil liberties, alongside socialist aims, which shows that its leaders regarded political freedom as the necessary first step toward social change.

A series of attempts followed. In April 1879, before the formal organization of People's Will, Aleksandr Solovyov had fired at the emperor on a walk near the Winter Palace and missed. In November 1879 members of the organization mined the railway line near Moscow over which the imperial train was expected to pass, but the bomb exploded under a different train. In February 1880 Stepan Khalturin, a worker who had obtained employment as a carpenter in the Winter Palace, detonated an explosive in the building; the blast killed eleven soldiers and wounded dozens, but the emperor, who was delayed, was unhurt. These attacks created a sense of crisis and shook confidence in the security apparatus.

The government's response combined repression and an effort at accommodation. In February 1880 Alexander appointed a Supreme Executive Commission headed by Loris-Melikov, a general who had served in the Caucasus and the Russo-Turkish War, with extraordinary powers to restore order. In August 1880 the Third Section of the Imperial Chancery, the political police, was abolished and its functions transferred to a Department of State Police within a reorganized Ministry of the Interior, with Loris-Melikov as minister. He pursued a policy sometimes called the "dictatorship of the heart": he relaxed censorship somewhat, reduced some taxes, reinvigorated consultations with zemstvos and the press, replaced the unpopular minister of education Dmitry Tolstoy, and sought to isolate the terrorists from liberal society while continuing to prosecute them. He also developed a proposal to bring representatives of zemstvos and towns into limited consultation in the legislative process.

The attempt on the emperor's life that succeeded occurred on 1 March 1881 O.S. (13 March N.S.). Zhelyabov had been arrested on 27 February, but the plot continued under Perovskaya's direction. Bomb throwers were stationed along the likely route, and when the emperor's carriage turned onto the Catherine Canal embankment, Nikolai Rysakov threw the first bomb, which damaged the carriage and wounded several people. Alexander stepped out; Ignaty Grinevitsky, standing nearby, threw a second bomb, which killed Grinevitsky and fatally wounded the emperor. Alexander was taken to the Winter Palace, where he died that afternoon. Five members of People's Will, including Zhelyabov, Perovskaya, Kibalchich, Mikhailov, and Rysakov, were tried and hanged in public in April 1881, while Gesya Gelfman, who was pregnant, had her sentence commuted and died in prison in 1882.

The Loris-Melikov proposals require particular caution. The report that Alexander approved in principle on the morning of 1 March did not create a constitution. It proposed temporary preparatory commissions that would include appointed officials and elected representatives of zemstvos and city dumas, with a consultative role in drafting legislation on certain questions, subject to review by the State Council and the emperor. The arrangement was consultative, and it did not provide for parliamentary sovereignty or responsible government in the Western constitutional sense. Reports of the emperor's views and intentions vary, and historians dispute whether he intended the scheme as a first step toward something larger or as a limited concession that would leave autocracy untouched.

The coincidence nevertheless matters. The emperor associated with the Great Reforms was killed at the moment when his government was considering another limited expansion of consultative participation. The question of whether further reform was possible, and whether the assassination prevented or merely interrupted a process, cannot be answered with certainty. Some historians argue that a reform-minded policy might have continued and might have narrowed the gap between state and society; others note that Alexander's commitment to autocracy, the opposition of conservatives, and the dynamics of terror made further movement uncertain. What is clear is that the assassination shocked officials and much of the public, discredited the terrorist strategy in the eyes of most Russians, and strengthened conservatives who argued that reform had gone too far. People's Will did not provoke the popular uprising it anticipated; instead, the new emperor, Alexander III, issued in April 1881 a manifesto reaffirming the principle of autocracy, and the police, with expanded powers, dismantled the organization over the following years (Chapter 43).

A fiscal-military state dependent on serfdom and the estate system; earlier reform thinking under Nicholas I; moral and economic criticism of serfdom; weak transportation and limited administrative capacity.

Rescripts of 1857 opening public preparation; peasant unrest and fear of disorder; the drafting of the Statutes by the Editing Commissions; the emperor's decision to proceed in 1861.

The emancipation of 1861 and the subsequent institutional reforms: zemstvos, courts, military service, education, and economic change.

Peasant disappointment and unrest; the creation of new local institutions and professional groups; student and radical mobilization; conservative reaction after 1866.

Long-Term Consequences

Land hunger and arrears; an expanded educated public and liberal opposition; populist radicalization and terrorism; the assassination of 1881 and the conservative turn of the 1880s.

Historical complexity: No single cause explains the reforms or their outcome. Military defeat made change urgent but did not determine its form; emancipation altered legal status without resolving land; new institutions created expectations the autocratic framework could not meet; and terrorism emerged from a combination of frustrated radicalism, state repression, and political choice, not from structural inevitability.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Russia in 1855 and Russia in 1881

What Changed

  • Landlord serfdom ended as a legal institution, altering the status of tens of millions of people.
  • Elected local institutions, independent courts, and a legal profession appeared; the recruit levy gave way to universal obligation.
  • Railways, banks, and markets expanded, and a professional and educated stratum grew.
  • A radical movement emerged that was capable of sustained organization and political violence.

What Remained

  • The emperor retained unlimited sovereign authority; there was no national representative assembly.
  • The estate system, the separate legal status of peasants, internal passports, and the communal fiscal order persisted.
  • Russia remained overwhelmingly agrarian, with a limited industrial base.
  • Censorship, political policing, and exceptional measures against political crimes continued.

Why the pattern matters: Change was rapid in legal and institutional terms but uneven in social and economic terms. Peasants, nobles, professionals, and borderland populations experienced it very differently, and the persistence of autocracy shaped how the new institutions developed.

Chapter Review and Historical Thinking Checkpoint

This chapter has argued that the Great Reforms were neither a failed liberal revolution nor a simple continuation of autocracy. They were a coherent but contradictory project: a state-directed effort to repair the weaknesses revealed in the Crimean War while preserving the emperor's unlimited authority. Students who assess the reforms simply as a "success" or a "failure" will miss the point. Their significance lies in what they changed, whom they affected, and which problems they created or left unresolved.

Transformations. Emancipation changed the legal status of landlord serfs and created new institutions of peasant administration. Zemstvos, municipal councils, and new courts altered local government and justice. Military service was reorganized and extended across estates. Schools, universities, and the press expanded. Railways, banks, and markets grew. These changes constituted a significant restructuring of imperial institutions.

Continuities. The autocratic principle, the estate system, the separate legal and fiscal status of peasants, and the reliance on administrative discretion persisted. The settlement of 1861 left land and debt as persistent problems. The state retained the capacity to limit the new institutions when they appeared to threaten political order.

Causation. Military defeat, fiscal pressure, moral criticism, fear of peasant disorder, and the political positioning of reform-minded officials combined to make change possible. The reforms in turn generated new causes: expectations among peasants and educated people, new professional groups and institutions, and a radical movement that responded to both the opportunities and the limits of reform.

Evidence and interpretation. Students should recognize the types of sources available: official legislation, drafting records, local reports, trial records, periodicals, memoirs, and statistical surveys, and the difficulty of recovering peasant perspectives. The debate over emancipation shows how historians use similar evidence to reach different judgments, and the Loris-Melikov question shows the danger of reading later events backward.

Significance. The reform era created institutions, social groups, and political questions that shaped the next four decades: the agrarian question, the problem of representation, the role of the zemstvos and the legal profession, the conflict between state and educated society, and the relationship between reform and revolutionary politics. It also reminds students that the empire's diversity meant the reforms were experienced very differently across its regions.

Chapter Summary

This chapter examined the reign of Alexander II as an attempt to strengthen the Russian Empire after the Crimean War without surrendering autocracy. Its governing question was whether an empire could modernize institutions and society while leaving unlimited sovereign authority intact. The chapter argued that the Great Reforms were a major, state-directed transformation that changed legal status, local government, justice, military service, education, and the economy, but that they also produced inequalities, expectations, and social forces that the autocratic framework could not fully accommodate.

Reform became possible after 1856 because defeat altered the balance of opinion among the governing elite. Earlier reform thinking under Nicholas I, the criticism of serfdom by educated Russians, and the experiments of some landowners meant that the war did not create the problem but made change appear necessary for state survival. Alexander II, neither a liberal idealist nor a cynic, wished to preserve dynasty and empire and chose to proceed from above, as his reported remark to the Moscow nobility in 1856 suggests.

The emancipation of 1861 ended landlord serfdom as a legal institution. Peasants gained personal rights, including the right to marry, to own property, to make contracts, and to use the courts, and landlords lost legal authority over their persons. These changes were enormous. At the same time, peasants remained a separate legal estate under village and volost institutions, subject to the poll tax, communal responsibility, internal passport controls, and corporal punishment by volost courts.

The land settlement was a compromise among peasants, who wanted land, landlords, who wanted compensation and preservation of estates, and the state, which wanted order. Allotments were set by regional norms, cutoffs reduced peasants' holdings in some black-earth provinces, a period of temporary obligation preserved landlord claims to labor or rent, and the state financed the redemption of land by compensating landlords and collecting payments from peasants over forty-nine years. The payments were reduced in the 1880s and after 1905 and abolished in 1907. Peasant disappointment produced disturbances in 1861, and land shortage and arrears became lasting problems.

The zemstvo statute of 1864 and the municipal statute of 1870 created elected local institutions that managed schools, medicine, roads, and statistics. They were not parliaments, because representation favored landowners, governors supervised them, and no national body existed. The Judicial Statutes of 1864 introduced public trials, professional judges and attorneys, prosecutors, justices of the peace, and juries. The new courts were introduced gradually, excluded many regions and groups, and were restricted when they clashed with the state's security concerns, as the Zasulich acquittal of 1878 showed.

Military reform under Dmitry Milyutin created military districts, improved officer education and equipment, and culminated in the Universal Military Service Statute of 1874, which extended obligation across estates while retaining extensive exemptions. Educational reform expanded schooling and gave universities autonomy, and women's higher courses appeared, but student unrest and the radical politics of educated youth led the government to restrict these measures. Railways, banks, markets, and labor migration expanded, and new professional groups emerged, though the more rapid industrialization of the 1890s belongs to Chapter 44.

Populism, which arose among intellectuals who looked to the peasantry and the commune as the basis of a distinctive socialism, was broader than terrorism. After the disappointments of "Going to the People" and the repression that followed, a minority within Land and Liberty adopted terror, forming People's Will in 1879. The group's attempts on the emperor culminated in the assassination of 1 March 1881 (O.S.), shortly after Alexander had approved in principle Loris-Melikov's proposal for limited consultative participation. The proposal was not a constitution, and the question of whether further reform was possible cannot be settled with certainty.

The chapter also showed that the reforms were experienced differently across the empire. The Baltic provinces, Poland, the Caucasus, right-bank Ukraine, Siberia, and Turkestan each experienced distinct settlements, institutions, or exclusions, and the reform era was bound up with imperial security and nationality policy. Comparison with other nineteenth-century emancipations and state reforms places Russia within a global pattern without implying that systems were identical.

Historiographically, the central debate concerns the success of emancipation. Interpretations emphasizing liberation, landlord advantage and peasant burden, and the variability of outcomes each draw on legitimate evidence, and substantial agreement exists on the magnitude of legal change and the persistence of the land problem. The assassination of Alexander II, in turn, opens Chapter 43, which examines the conservative response of Alexander III, the strengthening of autocratic controls, and the nationalist and Russifying policies of the 1880s and 1890s.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because the Great Reforms were the Russian Empire's most ambitious attempt to reshape itself from within. They ended a legal institution that had defined rural life for centuries, built local and judicial institutions that gave a larger public a stake in governing, reorganized the army, and widened education. They show that autocratic states can pursue extensive institutional change, and that such change can have effects the state did not intend.

What continued afterward was the tension at the center of the chapter. The land question remained unresolved and fed later agrarian politics (Chapters 44, 47). The zemstvos and the legal profession became sites of liberal aspiration that conflicted with the principle of unlimited sovereignty (Chapter 46). Populist ideas about the peasantry, the commune, and moral duty shaped later socialist movements, even as Marxism and Bolshevism took different directions (Chapter 51). The state's repeated pattern of reform, restriction, and repression shaped the conservative response examined in Chapter 43.

The chapter also shows several textbook themes: the interaction of fiscal, military, social, and ideological causes; the differing experiences of peasants, nobles, professionals, and imperial borderlands; the importance of evidence and historiographical debate; and the danger of reading outcomes backward. The assassination of Alexander II was not the inevitable result of reform, nor was the later revolution the inevitable result of the assassination. Questions remain open, including how far peasants' welfare changed after 1861 and whether limited consultation could have grown into a different political order. Chapter 43 begins with the shock of 1881 and the government's decision that the reform era had gone too far, which sets the course for the late imperial crisis.
