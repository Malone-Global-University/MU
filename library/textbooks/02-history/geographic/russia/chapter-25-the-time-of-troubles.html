Part V — Crisis, Recovery, and the Seventeenth Century
25

The Time of Troubles

A History of Russia: From the Eurasian World to the Russian Federation

Chapter Opening

In the autumn of 1601, hungry people began arriving in Moscow from the countryside in numbers the city's charitable institutions and grain reserves could not absorb. Contemporary chroniclers describe streets filled with the starving and dying, mass burials, and a government straining to distribute grain even as prices climbed beyond ordinary means. The tsar occupying the throne at that moment, Boris Godunov, had been formally and, by the standards of the time, legitimately elected in 1598. He now faced a catastrophe that no election could resolve.

Chapter 24 closed with Muscovy's dynastic center emptied out: the deaths of Ivan IV's sons, the mysterious death of the youngest, Dmitry, at Uglich in 1591, the accession and childless death of Fyodor I, and the elevation of Boris Godunov, an experienced but non-Rurikid statesman, to the throne through an Assembly of the Land in 1598. That chapter argued that Muscovy entered the last years of the sixteenth century carrying accumulated vulnerabilities — a broken succession, fiscal exhaustion from war and oprichnina-era disruption, an unresolved legal transformation of the peasantry, and pressure along a long southern frontier. None of those vulnerabilities by itself amounted to catastrophe. This chapter explains how, between 1601 and 1613, they combined with a genuine natural disaster, competing claims to dynastic legitimacy, social rebellion, and the armed intervention of neighboring powers to produce what Russian historical tradition calls the Smuta, conventionally translated as the Time of Troubles: a period in which no single government could reliably command the obedience of the Muscovite realm.

The scale of what occurred deserves emphasis before the narrative begins. Within little more than a decade, Muscovy experienced a famine that may have killed a substantial share of its population in the affected regions, two men who claimed to be the resurrected Tsarevich Dmitry and briefly commanded significant political and military followings, a tsar deposed by his own boyars and forced into a monastery, a period in which two rival courts and two rival patriarchal establishments operated simultaneously within the same country, formal Polish-Lithuanian intervention, the occupation of the Moscow Kremlin by a Commonwealth garrison, and Swedish occupation of Novgorod, and finally a broadly based coalition of towns, provincial servicemen, clergy, and Cossacks that expelled the occupying garrison and convened an assembly that elected a sixteen-year-old boy as the founder of a new ruling dynasty. No single cause explains this sequence, and no single group of actors drove it from beginning to end.

This chapter's central historical argument is that the Time of Troubles should not be reduced to any one of the labels later historians and political traditions have applied to it. It was not simply a foreign invasion resisted by a unified Russian nation, although foreign intervention was real and consequential. It was not simply a peasant uprising against noble oppression, although peasant, Cossack, and other lower-status participants were central to several of its episodes. It was not simply a succession crisis resolved by the restoration of proper hereditary rule, since the Romanov dynasty itself began with an election. It was, this chapter argues, a prolonged crisis of legitimate authority, in which environmental disaster, economic collapse, competing dynastic claims, elite faction, social grievance, and international politics interacted and reinforced one another until reconstruction became necessary from the ground up. Readers who keep that interaction in view, rather than searching for a single explanation, will understand both why the crisis proved so severe and why its resolution in 1613, examined in this chapter's final sections, left so much work for Chapter 26's account of the early Romanov reconstruction.

The Time of Troubles also poses distinctive evidentiary problems that this chapter addresses directly. Much of what is known about the period comes from chronicles, tales, and other narrative accounts composed after 1613, often by writers connected to the new Romanov dynasty or to institutions, including the Orthodox Church, with strong interests in presenting particular participants as legitimate and others as usurpers or traitors. Boris Godunov in particular became, in later tradition, a figure blamed retrospectively for crimes — including, in some versions, direct responsibility for Dmitry's death at Uglich — that cannot be established from evidence contemporary with the events themselves. Students should apply here the same source-critical habits Chapter 24 introduced: asking who wrote an account, when, for what audience, and with what interest in shaping how later readers would judge the period's rival claimants.

Finally, this chapter asks students to hold in mind a question that will recur later in this textbook, particularly in the chapters on 1917 examined in Part IX: what happens to a state and a society when central political authority genuinely fragments, when multiple armed groups each claim legitimate sovereignty, and when foreign powers become entangled in a domestic conflict through both invitation and invasion? The Time of Troubles was Muscovy's first sustained experience of this kind of crisis, and the patterns of collapse, mobilization, and reconstruction examined here recur, in transformed form, at several later points in Russian history.

By the chapter's end, students should be able to trace the sequence of crises from Boris Godunov's election through Mikhail Romanov's, explain how famine, pretender politics, social rebellion, and foreign intervention interacted rather than operating as separate causes, evaluate the limitations of sources produced after 1613, and assess competing historical interpretations of what kind of crisis the Troubles actually were.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain the political situation Boris Godunov inherited as elected tsar in 1598 and the political vulnerabilities surrounding his legitimacy after the extinction of the direct Rurikid line.
  2. Analyze the Great Famine of 1601–1603 as an interaction of environmental disaster and existing social and political vulnerability.
  3. Trace the rise, brief reign, and fall of False Dmitry I and evaluate why his claim attracted genuine support.
  4. Describe Vasily Shuisky's contested accession and explain why removing a pretender did not restore stable government.
  5. Analyze the Bolotnikov rebellion and related unrest as a broad, socially mixed civil conflict rather than a unified peasant war.
  6. Explain the significance of False Dmitry II and the Tushino camp as evidence of fragmented, competing sovereignty within Muscovy.
  7. Distinguish informal Polish-Lithuanian participation in pretender movements from the formal intervention of King Sigismund III after 1609.
  8. Evaluate the First and Second National Militias as examples of political authority re-forming from towns, provincial servicemen, clergy, and Cossacks rather than from the central monarchy.
  9. Explain why the Assembly of 1613 selected Mikhail Romanov and assess the relationship between election and hereditary legitimacy in his selection.
  10. Critically evaluate sources produced after 1613 for their retrospective political interests, particularly regarding Boris Godunov.
  11. Compare multiple historiographical interpretations of the Time of Troubles, including dynastic-crisis, peasant-war, and civil-war framings.
  12. Connect the fragmentation and reconstruction of authority examined in this chapter to Chapter 26's account of early Romanov state-building and to later Russian crises of political legitimacy.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did the vulnerabilities established in Chapter 24 interact with the famine of 1601–1603 to produce a political crisis rather than only a subsistence crisis?
  2. Why did claims to be the surviving Tsarevich Dmitry prove politically powerful, and what did that power depend upon?
  3. Why did the removal of a discredited ruler or pretender fail, repeatedly, to restore stable government during this period?
  4. What does the wide social range of participants in the Bolotnikov movement and later coalitions suggest about the nature of political loyalty in early modern Muscovy?
  5. In what ways did Muscovite domestic factions themselves invite or provoke outside intervention, rather than simply suffering foreign invasion?
  6. How could political authority begin to re-form from provincial towns, servicemen, clergy, and Cossacks when the central monarchy had ceased to function reliably?
  7. Why might historians disagree about whether the events of 1598–1613 are best understood as a dynastic crisis, a famine-driven social collapse, a civil war, or a war of foreign intervention?
  8. What does the election of Mikhail Romanov reveal about how Muscovites understood the relationship between heredity and election in constituting legitimate rule?

Historical Context

Chapter 24 traced the destabilization of the Muscovite dynastic order in the final decades of the sixteenth century: the later reign of Ivan IV, marked by war, internal repression, and economic disruption; the extinction of the direct Rurikid male line following the deaths of Ivan's sons, including the disputed death of the youngest, Dmitry, at Uglich in 1591; the reign of Fyodor I; and the elevation of Boris Godunov, Fyodor's brother-in-law and an experienced court statesman, to the throne by an Assembly of the Land (zemsky sobor) in 1598. That chapter also examined the legal tightening of peasant mobility restrictions, Crimean Tatar raiding pressure on the southern frontier, and the early stages of Siberian expansion, each of which shaped the social and fiscal landscape Boris inherited.

Two further points from that background deserve restatement here. First, Boris Godunov's legitimacy rested on election by an assembly representing boyars, clergy, service gentry, and townspeople, rather than on direct hereditary descent from the extinct ruling line. Muscovite political culture did not necessarily treat such election as an inherently inferior or illegitimate path to the throne; election and hereditary connection could work together to establish legitimacy, as they had at points earlier in Muscovite history. What made Boris's position vulnerable was not the mechanism of his accession as such, but the combination of contested elite politics among boyar families who considered their own birth at least equal to his, the absence of any close blood tie to the extinct dynasty, and the later shocks of famine and pretender politics examined in this chapter. Second, the unresolved circumstances of Tsarevich Dmitry's death at Uglich — officially ruled an accident during an epileptic seizure by a commission that Vasily Shuisky himself had headed — left open a dangerous political possibility. Accusations of murder arose immediately against alleged local perpetrators at Uglich; only in subsequent years, and especially amid the political struggles following False Dmitry I's rise, did explicit narratives increasingly attribute responsibility for the supposed assassination to Boris Godunov himself. This left open the possibility that someone claiming to be the surviving Dmitry could challenge Boris's rule with a claim resting on direct hereditary right.

This chapter begins at the point where that latent vulnerability met a sudden, unrelated catastrophe. Beginning in 1601, and continuing through 1603, Muscovy experienced repeated and severe harvest failures caused by unusually cold and wet growing seasons. Modern climatological and tree-ring research has connected the extreme Northern Hemisphere cooling of these years to the massive 1600 eruption of the Peruvian volcano Huaynaputina, one of the largest volcanic eruptions of the second millennium, whose atmospheric effects appear to have depressed summer temperatures across much of the hemisphere. Historians are careful not to claim that a volcanic eruption on the other side of the world mechanically caused the Russian Time of Troubles: the eruption explains the weather, not the political choices, social grievances, and institutional weaknesses that turned bad weather into catastrophe. But the coincidence of environmental shock and political vulnerability is central to this chapter's explanation of how the crisis began.

Students should also understand, before entering the chapter's numbered sections, that the very name conventionally given to this period — the Smuta, or Time of Troubles — is itself a retrospective label, applied by writers and officials in the aftermath of 1613 who had strong interests in describing the preceding years as a dangerous departure from legitimate order that the new Romanov dynasty had heroically overcome. This chapter uses the conventional English term while asking students to examine, particularly in the Historical Debate feature below, how much that framing shapes — and potentially distorts — historical understanding of what actually happened.

Section 25.1

Boris Godunov as Tsar

Boris Godunov's election in February 1598 resolved, at least on paper, the succession crisis that had followed Fyodor I's death without an heir. The Assembly of the Land that chose him drew on boyars, senior clergy led by the recently established Moscow Patriarchate, representatives of the service gentry, and townspeople, and its proceedings were carefully staged to present Boris's accession as the expression of a broad and willing national consensus rather than as the self-promotion of a single powerful court faction. In the years immediately following, Boris governed with real competence: he continued the cautious foreign policy and internal administrative consolidation that had marked his years as the dominant figure behind Fyodor's throne, sponsored building projects in Moscow, sought closer contact with Western European states and technical specialists, and made efforts, examined further in Section 25.2, to project an image of orderly and benevolent rule.

Boris's political position nevertheless differed from that of a ruler directly descended from the preceding line. His vulnerability arose from the extinction of that line, intense boyar competition, his lack of a close blood connection to it, and the later crises that gave rivals opportunities to challenge his authority — not from election itself being an inherently inferior basis of succession. Boris's most dangerous rivals were not distant enemies but boyar families, including the Romanov and Zakharyin-Yuriev circle connected by marriage to the extinct dynastic line, who considered their own birth at least equal to his and who had reason to regard his rule as a temporary arrangement rather than a settled new order. Boris moved against several such rivals in the years after his accession, including measures against members of the Romanov family that later, Romanov-sponsored sources would present as unjust persecution — a characterization students should treat with the same caution Chapter 24 applied to retrospective accounts, since sources written after 1613 had strong interests in portraying the family that would found the new dynasty as innocent victims of Boris's suspicion.

It is important not to read Boris's reign backward through the catastrophe that followed, as though his fall were predetermined from the moment of his election. For roughly three years, Boris presided over a functioning, internationally engaged state that faced no crisis remotely comparable to what would follow after 1601. What transformed a politically vulnerable but stable government into an acute national crisis was not any single decision of Boris's own making, but a natural disaster whose scale no early modern government could have fully absorbed. That disaster, examined in the next section, arrived in the autumn harvest of 1601.

Section 25.2

The Great Famine

Beginning in the summer of 1601, Muscovy experienced an extraordinary sequence of harvest failures driven by unusually cold, wet growing seasons that prevented grain from ripening across large parts of the realm. The same pattern recurred in 1602 and, with somewhat less severity, in 1603, producing three consecutive years in which normal agricultural output could not be sustained. Modern paleoclimatic research, including tree-ring evidence, has identified 1601 as one of the coldest Northern Hemisphere summers of the past several centuries, and has connected this cooling to the enormous 1600 eruption of the Peruvian volcano Huaynaputina, whose atmospheric aerosols are understood to have reduced solar warming across much of the hemisphere for one or more growing seasons. This environmental explanation supplies the trigger for the famine's onset; it does not by itself explain why the famine produced political catastrophe rather than mere hardship, a distinction this section's companion Cause and Effect feature examines directly.

The human consequences were severe. Grain prices rose to many times their ordinary level, and reports from contemporary chroniclers, foreign observers resident in Moscow, and later commemorative accounts describe mass starvation, resort to substitute foods, and significant mortality, particularly among the rural and urban poor who lacked reserves or the means to purchase scarce grain at inflated prices. Hungry rural populations moved toward Moscow and other towns in search of relief, straining urban food supplies further and creating large concentrations of desperate, uprooted people in a period before Muscovy possessed administrative capacity for large-scale, sustained famine relief. Precise mortality figures from this period cannot be established with confidence; contemporary estimates, some running into the hundreds of thousands for Moscow alone, should be treated as impressionistic rather than statistically reliable, though the consistency of independent accounts in describing a catastrophe of major scale supports treating the famine as one of the most severe demographic shocks in Muscovite history to that point.

Boris Godunov's government did attempt relief measures, including the distribution of grain from state reserves and, according to some accounts, funds for the urban poor, along with price controls intended to check profiteering. These measures proved insufficient to the scale of the disaster and, in some respects, appear to have worsened conditions by drawing even larger numbers of destitute people toward the capital in hope of assistance the government could not fully provide. Landlords in some cases released serfs and slaves they could no longer feed, adding further to the numbers of uprooted, unattached people moving through the countryside and toward towns and the southern frontier — a development with lasting consequences for the social base of rebellion examined in Section 25.5.

The famine's political significance lay less in any single administrative failure than in its corrosive effect on confidence in Boris's government. Lacking a close blood tie to the extinct ruling line and already contending with rival boyar families who considered themselves his equals in birth, Boris was especially exposed to being blamed, fairly or not, for a catastrophe of this scale; popular and later chronicle tradition increasingly interpreted the famine as divine punishment for Boris's presumed sins, prominently including the by-then increasingly widespread accusation that he had arranged Tsarevich Dmitry's death at Uglich a decade earlier. Whether or not such interpretations reflected genuine contemporary belief or were amplified retrospectively by writers hostile to Boris, the famine created exactly the kind of widespread desperation and eroded political confidence that made the population receptive to an alternative claimant to the throne — the subject of the following section.

Section 25.3

False Dmitry I

In 1603 and 1604, reports began to circulate, first within Polish-Lithuanian territory and then within Muscovy itself, of a man claiming to be Tsarevich Dmitry, the youngest son of Ivan IV, who asserted that he had survived the events at Uglich in 1591 through a substitution and had spent the intervening years in hiding. Muscovite and later Russian tradition has commonly identified this claimant with Grigory Otrepyev, a monk with connections to Moscow monastic and boyar circles, though this identification, while part of the historical record from an early date, rests substantially on testimony produced by his political opponents after his fall and has been questioned by some modern scholars. Students should understand that the pretender's precise identity and his own state of belief about his claim — whether he was a deliberate impostor, a sincere believer in a story others had constructed around him, or something in between — cannot be established with certainty from the surviving evidence, and that older historical writing describing him simply as an obvious fraud understates both the genuine uncertainty of the sources and the extent to which many contemporaries found his claim credible or, at minimum, politically useful.

The claim's plausibility depended directly on Chapter 24's unresolved Uglich story: because the circumstances of the historical Dmitry's death had never been established beyond dispute, and because rumors of a substitution or survival had circulated for years before 1603, a claimant asserting royal identity did not have to overcome the kind of immediate, obvious contradiction that would have discredited a claim to be, for example, a still-living and publicly known figure. The pretender found early support among Polish-Lithuanian nobles and other private patrons, including the magnate Jerzy Mniszech, whose daughter Marina Mniszech he betrothed, but this support came from private individuals and factions rather than from the Polish-Lithuanian crown itself, a distinction students should hold clearly in mind, since formal royal intervention by King Sigismund III did not begin until several years later, examined in Section 25.7.

Crossing into Muscovite territory in 1604, the pretender drew support from the volatile southern frontier region, where discontented service gentry, Cossacks, and a population already destabilized by famine and by the harsh peasant-mobility restrictions examined in Chapter 24 proved receptive to a claimant promising restored legitimate rule and relief from Boris's government. Boris's sudden death in April 1605 removed the one figure whose personal authority, however contested, had continued to anchor the government, and the accession of his young son Fyodor II proved unable to withstand the combined pressure of military defections and popular unrest in Moscow. In June 1605, Fyodor II and his mother were killed in a coup that opened the path for the pretender's triumphant entry into Moscow that same month; he was formally crowned tsar the following month, in July 1605.

False Dmitry I's reign lasted less than a year. His prominent Polish favorites and retainers, departures from established Muscovite court ceremony and convention, a degree of religious toleration unusual for the period, and his marriage to the Catholic Marina Mniszech gave his opponents concrete material to portray him as a servant of Polish and Catholic interests hostile to Orthodox Muscovy. Much of the vivid hostile characterization that later became standard — including claims about his personal impiety and foreign habits — took shape as propaganda in the political struggle that followed his fall, and modern scholarship cautions against accepting this portrayal uncritically as a neutral description of his brief rule. In May 1606, a boyar-led conspiracy headed by Vasily Shuisky organized an uprising in Moscow that killed the pretender and installed Shuisky himself on the throne — an outcome examined in the following section, which shows that removing one discredited claimant did nothing to restore the underlying stability of Muscovite political authority.

Historical Vocabulary
Samozvanets (Pretender)

Samozvanets, conventionally translated "pretender" or "self-proclaimed one," is the Russian term applied to individuals who claimed royal identity, most prominently the surviving Tsarevich Dmitry, without an established or universally accepted hereditary right. The Time of Troubles produced Muscovy's first and most consequential experience of sustained pretender politics, with at least two major claimants — the subjects of Sections 25.3 and 25.6 — commanding real political and military followings.

Pretender claims mattered historically not primarily as personal deceptions but as vehicles through which discontented groups — frontier servicemen, Cossacks, peasants, and at times boyar factions — could mobilize opposition to a sitting government under the banner of restoring supposedly legitimate rule rather than openly rejecting monarchy itself. The phenomenon recurred in later Russian history, including the eighteenth-century Pugachev rebellion examined in Chapter 35, in which a Cossack leader claimed to be the deposed Peter III.

Section 25.4

Vasily Shuisky

Vasily Shuisky's path to the throne in May 1606 connected directly to Chapter 24's unfinished business: as the boyar who had headed the 1591 commission investigating Dmitry's death at Uglich, and who had at that time officially concluded the death was an accident, Shuisky was, by 1606, uniquely positioned — and uniquely compromised — to pronounce authoritatively on the falseness of the man who had just been killed as an impostor. His own accession followed not through a broadly representative Assembly of the Land of the kind that had elected Boris Godunov, but through acclamation by a narrower Moscow constituency dominated by the boyars who had organized the coup against False Dmitry I, a narrower base of legitimacy that left him vulnerable from the outset to rivals who questioned whether he possessed any more legitimate a claim than his predecessor had.

Shuisky attempted to strengthen his position through several means: a formal "kissing of the cross" record (krestotselovalnaia zapis) in which he pledged to respect certain boyar prerogatives and judicial norms; an effort to project stability through the exhumation of Dmitry's remains at Uglich and their ceremonial transfer to Moscow's Archangel Cathedral, accompanied by his promotion as a saint and intended in part to demonstrate that the historical tsarevich had truly died in 1591 and that any further claimants were impostors; and continued reliance on boyar and court networks. None of these measures resolved the underlying problem that had destabilized Boris Godunov's rule and had now destabilized his own: a tsar who had come to power through a narrow boyar acclamation rather than a broadly representative assembly, and who lacked any close blood connection to the extinct ruling line, remained exposed to rival claims and shifting elite loyalties in a political culture that placed great weight on secure, broadly accepted legitimacy, whether established through heredity, election, or some combination of the two.

Shuisky's reign, examined further in the following sections, would be consumed by the very crises his own accession had failed to resolve: a major rebellion within months of his accession, a second pretender claiming to be Dmitry within roughly a year after that, and eventually formal foreign intervention and his own deposition in 1610. His government illustrates this chapter's central argument in miniature: simply removing a discredited or fraudulent ruler did not restore stable political order, because the deeper problem — a genuine, unresolved crisis of legitimate authority — persisted beneath the surface of each successive change of ruler.

Section 25.5

Peasant and Cossack Rebellion

Within months of Shuisky's accession, a major armed movement emerged under the military leadership of Ivan Bolotnikov, advancing on Moscow in 1606 and besieging the capital before being pushed back and ultimately defeated at Tula in 1607. Older historical writing, particularly within the Soviet historiographical tradition, described this movement as a unified "peasant war," presenting Bolotnikov as a leader of oppressed serfs rising against noble exploitation in a manner that anticipated later revolutionary class struggle. Modern historians treat this framing with considerable caution. The coalition that fought under Bolotnikov's banner in fact included peasants and other dependent laborers, but also Cossacks from the southern frontier seeking greater autonomy and reward, townspeople dissatisfied with Shuisky's government, and — crucially — significant numbers of provincial service gentry and even some boyars who had their own grievances against Shuisky's narrow, boyar-dominated accession and who did not necessarily share any interest in overturning serfdom or noble privilege as such.

This socially mixed character is essential to understanding the rebellion correctly. Participants were bound together less by a single shared social program than by opposition to Shuisky's government and, in many cases, continued attachment to the idea that a legitimate Dmitry — by this point understood by different participants in different ways, some perhaps still believing the executed False Dmitry I had somehow survived, others prepared to support whatever figure might next claim that identity — represented a more legitimate alternative. The movement should therefore be understood as an episode of broad civil conflict mobilizing multiple, sometimes contradictory, social interests under a loosely shared banner of opposition to the sitting government, rather than as a coherent uprising with a unified social objective.

The rebellion's defeat at Tula in 1607, following a prolonged siege in which Shuisky's forces dammed a nearby river to flood the rebel stronghold, ended Bolotnikov's movement as an organized military force, and Bolotnikov himself was executed. But the defeat did not resolve the underlying instability that had produced the rebellion, nor did it exhaust the pool of discontented Cossacks, servicemen, and others prepared to support a new claimant to legitimate authority. Within the same year, a second pretender claiming Dmitry's identity had already begun to attract exactly this kind of support, a development examined in the next section.

Section 25.6

False Dmitry II

By 1607, a second individual claiming to be the surviving Tsarevich Dmitry had appeared, his precise origins even less clearly established in the sources than those of his predecessor. This second pretender attracted support from remnants of the defeated Bolotnikov coalition, additional Cossack forces, and, increasingly, Polish-Lithuanian nobles and mercenary bands operating with less restraint than the earlier private patrons of False Dmitry I, since the Commonwealth's own internal politics at this moment provided limited check on freelance military adventurism across the border. By 1608, False Dmitry II had established a substantial rival court and military encampment at Tushino, a village near Moscow, from which he issued appointments, maintained his own boyar council, and for a period commanded the loyalty of a significant share of Muscovy's elite and military resources.

The Tushino period, lasting from roughly 1608 to 1610, produced a situation without real precedent in earlier Muscovite history: two rival courts, two competing patronage networks distributing land and office, and — after Filaret Romanov, then Metropolitan of Rostov and father of the future tsar Mikhail Romanov, was brought to Tushino and installed there as a rival patriarch while Patriarch Hermogenes remained in Moscow as the recognized head of the Church — even two competing ecclesiastical establishments, operating simultaneously within a single political space. Members of the boyar elite moved between Moscow and Tushino, sometimes more than once, seeking whichever patron seemed likelier to prevail or offered better terms, a pattern of shifting allegiance that later moralizing accounts condemned as treachery but that reflected, more fundamentally, the genuine absence of any single center of authority capable of commanding consistent loyalty.

Marina Mniszech, the Polish noblewoman who had married False Dmitry I, became associated with False Dmitry II as well, in a development that illustrates how thoroughly personal, dynastic, and factional interests had become entangled with the pretender phenomenon by this stage of the crisis. The Tushino camp's dissolution in 1610, discussed further in the following section, did not result from any single military defeat so much as from the camp's own internal fragmentation and the arrival of more decisive Polish-Lithuanian royal intervention, which offered some of Tushino's boyar supporters an alternative path to legitimacy through the candidacy of Prince Władysław rather than through continued attachment to an increasingly discredited pretender. False Dmitry II himself withdrew from Tushino to Kaluga as his camp collapsed, and he was killed there in December 1610, removing the second pretender from the unfolding crisis even as the deeper questions of legitimate authority he had exploited remained unresolved.

The Tushino episode offers this chapter's clearest evidence that, by 1608–1610, Muscovy was experiencing not merely rebellion against a single government but a genuine fragmentation of sovereignty, in which more than one center simultaneously claimed to exercise legitimate rule over the same territory and population.

Section 25.7

Polish-Lithuanian Intervention

This section requires careful political geography, since Polish-Lithuanian involvement in the Time of Troubles changed character substantially between 1604 and 1610 and should not be narrated as a single, continuous foreign invasion. The earliest involvement, examined in Section 25.3, came from private magnates and adventurers who backed False Dmitry I on their own initiative and for their own political and financial reasons, without formal sanction from the Polish-Lithuanian crown. Similarly, much of the military support that flowed to the Tushino camp examined in Section 25.6 came from independent mercenary bands and noble retinues rather than from state policy. The Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth itself was not a unified actor with a single foreign policy toward Muscovy during these years; its king, its senate, and its powerful noble magnates often pursued distinct and sometimes conflicting courses of action.

Formal royal intervention began only in 1609, when King Sigismund III, calculating that Muscovy's internal collapse offered a genuine opportunity for territorial and dynastic gain, led Commonwealth forces to besiege the strategically vital fortress city of Smolensk. This siege, which would last into 1611, marked the clear transition from informal, private participation in Muscovite factional politics to open state-directed intervention. Vasily Shuisky, responding to this new threat, sought alliance with Sweden, whose forces assisted Shuisky's government against both the Tushino camp and, subsequently, against the advancing Commonwealth forces, though this Swedish alliance carried its own costs and complications, including territorial concessions and, eventually, Swedish designs on Muscovite territory in their own right. The decisive blow to Shuisky's position came in July 1610, when a Commonwealth army under Hetman Stanisław Żółkiewski destroyed the combined Russo-Swedish relief force at the Battle of Klushino, commanded by Shuisky's brother Dmitry Shuisky. The defeat eliminated Shuisky's last major field army, exposed Moscow directly to Commonwealth advance, and discredited his government beyond recovery.

The military and political crisis surrounding Smolensk and the collapse of Shuisky's authority culminated in July 1610, when a boyar faction deposed Shuisky and forced him into monastic vows, establishing a transitional government of seven boyars (semiboyarshchina) that proved unable to govern effectively on its own. Facing continued military pressure and the absence of any broadly accepted domestic candidate for the throne, this boyar government negotiated with Commonwealth representatives to offer the throne to Sigismund's son, Prince Władysław, on conditions that were intended to include his conversion to Orthodoxy and respect for Muscovite law and institutions. Commonwealth forces subsequently occupied the Moscow Kremlin itself, and for a period in 1610–1611, foreign troops garrisoned the symbolic and administrative heart of the Muscovite state while the negotiated conditions for Władysław's actual accession remained unresolved, since Sigismund proved unwilling to allow his son to convert or to accept the full terms Muscovite negotiators sought.

Sweden, meanwhile, pursued its own advantage amid the crisis, and Swedish forces occupied Novgorod in 1611, raising for a time the possibility that northern Muscovite territory might be permanently detached under Swedish control or a separately negotiated arrangement. Students should resist describing this entire sequence simply as "Poland invading Russia": Muscovite boyars themselves invited Commonwealth intervention and negotiated the terms of Władysław's candidacy, Swedish involvement began as an alliance requested by Shuisky's own government, and the Commonwealth's internal factions were themselves divided over how far to pursue Muscovite ambitions. The underlying political question throughout remained the one this chapter has traced from its opening section: who possessed legitimate authority to rule Muscovy, and on what terms could that authority be reconstituted?

Section 25.8

The National Militias

The occupation of the Moscow Kremlin by a foreign garrison, combined with the absence of any functioning central Muscovite government, provoked a response that this chapter's outline calls the National Militias, using a label that later nationalist historical tradition applied to these coalitions and that should be explained rather than treated as a neutral, self-evident description. The underlying reality was a series of broadly based, regionally organized coalitions of towns, provincial service gentry, clergy, and Cossack forces that attempted, with varying success, to reconstitute military and political authority from outside the paralyzed center.

A First Militia formed in 1611 under a leadership triumvirate representing three distinct constituencies: Prokopii Lyapunov, commanding provincial service gentry from the Ryazan region; Ivan Zarutsky, leading Don Cossack forces; and Prince Dmitry Trubetskoy, bringing a contingent originally associated with the former Tushino camp based around Kaluga. This coalition achieved some initial success in besieging the Polish-Lithuanian garrison in Moscow, but the very diversity of its constituent parts proved its undoing: deep mutual suspicion between Lyapunov's gentry servicemen, who sought the return of fugitive peasants and firmer social discipline, and Zarutsky's Cossacks, who resisted exactly such discipline and valued their frontier autonomy, erupted into violence when Cossack forces killed Lyapunov in the summer of 1611. His death effectively dissolved the coalition's gentry component and left the First Militia unable to dislodge the occupying garrison or establish a stable governing alternative.

A more durable coalition, the Second Militia, began forming later in 1611, organized from the merchant and administrative town of Nizhny Novgorod under the practical leadership of Kuzma Minin, a local merchant and elected town official who organized financial contributions and provisioning, and the military command of Prince Dmitry Pozharsky, a nobleman with prior military experience against both rebel and foreign forces. This coalition deliberately sought a broader and more disciplined base of support than the fractured First Militia had achieved, drawing on funding and manpower from numerous towns across northern and central Muscovy, on Orthodox Church institutions that lent both material and moral support to the cause of liberating Moscow from foreign occupation, and, eventually, on Cossack forces as well, integrated in ways intended to avoid the internal conflicts that had destroyed the First Militia.

The Second Militia advanced on Moscow through 1612 and, after prolonged fighting, secured the surrender of the Polish-Lithuanian garrison holding the Kremlin in October and November of that year. This achievement is genuinely significant, but students should resist the later nationalist framing that presents it simply as a unified Russian people rising to expel a foreign invader. The coalition succeeded because it managed to combine, more effectively than its predecessor, the interests and resources of towns, provincial servicemen, church institutions, and Cossacks — the same range of social and political actors whose competing loyalties had driven much of the preceding crisis. Its success demonstrates a central lesson of this chapter and of Chapter 22's earlier discussion of Muscovite institutions: political authority in Muscovy had never rested solely in the person of the tsar, and when the center failed entirely, the capacity to reconstitute authority still existed in the network of towns, service communities, and religious institutions that had continued functioning throughout the crisis.

Section 25.9

The Election of Mikhail Romanov

With Moscow liberated from foreign occupation but no agreed successor to the throne, the leaders of the Second Militia and other assembled authorities convened a large Assembly of the Land in early 1613, drawing representatives from a wide range of towns, social estates, and Cossack forces to resolve the question that had remained unsettled since Shuisky's deposition three years earlier. Several candidates were considered, including members of prominent boyar families and, briefly, foreign candidates whose recent association with occupation or intervention left them politically unacceptable to most of the assembly's participants. In February 1613, the assembly selected Mikhail Romanov, a sixteen-year-old member of the Romanov family, as the new tsar.

Mikhail's selection reflected several converging considerations rather than any single decisive factor. His family's close dynastic connection to the old ruling house through his great-aunt Anastasia Romanovna, Ivan IV's first wife, strengthened his acceptability even though Mikhail was not a direct descendant of the extinct Moscow ruling line. His youth and relative political inexperience made him acceptable to boyar factions who may have calculated that a young and untested tsar would prove more manageable than an established rival with independent power and ambitions of his own. His father, Filaret Romanov, though at this moment held in Polish-Lithuanian captivity following his earlier role at the Tushino camp examined in Section 25.6, commanded substantial respect within Muscovite clerical and boyar circles and would, upon his eventual release, become a powerful figure in his own right during the early years of his son's reign, a development taken up in Chapter 26. Cossack forces present at the assembly appear to have played a significant role in pressing for Mikhail's selection, seeing in the Romanov connection a candidate untainted by association with either the discredited Shuisky government or the various foreign candidacies.

Students should be cautious about interpreting 1613 as a simple restoration of hereditary principle over the disorder of election. Recent scholarship on Muscovite succession has emphasized that election and heredity were not necessarily understood as opposed principles in this period; rather, an assembly's election could work together with a claim of hereditary connection to establish a new line's legitimacy, much as Boris Godunov's own election in 1598 had drawn on his connections to the preceding dynasty through his sister's marriage to Fyodor I. Mikhail Romanov's dynasty began, in other words, through the same broad electoral principle used in 1598, though under radically different political circumstances; what differed was the scale, breadth, and broad determination to achieve a durable settlement that characterized the 1613 assembly after years of catastrophic fragmentation.

It is essential to state plainly what the 1613 election did and did not accomplish. It resolved the question of who would occupy the throne, ending the succession of rival claimants, occupying garrisons, and competing courts that had characterized the preceding decade. It did not, by itself, restore Muscovy's fiscal system, rebuild its military establishment, repopulate its devastated countryside, resolve its territorial losses to Sweden and the Commonwealth, or reconstruct effective local government across a realm that had experienced years of civil war, foreign occupation, and social upheaval. That work of reconstruction, which occupied Mikhail's government and his father Filaret for years after 1613, is the subject of Chapter 26.

Historical Vocabulary
Smuta (Time of Troubles)

Smuta, conventionally translated as the "Time of Troubles," is the term Russian historical tradition applies to the period examined in this chapter, roughly 1598 to 1613, though some accounts extend its beginning as early as Ivan IV's death in 1584 and others narrow it to the acute crisis years after 1603. The term itself carries an interpretive weight worth noting: it was popularized by writers and officials in the years and decades after 1613, who had strong interests in presenting the preceding period as a dangerous, disordered departure from legitimate rule that the new Romanov dynasty had heroically resolved.

Historians today generally retain the conventional term for practical purposes while remaining attentive to the way it can obscure the period's genuine complexity — treating what was, in fact, a combination of famine, dynastic crisis, social rebellion, and international war as a single undifferentiated episode of "trouble" and "disorder." This chapter's Historical Debate feature examines competing scholarly framings of the period in more analytical detail.

Primary Source Window

The Certified Charter Confirming the Election of Mikhail Fedorovich Romanov as Tsar (1613)

Creator: Chancery scribes of the new Muscovite government, acting on behalf of the Assembly of the Land · Date: 1613 · Type: Official state charter (gramota), a formal legitimating record of the assembly's proceedings

Context: This certified charter was produced by the new government shortly after the 1613 assembly to record and formally proclaim Mikhail Romanov's election. It describes the gathering of representatives, the case made for Mikhail's selection, and includes the text of the prepared speech carried by the delegation sent to Mikhail and his mother at the Ipatiev Monastery near Kostroma to formally offer him the throne. As an official charter meant to establish the new dynasty's legitimacy for a Muscovite and international audience, it is a document of state self-presentation rather than a neutral transcript of the assembly's actual deliberations.

Paraphrase of the charter's account (not a verbatim translated quotation):

The charter describes representatives gathered from across the Muscovite land as recognizing Mikhail Romanov's close kinship with the former ruling house and his family's suffering during the recent troubles as grounds for his selection, and it records the delegation's prepared address urging Mikhail and his mother to accept the throne for the sake of ending the realm's continued disorder and restoring proper government under God's favor.

Analysis: The charter is valuable precisely because of what it reveals about how the new government wished the election to be understood and remembered: it emphasizes kinship, divine sanction, and the will of the whole land, while giving far less attention to the extended negotiation among rival candidates, the sharp factional disagreements among boyars, and the significant pressure exerted by Cossack forces present at the assembly. Historians therefore treat the charter as strong evidence for the outcome and for the new dynasty's official legitimating narrative, while relying on other administrative records and comparative analysis of Muscovite assembly practice to reconstruct the more contested process that lay behind the document's smooth official account.

Questions for students:

  1. Who produced this charter, and for what audience and purpose?
  2. What does the charter emphasize about Mikhail's qualifications, and what does it leave out or minimize?
  3. Why might an official charter be a poor guide to how contested the actual assembly proceedings were?
  4. What other kinds of evidence would help historians reconstruct the negotiations behind this charter's polished official narrative?
Historical Debate

What Kind of Crisis Was the Time of Troubles?

The question: Should the events of roughly 1598–1613 be understood primarily as a dynastic succession crisis, a famine-driven social collapse, a civil war among domestic factions, or a war of foreign intervention? Historians have periodized and characterized the Troubles differently, with real consequences for how the period is explained.

The dynastic-crisis interpretation: Some historians emphasize the extinction of the Rurikid line and the resulting absence of an agreed mechanism for succession as the taproot cause, viewing famine, rebellion, and foreign intervention as consequences that could not have occurred, or would have occurred in far milder form, had a secure hereditary succession remained in place.
The peasant-war interpretation: An older historiographical tradition, especially prominent in Soviet-era scholarship, emphasized the Bolotnikov rebellion and related unrest as evidence of underlying class conflict between an oppressed peasantry and an exploitative landholding elite, treating the dynastic and foreign-policy dimensions of the crisis as a backdrop to more fundamental social struggle.
The civil-war interpretation: Many recent historians argue that no single social or dynastic explanation adequately captures a conflict in which peasants, Cossacks, townspeople, service gentry, and boyars all appear on multiple, shifting, and sometimes opposing sides at different moments, and instead describe the period as a broad civil war over legitimate authority, in which social grievance, regional interest, and personal calculation combined in complex, historically variable coalitions.

Evidence: Historians weigh chronicle and commemorative narrative sources, surviving administrative and land records, comparative study of participant social composition in major episodes such as the Bolotnikov rebellion, and analysis of how neighboring states' interventions were invited, negotiated, or resisted by different Muscovite factions.

Points of agreement: Most historians agree that no single cause is sufficient by itself, and that the crisis involved genuine fragmentation of central authority rather than merely a difficult but continuous government.

Points of disagreement: Historians differ over which factor — dynastic collapse, famine, social grievance, elite competition, or foreign intervention — deserves greatest causal weight, and over how far later nationalist and Soviet-era framings distorted understanding of participants' actual motives.

Student task: Using evidence from Sections 25.1 through 25.9, construct an argument for which single-cause framing is least adequate to the evidence, and explain what a multi-causal account gains that the single-cause framings lose.

Multiple Perspectives

Living Through the Fragmentation of Authority

A peasant household on a landlord's estate, 1601–1607: For a peasant family, the Troubles meant successive years of failed harvests, the collapse of ordinary landlord obligations amid famine, and the appeal, however temporary, of movements like Bolotnikov's that promised relief from noble authority and legal restriction, even as continued warfare and requisitioning brought further hardship regardless of which claimant currently held power.
A Cossack of the southern frontier: For a Cossack fighter, the crisis offered opportunities for reward, plunder, and greater autonomy through service to whichever pretender or militia seemed likeliest to prevail, while also exposing Cossack communities to shifting alliances, internal factional conflict such as that which destroyed the First Militia, and the risk of being labeled bandits or traitors by whichever government eventually triumphed.
A provincial service gentryman, holding a pomestie estate: For a member of the service gentry, the Troubles threatened the fragile basis of pomestie landholding examined in Chapter 22, since prolonged disorder disrupted labor supply, tax collection, and military organization alike; many such servicemen shifted allegiance repeatedly among Shuisky, Tushino, and eventually the National Militias in search of whichever authority could best protect their status and property.
A Moscow boyar navigating factional politics: For a boyar, survival and advancement required careful calculation among rival courts, since attachment to a losing claimant could mean loss of office, property, or life, while premature abandonment of a current patron risked accusations of treachery; the repeated movement of boyars between Moscow and Tushino reflects this constant recalculation rather than simple opportunism alone.

These perspectives illustrate why the Troubles cannot be reduced to a single social conflict: each group pursued distinct, sometimes overlapping interests, and the shifting coalitions this chapter has traced reflect the genuine absence of any single authority capable of commanding universal loyalty.

Cause and Effect

From Famine to Civil War

Long-Term Conditions

The extinction of the direct Rurikid line, the unresolved Uglich affair, fiscal strain from Ivan IV's later reign and the oprichnina, and tightening peasant-mobility restrictions, all examined in Chapter 24.

Intermediate Developments

The extinction of the direct ruling line and Boris Godunov's election amid unresolved elite competition, leaving his government exposed to boyar rivalry and popular suspicion despite several years of competent rule.

Short-Term Triggers

The volcanically driven climate shock and harvest failures of 1601–1603, producing the Great Famine, and the emergence in 1604 of a claimant to Dmitry's identity able to exploit both the famine's political fallout and the unresolved Uglich story.

Effects

Successive changes of ruler without restored stability; broad-based rebellion; a second pretender and a period of dual government at Tushino; formal foreign intervention and occupation; and, eventually, the improvised reconstruction of authority through the National Militias and the 1613 election.

Historical complexity: No single link in this chain was sufficient by itself to produce the Troubles; famine without dynastic vulnerability might have produced hardship without political collapse, while dynastic vulnerability without famine might have produced factional tension without mass mobilization.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Boris to Mikhail

What Stayed the Same

  • The zemsky sobor remained the mechanism through which Muscovy attempted to establish legitimate rule, used for both Boris's election in 1598 and Mikhail's in 1613.
  • Boyar factional competition persisted as a constant feature underlying every successive government of the period.
  • The pomestie service-landholding system, and the tensions it generated with peasant mobility, continued to shape social conflict throughout the crisis.

What Changed

  • Political authority moved from a single, if contested, central government under Boris to genuinely fragmented, competing centers of sovereignty during the Tushino and occupation periods.
  • Foreign powers moved from peripheral, private involvement to formal, state-directed intervention and occupation.
  • The base from which political authority could be reconstituted shifted, by 1611–1612, from the traditional court and boyar elite toward a broader coalition of towns, provincial gentry, clergy, and Cossacks.

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both continuity and change equips students to see 1613 not as a simple return to a prior status quo but as the establishment of a new, still-fragile settlement built on some old institutions and some newly demonstrated capacities.

Everyday Life and Social History

Famine and Flight, 1601–1603

For most Muscovites, the Time of Troubles began not with pretenders or foreign armies but with hunger. Ordinary rural households facing repeated harvest failure resorted to substitute foods, sold or pawned what possessions they had, and in growing numbers abandoned their villages in search of relief, work, or charity in towns and at monasteries, which served as significant, though insufficient, centers of famine relief. Urban households faced their own pressures as displaced rural populations swelled town populations, driving up demand for scarce food and straining municipal and ecclesiastical charitable capacity beyond its limits.

Some landlords, unable to feed dependents during the worst famine years, released serfs and household slaves from their obligations, a practice that added further to the numbers of uprooted people moving through the countryside without secure attachment to land or master. This displacement had lasting social consequences beyond the famine years themselves: many of the same uprooted, mobile populations that the famine had produced later provided recruits for the rebellions, pretender armies, and militias examined throughout this chapter, illustrating how a subsistence crisis could directly generate the human raw material for sustained civil conflict.

Regional Context

The Southern Frontier

Muscovy's southern frontier, examined in Chapter 24 as a zone of Crimean Tatar raiding and early fortified-line construction, played an outsized role in the Time of Troubles. This region combined several conditions especially favorable to unrest: a population of Cossacks accustomed to armed autonomy and only loosely integrated into central Muscovite administration, service gentry whose landholding and status often depended precariously on continued state support, and a frontier economy already stressed by military obligation and periodic raiding before the famine ever struck. When the Great Famine and the appearance of pretender claimants coincided with these existing frontier grievances, the region became, in effect, a reservoir of manpower and disaffection from which successive rebellions and pretender armies could draw.

This pattern would recur, in different form, throughout later Russian history: frontier regions with weaker central administrative penetration and populations accustomed to greater autonomy repeatedly served as sources of rebellion and unrest, a pattern examined again in Chapter 30's treatment of Stenka Razin's rebellion and Chapter 35's treatment of Pugachev, both originating in similar southern and southeastern frontier contexts.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the Time of Troubles, the period of roughly 1598 to 1613 in which the vulnerabilities Chapter 24 identified in late sixteenth-century Muscovy combined with natural disaster, competing dynastic claims, social rebellion, and foreign intervention to produce a genuine collapse and eventual reconstruction of central political authority.

The chapter began with Boris Godunov's election in 1598, an outcome that resolved the immediate succession crisis following the extinction of the direct Rurikid line but left the new tsar's legitimacy established by election amid the unprecedented extinction of the direct Moscow ruling line and continued elite competition. It then examined the Great Famine of 1601–1603, an environmental catastrophe, linked by modern climatology to the 1600 Huaynaputina eruption, whose severity converted Boris's latent political vulnerability into acute crisis by eroding confidence in his government and generating a large, uprooted population receptive to alternative political claims.

The chapter traced the rise and fall of False Dmitry I, whose claim to be the surviving Tsarevich Dmitry exploited both the unresolved Uglich affair and the desperation the famine had produced, and Vasily Shuisky's contested accession, which demonstrated that removing one discredited claimant did not restore underlying stability. It examined the Bolotnikov rebellion of 1606–1607 as a socially mixed civil conflict rather than a unified peasant war, and the emergence of False Dmitry II and the Tushino camp as evidence of genuine fragmentation of sovereignty, with rival courts, patronage networks, and even rival ecclesiastical establishments operating simultaneously.

The chapter distinguished carefully between informal Polish-Lithuanian participation in pretender movements before 1609 and the formal intervention of King Sigismund III thereafter, including the siege of Smolensk, Shuisky's deposition, the occupation of the Moscow Kremlin, and the negotiated but ultimately unrealized offer of the throne to Prince Władysław, alongside Swedish intervention and the occupation of Novgorod. It examined the First and Second National Militias as evidence that political authority in Muscovy could be reconstituted from towns, provincial servicemen, clergy, and Cossacks even after the central monarchy had effectively ceased to function, with the Second Militia's success under Minin and Pozharsky liberating Moscow in 1612.

Finally, the chapter examined the 1613 Assembly of the Land's election of Mikhail Romanov, explaining this outcome through converging considerations of dynastic connection, political acceptability, and broad coalition support rather than as a simple restoration of hereditary principle over disordered election, since the new dynasty itself began through the same broad electoral principle that had elevated Boris Godunov fifteen years earlier.

Throughout, the chapter emphasized that the Time of Troubles should not be reduced to any single causal or interpretive frame — dynastic crisis, famine, peasant war, civil war, or foreign invasion — but understood as the interaction of all of these factors, a conclusion reinforced by the chapter's Historical Debate feature on competing historiographical interpretations.

The chapter closed by emphasizing that 1613 resolved the question of who would rule without resolving the practical work of reconstructing Muscovy's fiscal system, military, local government, and international position — the subject to which Chapter 26 now turns.

These lessons—attention to interacting rather than single causes, recognition of genuine fragmentation of sovereignty, critical reading of retrospective sources, and appreciation for sources of political authority beyond the ruler—prepare students directly for Chapter 26's account of early Romanov state-building and recur in modified form in this textbook's later treatment of 1917 and other crises of legitimate authority.

Bolotnikov rebellion
The 1606–1607 uprising against Vasily Shuisky, drawing a socially mixed coalition of peasants, Cossacks, townspeople, and servicemen.
Krestotselovalnaia zapis
Vasily Shuisky's formal "kissing of the cross" pledge to respect certain boyar prerogatives upon his accession.
Commonwealth intervention
The formal state-directed involvement of the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth under Sigismund III beginning in 1609, distinguished from earlier private participation.
Siege of Smolensk
The Commonwealth siege of this strategically vital fortress city, begun in 1609 and concluded with its capture in 1611.
National Militia (opolchenie)
Broadly based armed coalitions of towns, gentry, clergy, and Cossacks organized in 1611 and 1611–1612 to reconstitute authority and expel occupying forces.
Kuzma Minin
Nizhny Novgorod merchant and town official who organized funding and provisioning for the Second Militia.
Prince Dmitry Pozharsky
Military commander of the Second Militia that liberated Moscow in 1612.
Interregnum
A period without a settled, universally recognized ruler, applicable to several phases of the Troubles, especially 1610–1613.
Election vs. heredity
The historiographical question of how Muscovite political culture combined elective and hereditary principles of legitimate rule, examined in Section 25.9.
Dual power / dual government
The condition, most visible during the Tushino period, of two simultaneously functioning courts and patronage networks claiming legitimate authority.
Assembly of 1613
The zemsky sobor that elected Mikhail Romanov, formally concluding the acute phase of the Troubles.

This project asks you to investigate one claim to legitimate authority examined in this chapter — Boris Godunov's election, False Dmitry I's or False Dmitry II's claim to be Tsarevich Dmitry, Vasily Shuisky's accession, or Mikhail Romanov's election — and evaluate the evidence and arguments used to support or challenge it.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has carried students through Muscovy's first sustained experience of genuine political collapse: a crisis that began with an elected tsar's uncertain legitimacy, was ignited by a natural disaster no government could have fully prevented, and unfolded through repeated cycles of pretender politics, social rebellion, dual government, and foreign intervention before a broad coalition of towns, gentry, clergy, and Cossacks reconstituted central authority in 1613. The Troubles were not a simple story of foreign conquest resisted by a unified nation, nor a straightforward peasant uprising, nor a tidy succession dispute resolved by the restoration of proper order; they were a genuinely contingent, multi-causal crisis whose resolution depended as much on exhaustion, improvisation, and coalition-building as on any inevitable return to legitimate hereditary rule.

The chapter's central methodological lesson — that periods of political fragmentation require multi-causal explanation and careful, skeptical reading of sources produced after the fact by the eventual victors — will recur throughout this textbook, particularly in Part IX's treatment of the collapse of imperial authority in 1917 and the civil war that followed, and again in Part XI's treatment of the Soviet collapse in 1991.

As the textbook proceeds to Chapter 26's account of how Mikhail Romanov's government and his father Filaret actually rebuilt Muscovy's fiscal system, military, and local administration after 1613, students should carry forward this chapter's recognition that electing a new ruler, however broadly accepted, is only the first step in reconstructing a state that a decade and a half of famine, civil war, and occupation had brought to the edge of collapse.
