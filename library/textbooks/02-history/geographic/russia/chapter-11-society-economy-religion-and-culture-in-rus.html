Part II — The Formation of Rus'
11

Society, Economy, Religion, and Culture in Rus'

A History of Russia: From the Eurasian World to the Russian Federation

Chapter Opening

A strip of birch bark, scratched with a stylus in Novgorod around the turn of the twelfth century, records a dispute over an enslaved woman purchased in Pskov who, it was alleged, had been stolen. A princess, a prince's retainer, and a hearing before princely authority all figure in the few surviving lines. No chronicle preserves this dispute. It survives instead because the letter itself was discarded into the wet, oxygen-poor soil of Novgorod, where it lay for roughly eight centuries until twentieth-century archaeologists thought to dig there.

Chapters 7 through 10 followed the princes of Rus' — Rurik and his successors, Oleg and Igor, Olga and Sviatoslav, Vladimir and Yaroslav — as they built tribute networks, sponsored the Christianization of Rus', developed princely law, and negotiated marriages with the royal houses of Europe. That narrative, built substantially from the Primary Chronicle and other texts composed by and for a narrow circle of princely, clerical, and boyar readers, necessarily kept its attention on a small number of powerful men and, occasionally, women. It is an indispensable narrative, but it is not the history of Rus' as a society. The overwhelming majority of the people who lived within the loosely bounded world the chronicles call Rus' — farmers in scattered forest clearings, artisans in the workshops of Kyiv and Novgorod, merchants moving goods along the Dnieper and the Volkhov, enslaved captives taken in raids, women managing households whose names never entered a single surviving document — left no chronicle entries of their own. Reconstructing their lives requires a different combination of evidence and a different set of questions.

This chapter turns from princes to people. It asks how Rus' society was organized: who commanded labor and loyalty, who owed service or tribute, who could be bought and sold, and who stood in between. It asks how people made a living, from the grain fields and forest clearings of the countryside to the workshops and marketplaces of towns strung along the river routes described in Chapters 6 and 7. It asks how ruler-sponsored Christianization, conventionally associated with Vladimir's conversion around 988 as described in Chapter 9, took root unevenly over subsequent generations without simply erasing older practices, in households, villages, and urban parishes. And it asks what evidence beyond the chronicles can tell historians about people who rarely wrote and were rarely written about.

The evidentiary shift this chapter requires is itself an important historical lesson. Where Chapters 7 through 10 relied heavily on the Primary Chronicle, princely treaties, and law codes such as the Russkaya Pravda examined in Chapter 10, this chapter must draw more heavily on archaeology, material culture, and one especially valuable body of evidence largely confined to a single city: the birch-bark documents of Novgorod, practical everyday writings on strips of tree bark that begin appearing in the archaeological record around the 1030s and that offer, in fragments, the voices of merchants, landholders, quarreling neighbors, and anxious parents in a way no chronicle ever does. These sources do not replace the chronicle; they supplement it, correct its silences in places, and remind students that a source produced by and for princes and clergy cannot, by itself, describe an entire society.

Several cautions govern this chapter throughout. Social categories in Rus' were not identical to the categories of later Muscovite or imperial Russian society, and readers should resist projecting terms such as "serf," "nobility," or "middle class" backward onto a medieval East Slavic world that had its own distinct vocabulary and its own distinct institutions. Christianization, examined in Chapter 9 as a political and diplomatic event, appears here as a slow and uneven cultural process that had not erased older practices by the time this chapter's coverage ends. Slavery and various forms of legal dependency existed in Rus', and they must be named plainly and distinguished carefully from one another and from the very different institution of serfdom that would develop in Russia many centuries later. And the very unevenness of the surviving evidence — rich for Novgorod, thinner elsewhere; rich for elite women, thin for ordinary ones; rich for princely law, thin for village custom — is itself a fact historians must reckon with rather than paper over.

By the chapter's end, students should be able to describe the major social groups of Rus' society and the relationships of obligation, service, and dependency that bound them together; explain how rural and urban economies functioned and connected to long-distance trade; describe the place of women within family, household, and society; explain how Orthodox Christianity spread and mixed with earlier practices; and evaluate the kinds of evidence — legal, archaeological, epigraphic, and documentary — historians must combine to reconstruct a society whose ordinary members rarely speak for themselves in the surviving chronicle record.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Describe the social organization of princes, boyars, and retainers (the druzhina) and their relationships of service and patronage.
  2. Explain the social and economic life of townspeople, merchants, and artisans in the major urban centers of Rus'.
  3. Reconstruct the rural world of peasant households and village communities without applying the later and distinct category of serfdom.
  4. Distinguish among the several legal categories of slavery and dependent labor attested in Rus', including kholopy, zakupy, and riadovichi.
  5. Analyze the position of women within family, household, inheritance, and public life, distinguishing prescriptive church norms from evidence of actual practice.
  6. Explain the regional diversity of agricultural and rural production across the forest, forest-steppe, and river-valley environments of Rus'.
  7. Evaluate the relationship between local agricultural production and long-distance trade as complementary, not competing, foundations of the Rus' economy.
  8. Trace the uneven spread of Orthodox Christianity into everyday religious practice and its interaction with earlier beliefs.
  9. Interpret a range of cultural and material evidence — manuscripts, icons, birch-bark letters, archaeological artifacts — as sources for the culture of Rus'.
  10. Critique the practice of projecting later Muscovite, imperial, or modern social categories backward onto medieval Rus'.
  11. Assess the strengths and limitations of different kinds of historical evidence for reconstructing the lives of people who left few or no written records of their own.
  12. Connect the social order examined in this chapter to the political fragmentation examined in Chapter 12.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How was social status in Rus' defined, and how did it differ from later Russian estate categories?
  2. What obligations bound peasants, townspeople, and dependent laborers to princes, boyars, and other elites?
  3. How should historians distinguish slavery from other forms of dependent labor in Rus', and why does the distinction matter?
  4. What can birch-bark letters reveal about literacy and everyday communication that the chronicles cannot?
  5. How did agricultural production in different ecological zones connect to the long-distance trade networks examined in earlier chapters?
  6. In what ways did the position of women in Rus' combine constraint with real, if limited, economic and social agency?
  7. How completely, and how quickly, did Christianity replace earlier religious practices after 988?
  8. Why must historians combine multiple kinds of evidence — legal, archaeological, documentary, material — to reconstruct a society whose ordinary members are largely absent from chronicle narratives?

Historical Context

Chapters 7 through 10 traced the political consolidation of Rus' from the ninth through the eleventh centuries: the emergence of a Rurikid princely line based first at Novgorod and then at Kyiv, the extension of tribute collection across a wide territory inhabited by diverse Slavic, Finnic, and Baltic populations, the conversion of Prince Vladimir and the subsequent Christianization of the ruling elite and, over time, a growing share of the population, and the codification of law and consolidation of princely authority under Yaroslav the Wise. That narrative, however, described a princely political order built primarily through the actions of a small ruling dynasty and its retinue. It said comparatively little about how the great majority of the population — cultivators, herders, artisans, and merchants who never appear by name in the chronicle — organized their households, earned their livelihoods, or understood their religious obligations.

Rus' by the mid-eleventh century encompassed an enormous and ecologically varied territory, from the dense northern forests around Novgorod and Ladoga, through the mixed forest-steppe of the middle Dnieper basin around Kyiv and Chernigov, to the more open, drier lands bordering the steppe frontier examined in Chapter 4. This environmental diversity, introduced in Chapter 2's discussion of forest, forest-steppe, and steppe zones, produced real regional variation in how people farmed, what they traded, and how they organized settlement — variation this chapter treats as a central fact rather than a complicating detail.

The evidence available for reconstructing this society is markedly uneven. The Primary Chronicle and other narrative sources, composed within monastic and princely circles, focus overwhelmingly on rulers, wars, and the church hierarchy, and they say little about ordinary rural or urban life except where it intersected with princely concerns. The Russkaya Pravda, examined in Chapter 10 as a layered legal text whose earliest core is traditionally associated with Yaroslav's reign and to which later rulers added further provisions, offers far richer evidence for social categories and economic life, since law codes must specify penalties and procedures applicable to slaves, dependents, freemen, and elites alike; but law describes formal categories and prescribed norms, which do not always match lived practice. Archaeological excavation — of rural settlements, urban neighborhoods, cemeteries, and, in Novgorod's unusual waterlogged soil, of everyday objects including wood, leather, and birch bark that would have decayed elsewhere — supplies a third body of evidence that can confirm, complicate, or contradict both the chronicle and the law code. This chapter draws on all three kinds of evidence together, while remaining candid about what each can and cannot show.

Section 11.1

Princes, Boyars, and Retainers

Chapters 7 through 10 traced the careers of individual Rurikid princes; this section examines the broader social world of princely rule, in which a prince was never an isolated ruler but the head of a household, a military retinue, and a network of dependents whose cooperation his authority required. The prince's closest associates formed his druzhina, an armed retinue that combined the functions of a standing military force, a council of advisors, and an extended household. Druzhina members, sometimes distinguished in the sources as senior ("the elder druzhina") and junior retainers, received maintenance and shares of tribute and plunder; some also acquired land and administrative posts over time, but these arrangements should not be understood as a standardized fief-for-service system, since landholding among the elite developed unevenly and evidence for formalized service-land grants is uneven across regions and periods. The relationship between prince and druzhina was reciprocal rather than purely hierarchical: a prince who failed to reward his retainers adequately, or who led them into unsuccessful campaigns, risked losing their loyalty, since retainers could and did transfer service to rival princes.

From this retinue, and from other sources of local wealth and standing, emerged the social layer chroniclers and later documents call boyars. It is important not to treat "boyar" as a fixed, hereditary noble title comparable to later European or even later Muscovite nobility. In the Kyivan period, boyar status derived from a combination of service to a prince, personal wealth (often in land, but also in movable wealth, trade, and dependents), and family standing, and its precise composition varied across different principalities and over time. Boyars advised princes, sometimes through informal councils, commanded troops, held administrative responsibilities, and accumulated landed estates worked by dependent laborers examined in Sections 11.3 and 11.4. Their wealth and local influence gave them real leverage relative to a prince, particularly in a political system, discussed further in Chapter 12, in which princes moved frequently among different territories and could not always rely on deep local roots of their own.

Some historians have described the social and economic relationships among princes, boyars, and their armed and landed dependents using the vocabulary of "feudalism," borrowed from the study of medieval Western Europe. This chapter's Historical Debate feature examines that comparison directly; for now, students should note that the term remains useful only as an analytical model subject to debate, not as a settled description of a distinctively Western institution simply transplanted onto Rus'. Princely and boyar households in Rus' combined military retinue, landholding, and dependent labor in ways that invite comparison with Western feudal arrangements, but the specific legal forms — including the vassalage contracts and fief-holding characteristic of parts of Western Europe — were not identical, and applying the term uncritically risks obscuring genuine local distinctiveness.

Princely and boyar households were also large domestic establishments in their own right, encompassing family members, retainers, servants, and enslaved and dependent laborers examined later in this chapter. Wealth display, hospitality, and the maintenance of a substantial household were themselves sources of political standing, since a prince or boyar who could feed, arm, and reward a large retinue commanded more effective power than one who could not. This chapter now moves outward from these elite households into the towns, villages, and dependent populations whose labor, tribute, and service sustained them.

Section 11.2

Townspeople and Merchants

The towns of Rus' — Kyiv and Novgorod above all, but also Chernigov, Smolensk, Polotsk, and a growing number of secondary centers — were sites of dense and socially varied population quite distinct from the surrounding countryside. Archaeological excavation of these towns, especially Novgorod, reveals densely packed wooden housing, paved or log-covered streets, workshops for metalworking, leatherworking, woodworking, bone- and antler-carving, pottery, and jewelry production, and clear evidence of craft specialization, in which particular streets or neighborhoods concentrated particular trades. Urban populations should not be imagined as a modern industrial working class, nor as anything resembling a modern middle class; rather, towns contained their own internal hierarchy, from wealthy merchants and boyar households maintaining urban residences down to laborers, servants, and enslaved persons working within urban households and workshops.

Merchants formed an economically significant, though not politically unified, urban group. Rus' merchants engaged in local retail exchange within town marketplaces, regional trade connecting town and countryside, and, for the wealthiest, long-distance commerce along the river routes toward Byzantium and the Baltic examined in Chapters 6 and 7 and revisited in Section 11.7 of this chapter. Merchant wealth could translate into social standing and, in some towns, political influence, particularly in Novgorod, whose distinctive political institutions are treated at length in Chapter 14 but whose economic foundations are relevant here: Novgorod's prosperity rested heavily on its role as an intermediary in trade linking the Baltic world to the Rus' interior and, through the river network, to Byzantine and eastern markets.

The single most valuable body of evidence for urban life in Rus' comes from Novgorod's unusual preservation conditions. The city's waterlogged clay soil, low in oxygen, has preserved wood, leather, and other organic materials that decay quickly elsewhere, including well over a thousand birch-bark documents recovered by archaeologists since systematic excavation began in the twentieth century. These documents, scratched with a stylus onto the inner layer of birch bark, record everyday practical communication: business correspondence, debt records, property transactions, tax and rent notations, personal letters, legal complaints, and, in later centuries, children's writing exercises and drawings. The earliest securely dated examples appear from around the 1030s. One influential scholarly interpretation connects this emergence with the expansion of church education associated with Yaroslav the Wise, examined in Chapter 10, though this remains an interpretation rather than a directly documented cause. What the documents themselves clearly show is that literacy of this practical, functional kind extended well beyond clerical and princely circles into merchant and artisan households.

These documents must be used carefully. They are overwhelmingly a Novgorod phenomenon; similar practical writing likely occurred elsewhere in Rus', but the evidence has simply not survived in other towns' different soil conditions, and historians should not generalize Novgorod's documented literacy directly onto Kyiv, Chernigov, or the countryside without qualification. Within that limitation, however, the birch-bark corpus offers something the chronicles cannot: the voices, however fragmentary, of ordinary urban residents conducting business, managing property, quarreling with neighbors, and communicating with family members, entirely outside the concerns of princely politics or church doctrine that dominate the narrative sources.

Section 11.3

Peasants and Rural Communities

The great majority of the population of Rus' lived not in towns but in the countryside, in villages and dispersed rural settlements whose material remains — house foundations, storage pits, agricultural tools, and refuse deposits — provide most of what is known about them, since chronicle and even legal sources describe rural life only glancingly. Rural settlement patterns varied considerably by region: the forest zone around Novgorod and the upper Volga favored smaller, more scattered settlements adapted to poorer soils and forest clearance, while the forest-steppe around Kyiv and Chernigov, with generally richer soil, supported somewhat larger and more concentrated villages.

The Russkaya Pravda and chronicle sources use the term smerdy for at least part of the rural population, but historians have long debated precisely what legal and social status this term denoted. Some evidence suggests smerdy were free or semi-free cultivators who owed specific obligations, sometimes including military service, to a prince; other evidence suggests the term covered a broader or more variable population whose relationship to princely and boyar authority differed by time and place. What can be said with confidence is that smerd should not simply be translated as "serf." The institution of serfdom that would eventually bind Russian peasants legally to specific landlords and estates, examined in this textbook's later chapters on Muscovy and the Russian Empire, developed centuries after the period this chapter covers and through a distinct legal and historical process; applying the term to Kyivan-era smerdy or other rural cultivators projects a later institution backward onto a society that had not yet developed it.

Rural households in Rus' combined agricultural production, discussed further in Section 11.6, with obligations to princes, boyars, or, in some cases, the church, in the form of tribute, rent in kind, or labor service. The precise mix of obligation and independence a rural household experienced likely depended heavily on local circumstances: proximity to a princely center, the presence or absence of a resident landlord, and regional variation in how thoroughly princely and boyar authority had penetrated the countryside. Village communities also maintained their own customary practices of cooperation, including, in at least some cases, collective responsibility for certain obligations and shared use of resources such as pasture, forest, and water. Some communities likely coordinated shared resources and obligations in this way, but the surviving evidence is too limited to equate these arrangements directly with the later Russian peasant commune examined in this textbook's chapters on Muscovy and the Russian Empire.

Rural populations also retained real mobility in many circumstances. Unlike the later serf population bound to specific estates by law, most free rural cultivators in Rus' were not legally restricted from moving, though the practical difficulties of relocation — distance, the need for new land, social ties, and obligations already owed — limited how often movement actually occurred. This distinction between legal freedom of movement and the practical constraints of rural life is essential to understanding why Rus' rural society, whatever its inequalities, differed fundamentally from the legally bound serfdom of later centuries.

Section 11.4

Slavery and Dependent Labor

Rus' society included genuine slavery, and this chapter names it directly rather than softening it into euphemism. The Russkaya Pravda distinguishes several categories of dependent and enslaved persons with different legal statuses, obligations, and paths, where they existed, toward freedom. Kholopy (singular kholop) were enslaved persons, comparable in important respects to chattel slaves elsewhere in the medieval world: they could be bought, sold, and inherited as property, and the law code specifies distinct, generally lesser, compensation for injury to a kholop than for injury to a free person, reflecting their diminished legal personhood. People became kholopy through several routes: capture in war or raiding, as examined in this textbook's treatment of steppe frontier warfare and Rus' military campaigns; voluntary or family self-sale in conditions of extreme poverty; marriage to an already-enslaved person under certain conditions specified in law; and, for some categories, punishment or default on obligations.

Distinct from full slavery, the Russkaya Pravda also specifies the category of zakup, a person who had entered a state of temporary, contractual dependency, most often by borrowing money, tools, or livestock from a wealthier patron and working off the debt through labor, retaining some legal protections and rights not extended to kholopy, but also facing specific legal restrictions and the possibility of enslavement if the arrangement broke down or if the zakup attempted to flee before satisfying the debt. A further category, riadovich, derived from the word for a contract or agreement (riad), denotes a person bound by some form of formal agreement to a patron, though the precise obligations distinguishing riadovichi from zakupy remain debated among historians working from limited legal evidence. The broader term cheliad' appears in some sources for household dependents collectively, encompassing enslaved and other dependent persons attached to a given household without always specifying precise legal status.

The crucial historical distinction this section must establish is that slavery and various forms of legal dependency in Rus' are not the same institution as the later serfdom of Muscovite and imperial Russia, examined in this textbook's later chapters. Slavery, in various forms, was an old institution across much of medieval Eurasia, including Rus', and it must not be equated with the distinct legal system of serfdom that bound peasants to specific landed estates and developed through a separate historical process beginning centuries later. At the same time, the two institutions were not wholly unconnected: the later systems of unfree and dependent labor that developed in Muscovy emerged within a historical environment in which slavery, and the legal habits of thought surrounding it, had long been familiar, even though serfdom itself was a distinct later creation rather than a simple continuation of Kyivan-era slavery. Conflating the two obscures both the specific character of slavery in Rus' — including the possibility, for kholopy and especially their descendants, of eventual manumission or absorption into other dependent categories — and the specific legal mechanisms that later produced serfdom.

The scale of slavery and dependent labor in Rus' also connected directly to the wider Eurasian slave trade examined in Chapter 6: enslaved captives, taken through warfare, raiding, and purchase, moved along the same river and steppe routes that carried furs, wax, and honey toward Byzantine and Islamic markets, making the export of enslaved people, alongside their use within Rus' households and estates, a significant feature of the Rus' economy from its earliest recorded period.

Historical Vocabulary
Smerd, Zakup, Kholop, Riadovich

These four terms, all drawn chiefly from the Russkaya Pravda and chronicle sources, describe distinct social and legal positions in Rus' society, and none should be treated as interchangeable with one another or with later Russian social categories.

Smerd most likely denoted a member of the rural population owing obligations to a prince, though the precise scope and status of the term — free peasant, semi-free tenant, or a broader category — remains genuinely debated among historians and should be presented to students as an open question rather than a settled definition. It should never be translated simply as "serf."

Zakup denoted a person temporarily bound to a patron through debt, retaining certain legal protections while working to satisfy an obligation, distinct from full slavery but carrying real legal vulnerability.

Kholop denoted an enslaved person who could be bought, sold, and inherited as property, occupying the most legally constrained position among these categories.

Riadovich denoted a person bound by a formal agreement (riad) to a patron, with obligations that current scholarship cannot fully distinguish from those of a zakup on the basis of surviving evidence.

Why this matters: a textbook that collapses these categories into a single translation such as "peasant" or "serf" misrepresents both the legal complexity of Rus' society and the very different historical institution of serfdom that developed many centuries later.

Section 11.5

Women, Family, and Household

The lives of women in Rus' must be reconstructed from unevenly distributed evidence: relatively rich sources describe the political and dynastic activities of a small number of elite women, such as Princess Olga's regency and reforms examined in Chapter 8, while evidence for the great majority of women — rural cultivators, urban artisans' wives and daughters, servants, and enslaved women — survives mainly through law, archaeology, and the incidental mentions scattered through birch-bark letters and other practical documents. This unevenness is itself an important lesson: historians must openly acknowledge what the evidence permits them to say about elite women and what remains largely inaccessible about the far larger population of ordinary women.

Marriage in Rus' combined customary practice with, from the late tenth century onward, an increasing degree of church regulation. Church teaching promoted monogamous, church-sanctioned marriage and opposed practices the clergy associated with pre-Christian custom, including polygyny among elites (attested for early princes such as Vladimir before his conversion, discussed in Chapter 9) and marriage by abduction or purchase. The degree to which church marriage norms actually governed ordinary practice, especially outside major towns and among the rural population, remains uncertain and likely varied considerably by region and social class; prescriptive religious and legal texts describe an ideal that lived practice did not always match. Property arrangements surrounding marriage, including forms of dowry and bridewealth, appear in the sources, and the Russkaya Pravda and related legal material address women's inheritance rights in at least some circumstances, suggesting women could hold and transmit property under specific conditions, though the scope of these rights, and how consistently they were honored in practice, requires the same caution appropriate to all legal evidence.

Within households, women's labor encompassed food preparation and storage, textile production (spinning and weaving, attested archaeologically through spindle whorls and loom weights found across rural and urban sites alike), childcare, and, particularly among rural populations, direct participation in agricultural tasks alongside men. Urban women, evidence from Novgorod's birch-bark letters suggests, could also manage household finances, conduct business transactions, and communicate independently in writing or through scribes, complicating any assumption that literacy and commercial activity were exclusively male domains, though the surviving examples remain too few to support confident generalizations about how common such activity was among women overall.

Elite women, including princesses and boyar wives, could exercise real political influence, particularly as regents for young sons, as intermediaries in dynastic marriage alliances examined in Chapter 10, and as patrons of church building and religious institutions. Historians should resist two opposite errors here: treating such women as evidence that Rus' society afforded broad freedom to women in general, and assuming that later Muscovite practices of elite female seclusion, examined in this textbook's chapters on Muscovy, already applied to Kyivan Rus'. The evidence instead suggests a society in which women's formal legal and social position was subordinate to men's in most respects, while individual women, particularly at the top of the social order, could and did wield significant practical influence.

Section 11.6

Agriculture and Rural Production

Agriculture formed the economic foundation supporting the great majority of the population of Rus', and its character varied significantly across the environmental zones introduced in Chapter 2. In the forest zone of the north, including the lands around Novgorod, poorer, more acidic soils and a shorter growing season favored smaller-scale cultivation, often involving forest-clearance techniques, including slash-and-burn methods in many localities, in which trees were cut and burned to fertilize the soil for a period of years before a plot was abandoned and a new one cleared. This method, however, coexisted in the northern zone with ploughed and increasingly permanent fields, and local variation was considerable; forest clearance should be understood as one method used under particular ecological conditions rather than a single, uniform northern farming system standing in clean opposition to the south. The richer soils of the forest-steppe around Kyiv and Chernigov generally supported more continuous, plow-based cultivation of the same fields over long periods, but this, too, was a matter of degree rather than an absolute regional divide.

Cereal crops—including rye, millet, barley, oats, and several varieties of wheat in proportions that varied by region and period—formed the staple of the rural diet, supplemented by garden vegetables, legumes, and, importantly across much of the forest zone, products of hunting, fishing, and beekeeping. Livestock — cattle, horses, sheep, pigs, and poultry — provided meat, dairy products, hides, wool, and draft power, though the scale of livestock holding likely varied with household wealth and available pasture. The forest economy contributed products with real commercial value well beyond local subsistence: furs (examined at length in Chapter 6 as a pillar of the wider Eurasian trade extending to Byzantine and Islamic markets), wax, and honey, gathered both from wild sources and, increasingly, from managed apiaries, moved from rural producers toward urban markets and, ultimately, toward the long-distance trade networks examined in the next section.

Agricultural tools recovered archaeologically — iron plowshares, sickles, scythes, and various woodworking implements — indicate a rural technology broadly comparable to that of contemporary agrarian societies elsewhere in medieval Europe, adapted to specific regional soil and climate conditions. Storage pits and granaries found at rural and urban sites alike point to the practical challenge every agrarian society faced: producing and preserving enough surplus to survive poor harvests, meet tribute and rent obligations, and support non-agricultural populations in towns, monasteries, and princely households. This last point connects directly to the wider economic system examined in the next section: rural agricultural surplus did not stay entirely within the village but moved outward, through tribute, rent, and market exchange, to sustain the urban and trading economy examined in Section 11.7.

Section 11.7

Trade and Urban Economies

Chapters 6 and 7 examined the river trade routes connecting Scandinavia, the Baltic, Rus', and Byzantium as a driving force behind the formation of Rus' political authority. This section returns to that trade from a different angle, asking not how trade routes shaped state formation but how trade, agriculture, and craft production combined to sustain the everyday economy of Rus' towns and their rural hinterlands. The two should not be understood as competing explanations of Rus' prosperity: most of the population farmed, and agricultural surplus, forest products, and craft goods together fed both local urban markets and long-distance commerce, each reinforcing the other rather than substituting for it.

Within towns, craft production — metalworking (including fine jewelry as well as tools and weapons), leatherworking, woodworking, bone- and antler-carving, pottery, and textile production — supplied both local consumption and goods for trade. Archaeological evidence from Novgorod and other excavated sites shows considerable craft specialization and, in some cases, evidence of production for a market beyond the immediate needs of the producing household, indicating a genuine urban commercial economy rather than purely subsistence-oriented craft work. Silver, circulating in the form of coins (including imported Islamic dirhams in earlier centuries and, later, Byzantine and Western European coin) and standardized silver ingots, served as a medium of exchange alongside barter, particularly for larger transactions and long-distance trade.

Long-distance trade connected Rus' towns to several major commercial worlds simultaneously: the Baltic and Scandinavian world to the north and west, reached via Novgorod and the Volkhov-Baltic route examined in Chapter 6; the Byzantine world to the south, reached via the Dnieper route to the Black Sea examined in Chapters 6 and 7; and, to the east, more intermittent but historically significant connections toward the Volga and, through Volga Bulgaria examined in Chapter 6, toward the wider Islamic commercial world. Rus' exports included furs, wax, honey, and enslaved people, discussed in Section 11.4; imports included silver, fine textiles, wine, glassware, and other luxury goods that appear archaeologically in elite burials and urban sites alike.

This commercial system connected village, town, and long-distance trade into a single, if uneven, economic network. A rural household's furs or agricultural surplus, collected as tribute or sold at a local market, might pass through several hands — local intermediaries, urban merchants, princely or boyar tribute collectors — before reaching a Byzantine or Baltic port. Princes and boyars profited from this system both by collecting tribute in kind and by taxing, protecting, or directly participating in commercial activity, a pattern of trade-based political power this textbook has already examined in a different historical and geographic context in Chapter 4's discussion of Scythian control over the Black Sea grain trade, and one that would recur, in different institutional forms, throughout later Russian history.

Section 11.8

Orthodox Christianity and Popular Religion

Chapter 9 examined Vladimir's conversion and the Christianization of Rus' as a political and diplomatic development; this section examines how Christianity actually entered, and transformed, everyday life over the following generations — a slower, more uneven, and more contested process than the chronicle's compressed account of a single baptismal event might suggest. Christianity's spread outward from Kyiv and other princely centers into the wider population depended on the construction of churches, the training and placement of clergy, the translation of core religious texts into Church Slavonic (examined further in Section 11.9), and a gradual reshaping of practices surrounding worship, marriage, burial, and the ritual calendar.

This process did not simply erase earlier religious practices on contact. The chronicle itself preserves evidence of continued resistance and alternative religious authority well after 988: entries for the year 1071 describe volkhvy — a term generally translated as pagan priests, diviners, or sorcerers — active in the Novgorod and Rostov lands, associated in the chronicle's hostile account with popular unrest and violence during a period of poor harvests. This evidence should not be read as proof that a fully intact, unchanged "pagan religion" persisted as a stable, organized alternative to Christianity indefinitely; rather, it shows that religious authority in Rus' remained genuinely contested for at least a generation or more after the official conversion, particularly outside the major princely centers where church institutions were most firmly established. Older ritual practices and local beliefs likely persisted in modified forms and blended, over time, with Christian practice in ways that varied by region and community, rather than existing as a single, uniform "dual faith" (a concept sometimes called dvoeverie in later scholarship) identical everywhere.

The 1054 schism between the churches of Rome and Constantinople, though conventionally dated as a single event, should not be understood as an instantaneous or complete barrier between Rus' and Western Christendom. Rus' princes continued to negotiate dynastic marriages with Catholic royal houses, as Chapter 10 describes for Yaroslav's children, and contact between Rus' and Latin Christian clergy and communities continued in various forms for generations after 1054, since the practical and doctrinal separation of the churches deepened only gradually rather than sealing itself shut in a single year.

Within Christian practice itself, the church shaped everyday life through the rhythms of the liturgical calendar, the sacraments surrounding birth, marriage, and death, moral instruction delivered through preaching and hagiography (examined further in Section 11.9), and an expanding network of parish churches and monastic communities, including the Kyiv Cave Monastery, founded in the mid-eleventh century, which became a major center of religious life, learning, and, eventually, historical writing. Monasteries also accumulated landed wealth and dependent labor of their own, making them significant economic institutions alongside their religious functions, a pattern that would grow considerably in later centuries of Russian history.

Section 11.9

Literature, Art, and Material Culture

The cultural life of Rus' extended well beyond the small circle of monastic and princely book culture that produced the chronicles cited throughout this textbook. This section surveys that wider culture, from formal religious literature and monumental art down to the everyday objects, inscriptions, and practical writings recovered through archaeology.

Formal literary culture centered on Church Slavonic, a liturgical and literary language derived from the Old Church Slavonic translations first developed for Slavic-speaking Christian communities in the ninth century and adapted for use in Rus' following Christianization. Translated religious texts — scripture, liturgical books, and hagiography, the literary genre recounting the lives of saints — formed the core of this literature, supplemented by original compositions produced within Rus' itself, including early historical writing that would eventually contribute to the compilation of the Primary Chronicle examined throughout Chapters 7 through 10. This literary culture was concentrated overwhelmingly within monastic and clerical circles and, to a lesser extent, princely courts; it should not be mistaken for evidence of widespread book literacy across the general population.

A very different, and in some ways more historically revealing, body of evidence for literacy comes from the practical, everyday writing preserved on birch bark, examined already in Section 11.2. Numbering well over a thousand documents recovered from Novgorod alone, with smaller numbers found at other sites, these texts demonstrate that functional literacy — sufficient to write a business note, record a debt, or send a short personal message — extended into merchant and artisan circles, well beyond the monastic and clerical literacy responsible for formal religious literature. Some birch-bark finds also include simple alphabet or writing exercises, suggesting that basic literacy instruction occurred outside monastic schools, though the best-known and most extensive evidence for a child's own writing and drawing at Novgorod belongs to a somewhat later period, in the thirteenth century, and should not be read back into this chapter's eleventh- and twelfth-century timeframe. None of this evidence means literacy was universal or even common across the whole population of Rus'; it means that literacy in Novgorod, at least, was not confined to a narrow religious elite, and historians should be cautious about assuming the same held true everywhere, given how heavily the physical evidence is concentrated in Novgorod's unusual soil conditions.

Visual and material culture in Rus' combined Byzantine artistic influence, transmitted through Christianization, with local craft traditions. Monumental church architecture and mosaic and fresco decoration, discussed further in Chapter 9's treatment of Christianization, drew directly on Byzantine models, while icon painting developed as a major devotional art form combining Byzantine technique with, over time, distinctive local characteristics. Archaeological finds of jewelry, metalwork, pottery, tools, and clothing fragments from both urban and rural sites provide evidence for everyday material life across the social spectrum, from simple utilitarian objects found in common household contexts to the elaborate gold and enamel work associated with elite burials and church treasuries.

Primary Source Window

Novgorod Birch-Bark Letter No. 109 (c. 1100–1120)

Type: Practical legal/commercial correspondence on birch bark · Creator: Zhiznomir, writing to Mikula · Location found: Novgorod · Approximate date: early twelfth century

Context: This document, one of the best known among the Novgorod birch-bark finds, is a letter from a man named Zhiznomir to a recipient named Mikula, concerning a dispute connected to Mikula's purchase, in Pskov, of an enslaved woman who was subsequently alleged to have been stolen rather than legitimately sold. Zhiznomir writes that he became caught up in the resulting accusation, that a princess had detained him in connection with the case, and that his companions had stood surety for him while he intended to appear at a hearing together with one of the prince's retainers. The letter reflects the practical, often terse style typical of the corpus: it was written to communicate a specific problem to a specific recipient, not to record history or doctrine for future readers.

Paraphrase of the document's substance (not a verbatim translated quotation):

Zhiznomir informs Mikula that the enslaved woman Mikula had purchased in Pskov is now the subject of a theft accusation, that a princess has detained Zhiznomir in connection with the matter, that his associates have stood surety for him, and that he plans to attend a hearing before princely authority, accompanied by one of the prince's men, to resolve the dispute.

Analysis: This single short document connects several major themes of this chapter at once. It demonstrates functional literacy being used for an ordinary commercial and legal problem rather than for religious or chronicle purposes. It attests that enslaved women could be bought and sold across some distance — here, from Pskov — and that disputes over such transactions could reach princely authority through a hearing; it should not, from a single letter, be treated as proof that such disputes were routine or common. It also shows a woman discussed throughout as a piece of transferable property, in the same practical, unemotional register characteristic of the wider birch-bark corpus. At the same time, the document cannot tell historians how such disputes were typically resolved, nor anything at all about the enslaved woman's own experience or perspective, since every surviving word in the letter belongs to Zhiznomir, addressing Mikula about a legal problem that had entangled them both — her voice is entirely absent from the source.

Questions for students:

  1. What does this letter reveal about literacy in Novgorod that the Primary Chronicle could not show?
  2. What does the letter reveal about slavery and property law in Rus', and what does it leave unanswered?
  3. Whose perspective is represented in this document, and whose is absent?
  4. Why is a single document like this valuable evidence, and why must historians avoid generalizing too broadly from it?

Historical Debate

Was Rus' "Feudal"?

The question: Is "feudalism," a term developed to describe social and military relationships in medieval Western Europe, a useful label for the relationships among princes, boyars, retainers, and dependent laborers in Rus'?

The comparative-usefulness position: Some historians argue that broad structural similarities — armed retinues bound to a lord by service and reward, landholding elites exercising local authority, and a rural population bound by various obligations to those elites — make comparison with Western feudalism illuminating, especially for showing that Rus' developed genuine class stratification and elite landholding rather than remaining a simple, undifferentiated tribal society.
The skeptical position: Other historians caution that "feudalism" carries specific legal connotations from Western European history — formal vassalage contracts, fief tenure, and a particular relationship between land tenure and military obligation — that do not map precisely onto the more variable and less formally codified relationships attested in Rus' sources, and that imposing the term risks assuming institutions the evidence does not actually demonstrate.

Evidence: Historians weigh the Russkaya Pravda's treatment of landholding and dependent labor, chronicle descriptions of druzhina and boyar behavior, and comparative evidence from Western European feudal societies.

Points of agreement: Most historians agree that Rus' had genuine social stratification, elite landholding, and multiple forms of dependent labor.

Points of disagreement: Historians differ over whether "feudalism" clarifies or obscures the specific, locally distinctive character of these Rus' institutions.

Student task: Using evidence from Section 11.1, identify one respect in which Rus' social relationships resembled Western feudal arrangements and one respect in which the comparison breaks down.

Multiple Perspectives

Five Lives in Rus'

A druzhina retainer: Status and livelihood depended on personal loyalty to a prince, military service, and maintenance drawn from shares of tribute or plunder, with land or office sometimes following over time, and with the constant possibility of transferring service to a more generous or successful prince.
A Novgorod merchant's wife: Household management, property, and, on the evidence of birch-bark letters, at least occasional independent business communication combined with a legal and social position generally subordinate to male household heads.
A rural cultivator described in some sources as a smerd: Life combined agricultural labor adapted to local soil and climate with obligations, of uncertain and debated precise scope, owed to a prince or boyar, alongside participation in village custom and cooperation.
An enslaved kholop: Legal status as property, limited protections under law, and dependence entirely on a household's treatment, with the specific path into slavery — capture, sale, or inherited status — shaping daily circumstances.
A parish priest in a provincial town: Responsibility for guiding a community only partially and unevenly Christianized, negotiating continued local attachment to older practices while representing an expanding institutional church.

These five perspectives illustrate why no single description of "life in Rus'" can substitute for attention to specific social position, gender, legal status, and region.

Cause and Effect

Social Stratification in Rus'

Long-Term Conditions

Environmental diversity across forest, forest-steppe, and river-valley zones, producing regionally variable agricultural and commercial opportunities examined in Chapter 2.

Intermediate Developments

Consolidation of princely tribute collection and druzhina service examined in Chapters 7–8; growth of towns and long-distance trade examined in Chapters 6–7; codification of law under Yaroslav examined in Chapter 10.

Short-Term Triggers

Individual princely grants of land and privilege to retainers; local accumulation of mercantile wealth in towns; specific transactions producing debt-dependency or enslavement.

Effects

A layered society of princes, boyars, townspeople, free rural cultivators, and several categories of dependent and enslaved persons, sustained by an economy combining agriculture, craft production, and long-distance trade.

Historical complexity: No single institution—not princely law alone, not trade alone, not conquest alone—fully explains the social order of Rus'; each reinforced the others across more than a century of development.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Christian Practice Across Generations

What Changed

  • Formal church institutions, clergy, and church buildings expanded steadily outward from Kyiv and other princely centers across the eleventh and twelfth centuries.
  • Church-sanctioned marriage and burial practices gradually became more widespread, though unevenly.
  • Literacy in Church Slavonic and, separately, practical literacy exemplified by birch-bark writing expanded in at least some urban centers.

What Stayed the Same, or Changed Only Slowly

  • Elements of pre-Christian belief and practice persisted in modified, regionally variable forms well beyond 988, as the 1071 volkhvy episode illustrates.
  • Rural communities, more distant from princely and church centers, likely Christianized more slowly and unevenly than towns.
  • Social hierarchy and dependent labor continued largely unaffected by Christianization in this period, despite church teaching on charity and moral conduct.

Why the pattern matters: Treating 988 as a single completed event, rather than the beginning of a long and uneven process, misrepresents how religious change actually occurred in Rus'.

Everyday Life and Social History

An Urban Household in Novgorod

Archaeological excavation of Novgorod's medieval residential blocks has reconstructed, in considerable physical detail, the layout of ordinary urban households: log-built houses along wood-paved streets, small household workshops attached to or near living quarters, storage areas for food and goods, and, in wealthier households, additional outbuildings and larger enclosed yards. Birch-bark documents recovered from these same residential contexts often correspond closely to the archaeological evidence for a household's economic activity, allowing historians to connect specific texts to specific physical households in a small number of well-documented cases.

A household of middling prosperity might include a craftsman or merchant, his wife, children, and one or more dependents or enslaved servants, engaged in some combination of craft production, small-scale trade, and household management. Daily concerns reflected in the birch-bark corpus include debts owed and collected, disputes over property and inheritance, requests for goods or assistance, and family news, offering a granular, if fragmentary, view of urban life that supplements, without replacing, the broader economic and legal picture drawn from the Russkaya Pravda and archaeological survey.

Regional Context

Forest, Forest-Steppe, and River

The society examined in this chapter did not develop uniformly across the territory of Rus'. The forest zone around Novgorod, Ladoga, and the upper Volga favored fur production, forest-clearance agriculture, and Baltic-oriented trade, and its society, evidenced richly through Novgorod's unique archaeological preservation, may not represent conditions elsewhere. The forest-steppe around Kyiv and Chernigov supported denser agricultural settlement and closer integration with Dnieper-route trade toward Byzantium. Regions further south, bordering the open steppe examined in Chapter 4, faced continued exposure to steppe nomadic groups such as the Pechenegs and, later, Cumans, examined in Chapter 12, a frontier condition that shaped settlement patterns and military obligations in ways distinct from the more settled northern forest zone.

This regional variation matters for how students read this entire chapter: conclusions drawn from Novgorod's rich birch-bark evidence, or from law codes issued from Kyiv, should not be assumed to describe rural life in every part of the vast and environmentally diverse territory this textbook associates with Rus'.

Section 11.10

Chapter Review and Historical Thinking Checkpoint

This chapter has reconstructed the society of Rus' beyond the princely narrative of Chapters 7 through 10: its layered social hierarchy of princes, boyars, retainers, townspeople, rural cultivators, and dependent and enslaved persons; its economy combining regionally varied agriculture, craft production, and long-distance trade; its uneven and gradual Christianization; and its literary and material culture, recovered through sources ranging from formal manuscripts to scratched birch bark. Before proceeding to Chapter 12's examination of the political fragmentation of Rus', students should apply this chapter's lessons to the following problem.

Central Problem — Reconstructing a Society Without Its Own Voice.

How can historians reconstruct the lives of people who rarely wrote chronicles or appeared by name in political narratives? Using evidence from this chapter, explain what legal sources such as the Russkaya Pravda can and cannot reveal about rural and dependent populations; what archaeological evidence can and cannot reveal about everyday material life; and what the Novgorod birch-bark corpus can and cannot reveal about literacy, commerce, and social relationships. Explain why combining these different kinds of evidence produces a more reliable picture than relying on any single source, while also identifying what remains genuinely unknown or uncertain about the lives of ordinary people in Rus'.

This problem reinforces the central methodological lesson of the chapter: a history built solely from chronicles and princely law would describe only a narrow slice of Rus' society. The habits of mind developed here — combining legal, archaeological, and documentary evidence, and remaining honest about the limits of each — will recur throughout this textbook's later treatment of Muscovite, imperial, and Soviet social history.

Chapter Summary

This chapter shifted the textbook's attention from the princes examined in Chapters 7 through 10 to the wider society of Rus': its social hierarchy, its economy, its religious life, and its material and literary culture. It argued that Rus' was a predominantly rural society organized around relationships of service, tribute, and dependency, in which princes and boyars stood at the top of a hierarchy sustained by the labor of free cultivators, townspeople, and several distinct categories of dependent and enslaved persons.

The chapter examined princes, boyars, and the druzhina as an interconnected elite bound together by reciprocal obligations of service and reward, while cautioning against uncritically applying the Western European term "feudalism" to describe these relationships. It then moved to the towns of Rus', particularly Novgorod, where archaeological evidence and, uniquely, a large corpus of birch-bark documents reveal a socially varied urban population of merchants, artisans, and craft workers engaged in both local and long-distance commerce.

Turning to the countryside, the chapter reconstructed the world of rural cultivators, cautioning explicitly against equating the term smerd with the much later institution of serfdom, and it examined the several distinct legal categories of slavery and dependent labor — kholop, zakup, and riadovich — attested in the Russkaya Pravda, insisting on the historical distinction between slavery and dependency in Rus' and the different institution of serfdom that would develop centuries later.

The chapter examined women's lives within family and household, distinguishing the fuller evidence available for elite women from the thinner evidence for ordinary women, and cautioning against both overstating women's freedom on the basis of exceptional elite examples and projecting later Muscovite seclusion practices backward onto Kyivan Rus'.

It examined agriculture and rural production as regionally varied rather than uniform, and it presented agriculture and long-distance trade as complementary, mutually reinforcing foundations of the Rus' economy rather than as competing explanations for Rus' prosperity.

The chapter then turned to religion, showing that Christianization after 988 was a slow, uneven, and genuinely contested process, evidenced by the chronicle's own reports of volkhvy activity in 1071, and that the 1054 schism did not instantly sever contact between Rus' and Latin Christendom. It closed with an examination of literature, art, and material culture, giving particular weight to the birch-bark evidence from Novgorod as proof that practical literacy extended well beyond monastic and princely circles, while cautioning against generalizing Novgorod's exceptional evidentiary record to the rest of Rus'.

Throughout, the chapter modeled a methodological shift necessary for studying any pre-modern society whose ordinary members left few records of their own: the combination of legal, archaeological, and documentary evidence, used together and read critically, to reconstruct a society that no single source can describe on its own.

This social and economic portrait of Rus' now sets the stage for Chapter 12, which examines how the relative dynastic cohesion and political primacy of Kyiv described in Chapters 7 through 10 gave way to deeper regionalization and fragmentation, a process this chapter's attention to genuine regional diversity — in economy, in religious practice, in evidentiary survival — has already begun to anticipate.

MGU Library Research Project

Reading the Birch-Bark Archive

This project asks you to investigate the Novgorod birch-bark corpus in greater depth, comparing several documents to evaluate what they reveal about everyday life in Rus'.

  1. Select several documents. Using library or database resources on Novgorod birch-bark texts, identify three to five documents covering different topics (commerce, family, dispute, property).
  2. Classify each document. For each, identify its likely author, audience, and purpose.
  3. Compare to the chronicle. Identify what each document reveals that the Primary Chronicle does not address.
  4. Evaluate limitations. Explain what each document cannot tell historians, and why.
  5. Write your findings. In a 3–5 page essay, synthesize what this small set of documents can responsibly establish about literacy, commerce, family, or social relationships in Rus', and what remains uncertain.

Library resources to use: The MGU History Library's collection on Novgorod archaeology, translated selections from the birch-bark corpus, and scholarship on literacy in medieval Rus'.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has asked a different kind of question than the four chapters before it. Rather than tracing what princes did, it has asked what it meant to live in Rus' as a boyar, a merchant, a rural cultivator, an enslaved person, or a woman managing a household — questions the chronicle sources answer only partially, and which require archaeology, law, and documents such as the Novgorod birch-bark letters to answer more fully. The picture that emerges is of a genuinely stratified society, sustained by a diverse and regionally varied economy, undergoing a slow and incomplete religious transformation, whose literary and material culture extended well beyond the narrow circle of chroniclers who wrote most of what survives about Rus'.

The chapter's central methodological lesson — that a society's ordinary members require different, and multiple, kinds of evidence to reconstruct — will recur throughout this textbook, particularly in later chapters examining peasants and workers under Muscovite serfdom, industrial labor in the late Russian Empire, and everyday life under Soviet rule.

As the textbook proceeds to Chapter 12's examination of the political fragmentation of Rus', students should carry forward this chapter's attention to regional diversity, its careful distinctions among social and legal categories, and its insistence that political history and social history, while connected, are not the same story.
