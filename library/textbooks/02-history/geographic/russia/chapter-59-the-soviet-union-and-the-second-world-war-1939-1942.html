Part X — Stalinism and the Second World War

The Soviet Union and the Second World War, 1939–1942

A History of Russia: From the Eurasian World to the Russian Federation

Chapter Opening

At about noon on 22 June 1941, Soviet citizens gathered around street loudspeakers and kitchen radios heard not Stalin but Vyacheslav Molotov, who announced that Germany had attacked the Soviet Union without a declaration of war. In paraphrase, he told listeners that the cause was just, that the enemy would be defeated, and that victory would be theirs.

For many people the broadcast was the first official confirmation of what had been happening since the early hours of the morning, when German aircraft had bombed airfields and cities from the Baltic to the Black Sea and German armies had crossed the frontier along a front of well over a thousand kilometers. Stalin himself did not address the country until 3 July. In the interval the Red Army lost much of its forward strength, the commander of one front would be shot for his failures, and the Soviet leadership discovered how little of its prewar planning applied to the war it actually had to fight. This chapter studies the roughly forty months that began in September 1939 and ended in December 1942, a period in which the Soviet Union was first a beneficiary of Nazi Germany’s aggression, then its principal victim, and finally the state whose armies encircled a German field army on the Volga.

The chapter has to hold together two stories that Soviet and later Russian memory have often kept apart. The first is the story of expansion between 1939 and 1941: the partition of Poland, the war against Finland, and the annexation of the Baltic states and of Romanian territory, all carried out under the terms of the August 1939 pact examined in Chapter 58 and all involving coercion, arrests, and deportations. The second is the story of the German invasion of June 1941, in which the Soviet state came close to collapse and survived at a staggering human cost. A historian cannot responsibly narrate the second without the first, because the territories seized in 1939–1940 became the ground on which the German offensive began, and because the memories of Soviet rule there shaped how local populations responded to German occupation.

This is not only a military history. The German war in the East was planned as a war of conquest, enslavement, and annihilation, and it was accompanied from its first days by the mass murder of Jews, the deliberate starvation and execution of Soviet prisoners of war, and the systematic plunder of occupied territory. The Soviet side of the war also included coercion: harsh discipline in the army, the deportation of whole nationalities on suspicion of disloyalty, the killing of prisoners by the NKVD as it retreated, and the mobilization of tens of millions of people for labor and combat under conditions of extreme hardship. The people who experienced these years included a Jewish family in Kyiv, a Belarusian peasant near a forest, a Leningrad schoolchild, an Estonian official awaiting deportation, a Soviet German taken from the Volga, a worker dismantling a factory in Kharkiv for shipment to the Urals, and a Red Army soldier from Uzbekistan or Tatarstan or Ukraine. They did not share a single war.

The evidence is correspondingly varied and uneven. German documents, including operational reports of the Einsatzgruppen, army orders, and economic planning memoranda, record intention and action with chilling clarity. Soviet archives opened since 1991 have made possible far more accurate studies of military losses, deportations, and decision-making, though access has been uneven and has narrowed again in recent years. Diaries, letters, memoirs, oral testimony, photographs, and propaganda illuminate experience but require critical reading. Casualty figures remain contested in many cases, and this chapter reports ranges and attributes estimates rather than selecting convenient numbers. The conventional Soviet and Russian term for the war against Germany, the Great Patriotic War, begins on 22 June 1941, while the Second World War began in 1939; the difference is itself a historical problem.

Nothing in these years was inevitable. In the autumn of 1941 the German army reached the suburbs of Moscow and the outskirts of Leningrad; in the summer of 1942 it advanced to the Volga and into the Caucasus. The Soviet victories at Moscow in December 1941 and the encirclement at Stalingrad in November 1942 were the products of decisions, errors, reserves, geography, industrial capacity, and luck, and at each stage the outcome could have been different. The chapter closes in December 1942, with the German Sixth Army surrounded but not yet defeated; the surrender of February 1943 belongs to Chapter 60.

The chapter connects directly to what precedes and follows it. It builds on the industrialization of Chapter 55, the collectivization and famine of Chapter 56, the terror and military purges of Chapter 57, and the diplomacy of Chapter 58. It invites comparison with Russia’s earlier invasions, including the Napoleonic campaign of Chapter 36 and the First World War of Chapter 48. It prepares for Chapter 60, for the postwar order of Chapter 61 and beyond, and for the national movements of the Baltic republics and Ukraine examined in Chapter 66. Throughout, the Soviet Union must be distinguished from Russia: the Red Army, the occupied territories, and the evacuated factories belonged to a multinational state of many peoples, not to a single nation.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Distinguish among Soviet neutrality, economic cooperation with Germany, and territorial expansion in 1939–1941, and explain how each operated.
  2. Compare the Soviet occupation and annexation of eastern Poland, the Baltic states, and Romanian territories, noting differences in method, legal framing, and local experience.
  3. Explain why the Winter War exposed Red Army weaknesses, and evaluate its military, territorial, and diplomatic consequences.
  4. Analyze the planning of Operation Barbarossa as a war of conquest and annihilation rather than a conventional campaign.
  5. Evaluate the causes of the Red Army’s catastrophic defeats in 1941, weighing surprise, purges, deployment, doctrine, and command.
  6. Describe the structure of Nazi occupation, the Holocaust in the occupied Soviet Union, and the treatment of Soviet prisoners of war and civilians.
  7. Assess the range of civilian choices under occupation, including resistance, collaboration, rescue, and survival, without assigning collective responsibility to any people.
  8. Distinguish Soviet internal repression during the war from Nazi occupation policy, including the deportation of Soviet Germans and NKVD prisoner killings.
  9. Trace the sieges and battles of Leningrad, Moscow, and Stalingrad through December 1942, connecting military operations to civilian experience.
  10. Analyze how evacuation, labor mobilization, and Allied supply sustained the Soviet war effort, and evaluate the limits of explanations based on geography or winter.
  11. Interpret primary sources and historiographical debates on Katyn, the Commissar Order, the Leningrad siege, and the failure of Barbarossa.
  12. Contextualize the memory of these events in Soviet, Russian, Polish, Baltic, Ukrainian, and Jewish historical narratives.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did the Soviet Union move from partnership with Nazi Germany in 1939 to near-collapse under German attack in 1941, and what choices shaped that trajectory?
  2. Why does the Soviet annexation of territory in 1939–1940 look different from the perspectives of Moscow, of Warsaw, Helsinki, Tallinn, Riga, Vilnius, and Bucharest, and of the local populations?
  3. What combination of institutional, doctrinal, and operational factors explains the scale of Soviet defeats in the summer and autumn of 1941?
  4. In what sense was Operation Barbarossa different from other German campaigns, and what evidence shows that difference?
  5. How did Soviet civilians of different nationalities, religions, and regions experience occupation, evacuation, siege, and mobilization?
  6. What explains the failure of German armies to achieve rapid victory, and why is “General Winter” an inadequate explanation?
  7. How did the Soviet state mobilize its economy and population, and what did that mobilization cost?
  8. How should historians treat memory of these events, including the delayed acknowledgment of Katyn and the later memory of the Great Patriotic War?

Historical Context

The Soviet Union that entered the Second World War had been transformed by the policies examined in the preceding chapters. The Five-Year Plans of Chapter 55 had built an industrial base, especially in metallurgy, machine building, and armaments, in the Urals and Western Siberia as well as in Ukraine and Leningrad. The collectivization of Chapter 56 had given the state control over grain procurement at the price of famine and rural devastation. The terror of Chapter 57, including the purge of the officer corps, had removed or silenced many of the most experienced commanders and discouraged initiative at every level. In foreign policy, the collective-security strategy of the mid-1930s had failed after the Munich Agreement of 1938, from which Moscow was excluded, and Stalin chose in August 1939 to sign the non-aggression pact with Germany and its secret protocol, as described in Chapter 58.

The geography of the western frontier was also the product of recent history. The Soviet-Polish War and the Treaty of Riga of 1921 had left millions of Ukrainians and Belarusians within interwar Poland. Estonia, Latvia, and Lithuania had gained independence in 1918–1920 from the wreckage of the Russian Empire, and Finland had become independent in 1917. Romania had united Bessarabia with itself in 1918, a union the Soviet Union never recognized. The secret protocol of August 1939 assigned Finland, Estonia, Latvia, eastern Poland, and Bessarabia to a Soviet “sphere of interest,” with Lithuania initially in the German sphere and shifted to the Soviet sphere in September. These were not “Russian lands” in any straightforward sense. They were the territories of other states with their own institutions and national histories, and the populations of the affected regions included Poles, Ukrainians, Belarusians, Jews, Lithuanians, Latvians, Estonians, Finns, Romanians, and many others.

A note on terminology and dating. The Soviet Union, not Russia, entered the war in 1941, and its army was multinational. The Russian Soviet Federative Socialist Republic was one of its constituent republics and contained much, but by no means all, of the territory under threat. All dates in this chapter follow the Gregorian calendar, which the Soviet Union had adopted in 1918, so no Old Style conversion is needed. “Second World War” names the global conflict that began with the German invasion of Poland on 1 September 1939; “Great Patriotic War” names the Soviet-German war from 22 June 1941, a term that appeared in the Soviet press within a day of the invasion and that evoked the “Patriotic War” of 1812. Both terms are used here with attention to what each includes and excludes.

Division I — War, Soviet Expansion, and the Collapse of the German-Soviet Relationship

Section 59.1

Europe at War and the Consequences of the German-Soviet Pact

The Non-Aggression Pact signed by Molotov and the German foreign minister Joachim von Ribbentrop on 23 August 1939 contained a published pledge of neutrality and a secret protocol dividing Eastern Europe into spheres of interest. Soviet authorities denied that the protocol existed until the Congress of People’s Deputies, acting on the findings of a commission, condemned it in December 1989. Chapter 58 examined the diplomatic origins and the debates over Soviet motives. This chapter begins with the consequences. Germany invaded Poland on 1 September; Britain and France declared war on Germany on 3 September; and the Soviet Union, which had declared itself neutral, joined the German attack in the east two weeks later.

Soviet strategic calculations in 1939 combined defensive and expansionist elements. Stalin’s government had good reason to fear a German attack and to doubt Anglo-French willingness to fight for Eastern Europe. It also faced a Japanese threat in the Far East, where Soviet and Mongolian forces under Zhukov defeated a Japanese force at Khalkhin Gol in the summer of 1939, with a cease-fire in mid-September. A pact with Germany promised time, a buffer of territory, and the recovery of lands lost in 1918–1921, and it offered an arrangement in which Germany and the Western powers might exhaust one another. It also reflected a view of international politics in which the capitalist powers were rivals, not natural allies. The Communist International reversed its line in the weeks after the pact: the war was now described as an imperialist conflict, and Communist parties in Western Europe were instructed to oppose it.

It is necessary to distinguish three things that the pact made possible. The first was formal neutrality: the Soviet Union proclaimed neutrality in the European war, yet it invaded Poland on 17 September and Finland on 30 November without a formal declaration of war, presenting the first as protective intervention and the second as a response to Finnish provocation. The second was economic cooperation: from August 1939 credit and trade agreements, followed by a larger commercial agreement in February 1940, sent Soviet grain, petroleum, cotton, and raw materials such as manganese and chromium to Germany and facilitated the transit of goods from Asia, in return for German machinery and technology. The third was territorial expansion by force. These were distinct policies with different logics, and they ended differently. Economic cooperation continued until the eve of invasion; neutrality ended on 22 June 1941; the territorial gains became the framework for Soviet security policy and for the postwar settlement.

The pact transformed Soviet public life and international position almost at once. Anti-Nazi themes disappeared from the Soviet press and cinema, and Eisenstein’s film Alexander Nevsky, with its portrayal of the defeat of the German knights, was withdrawn from distribution. Foreign Communist parties, which had spent the 1930s mobilizing against fascism, were told to treat the war as an imperialist contest in which neither side deserved support, producing confusion and defections. Cooperation also had a darker side: in 1939–1940 the NKVD handed over to the Gestapo, at Brest and elsewhere, a number of German and Austrian Communists and Jewish refugees who had been held in Soviet prisons. Soviet leaders regarded these steps as the price of keeping Germany at arm’s length.

Historians differ about how to weigh the motives behind the pact. Some emphasize defense: the Soviet leadership, having failed to secure a firm alliance with Britain and France in the negotiations of the summer of 1939, was unwilling to face Germany alone, and a buffer of territory and time seemed essential. Others stress opportunism: the protocol allowed the recovery of territory lost after the First World War and the expansion of Soviet power while the capitalist states fought one another. Most scholars see both elements and regard the question not as a choice between them but as one of proportion, since the same decisions served both ends. What is not in dispute is that the protocol’s existence, kept secret and later denied, made the pact an instrument for the territorial changes described in the following sections.

Historical Vocabulary
Sphere of Influence

Definition: A region in which one power claims a predominant right to political, economic, or military influence, usually by agreement with other powers and without the consent of the peoples concerned.

Historical context: The secret protocol of 1939 used the phrase “sphere of interest” to allocate territories of independent states, including Poland, Finland, Estonia, Latvia, and Romanian Bessarabia, between Germany and the USSR.

Why it matters: The concept shows how great-power agreements treated smaller states as objects of bargaining. Later Soviet and Russian interpretations of the pact depend heavily on whether the spheres are seen as defensive security arrangements or as a license for conquest.

Historical Vocabulary
Annexation and Occupation

Definition: Occupation is the military control of foreign territory, which international law treats as temporary and which does not transfer sovereignty. Annexation is the formal incorporation of territory into another state.

Historical context: The Soviet Union occupied eastern Poland, the Baltic states, and Romanian territories and then incorporated them through elections, assemblies, and decrees. Many Western governments, including the United States, refused to recognize the Baltic annexations.

Why it matters: The two terms frame an enduring dispute. Soviet sources described the actions as voluntary unification or liberation; Polish, Baltic, and Romanian sources and most Western governments described occupation and forced annexation. Historians must therefore name the process and attribute the labels.

Section 59.2

The Soviet Invasion and Partition of Poland, September 1939

By 17 September 1939, when Soviet forces crossed the Polish frontier, German armies had already defeated the main Polish forces and surrounded Warsaw. Molotov’s note to the Polish ambassador declared that the Polish state had ceased to exist and that the Soviet government must therefore protect the Ukrainian and Belarusian population of eastern Poland. The Polish commander-in-chief ordered troops not to fight the Red Army except where attacked, and the Polish government crossed into Romania that night. Fighting nevertheless occurred, notably at Grodno, Szack, and Wilno, and Lwów (Lviv) surrendered on 22 September after an attempted defense. Soviet armies deployed in two groups, the Belorussian and Ukrainian Fronts, and rapidly overran the area.

The German-Soviet Treaty of Friendship and Boundary of 28 September adjusted the line agreed in August. Lithuania was moved into the Soviet sphere, while the Lublin and part of the Warsaw voivodeships passed to Germany. Warsaw capitulated on the same day. The Soviet Union acquired territory of roughly 200,000 square kilometers with a population of about 13 million by commonly cited estimates, composed of Poles, Ukrainians, Belarusians, Jews, and smaller groups, in addition to hundreds of thousands of refugees who had fled east from the German zone. Soviet forces took a very large number of Polish soldiers prisoner, on the order of a quarter of a million by commonly cited figures, though many were released.

Experiences differed sharply. Some Ukrainian and Belarusian peasants, who had been politically and economically disadvantaged in interwar Poland, welcomed the end of Polish rule, and some Jews saw the Red Army as protection from German persecution, while others feared Soviet rule. Polish landowners, officials, policemen, and military settlers were attacked in some places by local people and arrested by Soviet organs. Soviet authorities staged elections on 22 October 1939 to People’s Assemblies in western Ukraine and western Belarus, with a single approved list, and the assemblies petitioned for admission to the Soviet republics. The Supreme Soviet agreed on 1–2 November, and a decree of 29 November conferred Soviet citizenship on residents. Wilno, which the Soviet Union transferred to Lithuania in October, was incorporated with Lithuania in 1940. Soviet publications named the events a liberation of kindred peoples; Polish memory names them an invasion and a fourth partition; Ukrainian and Belarusian national narratives often stress the unification of lands inhabited by their own peoples, while noting the Soviet context. These descriptions are interpretations to be examined rather than neutral terms.

The Red Army invaded in two main groups, the Belorussian Front under Mikhail Kovalev and the Ukrainian Front under Semyon Timoshenko, numbering together well over 400,000 men and several thousand tanks, against Polish forces that had already been mauled by the Germans and were deployed to the west. The Polish commander-in-chief’s order not to resist the Soviets, except when attacked, was not received or applied everywhere, and fighting was heaviest where local commanders chose to defend. At Grodno the defenders held for several days in a mixed force of regular troops and volunteers; Polish sources record that captured defenders were shot after the city fell. At Szack, at the end of September, a Polish group fought Soviet troops. Wilno fell after limited resistance, and Lwów surrendered on terms that Soviet commanders did not fully honor. Soviet leaflets and radio appeals told Ukrainian and Belarusian peasants that the army had come to free them from landlords, and told soldiers that Polish officers were class enemies.

The reception of the Red Army reflected the social and national structure of interwar Poland. The state had followed policies of assimilation and restriction toward Ukrainians and Belarusians, including the suppression of nationalist agitation in the 1930s; rural poverty and the shortage of land were severe in the eastern provinces; and antisemitism was widespread in Polish public life. Some members of these groups welcomed the end of Polish rule, and local Communist sympathizers formed militias and revolutionary committees that arrested officials, landowners, and military settlers and in places carried out summary killings. Others, including many Poles, Jews with property or religious institutions, and Ukrainian nationalists, feared the new order. The assemblies elected on 22 October reported overwhelming majorities for union with the Soviet republics, results that independent observers could not and did not verify. In Lviv, which became the center of a new province of the Ukrainian SSR, the university was reorganized as a Ukrainian institution, Polish cultural bodies were closed, and the city’s population was swollen by refugees from the German zone. Many Jewish refugees faced a choice between accepting Soviet passports and risking deportation. The people of eastern Poland therefore entered the Soviet system not as a single population but as communities in conflict with one another.

Section 59.3

The Winter War with Finland, 1939–1940

Soviet negotiations with Finland in October–November 1939 concerned a border on the Karelian Isthmus only about thirty kilometers from Leningrad. Moscow proposed that Finland withdraw its frontier on the Isthmus, cede islands in the Gulf of Finland, and lease the Hanko peninsula for a naval base, in exchange for a larger but less valuable territory in Soviet Karelia. The security concern was real, because Leningrad was the second city of the Union and the Baltic fleet’s base, but Finland rejected terms that would have dismantled its defenses and compromised its neutrality. On 26 November the Soviet side reported shelling at the village of Mainila, which it blamed on Finland; most historians, relying on Soviet and Finnish evidence, conclude that the incident was staged or at least was not a Finnish attack. The Red Army invaded on 30 November, and Moscow announced a puppet “Finnish Democratic Republic” under the Finnish Communist Otto Kuusinen at Terijoki.

The expected quick victory did not occur. Finnish forces, outnumbered and short of heavy weapons, used the forests, lakes, frozen ground, and winter cold to cut Soviet columns on narrow roads. At Suomussalmi and on the Raate road in December and January, Finnish ski troops encircled and destroyed Soviet divisions. The Mannerheim Line on the Isthmus held until a reorganized and reinforced Soviet force under Timoshenko, with massed artillery and better coordination of infantry, tanks, and aircraft, broke through at Summa in February 1940. Soviet casualties were far greater than Finnish: Soviet authorities initially reported tens of thousands of dead, while Russian archival studies since 1990 suggest a figure of more than 120,000 killed and missing, against roughly 25,000 Finnish dead.

The Moscow Peace Treaty, signed on 12 March and effective on 13 March 1940, required Finland to cede the Karelian Isthmus including Vyborg (Viipuri), parts of Ladoga Karelia, territory in the north and the Rybachy peninsula, and a lease of Hanko. About 400,000 Karelian Finns left the ceded areas. The Soviet Union gained the territory it had demanded and more, but Finland retained independence, and the Kuusinen regime was abandoned. The League of Nations had expelled the USSR on 14 December 1939, and Western opinion turned sharply against Moscow. The war also exposed weaknesses that the Soviet military then tried to correct, replacing Voroshilov with Timoshenko as defense commissar in May 1940. Historians still debate how far German observers drew conclusions about Soviet weakness from the Finnish campaign, and how far the Red Army’s reforms after 1940 repaired its deficiencies before June 1941. The war also shaped Finland’s alignment: in 1941 the Finnish government accepted cooperation with Germany to recover lost territory.

The Soviet plan underestimated the task. The Leningrad Military District under Kirill Meretskov prepared for a campaign measured in days or weeks, with the Seventh, Eighth, Ninth, and Fourteenth Armies advancing along a broad front from the Karelian Isthmus to the Arctic coast. The troops lacked winter clothing, skis, and white camouflage in adequate quantities, intelligence on the Mannerheim Line’s fortifications was poor, and tanks and infantry were poorly coordinated. The officer corps, thinned by the purges, was less experienced than the plan required, and in the forests north of Lake Ladoga Soviet columns confined to a few roads were attacked from the sides and cut into isolated groups. Soviet aircraft bombed Helsinki and other Finnish cities on the first day. Finns mockingly called Soviet cluster bombs “Molotov bread baskets,” and the name Molotov cocktail was applied to their improvised incendiary bottles. The familiar story that Molotov personally described the bombs as food deliveries is not securely documented and should not be treated as an authenticated quotation.

The Finnish army, about 300,000 men at the start, fought a defensive campaign with limited artillery, few tanks, and few antitank weapons, but with good use of terrain, mobile ski units, and strong morale. Foreign sympathy was extensive: Finland received volunteers, mostly from Sweden, and arms from several countries, and Britain and France planned an expeditionary force, partly with an eye on Swedish iron ore, that never reached the field. Germany remained benevolently neutral toward the Soviet Union. By the time of the peace Finland had lost about a tenth of its territory including Viipuri, one of its largest cities, and some 400,000 evacuees had to be resettled; but it had preserved its independence. The Soviet leadership drew lessons: the defense commissariat was reorganized, training and logistics reviewed, and in 1940 the army received new regulations on discipline and command. German observers drew their own conclusions about Soviet weakness, though how far these reinforced Hitler’s expectations in 1941 is difficult to establish.

Section 59.4

The Baltic States, Bessarabia, and Northern Bukovina, 1939–1940

The Baltic states of Estonia, Latvia, and Lithuania were independent republics with authoritarian governments in the late 1930s, small armies, and economies tied to German and British markets. Under pressure after the partition of Poland, they signed mutual-assistance pacts with Moscow between 28 September and 10 October 1939, allowing Soviet garrisons totaling tens of thousands of troops and bases. Lithuania received Vilnius in the arrangement. For several months the formal independence of these states remained intact. After the German victory in France in June 1940, Moscow acted. It issued ultimatums to Lithuania on 14 June and to Latvia and Estonia on 16 June, alleging hostile conspiracies, and demanded new governments and the entry of further troops. Red Army forces occupied the three countries on 15–17 June, and emissaries from Moscow (Zhdanov in Estonia, Vyshinsky in Latvia, Dekanozov in Lithuania) supervised the installation of new cabinets.

Elections held on 14–15 July 1940, with a single approved slate and with opposition candidates excluded or disqualified, produced parliaments that requested admission to the USSR. The Supreme Soviet admitted Lithuania on 3 August, Latvia on 5 August, and Estonia on 6 August. The United States announced on 23 July 1940, in the Welles Declaration, that it would not recognize the annexations, and several other governments adopted similar policies of non-recognition. Soviet and later official Russian positions maintained for decades that the Baltic entry was voluntary and lawful; the Congress of People’s Deputies in December 1989 condemned the secret protocols that had made it possible, and the Baltic states have held ever since that they were illegally occupied and annexed. The Russian government’s positions since 1991 have disputed the term “occupation,” and the question remains politically contested. Historians distinguish between the legal claims advanced at the time, the evidence of coercion in the ultimatums, occupation, and rigged elections, and the diverse attitudes of the inhabitants.

Romania was the target of a similar process. The Soviet note of 26 June 1940 demanded Bessarabia and northern Bukovina, the latter a region not included in the secret protocol of 1939. Romania, isolated after the fall of France and advised by Germany to yield, accepted, and Soviet forces occupied the territory between 28 June and 3 July. On 2 August the Soviet authorities created the Moldavian Soviet Socialist Republic from most of Bessarabia and a strip of Ukrainian territory east of the Dniester, while northern Bukovina and southern Bessarabia were assigned to the Ukrainian SSR. The population included Romanian speakers, Ukrainians, Russians, Jews, Bulgarians, and Gagauz. The loss of territory weakened the Romanian government and contributed to Romania’s alignment with Germany and its participation in the invasion of 1941. The new western frontier of the USSR was thus the result of coercive territorial gains over only twelve months.

The three Baltic governments reacted differently to the ultimatums but none chose to fight. Lithuania’s president Antanas Smetona favored resistance but found no support in his cabinet and left for Germany; the government of Justas Paleckis was installed under Soviet supervision. In Latvia Kārlis Ulmanis remained as president until the summer of 1940 and in Estonia Konstantin Päts was kept in office until the new Soviet-managed assembly met; both were later deported and died in the USSR. The legal background mattered: in the 1920 peace treaties and in non-aggression agreements of the early 1930s Moscow had recognized the independence of the Baltic states and renounced the use of force against them, which is why the Baltic governments and the Western powers that refused recognition regarded the events of 1940 as violations of binding commitments. Soviet and later Russian arguments have stressed the mutual-assistance pacts of 1939, the strategic vulnerability of the Baltic coast, and the actions of the new parliaments.

The incorporation brought rapid social change. Banks, large industries, and most trade were nationalized in the autumn of 1940, land was redistributed, the currencies were replaced under unfavorable terms, and national armies were folded into the Red Army, with many officers arrested. The local Communist parties were tiny and the first secretaries were supervised from Moscow. Jewish communities, which had faced discrimination in some places, found some barriers lifted but saw their religious and civic institutions closed. Bessarabia and northern Bukovina had a different history. The Romanian-speaking majority of Bessarabia lived alongside Ukrainian, Russian, Jewish, Bulgarian, and Gagauz minorities, and the territory had been Russian from 1812 until 1918, a fact Soviet leaders invoked. The Romanian withdrawal was chaotic, and in its aftermath violence broke out against Jews, who were accused of welcoming the Soviet troops, as in the Dorohoi pogrom of July 1940. The annexations left Romania humiliated and available to German pressure.

Section 59.5

Sovietization, Deportations, and Repression in Newly Controlled Territories

Sovietization meant the extension of Soviet institutions, property relations, and political controls to the annexed territories. Banks, large industry, and trade were nationalized; landed estates were seized and redistributed, though large-scale collectivization was limited before 1941; schools and universities were reorganized and Russian, Ukrainian, or Belarusian curricula and Communist ideology introduced; political parties, religious associations, and civic organizations were closed or restricted; and the NKVD arrested people it classified as hostile. These included former officials, police, army officers, landowners, industrialists, clergy, leaders of national parties, and members of political organizations. Experiences varied: land reform benefited many poor peasants, and in some places Jews gained access to jobs and education from which they had been excluded, yet Jewish communal institutions, Zionist and Bundist organizations, and religious life were suppressed, and Ukrainian and Lithuanian nationalists faced arrest.

Deportations were a defining feature. Polish citizens from the annexed territories were deported to special settlements in Kazakhstan, Siberia, and northern Russia in four large waves, in February, April, and June 1940 and in June 1941. NKVD records suggest figures on the order of 320,000–330,000 people, while Polish estimates, which include other categories of deportation and arrests, run considerably higher; the totals remain debated. Families were loaded into freight cars, often with a few hours’ notice, and many died of cold, hunger, and disease in transit and in settlement. In the Baltic republics a large operation began on 14 June 1941, a week before the German invasion, deporting some 40,000 people by commonly cited figures, among them officials, officers, and their families, to camps and special settlements; similar operations occurred in Moldova and western Ukraine. Deportation was an instrument of social engineering and political security, not wartime emergency.

The most notorious crime of the period was the Katyn massacre. In the autumn of 1939 the Soviet Union held a large number of Polish military officers, police, border guards, and officials, in camps at Kozelsk, Starobelsk, and Ostashkov and in prisons in western Ukraine and Belarus. In March 1940 the Politburo approved an NKVD proposal to execute them. Between April and May 1940, approximately 22,000 people were shot, in the forest at Katyn near Smolensk, at Kalinin (Tver), at Kharkiv, and at other sites. The victims included reserve officers who were doctors, lawyers, teachers, and engineers in civilian life. The German discovery of mass graves at Katyn in 1943 led to years of Soviet denials and the attribution of the crime to Germany. The Soviet government acknowledged responsibility only in 1990, and archival documents were handed to Poland in 1992. The significance of Katyn for Polish memory and for Polish-Russian relations makes it one of the most contested issues in the history of the war.

Deportations followed procedures developed in the 1930s. NKVD teams arrived at night, listed members of a family, allowed little time to pack, and took them to railway stations where they were loaded into freight cars for journeys of weeks. The destinations were special settlements (spetsposelki) in the Arkhangelsk region, the Komi republic, the Urals, Siberia, and Kazakhstan, where deportees worked in forestry, mining, and agriculture under the supervision of the security police, in conditions of shortage and cold that produced high mortality among children and the elderly. The June 1940 wave struck many Jewish refugees from German-occupied Poland who had declined Soviet passports, in numbers of tens of thousands. In the Baltic operation of 14 June 1941, based on decisions taken in May, men were separated from their families and sent to Gulag camps, where mortality was very high, while women, children, and the elderly were sent to settlements in Siberia and the Altai. The Polish citizens deported earlier were released under an amnesty in August 1941, after the Sikorski-Mayski agreement.

The Katyn operation shows the mechanism of the killings. According to Soviet records later released, some 21,857 persons were shot under the decision of March 1940, a figure given in a 1959 note of the security chief Alexander Shelepin to Khrushchev and commonly cited: about 4,400 from the Kozelsk camp at Katyn, about 6,300 from Ostashkov at Kalinin (Mednoye), about 3,800 from Starobelsk at Kharkiv, and about 7,300 from prisons in western Ukraine and Belarus. Prisoners were told they were being released, taken in groups to execution sites, and shot, usually with pistols. The families of the missing received no news. When the German army exhumed the graves at Katyn in April 1943, the Soviet Union denied responsibility and attributed the killings to Germany; the Polish government-in-exile’s request for an international investigation led Moscow to break relations. Soviet authorities maintained this account until 1990, when Gorbachev acknowledged NKVD responsibility. The Russian State Duma stated in 2010 that Stalin and other Soviet leaders had ordered the killings.

Section 59.6

German-Soviet Cooperation, Strategic Tensions, and the Road to June 1941

For twenty-two months the Soviet Union was a supplier of raw materials to the German war economy. The commercial agreement of February 1940 and its successor of January 1941 provided for deliveries of grain, petroleum, cotton, and metals, and Soviet territory served as a route for goods imported from East Asia, including rubber, which was vital to the German economy under British blockade. Germany’s deliveries of machinery and military equipment lagged behind schedule, and Soviet deliveries continued until the eve of the invasion. This economic relationship gave the Soviet government a motive to avoid provocation, and it gave Germany a temporary advantage in the war against Britain.

Meanwhile strategic tensions deepened. German victory over France in June 1940 upset Soviet calculations of a prolonged war in the West. Soviet moves into the Baltic states and Bukovina, the latter beyond the agreed sphere, alarmed Berlin, as did Soviet interests in the Balkans and the Straits. The Tripartite Pact of September 1940 tied Germany, Italy, and Japan together, and German guarantees of Romania, and the presence of German troops there, signaled a growing German presence. Molotov’s visit to Berlin in November 1940 failed to resolve differences over Finland, Bulgaria, and the Straits. Hitler had decided in the summer of 1940 to prepare an attack on the Soviet Union, and the planning that began then led to Directive No. 21 in December (§59.7).

Soviet preparations included an extended military buildup. The Red Army expanded to several million men in 1940–1941, mechanized corps were created, new tanks such as the T-34 and KV and new aircraft entered production, fortified regions were built on the new frontier while older fortifications on the pre-1939 border were partly dismantled, and Zhukov became Chief of the General Staff in January 1941. In May 1941 Stalin became chairman of the Council of People’s Commissars, and in the spring reservists were called to training camps and divisions were moved west. Soviet planning, as revealed by documents declassified since the 1990s, included offensive options, including a draft plan proposed by the General Staff in May 1941. The thesis that Stalin planned a preventive war for the summer of 1941 has been rejected by most historians, who regard the documents as contingency planning in the context of a defensive strategy, and who note that the Red Army’s deployments and unfinished reorganization were not those of an army about to attack.

Intelligence warnings were numerous. They came from agents in Germany and Japan, from British and American sources, and from German deserters, and they pointed with increasing clarity to an invasion. Stalin received and read them, but he doubted their source and their interpretation. He believed Hitler would not open a second front before defeating Britain, suspected that some warnings were British attempts to provoke a Soviet-German war, and considered that a German ultimatum would precede any attack. The TASS communique of 14 June 1941 denied rumors of tension. The Soviet leadership was therefore neither wholly uninformed nor fully prepared; it received intelligence, interpreted it in line with its assumptions, and took partial steps while avoiding actions that might precipitate the war it feared. A directive issued on the night of 21–22 June ordering the armies to combat readiness arrived too late and was often not understood.

Soviet war planning in 1940–1941 went through several drafts. The General Staff, under Timoshenko and Zhukov, concluded in late 1940 that the main German blow would fall in the south, toward Ukraine, with its grain, coal, and industry, and concentrated the strongest forces, the Southwestern Front, there. In the event the German main effort fell in the center, through Belarus toward Smolensk and Moscow, where Soviet forces were weaker. Spring 1941 brought mobilization by stealth. Hundreds of thousands of reservists were called to training camps, additional armies were moved from the interior toward the line of the Western Dvina and Dnieper, and the border districts were reinforced; but the forces were dispersed, partially trained, and in many cases short of transport and fuel, and the Stalin line of old fortifications was no longer maintained while the new fortified regions were not complete. Stalin’s speech to military graduates of 5 May 1941 spoke of the need to prepare for war, and historians have debated whether it signaled preparations for attack; most regard it as reflecting a view that war was coming without a decision to start it.

The warnings multiplied in the final weeks. The Soviet intelligence officer Richard Sorge in Tokyo and agents in Berlin and London reported German preparations and dates; Churchill sent a personal warning in April; German reconnaissance flights over Soviet territory increased, with Soviet pilots ordered not to fire. On the evening of 21 June, according to Soviet accounts, a German soldier crossed the line and told Soviet border guards that the attack would come at dawn, and the report reached Moscow. Stalin and his colleagues, who had received many false alarms and who suspected that some came from British sources seeking to involve the USSR in the war, weighed this information against the belief that Hitler would not attack until he had settled the war in the West or issued demands. The directive sent to the border districts that night instructed commanders to prepare for possible surprise attacks but not to respond to provocations, a formula that left units confused when the attack came. The deliveries of Soviet grain and fuel to Germany continued to the last days.

Division II — Operation Barbarossa and the Soviet Military Catastrophe

Section 59.7

Operation Barbarossa: Planning a War of Conquest and Annihilation

Hitler’s decision to attack the Soviet Union combined strategic calculation with ideological conviction. Strategically, he hoped that destroying the Soviet Union would remove Britain’s last potential continental ally, secure grain, oil, and raw materials, and give Germany an economic base for a long war. Ideologically, he regarded the Soviet state as the instrument of what he called Judeo-Bolshevism and the territories of Eastern Europe as living space (Lebensraum) for German settlement, to be obtained by removing or reducing the existing population. These aims were stated in his speeches and in planning documents, and they shaped the war from the start.

Directive No. 21, signed on 18 December 1940, ordered the armed forces to prepare to crush Soviet Russia in a rapid campaign, with armored spearheads to destroy the Red Army in western Russia before it could withdraw into the interior, and an advance toward a line from Arkhangelsk to Astrakhan. The attack was originally planned for May 1941 and took place on 22 June. The Balkan campaign of April 1941 against Yugoslavia and Greece affected the timetable, but historians debate how much of the delay it explains, since the late spring thaw and flooding, incomplete logistical preparations, and the availability of motor transport also bore on the date. The plan assumed three army groups, each led by armored formations and supported by the Luftwaffe, and anticipated that the main Soviet forces would be destroyed near the frontier within weeks; logistical preparations for a long campaign were limited, and planners assumed the Red Army was weak and the Soviet system brittle. Romania, Finland, Hungary, Slovakia, and later Italy and Croatia joined the campaign as German allies.

Planning for the conduct of the war was as important as planning for operations. In March 1941 Hitler told senior officers that the war would be one of annihilation and that the usual rules of war did not apply. The “Barbarossa Jurisdiction” decree of 13 May removed the requirement that German soldiers be prosecuted for crimes against civilians. The “Commissar Order” of 6 June directed that captured political officers be killed. Economic planners in May 1941 accepted that the Wehrmacht would live off the land and that many millions of people in the Soviet Union would starve as a result, the policy often called the Hunger Plan. Planning for the postwar order, known in drafts as Generalplan Ost, envisioned the expulsion, enslavement, or death of tens of millions of Slavs and the elimination of the Jews. Not all plans were implemented, and some were revised, but the scale of intended violence is documented in German sources and explains the character of the war.

Within the German command there was disagreement over where the main blow should fall. The army leadership under Franz Halder, working from plans prepared by General Erich Marcks and refined in staff exercises in December 1940, favored concentrating strength in Army Group Center on the road to Moscow, which they regarded as the political and industrial heart of the country and where they expected the Red Army to stand. Hitler favored the flanks, with the Baltic and Leningrad in the north and Ukraine and its resources in the south, and the plan left the question of what to do after the border battles to be decided later. This ambiguity would shape the campaign of 1941. Logistics rested on the assumption that supplies could be moved by rail and by trucks over distances not exceeding about 500 kilometers beyond railheads; several hundred thousand horses, together with a mixed fleet of German and captured trucks, moved much of the infantry’s baggage, and the railways of the Soviet Union had to be converted from the wider Soviet gauge. Planners accepted that the campaign had to be won quickly because Germany lacked the reserves for a long war of attrition.

The economic and racial planning was integral. A memorandum of May 1941 from officials around Herbert Backe, the state secretary for food, stated that the army would be fed from Soviet resources and that the industrial cities of northern Russia would be cut off from the grain surplus of the south, with the result that many millions of people would starve. Göring’s Economic Staff East prepared the administration to seize industry, agriculture, and raw materials. Alfred Rosenberg, tasked in April 1941 with preparing the administration of prospective conquered territories, developed plans for civilian rule; the Reich Ministry for the Occupied Eastern Territories was established only after the invasion, on 17 July 1941. The SS under Himmler secured agreement with the army for the deployment of Einsatzgruppen in the army’s rear areas. The Wehrmacht was thus a participant in, not simply an observer of, the war’s criminal planning. Romania’s dictator Ion Antonescu and Finland’s leaders agreed to join the war for their own reasons, the recovery of lost territory above all. German propaganda presented the campaign as a defensive crusade against Bolshevism, but the evidence of planning shows that conquest, plunder, and racial policy were the objectives.

Section 59.8

June 22, 1941: The German Invasion and the Border Battles

In the early hours of 22 June 1941 German artillery and aircraft struck along a front stretching from the Baltic to the Carpathians and the Black Sea. Roughly three million German soldiers, supported by Romanian troops and other allied contingents, more than three thousand tanks, and roughly two and a half thousand aircraft, attacked in three directions. Army Group North under Field Marshal Leeb advanced from East Prussia through Lithuania and Latvia toward Leningrad. Army Group Center under Field Marshal Bock, with two panzer groups commanded by Guderian and Hoth, aimed through Belarus toward Minsk, Smolensk, and Moscow. Army Group South under Field Marshal Rundstedt, with Kleist’s panzer group and Romanian armies, attacked toward Kyiv and Ukraine. The Luftwaffe destroyed on the order of a thousand Soviet aircraft on the first day, most of them on the ground.

The Red Army’s western forces were organized in the Baltic, Western, and Kyiv special military districts, which became the Northwestern, Western, and Southwestern Fronts on the outbreak of war. They were deployed forward, close to the frontier, with supplies and airfields within reach of the German assault, and many units were not in combat order. Stalin’s caution about provocation and the late arrival of the warning directive left commanders uncertain. Soviet resistance was strongest where units were prepared or isolated; the garrison of the Brest fortress held out for weeks, and numerous local actions delayed German columns. Central directives, however, ordered counterattacks that exceeded the capacity of the troops and sometimes presumed a transfer of the war into enemy territory. In the largest tank battles of the early war, around Dubno, Lutsk, and Brody from 23 to 30 June, Soviet mechanized corps counterattacked in piecemeal fashion, suffered heavy losses, and delayed the German advance only briefly.

Allied participation extended the war’s scope. Romanian armies joined the invasion in the south on 22 June, Slovakia and Hungary followed within days, while Finland, which had mobilized and hosted German units in the north but did not attack on 22 June, entered what Finland called the Continuation War only after Soviet air raids on 25 June, aiming to recover territory lost in 1940 and, for some Finnish nationalists, to expand into eastern Karelia. These allies pursued their own aims and interests, and their conduct varied. The border battles of June 1941 destroyed or isolated large Soviet forces and opened the interior to German columns.

Soviet forces in the western military districts were numerically comparable to or larger than the attackers in men and, on paper, in tanks and aircraft; but many tanks were obsolete or awaiting repair, crews and fuel were short, divisions were understrength and dispersed, and the new mechanized corps were incomplete. Communications broke down at once: German saboteurs, including the Brandenburg special unit, cut telephone lines, and air attacks disrupted headquarters, so that Pavlov at the Western Front lost contact with armies and divisions within hours. The first directive of the Soviet high command, sent at about 7:15 on the morning of 22 June, ordered troops to destroy enemy forces that had crossed the border but forbade them to cross it themselves; a second directive that evening ordered a counteroffensive toward Suwałki and Lublin, which could not be carried out. The gap between orders and reality widened in the following days.

The border battles did not unfold uniformly. In the north, the Soviet Northwestern Front under Fyodor Kuznetsov fell back through Lithuania and Latvia. On 23 June, Lithuanian nationalists of the Lithuanian Activist Front rose against the Soviet rear, and Kaunas and Vilnius fell on 24 June; a provisional Lithuanian government was proclaimed but the German authorities did not permit it to rule. Riga fell on 1 July. In the center, the Western Front lost its forward armies in Belarus within days, and Minsk fell on 28 June. In the south, the Southwestern Front under Mikhail Kirponos held longer, and the mechanized counterattacks at Dubno–Brody delayed the German First Panzer Group by several days, but Lviv fell on 30 June, and in western Ukraine the Soviet retreat was accompanied by the NKVD prisoner killings described in §59.16. In many places civilians fled east on foot, by cart, and by train, and the evacuation of people, institutions, and factories was disorganized and incomplete.

Section 59.9

Minsk, Smolensk, Kyiv, and the Great Encirclements of 1941

The Wehrmacht’s method was to use armored groups to penetrate or bypass Soviet defenses and close their pincers behind large concentrations of troops, and then to use infantry to reduce the pockets. In the first such battle, around Białystok and Minsk, German armored forces linked up east of Minsk, which fell on 28 June, and German reports claimed more than 300,000 prisoners. Pavlov, commander of the Western Front, was recalled and was executed in July with several of his subordinates, an indication of how the Soviet leadership explained defeat. Army Group Center then advanced toward the Dnieper and Smolensk, which fell in mid-July; but Soviet counterattacks around Smolensk and Yelnya, where the first units later designated Guards formations fought in September, delayed the German advance for weeks.

In the south, German and Romanian forces encircled Soviet armies near Uman in August, taking roughly a hundred thousand prisoners, while in the north Army Group North advanced through the Baltic region, crossing Soviet and Estonian territory, and reached the approaches to Leningrad. The largest encirclement came at Kyiv. In August Hitler, preferring to seize the economic resources of Ukraine and the Crimea over an immediate advance on Moscow, diverted Guderian’s panzer group south, and Stalin refused permission to abandon the Kyiv salient. The pincers closed east of Kyiv on 16 September, the city fell on 19 September, and the Southwestern Front lost, by German claims, some 665,000 prisoners; the front commander Kirponos died in the breakout. Russian archival studies count irrecoverable Soviet losses, killed, missing, and captured, of more than three million for 1941 as a whole, though the figures and categories differ among sources.

These campaigns brought under German control a region including Belarus, the Baltic republics, most of Ukraine, and a large portion of western Russia. The German advance was not uniform and not without cost. German casualties by the end of November 1941 had reached roughly three quarters of a million, according to German army records, and the infantry divisions, dependent on horses and railways, struggled to keep pace with the armor. The rapid conquest of territory did not destroy the Soviet state’s capacity to field new armies.

The Białystok–Minsk operation showed the German method at its most effective. Panzer Groups 2 and 3 advanced on the flanks of the Western Front, met east of Minsk on 28 June, and left the Soviet Third, Tenth, and part of the Fourth Armies cut off to the west, where German infantry armies sealed the pockets against fragmentary breakouts. Most of the Western Front’s armies were destroyed or captured within two weeks, and in July the survivors fell back toward the Dnieper. At Smolensk the German advance met more resistance. Guderian’s and Hoth’s panzer groups crossed the Dnieper and Dvina in early July, encircled Soviet forces around Smolensk by late July, and took many thousands of prisoners; but Soviet counterattacks by the armies of Timoshenko’s Western Direction, including the successful recapture of the Yelnya salient in early September, delayed Army Group Center for two months and cost the Germans men and equipment they could not replace easily. On 30 July Hitler’s Directive No. 34 shifted priority from Moscow to the flanks, and the argument over that decision has been central to the debate about the campaign.

In the south, German and Romanian forces encircled the Soviet Sixth and Twelfth Armies near Uman in early August, a pocket in which the Soviet commanders were captured. Odesa was besieged by Romanian forces from August, defended by Soviet naval and army forces supplied by the Black Sea Fleet, and evacuated by sea in October, so that its defenders could join the defense of the Crimea. In the north, Army Group North took Pskov in July and reached the Luga line, and in August the evacuation of Tallinn by the Baltic Fleet cost many ships and thousands of lives under air and mine attack. The Kyiv disaster of September was the largest of these encirclements. Its causes included Stalin’s refusal to permit a timely withdrawal, in the face of advice from the General Staff, and the German decision to divert armor from the center; its consequence was the loss of an entire front and open access to the Donbas and the Crimea. After the Kyiv disaster, the Red Army had lost most of the formations that existed on the frontier in June, and the question of how it could survive without them became the central problem of the autumn.

Cause and Effect

Reasons for the Initial Soviet Military Disasters

Long-Term Conditions

The 1937–1938 purge of the officer corps, which removed or discouraged many experienced commanders; rapid expansion of the army and mechanized forces; doctrine that assumed the war would be fought on enemy territory; incomplete modernization of equipment and communications.

Intermediate Developments

The western frontier moved forward in 1939–1940; fortifications on the old border were partly dismantled while new ones were unfinished; mechanized corps were being formed but lacked equipment; reorganization of the high command in 1941.

Short-Term Triggers

Stalin’s caution about provocation and delayed alert; forward deployment of forces and depots close to the border; German surprise, air superiority on the first day, and combined-arms tactics; counterattack orders that exceeded available forces.

Effects

Encirclement and loss of millions of men and large stocks of equipment; loss of industrial and agricultural regions; German advance to Leningrad, Moscow, and the Don within months; harsh discipline and command reforms.

Historical complexity: No single cause is adequate. The purges reduced experience, but the army’s failures also reflected the speed of its expansion; surprise mattered, but German advantages in training, coordination, and air power would have produced heavy losses in any case; and the Soviet leadership’s mistakes interacted with structural factors rather than replacing them. Equally, the German victories did not decide the war because the Red Army’s depth, manpower, and capacity to reconstitute remained.

Section 59.10

Stalin, the State Defense Committee, and the Crisis of Soviet Command

The Soviet system of command was reorganized in the first weeks of the war. On 23 June the Stavka (the headquarters of the supreme command) was created, and was reorganized on 10 July and again in August, when Stalin was named Supreme Commander. The State Defense Committee (GKO), created on 30 June 1941 with Stalin as chairman, Molotov as deputy chairman, and Voroshilov, Malenkov, and Beria among its early members, concentrated emergency authority over the party, state, military, and economy; later members included Voznesensky, Mikoyan, and Kaganovich. Stalin also served as chairman of the Council of People’s Commissars from May and as defense commissar from July. His address of 3 July, the first after the invasion, appealed to the people as “brothers and sisters,” described the war as a patriotic struggle, and called for scorched-earth tactics and partisan warfare in occupied territory.

Stalin’s reaction to the first days of the war has been described, on the basis of memoirs, notably Anastas Mikoyan’s, as a period of withdrawal in late June, but the evidence is partial and later accounts were shaped by the political context of de-Stalinization, so historians treat it with caution. Whatever the truth of the episode, Stalin resumed direction of the war and became its principal strategic authority. He often micromanaged operations, refused withdrawals that would have saved forces, as at Kyiv, and accepted advice from the General Staff only gradually, especially from Shaposhnikov, Zhukov, and later Vasilevsky.

Discipline was enforced harshly. A decree reintroduced political commissars with equal authority to commanders on 16 July, a dual command that lasted until October 1942. NKVD special sections monitored soldiers. Order No. 270 of 16 August 1941 declared those who surrendered to be traitors and made their families liable to reprisal. Military tribunals sentenced large numbers of soldiers: Russian archival data indicate that more than 150,000 soldiers were sentenced to death during the war, though not all sentences were carried out. These measures reflected both a real crisis of morale and a political system that treated failure as treason. They coexisted with changes aimed at efficiency, including the abolition of some of the more ineffective regional high commands and the gradual appointment of younger commanders.

The new structures were improvised and changed several times. The Stavka created on 23 June under Timoshenko was reorganized on 10 July with Stalin as chairman and with the General Staff chief, Zhukov, and others among its members; on 10 July three regional “directions” under Voroshilov (Northwest), Timoshenko (West), and Budyonny (Southwest) were created to coordinate fronts, but these headquarters, staffed by prominent figures of the civil-war generation, proved of limited use and were gradually dissolved. Zhukov, who had warned Stalin about the Kyiv salient, was replaced as Chief of the General Staff by Boris Shaposhnikov in late July, and was sent to command the Reserve Front near Smolensk and then at Leningrad. Stalin’s decision to take direct charge of the defense commissariat, the Stavka, and the GKO ensured that all decisions of consequence passed through him, which gave unity of direction at the cost of rigidity and delay. The party’s role in the armed forces was increased by the reinstatement of political commissars, whose duty was to ensure the political reliability of commanders and the morale of the troops, and the power of the NKVD’s special departments to arrest soldiers on suspicion of defeatism was reaffirmed.

The treatment of failure was severe. After the destruction of the Western Front, Pavlov, his chief of staff Vladimir Klimovskikh, the signals chief Andrei Grigoriev, and others were arrested and shot in July, charged with treason and cowardice, though the evidence suggests that responsibility lay as much with the Soviet leadership’s own decisions as with the commanders on the spot; they were posthumously rehabilitated in 1957. Order No. 270 of August 1941 branded Lieutenant-General Ivan Kachalov and other commanders who had been surrounded as traitors, and ordered that the families of those who surrendered be arrested. Stalin’s son Yakov Dzhugashvili, captured in July, was among those affected. The harshness of these measures reflected fear that the army would disintegrate, and it coexisted with pragmatic adjustments, such as the promotion of commanders who had shown initiative and the creation of the Guards title to reward units that fought well. The balance between terror and incentive would continue to characterize Soviet command throughout the war.

Section 59.11

Retreat, Resistance, and the Reconstitution of the Red Army

The Red Army that survived the summer was a different force from the one that had met the invasion. The mobilization decree of 22 June called up men born between 1905 and 1918, and the regular call-up continued throughout the year. Reserve armies were formed in the interior, including divisions from Siberia and the Far East. In Moscow and Leningrad, volunteer people’s militia divisions were organized from workers and students with little training, and some were sent to the front with heavy losses. Hundreds of thousands of civilians, mostly women, were mobilized to dig anti-tank ditches and trenches around the major cities.

Soviet commanders adapted. The unwieldy mechanized corps were dissolved in July and replaced by smaller tank brigades; rifle divisions were reduced in size; and units that fought well, as at Yelnya, received the honorific title of Guards. New weapons arrived, including the T-34 and KV tanks, the Il-2 attack aircraft, and the Katyusha multiple rocket launcher, first used at Orsha in July. Communications and logistics improved slowly, and the Red Army’s losses in equipment were partly made good by factories moving east (§59.26). The army’s tactical doctrine remained crude, and many attacks suffered heavy casualties because of insufficient coordination.

The German advance met resistance of varying quality. Some units surrendered quickly, and others fought to the end; in some regions civilian populations hindered or helped the Red Army depending on their experience of Soviet rule. The army’s capacity to continue depended on factors beyond the front line: the size of the population and its available reserves, the vast distances that stretched German supply lines, the change of rail gauge, and the German army’s limited reserves. The failure of the German plan to destroy the Red Army in the border area gave the Soviet Union time to rebuild, and the disasters of 1941 did not lead to a collapse of the state, which remained able to mobilize resources and enforce obedience.

The Soviet mobilization system proved to be a source of strength. The reservists called up in June, men with military service behind them, supplied the bulk of the divisions formed in the interior during the summer and autumn, and the mobilization in the war’s first six months brought millions of men into the army to replace those lost. New divisions were formed in the Volga region, the Urals, Siberia, and Central Asia, trained for a few weeks, and thrown into battle with whatever equipment was available. The loss of arms at the frontier was severe: many armories were captured or abandoned in the retreat, and in July Soviet rifle divisions on the Smolensk front were sometimes short of rifles and ammunition. Training was rudimentary, and in the early battles the inexperience of officers and men produced casualties out of proportion to results. The civilian population contributed labor: in Moscow alone several hundred thousand men and women, many of them students and housewives, dug trenches and tank traps in the summer and autumn, a hardship that is easily forgotten in accounts of the battles themselves.

Soviet staff officers learned from experience that large mechanized formations required better coordination, communications, and maintenance than the Red Army could provide, and that smaller, simpler units were more practical. The reorganization of rifle divisions and the replacement of mechanized corps by tank brigades reduced the need for sophisticated command but also reduced capability. The Soviet air force, having lost much of its strength on the ground, concentrated on ground support and fought at a technological disadvantage until new aircraft reached the front. The army also benefited from conditions on the German side. The advance had stretched supply lines, the Wehrmacht’s casualties among its best formations were considerable, and German intelligence underestimated the number of Soviet divisions that could be raised: in July, German staff officers estimated about two hundred Soviet divisions and found, in the following weeks, that they had to destroy many more. The Red Army’s survival in 1941 depended on these combinations of population, space, improvisation, and German miscalculation.

Section 59.12

Nazi Occupation and the Administration of Conquered Soviet Territories

German-occupied Soviet territory was governed by several overlapping authorities. In army group rear areas the Wehrmacht retained control. Civil administration was established in the Reichskommissariat Ostland, covering the Baltic states and much of Belarus under Hinrich Lohse, and the Reichskommissariat Ukraine under Erich Koch, with its capital at Rivne, while eastern Galicia was added to the General Government in August 1941. Romania administered the area between the Dniester and the Southern Bug as Transnistria, with Odesa as a center. Alfred Rosenberg headed the Reich Ministry for the Occupied Eastern Territories. Economic exploitation was directed by an economic staff under Göring’s authority.

German policy aimed at extracting food and resources for the Wehrmacht and the Reich and at establishing racial hierarchy. Jews were targeted for murder, Roma were persecuted, and Slavs were regarded as inferior and in part as a labor reservoir. Baltic peoples were treated somewhat more favorably, in part as potentially assimilable. Collective farms were largely retained, renamed, under the “New Agrarian Order” of 1942, because they facilitated requisition. Food policy led to famine in cities such as Kyiv and Kharkiv, where tens of thousands died of hunger in the winter of 1941–1942, and to the starvation of prisoners (§59.14). Education was restricted, and cultural institutions were closed or controlled. In 1942 labor recruitment for the Reich expanded, first by incentives and then increasingly by coercion and raids, sending millions of “Ostarbeiter” (eastern workers) to Germany over the course of the war.

Local administrations, police, and economic agencies were staffed by local people, and some regions saw experimental autonomy, such as the Lokot district in the Bryansk area under Bronislav Kaminski. In some regions, especially western Ukraine and the Baltic states, where Soviet rule since 1939 had brought arrests and deportations, parts of the population initially welcomed German forces as liberators or hoped for national recognition. German policy disappointed such hopes: when Ukrainian nationalists proclaimed a state at Lviv on 30 June 1941, German authorities arrested the leaders, including Stepan Bandera. The experience of occupation varied by region, nationality, social position, and time, and the gap between expectation and reality widened as the occupation continued.

The administrative picture was complicated by rivalries among German agencies. Koch, the Reichskommissar for Ukraine, was a Nazi party official who treated Ukrainians as a subject people to be exploited, closed most secondary schools and universities, and opposed concessions to Ukrainian nationalism; he sometimes clashed with Rosenberg, who favored more differentiated policies, and with the army, which wanted order and food. In Belarus, the General Commissariat of White Ruthenia under Wilhelm Kube was part of Reichskommissariat Ostland; Kube protested against the killing of German Jews deported to Minsk but supervised the policy that destroyed local Jews. In the Baltic territories German policy allowed limited self-administration and the formation of auxiliary units, while closing parliamentary institutions and expropriating property. In the military zone, the army’s economic inspectorates took control of agriculture and industry, requisitioned livestock and grain, and sent resources to Germany and to the army. In Transnistria, the Romanian administration under Gheorghe Alexianu ran a regime of deportation, forced labor, and killings of Jews.

The economic consequences for civilians were severe. The Germans took grain and livestock from villages, often leaving little for the owners, and took machinery and raw materials from factories. The great cities, cut off from rural supplies, were left to starve: in Kyiv, the food supply for civilians was cut to a minimum in autumn 1941, and tens of thousands died in the following winter, as in Kharkiv, where the population fell by hundreds of thousands through death, flight, and deportation. Villages suffered from requisitions and the burning of settlements in reprisal for partisan activity. At the same time the occupation brought unexpected openings for some groups. Churches that had been closed under Soviet rule reopened in many areas, in part because German authorities hoped to use religion to win cooperation; Orthodox parishes revived, and the Ukrainian autocephalous church re-emerged in parts of Ukraine. This religious revival, of which German authorities took advantage, is one of the many ways in which occupation was experienced not as a single process but as a set of local situations.

Differences among regions were large. The western territories annexed in 1939–1940, with their memory of Soviet deportations, saw more initial cooperation and more nationalist hope; the eastern Ukrainian cities and the central Russian areas, with their longer Soviet history, saw less enthusiasm. The Crimea and the Caucasus, which experienced shorter occupations, saw different dynamics. The occupation of western Russia, including Smolensk, Bryansk, and Orel, was largely a military one with local self-administration, as in the Lokot district. These differences make it impossible to speak of a single occupation experience, and the Soviet state’s later treatment of the occupied populations, including investigations of “collaborators,” cannot be understood without them.

Section 59.13

The Holocaust in the Occupied Soviet Union

Mass murder of Jews followed the German advance from the first days. Four Einsatzgruppen (A, B, C, and D), mobile killing units of the SS Security Police and Security Service numbering a few thousand men, operated behind the front, assisted by Order Police battalions, Waffen-SS units, and local auxiliaries and, in many instances, with logistical or operational help from the Wehrmacht. In the early weeks the victims were mostly Jewish men, but by August 1941 shootings were extended to women and children. This pattern, usually described as the “Holocaust by bullets,” took place in ravines, pits, forests, and anti-tank ditches at or near the victims’ homes. Gas vans were introduced in the autumn. German reports in early 1942 claimed hundreds of thousands of victims, and scholars estimate that roughly two million Jews were killed in the territories of the prewar Soviet Union, depending on how boundaries are defined.

Several sites became symbols of the killings. At Babi Yar (Babyn Yar), a ravine at Kyiv, German units shot, by their own report, 33,771 Jews on 29–30 September 1941, shortly after the capture of the city; killings there continued in later months and included Roma, Soviet prisoners, and others. At Ponary (Paneriai), near Vilnius, tens of thousands of Jews and others, about 70,000 according to commonly cited estimates, were shot. At Rumbula, near Riga, some 25,000 Latvian and German Jews were killed in two days at the end of November and the beginning of December 1941. At Kaunas’s Ninth Fort, Kamianets-Podilskyi, Minsk, and many other places, thousands more perished. Romanian forces and authorities carried out massacres at Odesa in October 1941 and subsequent deportations to Transnistria in which many thousands died.

The perpetrators included Germans and their local collaborators. Pogroms in Lviv, Kaunas, and other towns in late June and July 1941 involved local people, sometimes encouraged or organized by German units and, in Lviv, exploited after the discovery of NKVD prisoner killings (§59.16). Local auxiliary police, such as the Arajs Kommando in Latvia, took part in mass shootings. Historians debate the proportions of German direction and local initiative in specific events, such as the killings at Jedwabne in July 1941, but agree that the Holocaust in the East was planned and directed by German authorities. Soviet authorities evacuated many Jews, in part by chance and in part because of their occupations, but Soviet propaganda did not warn the Jewish population of the specific danger, and many who could have fled did not know the threat. After the war, Soviet commemoration generally referred to the victims as peaceful Soviet citizens, obscuring the targeting of Jews, a point that later Soviet and post-Soviet writers and memorials contested.

The killings were embedded in a wider decision-making process. The decision to kill all Jews, not only men of military age, was taken by the SS leadership in the summer of 1941 in a process whose exact timing is debated among historians; what is not in doubt is that by August the Einsatzgruppen and their partners were murdering entire communities. The Wannsee Conference of 20 January 1942 discussed the coordination of the wider program, in which the Soviet Union’s Jews were already being killed. Reports from the Einsatzgruppen to Berlin, which survive, gave running totals by region. The report of Einsatzgruppe A’s commander Karl Jäger, dated 1 December 1941, enumerated well over 100,000 killings in Lithuania, a figure indicating the rate of killing in that country in the second half of 1941, and other reports for Belarus and Ukraine give similarly shocking totals. The scale and speed of killing differed by region and depended on the local administration, the availability of killers, and the policies of the German commanders.

Ghettos in the occupied Soviet Union were often temporary holding places on the way to killing. In Vilnius, Kaunas, Riga, Minsk, and other cities the Germans confined Jews in ghettos, used them for forced labor, and periodically carried out “actions” to reduce their number, while smaller communities were killed in place. Some Jews survived through labor in the ghettos, through hiding with non-Jews, or in partisan units. Most Jews in occupied Soviet territory had no opportunity to escape, but the evacuation of civilians by Soviet authorities, in which party officials, workers in strategic industries, and their families had priority, saved many Jews as well as others, especially from the eastern cities. Historians continue to study the evidence for the Holocaust in the East, including German reports, survivor testimony, postwar trials, the Soviet Extraordinary State Commission’s records of 1943–1945, and the results of forensic work at the sites, and have increasingly drawn attention to the role of local witnesses and of perpetrators’ testimony in trials.

Regional Context

Mass Violence in Occupied Ukraine and Belarus

Ukraine and Belarus bore the greatest weight of German occupation. Belarus, a territory of forests and marshes with a pre-1939 population of mixed Belarusian, Polish, Jewish, and Russian composition, lost a proportion of its population generally estimated at between one-fifth and one-quarter, through the killing of Jews, anti-partisan reprisals, famine, deportation, and military operations. Ukraine, whose territory combined the old Soviet republic with newly annexed western regions, suffered several million deaths, including the murder of most of its Jewish population. Occupation also destroyed cities and villages, and the retreating Soviet authorities had destroyed industrial installations, such as the Dnieper hydroelectric dam in August 1941.

These regions had experienced the Great Famine of 1932–1933 (Chapter 56), Soviet deportations, and the terror of the late 1930s, and in western Ukraine and Belarus the Soviet occupation of 1939–1941. The legacy shaped how people interpreted the arrival of the Germans, but the realities of occupation overwhelmed most early expectations. Historical memory in Ukraine and Belarus of this period has included the mass murder of Jews, the Soviet partisan movement, nationalist insurgency, and civilian victims, and remains a subject of research and political debate.

Section 59.14

Soviet Prisoners of War, Starvation, and Civilian Mass Death

About 5.7 million Soviet soldiers fell into German hands during the war, of whom about 3.3 million died in captivity, according to the estimate of the United States Holocaust Memorial Museum; the great majority of captures and deaths occurred in 1941–1942. The Soviet Union had not ratified the 1929 Geneva Convention on prisoners of war, and Germany treated Soviet prisoners as outside any legal protection. Non-ratification did not remove the protections owed to prisoners under customary international law, including principles codified in the Hague Regulations of 1907, which prohibited the starvation and murder of prisoners; German authorities disregarded them. Prisoners were held in open fields, in improvised camps and in former Soviet barracks, often without shelter, adequate food, or medical care. Many were forced to march long distances on foot. Typhus and starvation killed thousands each day in the late autumn and winter of 1941–1942, and by the spring of 1942 on the order of two million had died, according to historians who have studied the camps.

Selection and execution accompanied neglect. Einsatzkommandos screened prisoners and shot Jews, political officers, and others deemed dangerous, in numbers that run into the tens of thousands. Soviet prisoners were among the first victims of gassing experiments at Auschwitz in September 1941. German planners had decided before the invasion that the prisoners would not be fed from German resources, and when military and economic leaders recognized in late 1941 that labor was needed they improved conditions only partially. Historians generally agree that the mass deaths resulted from German decisions to neglect, starve, and kill, not simply from the disorganization of a rapid advance, even though logistics added to the catastrophe.

Civilian mass death had similar roots. Requisitioning of food, restrictions on the movement of people, blockade of cities, and reprisal killings led to deaths well beyond those from combat. The Soviet side compounded the plight of the prisoners: Stalin’s order of August 1941 treated surrender as treason, and Soviet authorities did not provide the International Committee of the Red Cross with the cooperation that might have benefited prisoners. Those who survived and returned in 1944–1945 would be subjected to screening and, in many cases, suspicion (Chapter 60).

Conditions in the camps were designed or permitted to kill. The first camps, in the army’s rear areas, were open enclosures of barbed wire in which tens of thousands were held without shelter in the autumn, and the transit and permanent camps in Poland and East Prussia, known as Dulags and Stalags, were hardly better. Rations, when they were provided, were far below subsistence, and prisoners ate grass, leaves, and, in some cases, the bodies of the dead; typhus and dysentery spread unchecked; prisoners too weak to walk were shot on the march. The mass deaths of autumn 1941, when thousands died daily in some camps, were not an accident of logistics, since the army and the economic authorities could have transferred some resources but chose not to. Only from late 1941, when labor shortages became acute and the war lasted longer than expected, did the authorities begin to use Soviet prisoners as workers in German industry and agriculture, improving conditions for some while continuing to treat them as the lowest category of prisoners.

Soviet policy also complicated the fate of captured soldiers. Soviet authorities often refused cooperation with prisoner-information exchanges, and Order No. 270 treated surrender in harsh and sweeping terms. These policies hindered contact, relief efforts, and the treatment of returnees, but they did not diminish Germany’s responsibility under international law for the mass starvation and murder of Soviet prisoners. In the occupied territories civilians sometimes brought food to prisoners at risk of shooting, and some prisoners were released to their families if relatives or local authorities vouched for them, a practice that in some regions saved lives. For those who survived, liberation in 1944–1945 brought screening by the NKVD, imprisonment or labor service for some, and a stigma that lasted for decades; the history of the prisoners did not become a subject of open scholarship until the late Soviet period. Historians generally attribute the deaths primarily to German policy, while continuing to analyze the proportion of deaths by region, camp type, and period.

Section 59.15

Resistance, Partisans, Collaboration, and Civilian Choices, 1941–1942

Soviet leaders called for partisan warfare in the rear in July 1941, and party and NKVD organizers were left behind in many regions to form underground and partisan detachments. The first year was difficult: many detachments were small, poorly supplied, lacked local support, and were destroyed. In the winter of 1941–1942 and during 1942 the movement expanded, especially in the forests and marshes of Belarus, northern Ukraine, and the Bryansk region, and the Central Staff of the Partisan Movement was created in May 1942. Partisan operations disrupted communications and diverted German forces, but they also exposed villages to reprisals and sometimes involved requisitions and violence against civilians who were suspected of cooperating with the Germans. German anti-partisan policy involved hostage-taking, with orders in September 1941 that set the ratio of executions to German deaths at fifty to a hundred, and the burning of villages.

Collaboration took many forms: administrative service in local governments, police and auxiliary units, economic cooperation, informing, and military service as volunteer auxiliaries (Hiwis) in German units, which by 1942 numbered in the hundreds of thousands. Motives varied: opposition to the Soviet system after collectivization, the terror, and deportations; the desire for national autonomy; antisemitism; opportunism; fear of starvation; coercion. Nationalists in western Ukraine and the Baltic hoped for state independence, and many soon fell out with the German authorities. These categories were not fixed, and individuals sometimes moved between roles. Assigning collective responsibility to a nationality or region misrepresents the evidence; the scale and nature of collaboration differed by place and time and are subjects of continuing research.

Civilians in occupied territory made choices within narrow constraints. Many tried to survive through agriculture, barter, and the black market. Some hid or aided Jews at great risk; individuals recognized as Righteous Among the Nations include people from every part of the occupied USSR. Jewish people also resisted: Jewish partisans, such as the groups led by the Bielski brothers in the forests of western Belarus, organized rescue and fighting units from 1942. The overall pattern shows a spectrum of behavior, from active resistance through accommodation to participation in German crimes, shaped by local circumstances and by the actions of occupiers and Soviet authorities.

The partisan movement in 1941–1942 was smaller and less effective than later Soviet accounts suggested. In Belarus, where forests and swamps offered cover, detachments grew from remnants of encircled Red Army units, local Communists, and escaped prisoners, and in the Bryansk forests, northern Ukraine, and the Smolensk region units operated against railways and supply lines. They were poorly armed, short of food, and dependent on villagers, and many were destroyed in the first winter. A regional underground of party and Komsomol members operated in towns such as Minsk, where the Germans destroyed it in 1942. After the creation of the Central Staff in May 1942, supplies by air and trained officers arrived, and the movement began to grow, but it was in 1943 that it reached its height. Relations between partisans and villagers were ambivalent. Partisans needed food, which they took by requisition, and they punished suspected collaborators, while German reprisals often fell on villages near partisan activity, producing cycles of violence in which civilians were the principal victims.

German security forces responded with large “anti-partisan” operations in which entire villages were burned and their inhabitants killed, a policy that went beyond military necessity and was shaped by racial assumptions. Collaboration took many local forms. Auxiliary police units, formed from local volunteers under German command, took part in guarding ghettos, escorting deportations, and killings, in some cases at high levels of responsibility, as in the Latvian Arajs Kommando. Ukrainian nationalists of the OUN under Stepan Bandera and a rival faction under Andrei Melnyk had hoped for support in building an independent state; after the Germans suppressed the proclamation of 30 June 1941, many nationalists turned against the occupiers, while others continued to serve in auxiliary units, and in 1942–1943 a nationalist insurgent army began to form. In the Baltic republics, local police and security battalions were formed. People who took such roles acted for different reasons, and historians have drawn attention to the importance of examining individual cases, the changing choices of 1941, 1942, and 1943, and the effect of Soviet and German policies on local communities.

Rescue of Jews occurred in every region, usually at great risk to the rescuers, whom the Germans punished by death, in many cases with their entire families. The scale of rescue varied, and the motives of rescuers ranged from religious conviction and personal friendship to payment. Survivors often relied on networks of acquaintances and on the cooperation of local families, convents, and priests, such as Metropolitan Sheptytsky of the Greek Catholic Church in Lviv, who sheltered Jews in monasteries. Jewish resistance included the partisan units in western Belarus and Ukraine, and ghetto underground organizations, although in 1941–1942 few had the weapons or opportunity to resist.

Few had the opportunity to escape, and rescue depended on the decisions of neighbors and on remote chance.
A peasant household in Belarus:For villagers the occupation brought requisition of grain and livestock, forced labor, and the risk of reprisals if partisans operated nearby. Many were forced to choose between German demands and partisan demands, with severe penalties from both sides.

The differences among these perspectives do not imply moral equivalence. German policy was the source of the occupation’s extreme violence, and the choices of civilians were made under conditions of coercion and terror.

Section 59.16

Soviet Internal Security, Wartime Deportations, and Political Discipline

The Soviet state also used violence against its own population. As the Red Army retreated from western Ukraine, western Belarus, and the Baltic region in June–July 1941, the NKVD murdered large numbers of prisoners held in local prisons, rather than evacuating them, in places including Lviv, Lutsk, and Tartu. Estimates of the total across the western region run into the tens of thousands, with figures for individual prisons disputed. When German forces entered Lviv and other towns they publicized the killings, and the discovery of the bodies was exploited in pogroms against Jews, who were blamed as supporters of Communism. The killings did not cause these pogroms, which were encouraged and organized by German authorities and local nationalists, but they formed part of the context.

The deportation of Soviet Germans began in late August 1941. A decree of 28 August, justified by allegations that the German population of the Volga region included saboteurs and spies, for which no evidence was offered, ordered the deportation of the Volga Germans, and a further decree of 7 September formally dissolved the Volga German Autonomous Republic. More than 400,000 people were deported to Siberia and Kazakhstan. Germans from Ukraine, Crimea, the Caucasus, and other regions were deported in the following months, and in 1942 German men were mobilized into labor battalions (the “labor army”). Over the following years total deportations of Soviet Germans reached the hundreds of thousands (estimates exceed 800,000). Finns from the Leningrad region and members of some other groups were also deported in 1942. These were collective punishments based on ethnicity, not individual guilt.

Surveillance and discipline intensified. A decree of July 1941 imposed military-tribunal penalties for spreading false rumors. Special departments of the NKVD supervised the army. Order No. 227 of 28 July 1942 (§59.22) required penal units and blocking detachments. These measures should be distinguished from Nazi occupation policy: the Soviet security apparatus acted as the agent of a state seeking to preserve itself and relied on arbitrary punishment, but it did not pursue a policy of racial extermination. The later deportations of 1943–1944, which affected Chechens, Ingush, Crimean Tatars, and other peoples, belong to the later phase of the war and are discussed in Chapter 60.

The NKVD’s killing of prisoners in the retreat was ordered from above. In late June 1941 the security leadership instructed local NKVD organs to evacuate or, where that proved impossible, to shoot prisoners considered dangerous, and in the prisons of Lviv, Lutsk, Dubno, Rivne, Berdychiv, Tartu, and elsewhere thousands of political prisoners and others were killed, often together with common criminals. In Lviv, bodies were found in prison cellars when the Germans arrived, and in the following days pogroms against Jews took place, partly encouraged by Ukrainian nationalist activists and German units who blamed Jews for Communism. Elsewhere prisoners were forced on marches east, in the course of which many were shot or died. The documentation is incomplete, and the numbers killed in specific prisons are still debated, but there is general agreement that the NKVD killed tens of thousands in the retreat in western regions.

Other wartime repression targeted people of particular nationalities or categories. Soviet Germans were deported in the autumn of 1941 from the Volga region, Ukraine, Crimea, and the Caucasus, and in the winter of 1941–1942 and in 1942 German men were mobilized into labor battalions in the Urals and Siberia, forming the “labor army,” where mortality from hunger, cold, and overwork was high. Finns in the Leningrad region were deported in 1942. Military tribunals and special NKVD detachments dealt with those accused of desertion, panic, and anti-Soviet statements. At the same time, wartime decrees released large numbers of Gulag prisoners, including some for army service and factory labor, primarily from selected nonpolitical categories. These releases reflected manpower and administrative needs rather than an official acknowledgment that their earlier convictions had been mistaken. The Soviet security apparatus was therefore both a source of fear and a resource for mobilization, and the wartime state used coercion alongside appeals to patriotism.

Division IV — Leningrad, Moscow, and the Survival of the Soviet State

Section 59.17

The Encirclement and Siege of Leningrad, 1941–1942

Leningrad was the Soviet Union’s second city, a center of heavy industry, shipbuilding, and science, the base of the Baltic Fleet, and a symbol of the revolution. Army Group North advanced rapidly through the Baltic region in July and August 1941. German units cut the Moscow-Leningrad railway at Chudovo on 20 August, reached the Neva at Mga at the end of the month, and captured Shlisselburg on 8 September, closing the land route to the city. Finnish forces advanced on the Karelian Isthmus to roughly the 1939 frontier and from the north of Lake Ladoga to the Svir River, but did not attack the city itself. Soviet forces held the Leningrad perimeter, with the Baltic Fleet’s guns and aircraft, and with the Ladoga route as the sole link to the rest of the country.

Hitler decided in the autumn of 1941 not to storm the city but to starve it into submission and destroy it by bombardment, in part because of the cost of street fighting and in part because the German administration would have had to feed its population. German armor was withdrawn from the Leningrad front for the attack on Moscow in September, which reduced the pressure but left the siege in place. Zhukov took command of the Leningrad front on 13 September and left for Moscow in October. Soviet counterattacks toward Sinyavino and the Volkhov front tried to break the ring, with severe losses; the German capture of Tikhvin in November, reversed by a Soviet offensive in December, threatened the supply line to Lake Ladoga. The Lyuban operation in early 1942 failed, and the siege lasted until January 1944.

The geography of the siege defined its character. Leningrad lay on the Neva delta at the head of the Gulf of Finland, bordered to the northeast by Lake Ladoga, the largest lake in Europe. German forces on the south and southeast and Finnish forces on the north left only the Ladoga route and a narrow strip of territory west of the city, the Oranienbaum bridgehead, in Soviet hands. The Baltic Fleet’s ships were anchored in the Neva and at Kronstadt, and sailors were drawn into ground units; the fleet’s guns supported the defense. Soviet forces held the Neva bridgehead near Nevskaya Dubrovka, the “Nevsky Patch,” which became the site of repeated bloody attacks to break the ring. The Germans, with their siege artillery and aircraft, shelled the city and bombed it throughout the autumn; the killing of civilians by bombardment, though far smaller than the toll of starvation, was constant.

Operations in 1941–1942 revolved around the effort to break the ring or to prevent its tightening. In the autumn the Germans and Finns attempted to link up east of Lake Ladoga, with the German capture of Tikhvin in November; the Soviet counteroffensive that recaptured Tikhvin in December preserved the rail connection that fed the Ladoga road. The Soviet Volkhov Front attempted to break the blockade in the Lyuban operation of January–June 1942, in which the Second Shock Army was surrounded and destroyed, and its commander Andrei Vlasov, captured in July, later cooperated with the Germans. The failure of these attempts meant that the siege continued into 1943 and 1944. On the Karelian Isthmus, Finnish forces generally stopped near the pre-Winter War frontier, and Mannerheim declined a direct assault on Leningrad. Elsewhere, however, Finnish troops advanced beyond the old frontier into Soviet East Karelia and reached the Svir River. Both this advance and the limits of Finnish participation in the siege matter to the debate over Finland’s relationship with its German partner.

Section 59.18

Leningrad Under Siege: Hunger, Evacuation, and Civilian Survival

At the start of the siege the city held roughly two and a half to three million people, including refugees from the Baltic and surrounding regions. Only a limited evacuation had taken place before the ring closed. German bombing of the Badaev food warehouses on 8 September destroyed part of the city’s food stocks, though the larger problem was that stocks were small relative to the population. Rations were cut repeatedly; on 20 November the daily bread ration fell to 250 grams for workers and 125 grams for office workers, dependents, and children, with bread containing a considerable proportion of cellulose and other substitutes. The winter of 1941–1942 was exceptionally cold, and fuel, water, and electricity failed. Transport broke down, and residents dragged bodies on sleds to cemeteries or left them in the streets.

Mortality reached its peak between December 1941 and February 1942, when thousands of people died each day by some accounts. The official postwar Soviet count, about 632,000, concerns deaths from starvation; casualties from bombing and shelling were tabulated separately, and broader estimates of civilian mortality, which depend on how the population and the geographic area are defined, range from roughly 800,000 to more than one million when deaths among evacuees and in the surrounding area are counted. The largest part of the starvation deaths occurred in the first winter. NKVD records documented cannibalism and the trade in human flesh as well as theft of ration cards, alongside acts of solidarity, rescue, and self-sacrifice. The ice road across Lake Ladoga, the “Road of Life,” opened on 22 November 1941. Trucks carried food and fuel into the city and evacuated civilians on the return trip under bombing, with heavy losses of vehicles. Ration increases from late December and especially after January 1942, together with improved supply, reduced the death rate; hundreds of thousands left over the ice, and over a million were evacuated by various routes over the course of the siege.

Cultural and civic life continued in ways that became central to the memory of the siege. Radio broadcasts, including the poet Olga Berggolts’s, sustained morale; staff of the Hermitage and Public Library protected collections; scientists preserved the seed collections of the Vavilov Institute. Shostakovich’s Seventh Symphony, begun in Leningrad and premiered in Kuibyshev in March 1942, was performed by a depleted orchestra in the besieged city on 9 August 1942. Historians now study the siege using diaries, NKVD reports, ration records, and oral history, which show a more complex picture than the later official narrative of unanimous heroism, including hunger crime, inequality in the distribution of food, and the divisions between privileged officials and the starving population.

Food supply was the central problem. At the start of the blockade stocks of flour, grain, and other foods in the city amounted to a few weeks of supplies at normal consumption, and the authorities had not stockpiled for a long siege. Rationing was introduced in July and the norms cut five times between September and November, with workers in heavy industry receiving more than other categories; by 20 November the lowest norm was 125 grams of bread a day, much of it made from substitutes. The ration, even when distributed, was insufficient, and people died of dystrophy, as the condition was called in Soviet medicine, in factories, in the streets, and in their homes. Leningrad’s party and city leaders, including Andrei Zhdanov, received better food than most, and the privileged access to canteens and stores was a source of resentment recorded in diaries and NKVD reports. Ordinary people sought food through barter, by selling possessions, by eating pets, wallpaper paste, and leather, and in some cases by crime.

Everyday life was transformed by the failure of utilities. Water pipes froze in the winter of 1941–1942, forcing residents to draw water from the Neva and canals; heating failed, so that people burned furniture and books and lived in a single room with a small stove; electricity was cut except for priority users; trams stopped running in December. Hospitals lacked heating and medicines, and schools and universities, where they functioned, did so under extraordinary conditions. Children and the elderly died first, and men tended to die earlier than women. The dead were taken to collection points, often by relatives pulling bodies on sleds, and buried in mass graves such as those at the Piskarevskoye cemetery. At the same time acts of solidarity, such as the sharing of rations, the organization of children’s homes, the care of orphans, and rescue by neighbors, were frequent and are recorded in diaries and memoirs.

The Ladoga route gradually relieved the crisis. The ice road, opened in November, was at first unreliable, since the ice was thin and vehicles broke through; by January 1942 it was carrying growing quantities of food, and the ration was increased in late December and again in January and February. Anti-aircraft guns and fighter cover protected the route, and traffic controllers kept vehicles apart. In the summer of 1942 a fuel pipeline was laid along the bed of Lake Ladoga, and in the autumn an electric cable followed, providing energy for the city’s factories. Evacuation continued in the spring, by boat across Ladoga in the navigation season, and the population of the city fell to well under a million by 1943. The tenacity of life in the city, which became a symbol of Soviet endurance, was accompanied by cultural resilience, including the Public Library’s exhibitions, the radio orchestra’s performance of Shostakovich’s symphony, and the diaries of writers and ordinary people such as Lidiia Ginzburg and Dmitry Likhachev, the last written decades later, so that historians have to treat each source type separately.

Section 59.19

The German Drive on Moscow, Autumn 1941

Operation Typhoon, the German offensive toward Moscow, began on 30 September 1941 in the south of Army Group Center’s sector and in full on 2 October. The Germans concentrated well over a million and a half men and large armored forces; the Soviet Western, Reserve, and Bryansk Fronts were somewhat weaker in the opening phase, by most counts. In the first days German forces encircled large Soviet forces at Vyazma and Bryansk, and by 7 October the pockets had closed; German reports claimed more than 600,000 prisoners. The road to Moscow was momentarily open. Zhukov took command of the Western Front on 10 October, and the Soviet command organized a defensive line at Mozhaisk and drew reserves from other regions.

The crisis in the capital was acute. On 15 October the State Defense Committee ordered the evacuation of government agencies and the diplomatic corps to Kuibyshev (now Samara) on the Volga, and the body of Lenin was moved to Tyumen. In the following days panic and disorder occurred in Moscow, with officials fleeing, looting, and rumors of surrender, and a state of siege was declared on 19 October. Stalin remained in Moscow, and on 6–7 November the anniversary of the revolution was marked by a speech in a metro station and by a parade on Red Square after which some of the troops marched directly to the front. Hundreds of thousands of Muscovites, mostly women, dug defensive works.

Autumn rains and mud, the rasputitsa, slowed the German advance in October and November; the German army, lacking winter clothing and reliant on a strained supply line, resumed the attack in mid-November after the ground froze. Tula held out against Guderian’s forces; Soviet units under Rokossovsky and others defended Klin and Solnechnogorsk, and reconnaissance elements reached the outskirts of Moscow in early December. The defense included the 316th Rifle Division, whose fight near Dubosekovo became the subject of the legend of “twenty-eight Panfilov men”; a Soviet military prosecutor’s investigation in 1948 concluded that the account of the heroic stand was largely a journalistic construction, though the division had fought in the area. Historians also debate the timing and importance of reinforcements from Siberia and the Far East, partly informed by the Soviet agent Richard Sorge’s reports that Japan would not attack the USSR, a reading that must be weighed against other factors, including the Soviet-Japanese Neutrality Pact of April 1941.

The German plan for Typhoon was to destroy the Soviet armies in front of Moscow by double envelopment and to take the capital before winter. Army Group Center, reinforced with panzer groups from the north and south, concentrated about a million and a half men in three armies and three panzer groups. Soviet forces had built successive lines of defense but had insufficient reserves and Soviet commanders misjudged the timing and weight of the attack. The offensive caught the Soviet Western and Reserve Fronts off balance, and in the first days the Germans encircled forces near Vyazma, while Guderian’s panzers captured Orel on 3 October and cut off the Bryansk Front. The speed of the advance startled the Soviet command: it took several days to understand that two entire fronts had been encircled. The losses opened a gap about 500 kilometers wide in the defense, and the Stavka rushed whatever it could find to block the roads at Mozhaisk.

The Soviet defense in October and November involved successive stands that traded space for time. The cadets of the Podolsk military schools, men of the Moscow militia, and the units transferred from the Far East and Siberia fought at Borodino field and in the Mozhaisk line. Tula, where Guderian’s attacks in late October were stopped, remained in Soviet hands throughout. The city of Kalinin fell in mid-October, was in German hands for two months, and threatened Moscow’s northern flank. The German second offensive began on 15–16 November, as the ground froze, with panzer groups attempting to envelop the city from north and south. In the north, the Soviet Thirtieth, Sixteenth, and Twentieth Armies and the forces defending Klin and Solnechnogorsk gave way slowly, and the Germans reached the Moscow–Volga canal; in the south, Guderian was halted at Tula and Kashira. By early December German units were within about thirty kilometers of the Kremlin, but the divisions were exhausted, with frostbite casualties mounting and tank strength low.

Within Moscow, the population experienced the autumn as a time of upheaval. Factories and offices were evacuated; anti-aircraft defenses, air-raid shelters in the metro, and blackouts were organized; and on 16 October a collapse of order took place, with officials abandoning posts and people fleeing to the east, which the leadership put down by decree on 19 October. Several hundred thousand civilians were mobilized for the construction of defenses, and many factories turned to producing weapons and munitions. The role of Stalin’s decision to remain in the capital, publicized in the speech of 6 November and the parade of 7 November, has often been described as turning the psychological tide; it is a decision that cannot be separated from the facts that the Red Army and the party were able to restore control and that troops from outside the region arrived.

Section 59.20

The Soviet Counteroffensive and the Battle of Moscow, December 1941–Early 1942

On 5–6 December 1941 the Kalinin and Western Fronts, with units of the Southwestern and Bryansk Fronts, attacked the exposed flanks of Army Group Center. Soviet forces had assembled reserve armies, including the First Shock, Twentieth, and Tenth, behind the front, and the attack surprised German commanders who had believed the Red Army exhausted. German units were short of fuel, ammunition, and winter equipment, and had suffered heavy casualties; temperatures fell well below freezing. German forces were forced back from Moscow and Soviet troops recaptured Klin and Kalinin in mid-December and Kaluga at the end of the month. Hitler’s order of 16 December to hold positions prevented a wider collapse but contributed to the dismissal of commanders, including Brauchitsch, Bock, Guderian, and Hoepner, and to Hitler’s assumption of direct command of the army on 19 December.

The Soviet leadership, encouraged by success, ordered a general offensive in January 1942 along the whole front from Leningrad to the Crimea. The offensive overreached. It achieved some gains, trapped German formations at Demyansk and Kholm that were supplied by air, and drove back Army Group Center by 100–250 kilometers, but failed to destroy the Rzhev-Vyazma salient. The Soviet Thirty-Third Army was lost near Vyazma and the Second Shock Army was encircled in the Lyuban operation. Soviet losses in the Moscow operations were extremely heavy, in the hundreds of thousands, with estimates varying by how the operation is defined.

The Battle of Moscow ended German hopes of a rapid victory and had international consequences. It occurred as the Japanese attack on Pearl Harbor on 7 December 1941 brought the United States into the war and Germany declared war on the United States on 11 December. The Soviet success was real but limited: German armies were not destroyed, and the Red Army’s capacity to carry out offensive operations was shown to be modest. Civilians suffered during the German retreat, as retreating forces destroyed villages and took hostages, and the Soviet Union’s ability to avoid collapse depended on forces that the Moscow victory only partially replaced.

The Soviet counteroffensive had been planned during late November. The Stavka assembled reserves, including the First Shock Army, the Twentieth Army, and parts of the Tenth, that the Germans had not detected, together with divisions released from Siberia and the Far East and from the interior. The original plan was limited to eliminating the immediate threat to Moscow by striking at the flanks of the German salients at Klin and Tula; the attack of the Kalinin Front under Konev on 5 December and of the Western Front under Zhukov on 6 December, followed by Southwestern and Bryansk Front attacks in the south, drove the Germans back. Soviet forces had more tanks and infantry than their opponents on the critical sectors, though their supply and communications were also stretched. The Germans lacked winter clothing in sufficient quantity, vehicles froze, and weapons malfunctioned in the low temperatures. Hitler’s refusal to permit withdrawal on 16 December, which he justified as necessary to avoid a rout, was followed by the dismissal of Brauchitsch, Bock, Guderian, and others.

The success encouraged Stalin to order a general offensive on 5–10 January 1942 against all three German army groups, from Leningrad to the Crimea. In the center, Soviet armies advanced west of Moscow, penetrated the German lines near Rzhev, and cut off large German forces at Demyansk and Kholm in the north; but the Germans held the Rzhev–Vyazma salient, resupplied Demyansk by air, and stopped Soviet advances with counterattacks. At Kerch, a Soviet amphibious landing in December 1941 and January 1942 established a bridgehead that proved difficult to sustain. The Soviet offensive was too ambitious: Soviet commanders, unable to concentrate forces and short of reserves and ammunition, attacked in many places and failed to achieve a breakthrough. By April the thaw halted operations, and the front stabilized in positions from which both sides prepared to resume the offensive.

Moscow’s significance was strategic, psychological, and political. It demonstrated that Germany could not win the war in the East quickly, that the Red Army could mount offensives, and that the Soviet state could survive the loss of much of its territory. The cost was great: the Soviet armies lost hundreds of thousands of men in the battles of 1941–1942. The victory had a lasting influence on Soviet memory, in which the battle of Moscow became one of the foundational episodes of the Great Patriotic War; historians point out that the narrative of heroic defense should be combined with attention to command failures, the role of terror, and the limited nature of Soviet success. It also shaped the German view of the war: the army’s leadership lost authority with Hitler, who took direct command, and the army entered 1942 with fewer men and equipment than the previous year.

Division V — The Campaigns of 1942 and the Changing Military Balance

Section 59.21

Soviet Offensives and Military Setbacks in Early 1942

The spring and early summer of 1942 brought a series of Soviet defeats. Stalin believed that the Germans would renew their advance toward Moscow and kept strong reserves in the center while authorizing limited offensives elsewhere. Fighting around Rzhev continued through the year at heavy cost. In the Crimea, a Soviet landing at Kerch in December 1941 had established a bridgehead, but in May 1942 the German Eleventh Army under Manstein destroyed the Crimean Front on the Kerch peninsula, with Soviet losses of well over a hundred thousand men. Sevastopol, besieged since the autumn of 1941 and defended by the Black Sea Fleet’s forces and the Coastal Army, fell on 4 July 1942 after a German assault supported by heavy siege artillery.

At Kharkiv, the Southwestern Front under Timoshenko attacked in May from the Izium salient. The offensive, launched on 12 May, achieved initial gains, but German forces cut off the advancing Soviet armies in a counterattack; according to one Soviet archival count, roughly 277,000 Soviet casualties resulted, of which about 171,000 were irrecoverable. Soviet advances repeatedly produced heavy losses because commanders and staff were inexperienced in coordinating large mechanized operations, because logistics and reconnaissance were inadequate, because Stalin’s strategy of simultaneous offensives dispersed resources, and because the German army retained tactical skill and operational reserves. The defeat at Kharkiv left the southern front exposed to the German summer offensive.

Strategic misjudgment lay behind much of the spring’s failure. Stalin and the Stavka, having seen the German army driven from Moscow, expected the main German effort in 1942 to resume against the capital, and held large forces in the center, including the Western and Kalinin Fronts. Zhukov and the General Staff advised strategic defense and limited offensives, while Stalin and some front commanders favored active operations. The result was a series of offensives that dispersed Soviet strength. Rzhev, which Soviet forces attacked repeatedly in 1942, became a byword for attritional fighting with heavy losses and slight gains. In the northwest, the Lyuban operation and the encirclement of the Second Shock Army ended in disaster, and in the Crimea the Soviet Crimean Front was reinforced by commissars and officers from Moscow but poorly commanded.

The collapse of the Crimean Front on the Kerch peninsula in May 1942, when the German Eleventh Army struck a front crowded with troops and poorly prepared for defense, led to the evacuation of the remaining forces across the strait and loss of much equipment. It left Sevastopol isolated. The city’s defense, directed by the Coastal Army and the Black Sea Fleet, had repulsed German assaults in December 1941; in June 1942 Manstein’s forces, with massive artillery support, including the heaviest siege guns in the German army, and with the Luftwaffe, attacked from the north and east, and after a month the defenders were driven back to the Chersonese peninsula. Many were evacuated by submarines and ships; thousands of soldiers and civilians were left, and many were captured. The loss of Sevastopol deprived the fleet of its main base and eliminated a long-standing source of pressure on the German southern flank.

At Kharkiv, Timoshenko and his political officer Nikita Khrushchev planned an offensive from the Izium salient south of the city, partly to disrupt German preparations. The offensive began on 12 May and made progress for several days. German commanders then launched a counterattack from the south, closing the salient’s neck, and Soviet armies were encircled at Barvenkovo. Requests to halt the offensive were not approved in time. The Soviet failure resulted from overconfidence, poor reconnaissance, delay in recognizing the threat, and the inexperience of commanders in operational maneuver, while German forces had prepared the counterattack with skill. The losses, in the range of a quarter of a million men, left the southern front without reserves just before the German offensive. In the 1950s Khrushchev blamed Stalin for the disaster in his secret speech, an interpretation that historians have since qualified by pointing to the responsibility of the front command.

Section 59.22

Operation Blue and the German Offensive toward the Caucasus

Hitler’s Directive No. 41 of 5 April 1942 ordered a summer offensive in the south to destroy Soviet forces west of the Don and to seize the oil of the Caucasus. The Soviet Union’s main oil fields lay at Baku, Grozny, and Maikop, and Germany, dependent on Romanian production and synthetic fuel, urgently needed oil. Operation Blue began on 28 June. German armies advanced from the Kursk-Kharkiv area to the Don at Voronezh, and then southeast; Rostov fell on 23 July. On the same day Directive No. 45 divided the southern force into Army Group A, to advance into the Caucasus, and Army Group B, to protect the flank along the Don and advance on Stalingrad, splitting the offensive into two diverging thrusts.

The Soviet response combined retreat and coercion. On 28 July Stalin issued Order No. 227, “Not one step back,” which prescribed severe penalties for withdrawal without orders, authorized penal battalions and blocking detachments behind unreliable units, and was read to the troops. Army Group A took Maikop on 9 August, though Soviet forces had destroyed the wells; mountain troops raised the swastika on Mount Elbrus on 21 August; but the advance was halted before Grozny and the Caspian by distance, supply difficulty, Soviet resistance in the Caucasus mountains and along the Terek River, and the diversion of German forces to Stalingrad. German flank defense along the Don rested on Romanian, Italian, and Hungarian armies with limited equipment, a weakness that would be exploited in November. Hitler dismissed Field Marshal List in September and Halder, the Chief of the General Staff, at the end of the month.

The plan for Operation Blue was prepared in secrecy. The Germans concealed their intentions by a deception, known as the Kremlin plan, that suggested a renewed drive on Moscow, and the capture of a German officer’s plans in June by Soviet forces led many to believe the information a plant, so that the Stavka continued to expect the main blow in the center. The offensive began on 28 June with Army Group South’s attack toward Voronezh, followed in the next days by the forces further south. German forces, strengthened by Hungarian, Italian, and Romanian armies, advanced rapidly through the open steppe of the Don basin against Soviet armies that had been weakened at Kharkiv. The rapid fall of Rostov, a city of great importance, on 23 July, was a shock because Soviet forces had held it in 1941; the Soviet command, in retreat, tried to avoid encirclement.

In the Caucasus, Army Group A drove south through the Kuban and the northern foothills, while its panzer forces advanced toward the Terek and the oil fields at Grozny. The German logistic burden was heavy: fuel supply was inadequate, the distances stretched supplies, and the Luftwaffe was divided between two directions. The Soviet defense, consisting of the Transcaucasian Front and the Black Sea Group of Forces under Ivan Tyulenev, utilized mountain passes and rivers, while the Soviet command evacuated equipment and destroyed oil facilities. The capture of the Maikop fields yielded little oil. The German advance into the Caucasus meant that a region of many peoples, including Russians, Circassians, Karachays, Ossetians, Chechens, Ingush, Georgians, Armenians, and Azerbaijanis, came under threat of occupation. Occupation of parts of the northern Caucasus lasted until early 1943, and its political consequences for the peoples there, including the deportations of 1943–1944 discussed in Chapter 60, were serious.

The dismissal of commanders reflected the strain. Hitler dismissed List as commander of Army Group A in September and took personal command; the dismissal of Halder followed his disagreements with Hitler about the direction of the offensive. The war in the Caucasus, which was supposed to deliver oil to the German war machine, produced instead a stalemate, with German forces holding positions at the end of the year that they could not extend and could not easily supply. At the same time, Soviet leaders began to regain confidence, and the mobilization of resources continued. The southern campaign therefore combined a great German territorial gain with a strategic failure, a combination that was to become more evident in the next phase.

Section 59.23

Stalingrad in 1942: The Struggle for the Volga

Stalingrad, known as Tsaritsyn before 1925, was an industrial city stretching for some forty kilometers along the west bank of the Volga. It had a tractor factory that produced tanks, steel plants, and a role in the Volga river traffic that carried oil and grain. Its name gave it symbolic importance for both Stalin and Hitler. The German Sixth Army under General Paulus, supported by the Fourth Panzer Army, advanced to the Don bend in the summer, and on 23 August a German spearhead reached the Volga north of the city while the Luftwaffe carried out an intense bombing raid that killed thousands of civilians. Many residents remained in the city, since evacuation had been limited, and workers and militia were drawn into the defense.

The battle for the city, from September to November, was fought among factory halls, apartment blocks, grain elevators, the Mamayev Kurgan hill, and the ruins of the center. General Vasily Chuikov, commanding the Sixty-Second Army from 12 September, ordered his troops to fight at close quarters to limit the effect of German air and artillery superiority. Soviet forces held narrow strips of the western bank, supplied across the river under fire, while German assaults in October on the Tractor Factory and in November on the Barrikady and Red October plants captured most of the city but failed to destroy the defenders. Women served as nurses, anti-aircraft crews, and communications staff. Soviet discipline was severe, with executions for retreat, and the narrative of the battle as an unqualified triumph of spirit omits both the cruelty of command and the suffering of civilians. By mid-November the German offensive was spent, and the Sixth Army had concentrated its strength in the city with exposed flanks.

Stalingrad’s importance to the Soviet leadership was threefold: its location on the Volga, which carried oil from the Caucasus northward; its industrial plants, including the tractor factory that produced tanks; and its name. For Hitler the city had a symbolic as well as strategic value, and in speeches he promised its capture. The initial German advance across the steppe between the Don and the Volga in August was rapid, but the Soviet defenders, including units of the Sixty-Second and Sixty-Fourth Armies, fought for each village and ravine. On 23 August the Luftwaffe’s bombing of the city, in the course of which the center and wooden housing burned, killed thousands of civilians, and the city’s inhabitants, who had been encouraged to remain, found themselves under attack. Limited, selective evacuation had begun before the raid, but larger-scale civilian evacuation across the Volga accelerated only afterward, and many residents remained in the city during the autumn battles.

The fighting in September and October was among the most intense of the war. The Sixty-Second Army, which retained a fragile hold on the west bank of the Volga, received reinforcements ferried across the river at night, and soldiers fought among the ruins in small groups. Chuikov’s tactic of “hugging” the enemy, staying close to German positions so that artillery and aircraft could not be used without hitting their own men, and the use of snipers such as Vasily Zaitsev, became part of the battle’s mythology. Landmarks such as the Mamayev Kurgan, the grain elevator, the railway station, and the building known as Pavlov’s House became symbols. The German Sixth Army, drawn into urban combat in which its armor and air support were less effective, suffered heavy casualties and used up its infantry; it also had to rely on weak allied armies for the protection of its flanks. Soviet losses were very high, and estimates of casualties in the battle vary depending on the period and area covered.

Civilians and women played substantial roles. Tens of thousands of civilians were killed or wounded in the bombing and shelling, workers in the Tractor Factory repaired and produced tanks under fire in the first weeks of September, and women served as anti-aircraft gunners, nurses, telephonists, and in some cases as snipers. Order No. 227 and the blocking detachments, which shot soldiers who retreated without orders, were part of the battle’s conditions, as were the executions of soldiers for desertion, which Soviet sources record in the thousands in the Stalingrad area in this period. The German side saw its own tensions, including commanders’ concerns about the Sixth Army’s exposure. By November the Sixth Army had taken most of the city and was exhausted, while the Soviet command, having kept the defenders on the Volga’s west bank, was preparing a counterstroke.

Section 59.24

Operation Uranus and the Encirclement of the German Sixth Army

The Soviet high command, led by Zhukov and Vasilevsky, planned a counteroffensive from September, accumulating reserves behind the Volga and the Don. Operation Uranus began on 19 November 1942 when the Southwestern Front under Vatutin and the Don Front under Rokossovsky attacked northwest of the city, breaking through the Romanian Third Army; on 20 November the Stalingrad Front under Yeryomenko attacked from the south against the Romanian Fourth Army. The two pincers met at Kalach on the Don on 23 November, encircling the Sixth Army and elements of the Fourth Panzer Army, a force of roughly 250,000 to 300,000 men, including Romanians, Croats, and Soviet auxiliaries. Soviet intelligence had underestimated the size of the encircled force.

Hitler ordered the Sixth Army to hold its positions, and Göring promised to supply it by air, though the army required several hundred tons of supplies daily and the Luftwaffe delivered only a fraction. A German relief effort under Manstein, Operation Winter Storm, began on 12 December and approached to within about fifty kilometers of the pocket by 19–23 December, but was halted by Soviet resistance, and Paulus did not attempt a breakout without Hitler’s permission. On 16 December the Soviet Operation Little Saturn broke the Italian Eighth Army on the middle Don and endangered the German position in the whole southern theater. Soviet efforts to destroy the pocket quickly failed in December, and the Sixth Army remained encircled at year’s end.

The same weeks saw another Soviet offensive that failed. Operation Mars, launched on 25 November against the German Rzhev salient under Zhukov’s direction, produced very heavy Soviet casualties with little gain; its purpose, whether a major offensive or a diversion to hold German forces in the center, is debated by historians. The campaign illustrates that the Soviet success at Stalingrad was not matched everywhere, and that at the end of 1942 the encirclement was a military opportunity, not yet a victory. Its completion belongs to Chapter 60.

The Soviet plan of encirclement was developed by the General Staff and the Stavka in September and October, with Zhukov and Vasilevsky as principal planners, and was kept secret from most commanders until shortly before the attack. Strategic reserves, including new tank and mechanized corps, were assembled behind the Volga and Don and moved forward at night; several thousand guns and mortars were concentrated, and bridgeheads were established across the Don. Soviet commanders chose to strike at the Romanian Third and Fourth Armies, which held the flanks of the Sixth Army and which were poorly equipped and short of antitank guns. The Soviet forces for the operation numbered more than a million men in three fronts, with thousands of tanks and guns. The attack on 19 November from the Don bridgeheads struck the Romanian Third Army in fog and snow, and Soviet tank corps broke through within hours; the attack from the south on 20 November broke the Romanian Fourth Army’s line.

The two prongs joined at Kalach on 23 November, trapping the Sixth Army and elements of the Fourth Panzer Army, a force of about 250,000 to 300,000 men, together with large amounts of equipment. German commanders, including Paulus and some of his generals, favored a breakout, but Hitler forbade it and the Luftwaffe commander Göring assured him the army could be supplied by air. The supply by air, requiring several hundred tons daily, never approached the amount needed, since airfields were lost, weather was bad, and Soviet fighters and anti-aircraft guns took a toll. The German relief attempt, Operation Winter Storm, came within about fifty kilometers of the pocket; but Hitler’s conditions on the breakout and Soviet reinforcement of the front made it impossible. The Soviet Operation Little Saturn, which began on 16 December, shattered much of the Italian Eighth Army and threatened German positions on the lower Don, contributing to the strategic crisis that led to the subsequent German withdrawal from the Caucasus.

At the end of December the military situation was far better for the Soviet Union than it had been in the summer, but the outcome was not resolved. The destruction of the encircled forces was a larger task than the Soviet command had expected, since the estimate of the number of trapped men had been low, and the Soviet armies were fighting on a wide front in winter. The failed Operation Mars at Rzhev, in which Soviet forces suffered very heavy losses, reminded commanders of the limits of Soviet capability, and German commanders such as Manstein were still able to restore a defensive line. Students should note that the surrender of the Sixth Army in February 1943 was not a foregone conclusion in the weeks after 23 November, and that the ultimate result depended on events described in Chapter 60.

Section 59.25

Logistics, Geography, Military Adaptation, and the Failure of Rapid German Victory

By the end of 1942 the German plan of rapid victory had failed, and explaining why requires attention to several factors. Logistics was a first: the German army depended on railways, which had to be converted from the wider Soviet gauge, and on horse-drawn transport, which stretched supply lines over hundreds of kilometers; fuel, ammunition, and winter clothing were inadequate. Geography mattered as well: the size of the theater, the breadth of the front, and the poor road network favored defenders who could trade space for time, though geography did not defend the Soviet Union by itself, as the German advance to the Volga showed. Weather, particularly the autumn mud and the winter cold of 1941, hampered German operations; but the German offensive had been stopped by Soviet defense before the coldest period, and the cold also hindered Soviet operations.

On the Soviet side, adaptation was critical. The army learned to coordinate larger mechanized and artillery formations, replaced commanders who failed with those who succeeded, and in October 1942 abolished the dual command of commissars. Industrial production recovered after the shock of the evacuation (§59.26), supplying more tanks, aircraft, and artillery than Germany could, and the manpower pool was large. Allied supply played a smaller but real role (§59.28). German operational decisions also contributed: the diversion of forces from Moscow to Kyiv, the division of the 1942 offensive between the Caucasus and Stalingrad, and the decision to hold the Sixth Army in the pocket. Explanations that emphasize any one of these causes alone, especially winter or geography, understate the interaction of German miscalculation, Soviet resilience, and the scale of Soviet sacrifice.

Logistics shaped every German campaign. The railways of occupied Russia had to be converted to German gauge or supplied by transshipment, and Soviet destruction of locomotives and rolling stock reduced capacity. The Germans relied on trucks that wore out on unpaved roads and on horses, which suffered in winter, and on supply columns that had to cover hundreds of kilometers from railheads. In 1942 the extension of the front to the Caucasus and the Volga multiplied the problem. Fuel, ammunition, and spare parts were delivered in insufficient quantities, and the units at the extremity of the advance were often short. The allied armies on the flanks, with limited transport and heavy weapons, were worse off. The Soviet army, defending, was closer to its supply bases, could use the railways behind the front, and could shift reserves along interior lines, while its own supply difficulties were mitigated by the large stockpiles gathered in 1942.

The Soviet armed forces improved during 1942, though not smoothly. Commanders learned to form tank armies and mechanized corps, to coordinate artillery with infantry in offensive operations, and to plan the use of reserves in a manner that avoided the dispersal of earlier months. The artillery, which Soviet planners regarded as the “god of war,” grew in importance and in the number of guns concentrated on breakthrough sectors, and the air force began to regain parity in some sectors with new fighters and ground-attack aircraft. The abolition of dual command in October 1942 gave commanders greater authority, while the political officers retained a role. Intelligence, radio security, and deception improved, as the success of Uranus showed. Yet Soviet casualties remained very high, and commanders still sometimes wasted lives in poorly prepared attacks.

The German decisions also mattered. Hitler’s decision to divide the 1942 offensive, to continue operations in the Caucasus and at Stalingrad at the same time, and his refusal to allow retreat, repeated the strategic overreach of the previous year; his insistence on holding the Sixth Army in place followed the pattern he had set at Moscow. The German economy, geared for short campaigns, could not make up the losses, and German armament production was only gradually increased in 1942. Wider factors, including the entry of the United States into the war, the campaign in North Africa, and the bombing of German cities, drew resources from the Eastern Front. None of these explanations by itself accounts for the outcome, and the failure of rapid German victory is best understood as the result of the interaction among them, with the human cost, on the Soviet side above all, extraordinary.

Historical Debate

Explaining the Failure of the German Campaign

The question: Why did Germany’s attack, which achieved extraordinary early victories, fail to defeat the Soviet Union in 1941–1942?

Interpretation A — Overextension and German miscalculation: Many historians argue that the plan rested on faulty assumptions: that the Red Army would collapse after the first battles, that the Soviet political system was fragile, and that the campaign could be completed in a few months. They point to inadequate logistics, underestimation of Soviet reserves and production, and Hitler’s strategic shifts as decisive.
Interpretation B — Soviet resilience and adaptation: Others emphasize the Soviet capacity to mobilize manpower, move industry east, replace losses, learn from defeats, and sustain popular and coercive mobilization, and argue that German strategy would not have succeeded even with better planning.
Interpretation C — Weather and geography: Older German memoirs, and some popular accounts, attribute the failure largely to the Russian winter, mud, and distances. Historians generally treat these as contributing conditions rather than sufficient explanations.

Evidence: Historians use German operational records, supply and casualty data, Soviet archival materials on losses and production, intelligence records, and memoirs.

Points of agreement: Most scholars agree that German planning was flawed, that the Soviet Union’s ability to rebuild its forces was essential, and that no single factor suffices. Points of disagreement: Historians differ on the weight of particular decisions, such as the diversion toward Kyiv in 1941, on the role of ideology in German misjudgment, and on the relative importance of Soviet economic capacity and military adaptation. Why it matters: The debate bears on how historians explain the war’s outcome and on how memory in Germany, Russia, and elsewhere has framed it.

Student task: Select two causes from the debate and evaluate how the evidence in this chapter supports or complicates each, without ranking them on political grounds.

Section 59.26

Industrial Evacuation, War Production, and Economic Mobilization

The Soviet economy entered the war with a mobilization plan and a large armaments sector, but the loss of territory in 1941 was a shock of extraordinary magnitude. By the end of the year German forces occupied regions that had accounted for a very large share of Soviet coal, steel, and grain output and that held roughly forty percent of the prewar population, according to commonly cited estimates. A Council for Evacuation was created on 24 June 1941. By Soviet tallies, approximately 1,500 industrial enterprises, over 1,300 of them large, were moved to the Urals, the Volga region, Western Siberia, Kazakhstan, and Central Asia in the second half of 1941, with more in 1942, together with millions of workers and their families. Plants from Leningrad, Kharkiv, Moscow, and Zaporizhzhia were reassembled in places such as Chelyabinsk, Nizhny Tagil, Sverdlovsk, Novosibirsk, and Tashkent, often in unheated buildings or in the open, with production resuming within weeks or months. The railways, operating under military priorities, carried the evacuation and the front’s needs together.

Industrial output fell sharply in the winter of 1941–1942 and recovered through 1942. New commissariats, including those for the tank industry and for mortar armament, centralized production of weapons, and the GKO and Gosplan under Voznesensky controlled allocation of materials. Soviet industry produced roughly 24,000 tanks and self-propelled guns and more than 20,000 combat aircraft in 1942, according to Soviet figures, concentrating on a limited number of standardized designs such as the T-34, the Il-2, and the Katyusha rocket launcher. Quality was sometimes sacrificed for quantity, but the output exceeded Germany’s in key categories.

The costs were borne by workers and farmers. Decrees of December 1941 and February 1942 tied workers in war industries to their jobs and mobilized the urban population of working age. Hours were extended, holidays suspended, and housing for evacuated workers consisted of barracks and dugouts. Women, adolescents, and older workers replaced men conscripted into the army. In agriculture, the loss of Ukraine, the Don, and the Kuban and the mobilization of men, horses, and tractors sharply reduced output; collective farm women, children, and the elderly produced the harvest, and the state’s procurement left villages short of food. Prisoners of the Gulag worked in war industries and construction, and mortality in the camps rose sharply in 1942–1943 because of reduced rations. The economy’s survival thus depended on both the planning apparatus and a high level of coercion and sacrifice.

The evacuation was an enormous organizational effort. Plans for the relocation of industry existed in a limited form before the war, but the speed of the German advance forced improvisation. Local committees dismantled machinery, loaded it onto railway cars, and sent it east together with key workers and their families, in convoys that were bombed on the way. Priority went to defense industries: aircraft factories were moved from Moscow, Leningrad, and Ukraine to Kuibyshev, Novosibirsk, Tashkent, and elsewhere; the Kharkiv diesel engine plant and tank factory were reestablished at Nizhny Tagil, where it combined with the Urals railroad car works to produce T-34 tanks; the Kirov plant in Leningrad moved to Chelyabinsk, which became known as “Tankograd”; and the Zaporizhzhia steelworks’ equipment went to Magnitogorsk and elsewhere. Frequently the new buildings did not exist and workers began production in the open, building walls around machines. The work force’s hardships were considerable, as housing, heat, and food were scarce, and mortality among evacuated workers was high in the first winter.

The mobilization of resources involved many sectors. The railways were organized on a military basis, with priority for military transport; river transport on the Volga was important; the new rail lines, such as the line from Kotlas to Vorkuta, which brought coal from the northern Komi region, and the line connecting the Volga and Siberia, were built in part with prison labor. Coal production in the Donbas, lost to the Germans, was replaced by coal from the Kuznetsk and Karaganda basins. Metallurgical production fell and recovered in the Urals and Siberia, and the chemical and aluminum industries were reorganized. State finance covered the costs by increases in taxes, war loans, and printing of money, and prices on the collective-farm market rose sharply, creating inequalities between town and country and between categories of workers. The state also relied on the output of the Gulag, whose prisoners built factories, mined coal and metals, and cut timber, though mortality in the camps was very high.

Costs were high for ordinary people. Consumers’ incomes shrank, living standards fell below prewar levels, and workers on the home front suffered from malnutrition and exhaustion. Peasants, who made up the majority of the population, paid heavy taxes and delivered grain at fixed prices, and in the absence of men and machines women and adolescents did the field work by hand or with cows. Many millions of people were uprooted, and the relocation of industry changed the geography of the Soviet economy for decades, strengthening the eastern regions. Soviet planners noted that the achievements of 1942 came at a cost that included the reduction of civilian production and the consumption of capital. Historians continue to debate how far the organizational skills displayed in the evacuation reflected the prewar planning system or improvisation by local managers, and what role was played by coercion and by willingness to sacrifice.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Peacetime Versus Wartime Economic Administration

What Changed

  • Authority concentrated in the State Defense Committee, which could override ordinary ministerial procedures.
  • Production priority shifted overwhelmingly to armaments; consumer goods and civilian investment fell.
  • Labor was mobilized and tied to workplaces; overtime became compulsory.
  • The geography of industry moved eastward, deepening the Urals and Siberian industrial bases.

What Remained

  • The system of central planning and commissariats built in the 1930s remained in place.
  • Heavy industry and defense had been priorities before 1941.
  • Coercion in the workplace and in agriculture, and the use of forced labor, continued earlier practices.
  • Procurement from collective farms continued to rely on state compulsion.

Why the pattern matters: The Soviet war economy was not an improvisation, but neither was it a mere continuation of peacetime planning. The prewar industrial base and administrative structures made rapid mobilization possible, while the war intensified and extended methods of command and compulsion.

Section 59.27

Soldiers, Workers, Women, Families, and Soviet Wartime Society

Over thirty million people passed through the Soviet armed forces during the war, a force drawn from every nationality of the Union: Russians, Ukrainians, Belarusians, Tatars, Uzbeks, Kazakhs, Georgians, Armenians, Jews, and many others. Language, literacy, and training varied widely, and national formations were created in the Caucasus and Central Asia in 1941–1942. Women made up a large part of the home-front workforce and also served in the armed forces, in the hundreds of thousands, as nurses, medical staff, signalers, anti-aircraft gunners, snipers, and pilots; three women’s aviation regiments were organized in late 1941. Children were evacuated from threatened cities, some orphaned, and many were received in Central Asia, Siberia, and the Urals, where local communities, particularly in Uzbekistan, took in displaced children and families of many nationalities.

Daily life was shaped by shortages. Rationing, introduced in Moscow and Leningrad in July 1941 and extended widely, graded food entitlements by occupation and category. People supplemented rations with kitchen gardens, barter, and black markets. Family separation, uncertainty about the missing, and the arrival of official notifications of death structured the emotional life of the period. Evacuees met varying reception, from hospitality to resentment, as local residents shared limited housing and food.

Propaganda sought to unify the population. The press and posters called for defense of the motherland, drawing on Russian historical figures such as Alexander Nevsky, Suvorov, and Kutuzov, and the term “Great Patriotic War” echoed 1812. Metropolitan Sergii of the Russian Orthodox Church issued an appeal to believers on 22 June 1941, and the state eased antireligious activity, a change that grew in later years. Appeals to the Soviet peoples as a whole coexisted with an emphasis on the Russian people. The relationship between voluntary commitment and coercion was complex: many people enlisted from patriotism or hatred of the invader, while the state relied on conscription, tribunals, the threat of reprisals against families, and blocking detachments. Both elements were real, and the evidence for popular attitudes, including the reports of the secret police and personal diaries, shows a spectrum of morale.

The composition of the armed forces reflected the diversity of the Soviet Union and the strains of mobilization. The army’s officer corps was predominantly Russian, Ukrainian, and Belarusian, while soldiers from Central Asia, the Caucasus, and the Volga region often had limited knowledge of Russian, which created difficulties of training and command. National divisions, formed in the Caucasus and Central Asia and in the Baltic republics, were established in 1941 and 1942, though some were later disbanded or integrated. In the early months, thousands of volunteers, many of them students and workers, joined the militias and were sent into battle with minimal training, while the Communist Party and Komsomol sent tens of thousands of members to the front. Over the war as a whole, the death toll among Soviet soldiers was heavy for all nationalities, and the proportion of Jews, Ukrainians, Belarusians, and others among the dead has been the subject of detailed work.

Women entered both the armed forces and the home-front economy in great numbers. In addition to the aviation regiments of Marina Raskova, which included the unit known as the “Night Witches” that flew light bombers, women served in the anti-aircraft forces, as snipers, as medical staff, and as telephonists and signalers; in the economy they took the place of men in factories, collective farms, and transport. Their burden included the care of children and elders under conditions of shortage. Children were also drawn into labor: adolescent trainees entered factories, and younger children collected scrap, worked in fields, and cared for siblings. The evacuation of children from Leningrad, Moscow, and other threatened cities, and the movement of orphans, were organized in part by state agencies and in part by individuals, and the reception of evacuees in Central Asia and Siberia was generally recorded as hospitable but varied.

Propaganda addressed different audiences. The newspapers Pravda and Krasnaya Zvezda, the poetry of Konstantin Simonov, the articles of Ilya Ehrenburg, the posters of Irakly Toidze, “The Motherland Calls!,” and the songs of the Red Army Ensemble formed a wartime culture that stressed hatred of the enemy and love of the motherland, and in many cases used the language of Russian national tradition. The Church’s role also grew: Metropolitan Sergii’s appeals, collections of funds for the army, and the easing of pressure on believers marked a change from the antireligious campaigns of the 1930s, and religion among ordinary people revived in many places. Letters from the front, passed through military censors, kept families in touch, and the diaries and reports of the secret police recorded attitudes: the evidence shows patriotic mobilization alongside fear, resentment, and, in some regions, hope for change. The war also changed expectations: many Soviet citizens came to believe that sacrifice would be rewarded with reform, a belief that would shape postwar politics.

Everyday Life and Social History

Food, Housing, Work, and Family Separation

Consider a family evacuated from a western city to an eastern industrial town in the autumn of 1941. The factory where the father worked was dismantled and shipped by rail, and the family traveled in freight cars for weeks. On arrival they were assigned a room in a barracks or shared a peasant house, with little heat in the winter. Food depended on ration cards, which were issued according to the worker’s category: factory workers in important industries received more than dependents. The mother and teenage children worked in the plant or on a collective farm. Letters from the front, delayed or lost, provided news of other members of the family, and news of a death arrived by official notice.

The evidence for such lives comes from letters, memoirs, oral histories, factory records, and ration regulations, and each has limitations: memoirs written decades later reflect memory and the official narrative of the war, official records reflect state priorities, and surviving letters were subject to censorship. Experience also differed by region, nationality, and class, so this example should not be taken as typical of all households.

Section 59.28

The Soviet Union, Britain, the United States, and the Allied War Effort, 1941–1942

Britain’s prime minister, Winston Churchill, announced on 22 June 1941 that Britain would aid any state fighting Germany, and the Anglo-Soviet agreement of 12 July pledged mutual assistance and no separate peace. The United States, not yet at war, sent Harry Hopkins to Moscow in July. The Moscow Conference of September–October 1941, with Lord Beaverbrook and Averell Harriman, set the first supply protocol, and in November Roosevelt extended Lend-Lease eligibility to the USSR. After Pearl Harbor and the German declaration of war on the United States, the Soviet Union joined 25 other states in the Declaration by United Nations of 1 January 1942. In May 1942 the Anglo-Soviet treaty of alliance was signed, and a Soviet-American Lend-Lease agreement followed in June.

Supplies reached the USSR by three main routes: the Arctic convoys to Murmansk and Arkhangelsk, begun in August 1941; the Persian corridor, secured after British and Soviet forces occupied Iran in August 1941; and the Pacific route to Vladivostok. The Arctic convoys were costly: convoy PQ-17 in July 1942 lost 24 of its 35 merchant ships to German aircraft and submarines after being ordered to scatter, and convoys were suspended temporarily. In 1941–1942 Western aid was small compared with Soviet domestic production, though in particular categories such as aluminum, explosives, and some aircraft and tanks, it was useful, and its volume and importance rose in the later war years (see Chapter 60).

Allied diplomacy was shadowed by the question of a second front in Western Europe. Stalin demanded it from July 1941. During Molotov’s visit to London and Washington in May–June 1942 the allies issued a communique that referred to the urgent tasks of creating a second front in Europe in 1942, but Churchill and Roosevelt in fact planned operations elsewhere. Churchill visited Moscow in August 1942 to explain that there would be no cross-channel landing that year; the Dieppe raid on 19 August was a limited operation, and Operation Torch in North Africa began on 8 November. Stalin accepted the decision with reproaches. Relations were also affected by the Polish question: after the Sikorski-Mayski agreement of 30 July 1941 restored Polish-Soviet relations, and an amnesty released Polish prisoners and deportees, a Polish army under General Anders formed in the USSR and in 1942 left for Iran. Polish inquiries about the missing officers of 1940 received evasive answers from Stalin.

The foundations of the alliance were laid in the summer and autumn of 1941. In July, the British and Soviet governments agreed on joint action, and the visit of Harry Hopkins, Roosevelt’s envoy, to Moscow persuaded the American administration that the Soviet Union would continue to fight. In August, Roosevelt and Churchill met at Placentia Bay and issued the Atlantic Charter, which the Soviet government endorsed in September with reservations. The Moscow conference of the autumn determined a supply program, and in the first protocol the Western powers promised tanks, aircraft, raw materials, and other items in return for Soviet raw materials. The Lend-Lease system, under which the United States supplied materiel without immediate payment, was extended to the Soviet Union in November. Roosevelt hoped to ensure the survival of the USSR as a counterweight to Germany, while Churchill, who had been a strong anti-Bolshevik, regarded the Soviet Union as a necessary ally in the struggle against Hitler.

Supply routes were dangerous and slow. The Arctic convoys sailed from Iceland and Scotland to Murmansk and Arkhangelsk, exposed to German submarines, surface ships, and aircraft based in Norway and to weather conditions of winter storms and ice; the Royal Navy’s escorts and the Soviet northern fleet protected them, but losses were considerable, and the disaster of PQ-17 led to a pause. The Persian route, through the Persian Gulf and Iran, which Britain and the USSR occupied in August 1941 to secure oil and the supply line, became increasingly important from 1942, since trucks, aircraft, and railway equipment could be assembled in Iranian ports and sent north. The Pacific route to Vladivostok, using Soviet-flagged ships to avoid Japanese interference given Soviet neutrality toward Japan, became a major channel for the later war. The volume of aid was modest in 1941–1942 compared with Soviet output, but it grew substantially from 1943 onward.

The diplomacy of the coalition involved sharp disagreements. Stalin pressed for a second front in Europe to relieve German pressure, for acceptance of the Soviet frontiers of 1941, including the Baltic states and the Polish territories annexed in 1939, and for financial and material support. Britain and the United States were unwilling to accept formal commitments on frontiers, and they held that a landing in France in 1942 was not feasible. The Anglo-Soviet treaty of May 1942 omitted territorial provisions, and the 1942 communique on the second front, whose wording was ambiguous, left a sense of grievance. Churchill’s visit to Moscow in August 1942 to explain the postponement was a difficult one, though Stalin accepted the plan for Torch. The Polish question, with the issues of the missing officers and the release of Polish deportees, illustrates how the legacy of 1939–1941 affected the alliance. From the Soviet perspective, the Allies carried too little of the burden; from the Western perspective, the Soviet leaders were demanding and suspicious. The coalition held because both sides recognized the necessity of holding it.

Russia and the World

Lend-Lease and the Allied Coalition, 1941–1942

The Soviet Union’s survival in 1941–1942 depended mainly on its own resources, but it also depended on a coalition that tied up German forces elsewhere, supplied selected materials, and, after December 1941, brought the world’s largest industrial economy into the war. The British and Americans supplied tanks that fought near Moscow and aircraft, trucks, and raw materials, while the Soviet Union supplied the human cost. The relationship was one of rivalry as well as alliance, with disputes about strategy, recognition of borders, and the treatment of Poland. Soviet leaders at times resented what they saw as delay; Western leaders worried about Soviet intentions and capabilities. The coalition was a product of necessity, and its terms, including its geographic routes through Iran, the Arctic, and the Pacific, connected Russia to Eurasia and the oceans in ways that had been less visible before 1941.

Students should resist two simplifications: that Allied aid was decisive in 1941–1942, and that it was negligible throughout the war. The first overstates its volume in these years; the second ignores the particular contribution of supplies and the political importance of the alliance.

Section 59.29

Chapter Review and Historical Thinking Checkpoint

This chapter has traced the Soviet Union from a partner in the partition of Eastern Europe to the victim of the largest invasion in history and then to the survivor of the first stage of that war. Several transformations stand out. The first is the westward shift of Soviet frontiers in 1939–1940, accomplished by force, ultimatum, and managed elections, and followed by arrests and deportations that shaped local memory and politics in Poland, the Baltic states, and Moldova. The second is the collapse of the German-Soviet partnership and the German invasion, which brought military catastrophe, genocide, mass starvation of prisoners, and occupation. The third is the Soviet state’s capacity to survive: through harsh discipline, the reconstitution of the army, the evacuation and expansion of industry, the mobilization of labor, and Allied support.

Continuity is as important as change. Soviet institutions of the 1930s, including central planning, the security police, and the system of party control, remained at the center of the war effort, and the coercive methods of the Stalinist state were extended in wartime. At the same time the war changed the relationship between the state and society, bringing appeals to patriotism, concessions to religious institutions, and the rise of new military commanders. Causation resists simple explanations: German defeat at the gates of Moscow and on the Volga resulted from German strategic miscalculation, logistical limits, ideological aims that made the occupation brutal and thereby fueled resistance, Soviet depth and adaptation, and international circumstances; the early Soviet defeats resulted from structural weaknesses, Stalin’s decisions, and German skill.

Evidence is central to the chapter’s arguments. German records document the intention to wage a war of annihilation; Soviet documents reveal the decision-making of the party-state, from Katyn to the evacuation of industry; diaries and testimonies show how people experienced siege, occupation, and mobilization. Interpretations differ on casualty figures, the proportion of local participation in killings, the role of Allied aid, and the reasons for German failure, and the memory of the war has been shaped by state narratives in the Soviet Union and its successor states, in Poland and the Baltic states, and among Jewish communities. At the end of 1942 the outcome remained uncertain: the German Sixth Army was surrounded but not defeated, and the Red Army had yet to demonstrate that it could sustain offensive operations. Students should be able to explain how historians reconstruct this period, which parts of the narrative are well established, and which remain debated.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the Soviet Union from September 1939 to December 1942, a period that began with territorial expansion carried out in cooperation with Nazi Germany and ended with the encirclement of a German army at Stalingrad. Its central problem has been how the Soviet state moved from partnership with Germany through near-collapse to the beginnings of recovery, and what that process meant for the peoples of the Union and of the neighboring territories.

The first part of the chapter examined Soviet expansion. In 1939–1940 the Red Army occupied eastern Poland, fought a costly war against Finland, and compelled the incorporation of the Baltic states and of Bessarabia and northern Bukovina. These actions rested on the secret protocol of the 1939 pact, employed ultimatums and managed elections, and were followed by arrests and deportations, including the Katyn executions of 1940. Historians distinguish Soviet security concerns from the coercion that defined the process, and they note that Poles, Finns, Balts, Romanians, Ukrainians, Belarusians, and Jews experienced it differently.

The second part addressed the German invasion. Operation Barbarossa was planned as a war of conquest and annihilation, with orders and economic plans that anticipated mass death. The Red Army suffered immense losses in the encirclements of Minsk, Smolensk, Uman, and Kyiv for reasons that included the purges, forward deployment, surprise, doctrine, and command decisions. The Soviet leadership responded with new command institutions, harsh discipline, and the mobilization of reserves.

The third part concerned occupation and violence. German occupation authorities administered Ukraine, Belarus, the Baltic region, and western Russia for economic exploitation and racial policy. The Einsatzgruppen and their auxiliaries murdered Jews at sites such as Babi Yar and Ponary, while Soviet prisoners of war died in enormous numbers from starvation, exposure, and execution. Civilians chose among resistance, collaboration, rescue, and survival under severe constraint. The Soviet state meanwhile killed prisoners as it retreated and deported Soviet Germans on collective grounds.

The fourth part dealt with the defense of Leningrad and Moscow. The siege of Leningrad caused extraordinary civilian mortality, with the Road of Life providing a limited lifeline. The battle for Moscow brought the German army to the outskirts and then to retreat under a Soviet counteroffensive that ended hopes of rapid victory, though the January 1942 offensive overreached and left the Rzhev salient in German hands.

The fifth part examined 1942. Soviet offensives and defeats at Kerch, Sevastopol, and Kharkiv preceded the German offensive of Operation Blue, which divided between the Caucasus and Stalingrad. At Stalingrad the Sixth Army became locked in urban combat, and the Soviet counteroffensive of November 1942 encircled it. Explanations for German failure combine logistics, geography, weather, German strategic errors, and Soviet resilience, and no single factor suffices.

The sixth part analyzed Soviet mobilization: the evacuation of industry, the recovery of war production, the use of labor under compulsion, the experience of soldiers, women, and families, and the Allied coalition and supply routes. Western assistance was modest in these years relative to Soviet output but became more important later.

Continuity and change run through these themes. The institutions of the 1930s were used and extended, while the war altered state-society relations. The period also shows how different groups, from Baltic officials to Jewish families to evacuated workers, experienced the same events in very different ways. The chapter has also drawn attention to historiographical questions concerning the character of the pact, the reasons for the 1941 defeats, the scale of local participation in genocide, and the causes of German failure, and to the role of memory.

These events prepare the way for Chapter 60, which follows the completion of the victory at Stalingrad, the Soviet advance westward, the end of the war in Europe, and the postwar reconstruction. They also prepare for the study of Soviet power in Eastern Europe and of the Baltic and Ukrainian national movements that would resurface in the late Soviet period (Chapter 66).

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a state that had expanded by coercion and cooperated with a genocidal regime became the victim of that regime’s attack, and how it survived at extraordinary cost. It reveals the interplay of military events with occupation, genocide, economic mobilization, and international diplomacy, and it demonstrates that the war cannot be reduced to battles and commanders.

The period changed the Soviet Union and Europe. The Soviet frontier moved west, the Jewish communities of Eastern Europe were destroyed, millions of prisoners and civilians died, and the Soviet leadership emerged with a record of survival that would become central to its legitimacy. These developments continued in the later war years and shaped the postwar order, the Cold War, and the politics of memory in Russia, Ukraine, Belarus, the Baltic states, Poland, and elsewhere.

The chapter also reveals larger themes of the textbook: the relationship between center and borderlands, the coexistence of coercion and mobilization, the importance of evidence and its limits, and the diversity of experience within one state. Some questions remain open, including the exact numbers of casualties, the balance of motives under occupation, and the weight of different causes of German failure. At the end of 1942 the outcome was uncertain, and Chapter 60 will show how the war turned, without implying that the result was determined in advance.
