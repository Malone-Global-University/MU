Part VII — Autocracy, Empire, and Reform

Westernizers, Slavophiles, Radicals, and the Russian Intelligentsia

A History of Russia: From the Eurasian World to the Russian Federation

Chapter Opening

In September 1836, a Moscow journal called the Telescope published a letter addressed, in form, to an unnamed female correspondent, but written, in substance, as an indictment of an entire civilization: Russia, the letter argued, had contributed nothing to the historical development of humanity, possessed no coherent past, and stood outside the main current of world history. Nicholas I's government responded by shutting down the journal, punishing its editor and censor, and declaring the letter's author officially insane.

The author was Pyotr Chaadaev, a retired army officer, veteran of the Napoleonic campaigns, and member of Moscow's educated gentry society, and the declaration of his insanity was not a medical judgment but a political one, an extraordinary additional measure layered on top of the government's other responses rather than simply a substitute for censorship it could no longer apply. Chaadaev was placed under the supervision of a physician, visited periodically to confirm he remained, in the official phrase, unwell, and forbidden from publishing further work in his own country for years afterward. He had composed the letter, one of a series written in French in the elevated philosophical idiom of his class, originally for circulation among a narrow circle of highly educated friends and acquaintances, and while the precise circumstances of its path into print remain debated among historians, the evidence suggests Chaadaev had allowed the text to circulate rather than having it published entirely without his knowledge. Whatever the exact mechanism, the government's heavy-handed response guaranteed that a philosophical meditation written for private circulation became one of the most famous political events in the intellectual history of Nicholaevan Russia.

This chapter examines the world of ideas, argument, and private conviction that produced Chaadaev's letter and the responses it provoked. Chapter 38 examined the institutional architecture of Nicholas I's autocracy: the doctrine of Official Nationality, the Third Section, the censorship apparatus, and the bureaucratic codification that together constituted one of nineteenth-century Europe's most determined attempts at political and intellectual control. Chapter 39 examined how that same state governed and expanded across an increasingly diverse imperial territory, from Poland to the Caucasus to Siberia and the borders of Central Asia. This chapter turns to a different, though closely related, question: what did educated Russians, living under this system of surveillance and censorship, actually think about their country, its history, its relationship to Europe, and its future? The restriction of formal political life under Nicholas I did not eliminate political thought in Russia. It displaced much of that thought into forms the censor found harder to police directly: philosophy, literary criticism, private correspondence, salon conversation, university lecture halls, and small discussion circles that met, often informally, in private apartments across Moscow and St. Petersburg.

Historians use the term intelligentsia, a word that would eventually travel from Russian into English and other languages, to describe the relatively small, educated, self-consciously critical social stratum that emerged from this environment in the 1830s and 1840s. Its members did not share a single political program, a single religious outlook, or a single vision of Russia's future. They disagreed, often passionately, about whether Russia belonged to Europe or stood apart from it, whether the reforms of Peter the Great had been a necessary modernization or a catastrophic rupture with an authentic national past, whether serfdom was a moral evil requiring urgent abolition or a complex institution entangled with questions of social order that could not be undone carelessly, and whether meaningful change could come from the state, from educated society, from the peasantry, or from revolution. What they shared was a common set of institutions, a common exposure to European philosophy and politics, and a common conviction that Russia's condition posed questions requiring serious, sustained intellectual engagement rather than simple acceptance or simple rejection.

This chapter traces several of the major currents that emerged from this ferment: the Westernizers, who argued, in various ways, that Russia's future lay in further integration with the legal, educational, and social achievements of European civilization; the Slavophiles, who argued, with equal seriousness, that Russia possessed a distinct historical path rooted in Orthodox Christianity and communal social forms that Western rationalism and individualism had obscured or destroyed; Alexander Herzen, who moved from an early Westernizing position toward a distinctive vision of Russian socialism built around the peasant commune; Vissarion Belinsky, whose literary criticism became one of the sharpest instruments of social and political argument available under censorship; and the circle gathered around Mikhail Petrashevsky, whose fate in 1849 demonstrated just how narrow the boundaries of tolerated discussion remained, encompassing everything from ordinary private conversation about European socialist theory to, within smaller satellite groupings, genuine clandestine publishing plans.

None of these figures operated in isolation from the others. They read each other's work, often in manuscript before publication, attended the same gatherings, argued in the same journals under the same censors, and frequently changed their positions over time, sometimes dramatically. The lines separating Westernizer from Slavophile, moderate reformer from committed radical, were considerably more permeable in practice than later historical summary sometimes suggests, and students should resist the temptation to sort every figure neatly into a single fixed camp.

By the chapter's end, students should understand why, in a political system that permitted no legal opposition party, no free press in the modern sense, and no representative institution capable of debating the nation's future, literature, philosophy, and private argument became unusually important vehicles of political thought — and why the vocabulary developed in these years, concerning Russia's relationship to Europe, the value and danger of the peasant commune, the moral authority of the writer, and the possibility of a distinctively Russian path of development, would continue to shape Russian political argument for the rest of the imperial period and beyond, through the reform era examined in Chapter 42, the populist movements that followed, and ultimately the revolutionary movements examined in Part IX.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain the social, institutional, and political conditions under Nicholas I that gave rise to the Russian intelligentsia as a distinct cultural formation.
  2. Describe the role of universities, journals, salons, censorship, and private circles in shaping how educated Russians debated political and philosophical questions.
  3. Analyze Chaadaev's critique of Russia's historical development and explain why it provoked such an extreme government response.
  4. Compare Westernizer and Slavophile positions on Russia's relationship to Europe, Orthodoxy, serfdom, and historical development.
  5. Distinguish internal variation within both the Westernizer and Slavophile tendencies rather than treating either as a unified, monolithic position.
  6. Trace Alexander Herzen's intellectual development from early Westernizing sympathies toward a distinctive theory of Russian socialism centered on the peasant commune.
  7. Evaluate Belinsky's literary criticism, and especially his 1847 Letter to Gogol, as a form of political argument conducted under censorship.
  8. Explain why literature, criticism, and fiction carried unusual political weight in a society with limited representative institutions and a powerful censorship apparatus.
  9. Assess the Petrashevsky circle and the state's 1849 crackdown as evidence of the narrow limits of tolerated intellectual discussion even in private settings.
  10. Connect Russian intellectual debates of the 1830s and 1840s to broader European currents, including German idealist philosophy, French socialism, and the Revolutions of 1848.
  11. Synthesize the competing visions of Russia's future articulated by different intellectual movements without treating any single position as the predetermined winner.
  12. Connect this chapter's intellectual history to the institutional history of Chapter 38, the imperial history of Chapter 39, and the reform and revolutionary movements examined in Chapters 41 and 42.

Historical Context

Chapter 38 described the architecture of Nicholas I's autocracy following his accession amid the Decembrist crisis of December 1825, examined in Chapter 37: the Third Section's political surveillance, the expansion and systematization of censorship under successive statutes, the doctrine of Official Nationality articulated by Minister of Education Sergei Uvarov around the formula of Orthodoxy, Autocracy, and Nationality, and a bureaucratic culture oriented toward order, codification, and the prevention of any recurrence of 1825's attempted uprising. That chapter also examined the condition of serfdom and the uneven, cautious beginnings of industrialization under a government deeply suspicious of social change it could not fully control.

This institutional environment shaped, rather than eliminated, Russian intellectual life. The generation that came of age in the 1830s and 1840s had typically been educated at Moscow University or, in smaller numbers, at St. Petersburg University, Kazan University, or one of the elite boarding institutions reserved for the nobility, and many had absorbed, through German-trained professors, foreign travel, private reading, and translated texts, the major currents of contemporary European philosophy, above all German idealism in the tradition of Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel and Friedrich Schelling. Hegelian philosophy of history, with its account of historical development unfolding through stages toward an ultimate rational end, provided an unusually attractive framework for a generation of young Russians anxious to understand where their own country stood in relation to this unfolding process — whether Russia had already entered the main current of world-historical development, stood poised to enter it, or occupied some permanently marginal or categorically different position outside it.

The institutional settings in which these ideas circulated deserve careful attention. Formal political organizations of the kind the Decembrists had attempted were now effectively impossible; the shock of 1825 and the vigilance of the Third Section foreclosed any open conspiratorial politics on a similar scale. In their place, educated society developed a rich, informal infrastructure of intellectual exchange: aristocratic and gentry salons, particularly in Moscow, where conversation could range across philosophy, literature, and current events with a degree of freedom unavailable in print; thick journals (tolstye zhurnaly), substantial monthly periodicals combining literature, criticism, history, and commentary on contemporary European thought, which became the primary public arena for intellectual debate despite operating under strict censorship; university lecture halls, where certain professors, Timofei Granovsky among the most celebrated, used ostensibly narrow historical or philological subjects to address broader questions about civilization, progress, and Russia's place in history; and small private circles (kruzhki), gatherings of friends who met regularly, often weekly, to read, discuss, and argue, sometimes expanding into networks that spanned cities and crossed generations.

Students should understand that this intellectual world remained numerically small. This emerging milieu was a socially narrow and numerically limited stratum, overwhelmingly drawn from the nobility and a narrow layer of educated commoners, concentrated in Moscow and St. Petersburg, with occasional outposts in provincial university towns such as Kazan. Its disproportionate historical importance rests not on its size but on its enduring influence over subsequent Russian political, literary, and intellectual culture, and on the vocabulary it developed for debating questions — Russia's relationship to the West, the value of the peasant commune, the moral responsibility of the educated toward the people, the legitimacy of autocracy — that would recur, in transformed and often radicalized form, across the remainder of the imperial period examined in this textbook's later chapters.

Section 40.1

The Emergence of the Intelligentsia

Historians use the term intelligentsia to describe a social and cultural formation that began taking shape in Russia during the 1830s and 1840s: a relatively small body of educated men and women, overwhelmingly drawn from the nobility and a thin stratum of educated commoners, who understood themselves as a group set apart by critical reflection on their society, by a sense of moral responsibility toward that society, and by sustained engagement with contemporary European thought. Students should understand that this application of the term is substantially retrospective. The word did not acquire wide currency in this specific social sense until the 1860s, a full generation after the events examined in this chapter, and historians who apply it to the circles of the 1830s and 1840s are identifying this generation as an important formative stage in the emergence of what later writers would call the intelligentsia, rather than claiming that contemporaries of Chaadaev, Herzen, and Belinsky already used the word to describe themselves in this way. With that caveat in view, something genuinely new did take shape in this period: earlier educated elites in Russia had included scholars, officials, and literary figures, but the 1830s and 1840s produced an unusually self-conscious community that increasingly saw its intellectual activity itself as a form of social and, implicitly, political engagement.

Several converging conditions produced this formation. The expansion of university education, especially at Moscow University, gave a growing number of young noblemen and, in smaller numbers, commoners sustained exposure to philosophy, history, and literature, frequently taught by professors trained in Germany or deeply versed in German philosophical currents. The aftermath of the War of 1812, examined in Chapter 36, and the subsequent occupation of parts of France, had already exposed a generation of Russian officers to European political and intellectual life, an experience Chapter 37 showed feeding directly into the Decembrist movement. The defeat and exile of the Decembrists in 1825 foreclosed any realistic prospect of organized conspiratorial politics, but it did not erase the underlying questions about Russia's political and social condition that had animated them; it redirected those questions into less immediately dangerous channels.

Censorship, examined at length in Chapter 38, played a paradoxical role in this redirection. By closing off direct political commentary, explicit criticism of autocracy, and open discussion of constitutional alternatives, the censorship apparatus pushed serious political reflection into genres the censor regarded as less threatening: philosophy, literary criticism, historical scholarship, and private correspondence. A historian such as Granovsky could lecture on medieval European towns and chivalry in ways that invited listeners to draw their own conclusions about contemporary Russian development without ever directly criticizing the tsar or the institution of serfdom. A literary critic such as Belinsky could review a novel in terms that amounted to a sweeping argument about human dignity, social justice, and the moral failures of serfdom, all while technically remaining within the bounds of literary commentary. This indirection should not be mistaken for timidity; it reflected, rather, an adaptation to a specific set of institutional constraints, and it produced some of the most intellectually serious writing of the period.

The institutional settings in which this intellectual life unfolded deserve close attention. Salons, particularly in Moscow, hosted by wealthy or well-connected families, brought together writers, scholars, and interested members of educated society for conversation that ranged freely, within the walls of a private home, across subjects that would have been far more dangerous to discuss in print. University lecture halls, especially at Moscow University, functioned as another crucial site, where students absorbed not only formal coursework but also the intellectual excitement of debate among professors and classmates. The thick journals, substantial monthly periodicals running to hundreds of pages and combining serialized fiction, poetry, literary criticism, historical essays, and digests of foreign scholarship, became the central public forum through which these ideas reached a wider, though still limited, reading public; journals such as the Telescope, the Fatherland Notes, and, from the mid-1840s, the Contemporary, published under the close supervision of government censors but nonetheless carrying arguments of real intellectual substance between their lines and, at times, openly within them. Finally, private circles, or kruzhki, brought together smaller groups of friends, often centered on a particular university generation or a particular intellectual mentor, who met regularly to read aloud, discuss philosophy, and debate contemporary events; the circle associated with Nikolai Stankevich at Moscow University in the 1830s, which included the young Belinsky and later figures connected to both Westernizing and Slavophile circles, exemplifies this pattern.

Students should understand this world as a numerically small and socially narrow educated milieu, concentrated especially in Moscow and St. Petersburg, but historically consequential far beyond its size. Membership in this emerging intelligentsia required leisure, education, and access to networks that remained the preserve of a narrow slice of Russian society; the overwhelming majority of the empire's population, bound by serfdom or by the far more modest education available to townspeople and state peasants, had no direct access to these debates. Yet the vocabulary and the questions this milieu developed, concerning Russia's relationship to Europe, the moral status of the peasantry, the legitimacy of autocratic government, and the possibility of a distinctively Russian path of social development, would echo through Russian political and intellectual life for the remainder of the century, shaping the populist movements, revolutionary parties, and liberal reform movements examined in this textbook's later chapters.

Historical Vocabulary
Intelligentsia

Intelligentsia denotes a socially and culturally distinct body of educated people who understand themselves as bound together by critical reflection on their society and a sense of moral responsibility toward it. In the specifically Russian social sense this chapter examines, the word is applied substantially in retrospect: it did not become common usage for describing this kind of group until the 1860s, after the period this chapter covers.

Historical context: Historians apply the term to the circles of the 1830s and 1840s because these circles exhibit, in nascent form, the self-consciousness, critical orientation, and sense of social mission that later generations would explicitly label with this word. The generation examined in this chapter did not uniformly use the term to describe itself.

Why it matters: Treating the term carefully, rather than projecting its later, fully developed meaning backward without qualification, helps students avoid assuming that this generation possessed a fixed collective identity or political program; it was a formative, still-developing social and cultural phenomenon whose later naming should not obscure its contemporary fluidity.

Section 40.2

Chaadaev and Russia's Historical Problem

Pyotr Chaadaev, born into a noble family and a veteran of the Napoleonic Wars, had spent years in Western Europe and corresponded with European intellectuals before composing, in French and in the form of a private letter to a female correspondent, a set of philosophical reflections now known as the Philosophical Letters. The first of these letters was published by the journal's editor, Nikolai Nadezhdin, in the Moscow journal Telescope in September 1836, after Chaadaev had agreed to its publication, although the precise details of its translation from French and its editorial preparation remain subjects of historical reconstruction. The letter advanced an argument that struck much of educated Russian society as shocking in its bluntness: Russia, Chaadaev contended, had developed in isolation from the central currents of European civilization, had absorbed neither the Roman legal inheritance nor the unifying cultural and religious development that, in his account, bound Western Christendom together, and had consequently produced no significant contribution to the universal history of human progress. Russia, in his vivid phrase, existed as if suspended outside of time, possessing a past that taught nothing and offering no clear direction toward the future.

The government's response to this argument illustrates, in concentrated form, the operation of the system examined in Chapter 38. Rather than engaging the argument through ordinary censorship procedures before publication, which in this instance had failed to prevent the letter's appearance, Nicholas I's government reacted after the fact with exceptional severity: the Telescope was closed permanently, its editor, Nikolai Nadezhdin, was exiled to the northern provinces, and Chaadaev himself was declared, by official decree, to be mentally ill, placed under compulsory medical supervision, and forbidden to publish further. This response should be understood carefully. It was not primarily a medical judgment but a political one: the declaration functioned politically to discredit and silence Chaadaev without a public trial, sparing the government a more explicit confrontation with the substance of his argument in open court. The episode also demonstrated the limits of even highly placed social status as protection against the state's displeasure once a document had become public in a way the government regarded as dangerous.

Students should also understand that Chaadaev's argument was not simply a secular comparison of Russian and European historical achievement; it rested on a distinctly providential and religiously inflected philosophy of history. Chaadaev understood Western European civilization's coherence as flowing substantially from the unifying institutional and spiritual authority of the Roman Catholic Church during the medieval period, an authority he believed had bound European peoples together around a shared set of ideas and historical purposes even amid political fragmentation. Russia's inheritance of Christianity from Byzantium rather than Rome, examined in Chapter 9, appeared in Chaadaev's analysis not merely as a matter of ecclesiastical administration but as a providential separation from the historical current he believed Catholic Christendom had generated. This religious dimension of his argument should caution students against reducing the first Philosophical Letter to a simple "Russia versus Europe" comparison; it was, at its core, a meditation on providence, historical purpose, and the religious foundations of civilizational coherence, one that later Slavophile thinkers would directly contest by offering an alternative, Orthodox-centered account of spiritual community.

Chaadaev's own position was considerably more complicated than the bare summary of his first letter suggests. In later writings, including his unpublished "Apology of a Madman," composed after the government's verdict, he qualified and partially revised his earlier pessimism, suggesting that Russia's very historical lateness and its freedom from certain entrenched European institutions might allow it to choose a different, and potentially more fortunate, path forward, learning selectively from Western achievements while avoiding Western errors. This qualification should not be read as a simple retraction; rather, it illustrates that Chaadaev's central concern was not a fixed verdict condemning Russia but a genuine, searching, and unresolved question about where Russia stood in relation to European historical development, and what that position implied for its future.

This chapter treats Chaadaev not as providing the answer to Russia's historical problem but as posing the question around which the chapter's other major figures would organize their arguments. His letter forced into sharp focus several issues that would occupy Westernizers and Slavophiles alike throughout the following decade: Had Russia's historical development actually been backward relative to Western Europe, or merely different? Was the separation from the main currents of Western Christendom, resulting from Rus's historical inheritance of Orthodox Christianity from Byzantium rather than from Rome, examined in Chapter 9, a weakness to be overcome or a source of genuine cultural distinctiveness to be valued? Did Russia need to follow a European pattern of development, adapting its institutions, law, and social organization to resemble those of France, Britain, or the German states? What role should Orthodoxy, the autocratic state, and traditional communal institutions play in any answer to these questions? Chaadaev did not resolve these questions; he made it impossible, for the educated public that read or heard of his letter, to avoid confronting them.

Section 40.3

Westernizers

The term Westernizer (zapadnik) describes a broad and internally diverse intellectual tendency that coalesced chiefly in Moscow during the late 1830s and 1840s, uniting figures who, despite real disagreements among themselves, generally shared the conviction that Russia belonged within the broader historical development of European civilization and needed substantial legal, educational, social, and institutional reform in order to realize that belonging fully. Westernizers should not be understood as a political party in any modern sense; they possessed no formal organization, no agreed platform, and no single leader, and their shared label described a loose affinity of outlook rather than a disciplined movement.

Among the most influential Westernizing voices was Timofei Granovsky, a historian at Moscow University whose public lectures on medieval and early modern European history drew enthusiastic audiences well beyond his formal students. Granovsky's lectures rarely addressed Russian politics directly, remaining, on their surface, scholarly treatments of European chivalry, the medieval town, and the Renaissance; but many listeners understood them as implicit arguments that Russia participated in, or ought to participate in, the same processes of legal, civic, and cultural development that Granovsky traced across Western Europe. His lecture hall became, in this sense, one of the central informal institutions of Westernizing thought, a space in which an ostensibly neutral historical subject carried unmistakable contemporary resonance for an audience attuned to hear it.

Konstantin Kavelin, a legal historian and close associate of Westernizing circles, developed arguments for the gradual historical emergence of individual rights and rational legal institutions in Russia, treating the development of personal legal status, including the eventual prospect of emancipation from serfdom, as part of a broader historical process comparable to developments elsewhere in Europe. Kavelin's historical-legal scholarship exemplified a characteristic Westernizing concern: the belief that Russia's historical development, properly understood, could be shown to follow patterns recognizable from European legal and social history, even if Russia remained, for the time being, considerably less advanced along that path.

Students should resist the common oversimplification that Westernizers simply wanted Russia to imitate Western Europe wholesale. Most Westernizing thinkers were engaged in a more complex project: identifying which aspects of European legal, educational, and social development represented genuine, transferable human achievements, applicable to Russia's own circumstances, as opposed to merely local European peculiarities. Many Westernizers were simultaneously critical of specific Western European institutions and political arrangements, and few advocated simple, uncritical imitation. Their shared commitments centered on several recurring concerns: opposition to serfdom as morally indefensible and economically inefficient; support for the rule of law and legal predictability as a check on arbitrary autocratic and bureaucratic power; support for the expansion of education, particularly university education, as a precondition for social progress; and a conviction that individual personal dignity, what Westernizers often discussed under German-influenced philosophical vocabulary concerning the development of personality or selfhood, deserved greater institutional recognition than existing Russian law and custom provided.

Students should also see that "Europe" itself meant something slightly different to each major Westernizing figure, a variation that gives the tendency its real intellectual texture rather than reducing it to a single imitative impulse. For Kavelin, Europe meant above all a model of gradual legal and constitutional development, in which personal rights and rational institutions had slowly displaced arbitrary privilege; his Westernism was chiefly a historical-legal argument. For Granovsky, Europe meant a connected historical process, traceable through the medieval town, the Renaissance, and the gradual expansion of civic life, that Russia might eventually be shown to share rather than merely imitate; his Westernism worked through historical narrative rather than direct political argument. Belinsky, examined fully in Section 40.6, drew on Westernizing assumptions about Europe's humanitarian and legal achievements but redirected them into a passionate, literature-centered argument for human dignity and against serfdom. Herzen, examined in Section 40.5, began from these same Westernizing premises before his direct observation of 1848 led him to conclude that Western Europe's own political achievements remained incomplete, redirecting his attention toward a specifically Russian socialist alternative. This internal variation should prepare students to read Sections 40.5 and 40.6 not as departures from Westernizing thought but as some of its most important and consequential extensions.

Westernizers divided among themselves over how far and how fast such change should proceed, and by what means. Some favored a cautious, gradualist path of reform initiated from within the existing autocratic framework, hoping an enlightened government might itself undertake the necessary legal and social changes; others, including Alexander Herzen, examined in Section 40.5, grew increasingly impatient with this gradualist hope and moved toward more radical conclusions, particularly after the disappointments of 1848. This internal variation matters: the Westernizing tendency encompassed cautious legal reformers and increasingly radical critics of the existing order alike, united chiefly by their shared conviction that Russia's future lay through, rather than against, engagement with the broader currents of European historical development.

Section 40.4

Slavophiles

The Slavophile tendency, emerging alongside and in direct dialogue with Westernizing circles during the same decades, should be understood as a serious and internally sophisticated intellectual position rather than as a simple defense of the existing autocratic order or an unreflective rejection of Western civilization. Slavophile thinkers, concentrated, like their Westernizing counterparts, chiefly in Moscow, argued that Russia possessed a distinct historical path, rooted in Orthodox Christianity and in forms of communal social life that Western European historical development, in their analysis, had progressively eroded through the growth of rationalism, individualism, and formal legalism.

Aleksei Khomyakov, perhaps the most systematic of the early Slavophile thinkers, developed the concept of sobornost, a term denoting a form of spiritual and communal unity achieved not through external compulsion or formal institutional authority but through the free, loving union of believers within the Orthodox Church. Khomyakov contrasted this ideal, which he traced to Orthodox ecclesiology and to the communal spirit he attributed to pre-Petrine Russian society, with what he viewed as the characteristic defects of Western Christendom: a Roman Catholic tradition he criticized for excessive institutional authority and legal formalism, and a Protestant tradition he criticized for excessive individualism and rationalist fragmentation, both of which he believed had damaged the possibility of genuine communal spiritual unity in the West.

Ivan Kireevsky extended this critique into epistemology and the philosophy of history, arguing that Western rational thought, however impressive its scientific and technical achievements, had become fragmented, analytical, and disconnected from the integral, lived wholeness of faith, tradition, and communal experience that he associated with authentic Orthodox and, in his account, authentically Russian ways of knowing. Kireevsky's critique of Western rationalism should not be mistaken for simple anti-intellectualism; he was himself deeply read in German philosophy, including the same Hegelian and post-Hegelian currents that shaped many Westernizers, and his argument proceeded through careful engagement with that philosophical tradition rather than through its dismissal.

Konstantin Aksakov developed a particularly influential account of pre-Petrine Russian political life, idealizing what he presented as a historical relationship between the tsar and "the land," meaning the Russian people and their traditional communal institutions, especially the peasant commune and the historical zemsky sobor examined in earlier chapters of this textbook, as a relationship of mutual trust and moral partnership rather than one of bureaucratic domination. In Aksakov's account, Peter the Great's reforms, examined in Chapters 31 and 32, had ruptured this relationship, imposing an alien, bureaucratic, Western-style state apparatus between the tsar and the Russian people and thereby damaging an authentic, communal political tradition that Slavophiles believed Russia should recover, in spirit if not in literal institutional form.

Two points deserve particular emphasis. First, Slavophiles were not, in general, simple defenders of Nicholas I's government or of serfdom. Many Slavophile thinkers, including Aksakov, directed sharp criticism at the bureaucratic apparatus, censorship, and coercive character of the contemporary autocratic state, and several prominent Slavophiles, including Khomyakov himself, were outspoken critics of serfdom on moral and religious grounds, even as they continued to idealize certain communal institutions associated with peasant life. Slavophile thought should therefore be read as offering its own, distinct critique of existing conditions rather than as a straightforward apologia for them. Second, the Slavophile idealization of pre-Petrine Russia and of the peasant commune should itself be treated critically, as a historically situated intellectual construction produced by educated nineteenth-century thinkers reflecting on an imagined past, rather than as a straightforward or objective historical description of how pre-Petrine Russian society and politics had actually functioned. Historians today generally treat Slavophile accounts of a harmonious, communal pre-Petrine order as idealized and selective rather than as reliable historical reconstruction.

The central relationship between Westernizers and Slavophiles, examined further in this chapter's Historical Debate feature, is best understood neither as simple opposition nor as hidden agreement but as a genuine disagreement over Russia's historical path conducted between two groups who shared a surprising amount of common ground: both were highly educated participants in the same Moscow intellectual world, both engaged seriously with contemporary European, and especially German, philosophy, both were critical of significant aspects of the existing Russian order, including, in varying degrees, serfdom and bureaucratic autocracy, and both were searching, through different historical and philosophical routes, for a coherent account of Russia's identity and its proper future.

Historical Vocabulary
Sobornost

Sobornost, a term developed most fully by Aleksei Khomyakov, denotes a form of spiritual and communal unity achieved through the free, loving union of believers within the Orthodox Church, rather than through external compulsion, formal institutional hierarchy, or individual rational assent alone.

Historical context: Khomyakov developed the concept in explicit contrast to what he viewed as the characteristic defects of Western Christendom: a Roman Catholic tradition he criticized for excessive institutional and legal authority, and a Protestant tradition he criticized for excessive individualism and rationalist fragmentation. He extended the concept, by analogy, to an idealized vision of communal life in pre-Petrine Russian society.

Why it matters: Sobornost functioned as the positive religious and philosophical foundation beneath the broader Slavophile critique of Western rationalism examined in this section; without understanding the concept, Slavophile arguments about community, tradition, and the peasant commune can appear merely nostalgic rather than philosophically grounded.

Section 40.5

Herzen and Russian Socialism

Alexander Herzen occupies a pivotal position in this chapter's narrative, illustrating how the Westernizer-Slavophile debate examined in Sections 40.3 and 40.4 could develop, for at least one major thinker, into something considerably more radical than either position, taken alone, had proposed. Born into a wealthy noble family as the illegitimate son of a Russian landowner, Herzen received an excellent education, became involved in student radical circles at Moscow University in the early 1830s, and was arrested in 1834 and sent into provincial exile for his association with circles the government regarded as politically suspect; after returning, he was rearrested in 1841 and exiled again, this time to Novgorod, well before his most important intellectual work had begun.

In his early career, Herzen aligned broadly with Westernizing positions, sharing the conviction that Russia's future depended on engagement with European legal, educational, and philosophical development, and participating actively in Moscow's Westernizing salons and circles during the 1840s, including close personal and intellectual relationships with Belinsky, examined in Section 40.6. Herzen's engagement with European thought during this period extended beyond German idealist philosophy to include the developing tradition of French socialism, associated with thinkers such as Charles Fourier, Henri de Saint-Simon, and Pierre-Joseph Proudhon, whose critiques of industrial capitalism, private property, and social inequality offered Herzen a vocabulary for thinking about social injustice that went considerably beyond the legal and educational reforms most Westernizers emphasized.

In 1847, Herzen left Russia, never to return, traveling first to France and later to Italy. His departure placed him in Paris at the moment the Revolutions of 1848 broke out across much of Western and Central Europe, and his direct observation of these events, and especially of their ultimate failure to produce the lasting social transformation many European radicals had hoped for, proved decisive for his subsequent thinking. The years that followed brought him severe personal losses alongside political disillusionment, and after further time on the European continent he settled in London in 1852, where he would establish the influential émigré Russian-language press whose later publications, including the Bell, this textbook's treatment of the reform era will reference. Herzen watched the June Days of 1848 in Paris, in which a workers' uprising was violently suppressed by the same republican government that had initially promised broader social reform, and concluded from this and related events that Western European bourgeois society, despite its republican and constitutional forms, remained fundamentally committed to the protection of private property and existing social hierarchy rather than to genuine social transformation on behalf of ordinary working people.

This disillusionment with the political trajectory of Western Europe, articulated most fully in his later work From the Other Shore, redirected Herzen's attention back toward Russia, and specifically toward an institution Slavophile thinkers had already idealized for different reasons: the peasant commune, or obshchina, the traditional Russian village institution responsible for collectively managing various aspects of village life and, in many repartitional communes, for periodically reassigning land among member households, though practice varied considerably by region and the institution itself was, as later scholarship has shown, substantially reshaped in the historical imagination of nineteenth-century intellectuals, including Herzen, who found in it what they were already inclined to look for. The commune is examined in greater social and economic detail in Chapter 42's treatment of the emancipation settlement. Where Slavophiles valued the commune chiefly as an expression of Orthodox communal spirit and traditional social harmony, Herzen came to see in it something different: a possible foundation for a distinctively Russian form of socialism, one that might allow Russia, precisely because it had not yet developed the full apparatus of Western industrial capitalism and bourgeois property relations, to move toward a socialist future without first passing through the stage of capitalist development and its attendant inequalities that Herzen now believed had corrupted the revolutionary promise of 1848 in the West.

This argument, often summarized under the label Russian socialism or, in later historiography, as an early form of agrarian populism, should be understood carefully and without anachronism. Herzen was not predicting, and did not intend to predict, the Bolshevik Revolution of 1917, examined in Part IX of this textbook; nor should his thought be read as a direct, unbroken intellectual lineage leading inevitably to later Marxist revolutionary politics, which rested on substantially different theoretical premises concerning industrial capitalism and the urban working class. Herzen's importance for this chapter lies in his demonstration of how the questions posed by Chaadaev, and the Westernizer-Slavophile debate that followed, could be reworked into a new synthesis: a vision of Russia's historical backwardness, relative to Western industrial development, as a potential advantage rather than simply a deficiency, because it allowed Russia the possibility of a different, and in Herzen's hopeful assessment, more humane path toward social transformation. This idea would resurface, transformed and contested, in the populist movements examined in Chapter 42 and in subsequent debates among Russian revolutionaries about the relationship between peasant communalism and industrial socialism.

Section 40.6

Belinsky and Literary Criticism

Vissarion Belinsky, the son of a provincial doctor and among the first prominent figures of the Russian intelligentsia to come from outside the landed nobility, built, over a brief career cut short by his death from tuberculosis in 1848, a reputation as Russia's most influential literary critic and one of the sharpest intellectual instruments available to educated society under the censorship regime examined in Chapter 38. Belinsky's importance for this chapter rests not on his biography alone but on what his career demonstrates: that literary criticism, an ostensibly narrow genre concerned with evaluating novels, poems, and plays, could function as a vehicle for sweeping arguments about human dignity, social justice, serfdom, religion, and the proper relationship between the writer and society.

Writing for successive thick journals, including the Fatherland Notes during the early 1840s and, from 1847, the Contemporary, Belinsky developed a critical practice that evaluated literary works not merely by formal or aesthetic criteria but by their truthfulness to social reality and their implicit moral and social argument. A novel's depiction of a landowner's cruelty toward serfs, or of a bureaucrat's petty tyranny, or of a woman's constrained social position, became, under Belinsky's treatment, an occasion for extended reflection on serfdom, bureaucratic power, and gender relations that direct political commentary could not have expressed so openly under the censorship rules then in force. Belinsky's enthusiastic promotion of the young Nikolai Gogol's early realist fiction, and later of an emerging generation of writers including the young Fyodor Dostoevsky and Ivan Turgenev, exemplified this approach: Belinsky read literary realism itself as implicitly progressive, valuable precisely because it exposed social conditions that more direct forms of commentary could not address.

Belinsky's intellectual development also illustrates the broader currents traced throughout this chapter. In his early career, under the influence of German idealist philosophy absorbed through the Stankevich circle at Moscow University, Belinsky passed through a period some historians describe as a reconciliation with reality, in which he attempted to find philosophical justification for existing Russian conditions, including aspects of autocratic authority, as necessary stages within a larger historical process of rational development. By the mid-1840s, however, Belinsky had decisively rejected this position, moving toward an increasingly explicit commitment to social criticism, human dignity, and opposition to serfdom, a shift closely connected to his personal and intellectual friendship with Herzen and to his deepening engagement with the moral and social questions examined throughout this chapter.

This trajectory culminated in 1847 with Belinsky's celebrated and furious response to Nikolai Gogol's Selected Passages from Correspondence with Friends, a work in which Gogol, departing sharply from the social criticism of his earlier fiction, advanced a paternalistic Christian defense of existing social hierarchy, landlord obligations toward peasants, Orthodoxy, and obedience to legitimate religious and political authority. Belinsky read this stance as an intolerable defense of serfdom and clerical-autocratic domination, and his response, composed as an open letter while he was traveling abroad for his health in Salzbrunn, in Prussian Silesia, condemned what he took to be Gogol's position in uncompromising terms, arguing that Russia needed not more prayer and submission but the rule of law, individual rights, and an end to serfdom. The letter could not be legally published within Russia and circulated instead in manuscript copies among educated readers; it was not printed in full until 1855, when Herzen published it abroad in his émigré periodical the Polar Star. This document, examined in detail in this chapter's Primary Source Window, stands as one of the most important texts of the Russian intelligentsia's early history, precisely because it shows literary and moral argument operating as direct political commentary, reaching a wide readership through manuscript circulation that bypassed formal censorship entirely even before its eventual printing abroad.

Belinsky's career should be understood as a case study preparing the broader analysis developed in Section 40.8: it demonstrates concretely how a particular critic, working within the institutional constraints of the thick journal and the censorship apparatus, transformed literary evaluation into one of the period's most consequential forms of political argument, and it illustrates the personal and intellectual networks, connecting Belinsky to Herzen, to Granovsky's Westernizing circle, and, through opposition, to Slavophile thinkers such as Khomyakov and Kireevsky, that bound this chapter's major figures together despite their genuine disagreements.

Section 40.7

Petrashevsky and Radical Circles

Where the Westernizer and Slavophile circles examined in Sections 40.3 and 40.4, and figures such as Herzen and Belinsky, generally operated at the edges of what the censorship and surveillance apparatus described in Chapter 38 would tolerate, the circle gathered around Mikhail Petrashevsky, a minor official in the Ministry of Foreign Affairs, tested those boundaries more directly, and the state's response demonstrated, with unmistakable clarity, how narrow those boundaries actually remained, even for conversation that never reached print and was never intended to.

Beginning in the mid-1840s, Petrashevsky hosted regular Friday evening gatherings at his St. Petersburg apartment, bringing together a loose and socially varied group of civil servants, military officers, writers, and students to discuss contemporary European political and social thought, including the utopian socialist theories of Charles Fourier, whose vision of cooperative communal living organized around small self-sufficient associations called phalanges attracted particular interest among some circle members. Discussions at these gatherings ranged widely, encompassing criticism of serfdom, censorship, and the limitations of Russian legal and educational institutions, alongside more purely theoretical discussion of European socialist and utopian literature. The circle maintained a lending library of banned or restricted foreign books and periodicals, and some participants explored the possibility of establishing a private printing press, a step that, had it been realized, would have moved the group considerably closer to the kind of clandestine political organization the Third Section existed specifically to prevent.

Students should understand that "the Petrashevsky circle" actually describes several overlapping, increasingly narrow levels of association rather than a single, uniform organization. The broad Friday gatherings at Petrashevsky's apartment drew dozens of occasional visitors interested chiefly in discussion, reading, and the exchange of ideas, many of whom never moved beyond conversation. Within and alongside this broader circle, a smaller satellite gathering associated with Nikolai Palm and Sergei Durov met separately and discussed more radical literary and political material. And within or alongside this milieu in turn, a still smaller, more genuinely secret and organized grouping formed around Nikolai Speshnev, whose members discussed the establishment of a clandestine press and the composition of incendiary pamphlets intended for distribution beyond the circle's own membership. This layered internal differentiation matters historically: the government's 1849 prosecution treated participants across all these levels of involvement with considerable severity, but historians today distinguish the loose, broad discussion network from the Palm-Durov satellite circle, and distinguish both from Speshnev's more organized and more genuinely conspiratorial inner grouping.

The young Fyodor Dostoevsky, not yet the author of the major novels for which he would later become famous, participated in Petrashevsky's broader Friday gatherings during the late 1840s, drawn initially by their discussion of social and literary questions, and also moved in the more radical Palm-Durov satellite circle; he is further identified, in the scholarship on the case, as having joined Speshnev's more narrowly conspiratorial grouping, whose members discussed clandestine printing and the preparation of inflammatory material. Dostoevsky's involvement, examined here as a case study in the limits of tolerated discussion rather than as an occasion for extended literary biography, should be understood as spanning this full range, from ordinary participation in open discussion to association with the network's most genuinely secretive element, rather than as evidence either of casual conversation alone or of a single, uniform revolutionary program shared by every participant.

In April 1849, in the context of the broader European reaction following the Revolutions of 1848, examined further in this chapter's Russia and the World feature, Nicholas I's government ordered the arrest of Petrashevsky and dozens of associates, including Dostoevsky. The subsequent investigation and military tribunal, operating with considerable severity, sentenced a number of the accused, including Dostoevsky, to death by firing squad, a sentence that reflected the government's determination to treat even private, informal discussion of socialist ideas as a serious political threat. In December 1849, the condemned men were brought to the Semyonovsky parade ground in St. Petersburg, prepared for execution, and subjected to the full ritual of a firing squad assembling before them, only to have the sentence commuted to penal servitude and exile at the final moment, a deliberately staged demonstration of the government's power over life and death that left a profound and lasting psychological impression on several of those who endured it, including Dostoevsky, who would later draw on the experience in his fiction.

The Petrashevsky affair matters for this chapter in several distinct ways. It demonstrates that the boundaries of tolerated discussion under Nicholas I, even in the final, most repressive years of his reign, remained narrow enough to criminalize private conversation about European socialist theory, circulation of prohibited books and periodicals, and, within parts of the network, early movement toward clandestine printing or conspiratorial organization, even where that movement had not yet produced any fully realized illegal press or revolutionary program. It illustrates the direct connection, examined further below, between the European Revolutions of 1848 and an intensified crackdown on Russian intellectual life, as the government, deeply alarmed by revolutionary upheaval abroad, grew correspondingly less tolerant of any domestic discussion that touched on socialism or political reform. Finally, it provides a clear and important contrast with the circles examined earlier in this chapter: Petrashevsky's gathering, unlike the Westernizer and Slavophile salons, had begun to move, in the activities of its most radical sub-group, from philosophical and literary discussion toward the early stages of conspiratorial organization, however limited and unrealized, a distinction this textbook's later chapters will show becoming increasingly important as Russian radicalism developed, in the following decades, into more fully organized revolutionary movements.

Section 40.8

Literature as Political Debate

Sections 40.1 through 40.7 have repeatedly encountered a single recurring pattern: in a political system that permitted no legal opposition party, no free press in the modern sense, and no representative national institution capable of openly debating the country's future, literature, literary criticism, and fiction became unusually important vehicles through which educated Russians conducted what amounted to political argument. This section draws that pattern together explicitly and asks why it took this particular form in Russia during the 1830s and 1840s.

Several institutional and cultural conditions converged to produce this outcome. Censorship statutes, examined in detail in Chapter 38, restricted direct political commentary, explicit criticism of the tsar, autocracy, or the church, and open discussion of constitutional alternatives, while permitting, with varying degrees of latitude depending on the political climate of a given year, a surprisingly wide range of literary and critical writing, provided its political implications remained sufficiently indirect. A novel depicting the petty cruelty of an estate owner toward serfs, or the suffocating pettiness of provincial bureaucratic life, or the constrained legal and social position of women, could pass the censor's review as a work of literary realism while nonetheless carrying unmistakable social and political argument for a readership attuned to recognize it. The thick journals, examined in Section 40.1, provided the essential infrastructure for this indirect political discourse, publishing serialized fiction alongside literary criticism that made the social and political implications of that fiction explicit, within the limits the censor allowed.

The careers and works of major writers of the period illustrate this pattern concretely, though students should understand that this section surveys literature's political function rather than attempting a comprehensive literary history. Alexander Pushkin, whose major work largely predates the period of this chapter but whose influence pervaded it, had already demonstrated, in works touching on serfdom, autocratic power, and historical memory, literature's capacity to address politically sensitive themes through historical distance, allegory, and artistic indirection. Nikolai Gogol's early fiction, including his depictions of provincial bureaucratic corruption and absurdity, offered a form of social satire that, as Section 40.6 examined, Belinsky read as powerfully progressive in its exposure of Russian social conditions, even as Gogol's own later political and religious views, expressed in his 1847 Selected Passages, moved in a sharply different, conservative direction. Mikhail Lermontov's poetry and prose explored themes of alienation, individual freedom, and conflict with arbitrary authority that resonated with the broader intellectual concerns of the period. Ivan Turgenev, beginning his career during these years, would soon produce fiction directly engaging the condition of serfdom and the social position of the educated gentry, work that gained particular resonance during the emancipation era examined in Chapter 42. The young Dostoevsky, before his 1849 arrest examined in Section 40.7, had already begun exploring, in his early fiction, themes of poverty, social humiliation, and the moral status of the socially marginalized that connected directly to the broader humanitarian concerns Belinsky championed.

This section's Multiple Perspectives feature examines how differently the political stakes of literature appeared to three distinct participants in this system: the government censor, charged with preventing dangerous ideas from reaching print while permitting enough literary and intellectual activity to maintain Russia's claim to cultural sophistication; a critic such as Belinsky, who understood literary evaluation itself as a form of moral and social argument with direct political consequences; and a writer or engaged reader, for whom literature offered one of the only available spaces for serious public reflection on social conditions that direct political commentary could not address. These differing perspectives help explain both why literature carried such disproportionate political weight in this period and why the government's relationship to literary culture remained persistently uneasy: Nicholas I's government valued literary and cultural achievement as evidence of Russia's civilizational standing, examined in Chapter 38's treatment of education and censorship policy, while simultaneously fearing literature's capacity to carry social and political criticism past the formal boundaries censorship was designed to enforce.

The broader significance of this pattern extends well beyond the 1830s and 1840s. The practice of using fiction, criticism, and literary argument to address questions that direct political commentary could not reach became a durable feature of Russian intellectual and cultural life, recurring in transformed forms throughout the remainder of the imperial period, examined in later chapters' treatment of the intelligentsia, the populist movement, and revolutionary thought, and persisting, in yet further transformed and more dangerous conditions, into the Soviet period examined in Part X, where writers and critics would again confront the challenge of addressing social and political reality under conditions of strict censorship and political control.

Section 40.9

Russia, Europe, and Competing Paths of Development

This chapter's final analytical section draws together the competing positions examined across Sections 40.1 through 40.8 and asks what, collectively, they reveal about Russian educated society's understanding of its own historical situation by the early 1850s, on the eve of the Crimean War crisis examined in Chapter 41.

Chaadaev's 1836 letter, examined in Section 40.2, posed a question rather than supplying an answer: did Russia belong to the central current of European historical development, stand apart from it, or occupy some distinct position requiring its own historical explanation? By the early 1850s, educated Russian society had generated several serious, internally coherent, and sharply competing answers to this question, none of which had achieved anything resembling consensus, and none of which, this textbook emphasizes, represented a predetermined or inevitable outcome of Russia's historical development.

Westernizers, examined in Section 40.3, answered that Russia's future lay through further engagement with the legal, educational, and social achievements of European civilization, understanding Peter the Great's reforms, examined in Chapters 31 and 32, as a necessary, if painful, step toward Russia's fuller participation in a shared process of historical progress. Slavophiles, examined in Section 40.4, answered that Russia possessed its own distinct historical path, rooted in Orthodox Christianity and in communal social institutions that Western rationalism and individualism had damaged in their own civilization, and that Peter's reforms, far from a necessary modernization, represented a rupture from an authentic national tradition that Russia needed, in some fashion, to recover. Herzen, examined in Section 40.5, developed yet a third position, agreeing with Westernizers that Russia's historical backwardness relative to Western Europe was real, while concluding, from his direct observation of the failures of 1848, that this very backwardness, embodied in the unindustrialized peasant commune Slavophiles had already idealized for different reasons, might allow Russia to bypass the social costs of Western industrial capitalism and arrive at a more humane socialist future by an alternative route.

These competing positions generated, in turn, further questions that educated Russians continued to debate without resolution. Was the emancipation of the serfs, not yet achieved at the time this chapter's events unfold but increasingly discussed as a moral and practical necessity across Westernizing, Slavophile, and radical circles alike, a sufficient reform on its own, or merely a first step toward deeper social and political transformation? Was the peasant commune, valued by Slavophiles as a vehicle of traditional communal harmony and by Herzen as a potential foundation for socialism, actually a backward, economically inefficient institution holding back agricultural development, as some critics, including certain Westernizers, argued? Could meaningful reform come from the autocratic state itself, as some moderate Westernizers continued to hope, or did genuine change require pressure from educated society, or, as the more radical implications of Petrashevsky's circle suggested, from more direct forms of political action? Should Russia's path toward a better future be constitutional and legal in character, moral and religious, socially communal, or, in the most radical formulations beginning to emerge by mid-century, revolutionary?

This chapter's central historiographical point, developed throughout but stated here directly, is that none of these questions had been settled by the early 1850s, and none of their eventual historical resolutions, whether the Great Reforms examined in Chapter 42, the populist movements that followed, or the revolutionary upheavals examined in Part IX of this textbook, should be read backward into this period as though they were already predetermined outcomes implicit in the Westernizer-Slavophile debate or in Herzen's turn toward Russian socialism. What this generation of Russian thinkers actually accomplished, whatever the eventual fate of their specific arguments, was the creation of a remarkably rich and enduring vocabulary, a set of questions, concepts, and reference points, including the relationship between Russia and Europe, the moral and practical status of the peasant commune, the responsibility of educated society toward the common people, and the political function of literature and criticism, through which subsequent generations of Russian political and intellectual life, across the reform era, the revolutionary movements, and even, in transformed and contested ways, the Soviet and post-Soviet periods examined in this textbook's later chapters, would continue to argue about Russia's identity, its relationship to the wider world, and its future.

Geography Study

The Intellectual Map of Moscow and St. Petersburg

To follow this chapter, students should understand the distinct geography of Russia's two major intellectual centers in the 1830s and 1840s:

  • Moscow University, the principal institutional home of lecturers such as Granovsky and a central meeting point for student circles, including the Stankevich circle of the 1830s
  • Private Moscow salons and townhouses hosting Westernizer and Slavophile gatherings, often located within the same social networks and sometimes attended by members of both tendencies
  • St. Petersburg, home to the imperial bureaucracy, the Third Section, and, in a very different register, Petrashevsky's private apartment, where the Friday gatherings examined in Section 40.7 took place
  • The Semyonovsky parade ground in St. Petersburg, site of the December 1849 mock execution of the Petrashevsky circle members
  • Salzbrunn, in the Prussian province of Silesia (a Central European spa town where Belinsky was traveling for his health when he composed the Letter to Gogol in 1847)
  • Paris and other Western European cities where Herzen witnessed the Revolutions of 1848 at close hand after his 1847 departure from Russia

Brief paraphrase of the letter's argument (not a verbatim translated quotation):

Belinsky argues that Gogol has profoundly misunderstood his own country and his own earlier literary achievement. Russia, in Belinsky's assessment, does not need more preaching about submission, humility, or the religious sanctity of existing authority; it needs the abolition of serfdom, the protection of basic legal rights for ordinary people, and the rigorous enforcement of laws that already exist but are routinely ignored by those in power. He contends that the Russian public, far from needing religious instruction from its writers, has shown itself most receptive to literature that exposes social injustice, and he criticizes Gogol for aligning himself with the forces of religious and political authority against the interests of the enslaved peasantry.

Analysis: This letter illustrates, more vividly than any other single document from the period, how literary criticism functioned as political argument under the censorship conditions examined in Section 40.8. Belinsky frames his criticism as a response to a literary and religious work, yet the substance of his argument addresses serfdom, legal reform, and the proper social responsibility of the writer directly. The letter's illegal manuscript circulation, rather than formal publication, demonstrates both the effectiveness and the limits of the thick-journal system examined in Section 40.1: ideas too dangerous for legal print could still reach a significant readership through private copying, though at greater risk to those involved in its circulation.

Historical Debate

Were Westernizers and Slavophiles True Opposites?

The question: Should historians treat Westernizers and Slavophiles as fundamentally opposed intellectual camps, or did they share enough common ground that the opposition between them has been overstated?

The sharp-opposition position: Some historians emphasize the genuine and substantial differences between the two tendencies: Westernizers generally affirmed the value of Peter the Great's reforms and Russia's engagement with European legal and rational traditions, while Slavophiles generally regarded those same reforms as a damaging rupture from an authentic communal and Orthodox national tradition. These differences produced real personal and intellectual conflict, including sharp public disagreements and, at times, lasting personal estrangements between individuals who had once been close friends.
The shared-ground position: Other historians emphasize that both tendencies emerged from the same narrow Moscow intellectual world, attended the same university lectures and salons, engaged seriously with the same German philosophical traditions, and were both, in varying degrees, critical of bureaucratic autocracy and censorship, and in many cases of serfdom as well. On this reading, the Westernizer-Slavophile debate should be understood less as a battle between reaction and progress and more as a disagreement between two different critical responses to the same underlying dissatisfaction with existing Russian conditions.

Evidence: Historians weigh the personal correspondence and social connections linking Westernizer and Slavophile circles, the specific institutional and political positions each group actually advocated (as opposed to later simplified characterizations), and the ways individual thinkers sometimes shifted their emphases over time or combined elements of both positions.

Points of agreement: Most historians agree that both tendencies were composed of serious, philosophically engaged thinkers responding to the same Chaadaevan question about Russia's historical identity, that neither tendency was monolithic or fully unified internally, and that prominent figures in both camps could be sharply critical of serfdom, even though they frequently disagreed over its proposed remedy, timing, and underlying justification.

Points of disagreement: Historians differ over how much weight to give the personal and social overlap between the two circles as against the substantive philosophical and political differences in their conclusions about the value of Peter's reforms, the proper relationship between individual and community, the authority of formal law and representative institutions, and Russia's proper relationship to Western Europe.

Student task: Identify one specific claim on which Westernizers and Slavophiles agreed and one on which they fundamentally disagreed, explaining why each matters for understanding Russian intellectual history in this period.

Multiple Perspectives

Censor, Critic, and Writer

A government censor: For an official charged with reviewing journal submissions under the statutes examined in Chapter 38, literature represented both a valuable sign of Russia's cultural sophistication, worth cultivating and permitting within limits, and a persistent danger, since fiction and criticism could carry implicit social and political argument that more direct censorship of overtly political writing could not fully anticipate or control. The censor's task required constant, often inconsistent, judgment calls about how much indirection to tolerate.
A literary critic such as Belinsky: For a critic committed to using literary evaluation as a form of social and moral argument, literature offered one of the only available spaces in which questions of serfdom, human dignity, and social injustice could be addressed with any seriousness, given the near-total prohibition on direct political commentary. The critic's task was therefore inseparable from a broader project of social enlightenment and reform.
A writer or engaged reader of the thick journals: For a provincial landowner, government clerk, or university student reading the latest issue of the Contemporary or the Fatherland Notes, literature and criticism offered access to a wider world of ideas and argument otherwise unavailable through official channels, allowing readers to participate, however indirectly, in debates about Russia's condition and future that no formal political institution permitted them to engage in openly.

These perspectives illustrate why literature's political function in this period cannot be reduced to simple evasion of censorship; it reflects a genuine, structurally produced convergence of literary and political life under conditions this chapter has examined throughout.

Cause and Effect

The Emergence of the Intelligentsia

Long-Term Conditions

The expansion of noble and professional education, the growth of Moscow and St. Petersburg universities, and Russia's accumulating exposure to European thought following the Napoleonic Wars examined in Chapter 36.

Intermediate Developments

The Decembrist crisis of 1825, examined in Chapter 37, foreclosing open conspiratorial politics; the expansion of censorship and the Third Section under Nicholas I, examined in Chapter 38; the spread of German idealist philosophy through university lecture halls and private circles.

Short-Term Triggers

Chaadaev's 1836 Philosophical Letter and the government's extreme reaction to it; the maturation of a generation of university-educated thinkers in Moscow during the late 1830s and 1840s; the growth of the thick-journal system as a public forum for indirect political argument.

Effects

The crystallization of distinct Westernizer and Slavophile tendencies; Herzen's turn toward Russian socialism; Belinsky's development of literary criticism as political argument; the Petrashevsky circle's crackdown, demonstrating the narrow limits of tolerated discussion.

Historical complexity: No single cause—neither censorship alone, nor European philosophical influence alone, nor the Decembrist legacy alone—fully explains the intelligentsia's emergence; each factor reinforced the others within a specific institutional environment.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From Decembrists to the Circles of the 1840s

What Stayed the Same

  • A small, educated, noble-dominated social stratum continued to serve as the primary source of political and social criticism in Russia.
  • Exposure to European political and philosophical thought, whether through military service abroad, as with the Decembrists, or through university study, as with the generation examined in this chapter, remained central to the formation of critical political consciousness.
  • Concern over serfdom, autocratic power, and Russia's relationship to Europe persisted as central preoccupations across both generations.

What Changed

  • Open conspiratorial organization of the kind the Decembrists attempted gave way to informal circles, salons, and indirect literary and philosophical argument, a shift produced directly by the post-1825 political environment examined in Chapter 38.
  • The generation of the 1830s and 1840s engaged far more systematically with German idealist philosophy and, later, French socialist thought than the Decembrist generation generally had.
  • Intellectual debate diversified considerably, producing distinct Westernizer, Slavophile, and early socialist positions rather than the comparatively narrower range of constitutional and republican alternatives the Decembrists had debated.

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both continuity and change helps students see the intelligentsia examined in this chapter as a genuine historical development building on, rather than simply repeating, the Decembrist experience, while operating under meaningfully altered political conditions.

Everyday Life and Social History

Inside a Moscow Intellectual Circle

For a young participant in a Moscow circle such as the one associated with Nikolai Stankevich in the 1830s, intellectual life centered on regular evening gatherings, often at a member's modest apartment, where friends read aloud from German philosophical texts, recent foreign periodicals, or manuscript drafts of their own essays and letters, followed by extended discussion and argument that could continue late into the night. Correspondence between gatherings extended the conversation further, with members exchanging long philosophical and personal letters that functioned simultaneously as intellectual exercises and as a vital circuit of emotional and social support within a small, close-knit community.

Financial circumstances varied considerably among circle members, from wealthy landowners such as Herzen, whose family estate provided him with a comfortable independent income, to figures of more modest means such as Belinsky, whose precarious finances depended heavily on journal payments and who lived for years in genuine material insecurity despite his substantial cultural influence. Women's participation in this intellectual world remained limited by the broader social and legal constraints examined elsewhere in this textbook, though women played important roles as hosts of influential salons, as correspondents and intellectual interlocutors, and, in some documented cases, as serious readers and critics within these circles, even when formal publication and public intellectual authority remained overwhelmingly available to men alone.

Russia and the World

German Idealism, French Socialism, and 1848

The intellectual movements examined in this chapter developed in constant dialogue with contemporary European thought. German idealist philosophy, particularly the work of Hegel, provided the dominant framework through which both Westernizers and Slavophiles, despite their disagreements, understood questions of historical development, national character, and the relationship between individual consciousness and broader historical processes; nearly every major figure examined in this chapter engaged seriously, whether through direct study in Germany, translated texts, or university instruction, with this philosophical tradition.

French socialist thought, associated with Fourier, Saint-Simon, and Proudhon, supplied a second crucial European influence, particularly for Herzen and for the Petrashevsky circle, offering a vocabulary for critiquing industrial capitalism and social inequality that extended beyond the legal and philosophical concerns most prominent among earlier Westernizers. The Revolutions of 1848, sweeping across France, the German states, the Austrian Empire, and much of the rest of Western and Central Europe, provided this chapter's most consequential direct point of contact between Russian intellectual life and contemporary European political upheaval: Herzen's firsthand observation of these events in Paris reshaped his entire subsequent intellectual trajectory, while news of the revolutions, received secondhand by those who remained in Russia, triggered the intensified governmental reaction examined in Section 40.7's account of the Petrashevsky crackdown.

These European currents reached Russian thinkers through several concrete channels rather than through abstract influence alone. Many professors and students, including figures associated with Moscow University, had studied directly in German university towns, above all Berlin, where they attended lectures grounded in the Hegelian and post-Hegelian philosophical tradition and brought its vocabulary and habits of argument back to Russia. Others encountered European thought at a greater remove, through French- and German-language books and periodicals circulating, often with difficulty, past the censorship apparatus examined in Chapter 38, through summaries and reviews published in the thick journals, and through private correspondence among Russians traveling or residing abroad, of whom Herzen himself became the most consequential example after 1847. It is important for students to recognize that Russian thinkers did not simply absorb these European currents unchanged; Westernizers, Slavophiles, and Herzen alike selected, reinterpreted, and transformed German idealist philosophy and French socialist theory to address specifically Russian conditions, including serfdom, autocracy, and the peasant commune, producing arguments that, however indebted to European sources, addressed questions no German or French thinker had needed to confront in the same form.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the intellectual ferment of Nicholaevan Russia during the 1830s and 1840s, a period in which the political restrictions described in Chapter 38, far from eliminating serious reflection on Russia's condition and future, displaced much of that reflection into philosophy, literary criticism, private correspondence, salons, and small discussion circles concentrated chiefly in Moscow and St. Petersburg.

The chapter began with the social and institutional conditions that produced the Russian intelligentsia as a distinct cultural formation: the expansion of university education, the aftermath of the Decembrist crisis examined in Chapter 37, and the paradoxical effect of censorship in redirecting political argument into ostensibly non-political genres. It then examined Pyotr Chaadaev's 1836 Philosophical Letter as the provocation that crystallized the central question around which much of the chapter's subsequent material organized itself: did Russia belong to the main current of European historical development, or did it occupy a fundamentally different historical position?

The chapter traced two major, internally diverse answers to this question. Westernizers, including Timofei Granovsky and Konstantin Kavelin, argued that Russia's future lay through continued engagement with European legal, educational, and social achievement. Slavophiles, including Aleksei Khomyakov, Ivan Kireevsky, and Konstantin Aksakov, argued for a distinct Russian historical path rooted in Orthodox communal tradition, while nonetheless directing serious criticism at existing bureaucratic autocracy and, in several cases, at serfdom itself. The chapter emphasized that these two tendencies, despite real disagreement, shared considerable common ground as participants in the same narrow Moscow intellectual world.

Alexander Herzen's intellectual trajectory, from early Westernizing sympathies through his direct observation of the failed Revolutions of 1848, illustrated how this debate could develop into a third position: a theory of distinctively Russian socialism grounded in the peasant commune, understood as a potential foundation for social transformation that might allow Russia to avoid repeating the social costs of Western industrial capitalism.

Vissarion Belinsky's career, and especially his 1847 Letter to Gogol, demonstrated concretely how literary criticism functioned as political argument under censorship, using the evaluation of fiction and the rebuttal of Gogol's conservative religious and political turn to advance a direct argument against serfdom and for the rule of law. The chapter then broadened this analysis to examine literature more generally as a vehicle for political debate, situating major writers of the period, including Pushkin, Gogol, Lermontov, Turgenev, and the young Dostoevsky, within this broader pattern.

The Petrashevsky circle's 1849 arrest and the staged mock execution of its members, including Dostoevsky, demonstrated the narrow actual limits of tolerated discussion, even in the broader, more informal gatherings never intended for publication, while also reflecting the government's severe response to the more committed sub-groups within the network that had begun moving toward clandestine printing and conspiratorial organization; the episode illustrated, in both respects, the direct connection between the European Revolutions of 1848 and an intensified crackdown on Russian intellectual life.

The chapter's concluding analytical section brought these competing positions together, emphasizing that by the early 1850s, educated Russian society possessed a rich and genuinely unresolved vocabulary of competing visions for the country's future, none of which represented a predetermined historical outcome, and all of which would continue to shape Russian political and intellectual debate across the reform era, the populist and revolutionary movements, and beyond.

These lessons—attention to the institutional conditions shaping intellectual expression, recognition of genuine diversity within broad intellectual tendencies, and the use of literature and criticism as evidence for political and social argument—prepare students directly for Chapter 41's examination of the Crimean War and the institutional crisis it exposed, and for Chapter 42's treatment of the Great Reforms, emancipation, and the revolutionary populist movements that drew directly on the intellectual vocabulary this chapter has traced.

Key Terms

Intelligentsia
A term applied retrospectively by historians to the educated, self-consciously critical milieu that began taking shape in Russia during the 1830s and 1840s; the word did not become common in this Russian social sense until the 1860s.
Westernizer (zapadnik)
A broad intellectual tendency holding that Russia's future lay through continued engagement with European legal, educational, and social development.
Slavophile
A broad intellectual tendency arguing that Russia possessed a distinct historical path rooted in Orthodox Christianity and communal social institutions.
Sobornost
Khomyakov's concept of spiritual and communal unity achieved through free, loving union within the Orthodox Church, contrasted with both Catholic institutional authority and Protestant individualism.
Thick journal (tolstyi zhurnal)
A substantial monthly periodical combining literature, criticism, and commentary, serving as the primary public forum for intellectual debate under censorship.
Circle (kruzhok)
A small, informal gathering of friends meeting regularly to read, discuss, and debate philosophy, literature, and current events.
German idealism
The philosophical tradition associated with Hegel and Schelling, providing the dominant framework through which Russian thinkers of this period understood historical development and national identity.
Peasant commune (mir / obshchina)
A traditional Russian village institution managing aspects of communal life; in many repartitional communes land was periodically reassigned among households, though practices varied considerably by region. Idealized by Slavophiles and reinterpreted by Herzen as a foundation for socialism.
Russian socialism
Herzen's theory that Russia's peasant commune might allow it to reach a socialist future without first passing through Western industrial capitalism.
Official Nationality
Nicholas I's governing doctrine, summarized as Orthodoxy, Autocracy, and Nationality, examined fully in Chapter 38 and forming part of the political backdrop for this chapter.
Philosophical Letter
Chaadaev's 1836 publication questioning Russia's place in European historical development, which prompted the government's declaration that he was mentally unwell.
Letter to Gogol
Belinsky's 1847 open letter responding to Gogol's conservative Selected Passages, circulated illegally in manuscript and examined as this chapter's central Primary Source.
Revolutions of 1848
A wave of political upheaval across Western and Central Europe, directly observed by Herzen in Paris and a major cause of intensified Russian governmental repression.
Utopian socialism
A family of European social theories, including Fourierism, proposing cooperative, non-revolutionary paths toward social transformation, discussed within the Petrashevsky circle.
Fourierism
The social theory of Charles Fourier, proposing cooperative communal living in self-sufficient associations called phalanges, discussed by the Petrashevsky circle.
Mock execution
The staged December 1849 execution ritual, halted at the final moment, inflicted on members of the Petrashevsky circle, including Dostoevsky, before their sentences were commuted.
Pre-Petrine Russia
The period before Peter the Great's reforms, idealized by Slavophiles, especially Konstantin Aksakov, as an era of communal harmony between tsar and people.
Censorship statute
Formal government regulations governing permissible published content, examined in detail in Chapter 38 and shaping the indirect literary strategies examined throughout this chapter.
Literary realism
A mode of fiction emphasizing accurate depiction of social conditions, promoted by Belinsky as implicitly progressive in its capacity to expose injustice.
Hegelianism
The philosophy of G. W. F. Hegel, particularly his account of historical development unfolding through rational stages, widely influential among the thinkers examined in this chapter.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has carried students through three decades of intense intellectual ferment, from Chaadaev's provocative 1836 letter through the Westernizer-Slavophile debate, Herzen's turn toward Russian socialism, Belinsky's transformation of literary criticism into political argument, and the Petrashevsky circle's sobering demonstration of the actual limits of tolerated discussion. None of these figures operated with the benefit of hindsight; none knew which of their competing visions, if any, would shape Russia's subsequent development, and this chapter has deliberately resisted reading later historical outcomes backward into their arguments.

What this generation of educated Russians accomplished, working under the close surveillance and censorship examined in Chapter 38, was the creation of an enduring vocabulary for debating Russia's identity, its relationship to the wider European and global world, and the proper sources and methods of social and political change. The questions they raised, concerning the value of the peasant commune, the moral responsibility of educated society toward ordinary people, the political function of literature, and Russia's place in a broader history of human development, would not disappear with the Crimean War crisis examined in the next chapter; they would instead be tested, transformed, and in some cases radicalized by the experience of military defeat, the emancipation of the serfs, and the reform and revolutionary movements examined across the remainder of Part VII and Part VIII.

As the textbook proceeds to Chapter 41's examination of the Crimean War and the institutional crisis that military defeat exposed, students should carry forward this chapter's central habits of mind: attention to the specific institutional conditions that shaped how political argument could be expressed, recognition of genuine diversity within broad intellectual movements too often flattened into simple labels, and a sustained skepticism toward any account of Russian history that treats a later outcome as the predetermined destination of an earlier debate.
