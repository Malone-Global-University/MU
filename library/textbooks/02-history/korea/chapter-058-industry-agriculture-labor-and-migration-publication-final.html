Industry, Agriculture, Labor, and Migration

Korean History: Ancient Kingdoms to a Divided Peninsula

Chapter Opening

A single decade can leave a colony with more rice mills, more electric power, and more wage laborers than it had before, and with more households short of rice, in debt, or living far from home. Colonial Korea is a case in which both statements are true at once, and the task of this chapter is to explain how.

Imagine three places in the 1930s. At the rice-exporting ports of the southwest, Gunsan and Mokpo among them, grain from the western plains was milled, graded, and loaded for shipment to Japan. On the northeastern coast, the chemical complex at Hungnam drew on electricity generated by dams in the mountains behind it. Across the Amnok and Duman rivers, Korean households farmed in Manchuria, and in Osaka, Korean workers lived in neighborhoods whose existence depended on factory employment. These places seem unrelated. Each was shaped by the same system: a colonial economy governed from Seoul and Tokyo, in which decisions about land, credit, investment, and labor were made within the institutions of the Japanese empire.

The central question of the chapter is how Japanese colonial rule transformed Korea's agricultural production, industries, labor markets, and migration patterns, and who controlled, benefited from, or bore the costs of these changes. The answer cannot be reduced to a single verdict. Measurable economic change did occur. Irrigation works expanded, rice yields rose, manufacturing grew, and power and transport networks were built. But production data alone do not tell us whose property was expanding, who received the income, what Korean farmers could eat, what factory workers were paid, or how many people left Korea because staying had become too hard.

Four processes organize the narrative: agrarian restructuring, industrial transformation, the formation of a wage-earning working class, and migration, which by the end of the colonial period included coerced movement. They are treated as interlocking. Rural indebtedness helped supply labor to cities, mines, and Japan; industrial growth in the 1930s depended on electricity, minerals, transport, and Japanese capital; and the wartime economy folded all of them into military procurement.

An important chronological distinction runs through the chapter. The rice-production programs that began in 1920 were designed chiefly to increase food supply for the Japanese market. The industrial expansion of the 1930s followed the Great Depression and Japan's occupation of Manchuria, and was increasingly tied to military expansion. The two periods had different objectives, financing, and social consequences, and they should not be merged into one story of steady development.

Historians reconstruct this economy from colonial statistical yearbooks, ordinances and administrative reports, corporate histories, newspapers, labor and tenancy dispute records, police files, censuses, and later oral and written testimony. Each of these sources was produced from a particular position. Government-General statistics tend to emphasize aggregate output. Tenant and labor sources survive partly because disputes generated records. Evidence from the poorest and least organized households is often indirect. Chapter 58 therefore teaches source criticism alongside narrative.

This chapter builds on the colonial institutions established in Chapter 55 and the urban and cultural transformations of Chapter 57. It owns the economic operation of tenant associations, strikes, and wartime labor recruitment. The broader political and ideological history of those movements belongs to Chapter 59, the history of assimilation to Chapter 60, and the fuller human experience of wartime mobilization to Chapter 62. The chapter closes by considering how the uneven economic inheritance of 1945 entered the liberation-era history examined from Chapter 64 onward.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how the institutions of the colonial state, including finance, taxation, and property law, structured economic decision-making in Korea.
  2. Distinguish landlords, owner-cultivators, tenants, and landless laborers, and explain how tenancy arrangements shaped rural insecurity.
  3. Trace the chronology of the rice-production programs of 1920–1925, 1926–1934, and 1940 onward, and explain why they must not be merged.
  4. Analyze why rising rice output and exports to Japan could coexist with declining rice consumption among many Korean households.
  5. Assess the responses of tenants and the colonial state to agrarian conflict, including the Amtae-do dispute and the ordinances of 1932 and 1934.
  6. Compare Korean and Japanese enterprise in terms of ownership, finance, management, and market access.
  7. Explain the geography and structure of 1930s industrialization, including hydroelectric power, mining, and the Hungnam complex.
  8. Analyze the formation of a colonial working class, its conditions, and its forms of collective action, including the Wonsan general strike of 1929.
  9. Distinguish voluntary migration, constrained choice, and coercive mobilization, and trace migration to Japan, Manchuria, and the Russian Far East.
  10. Interpret colonial statistics critically, separating output, income, consumption, ownership, and welfare.
  11. Evaluate competing interpretations of colonial economic development without accepting either denial of growth or equation of growth with legitimacy.
  12. Connect the economic structures of 1945 to the conditions of liberation and division.

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  2. How did the rules governing land, credit, and enterprise determine who could take advantage of economic change?
  3. Why might a village produce more rice in a decade and eat less of it?
  4. How did tenants, workers, and migrants respond to economic pressure, and what limits did the colonial state place on their options?

  8. What can colonial statistics establish, and what can they not?

Historical Context

By 1910 the Korean economy was overwhelmingly agricultural. Most households farmed small plots or rented land, and rice was the principal commercial and tax-bearing crop of the southern and western plains. Markets, ports, and a limited number of modern enterprises had developed after the treaties of the 1870s and 1880s, and the reforms of the Korean Empire (Chapter 53) had introduced new banks, utilities, and survey work. Japanese commercial interests, railways, and finance had expanded rapidly before annexation, and the protectorate period (Chapter 54) had already placed fiscal and monetary reform under Japanese direction.

The Government-General established in 1910 (Chapter 55) controlled the budget, banking regulation, property registration, and enterprise licensing. The land survey of 1910–1918 produced a cadastre and a system of registered ownership, which made land more easily taxed, mortgaged, and sold. Chapter 58 begins from those institutions and asks how they operated economically over the following three decades. It does not retell the administrative history of the survey, the March First Movement (Chapter 56), or the urban and educational changes of Chapter 57.

The chapter also sits within a wider regional economy. Japan's industrial cities required food, Japanese firms sought markets and raw materials, and Manchuria after 1931 became a new imperial frontier. Korea's location between Japan and Manchuria shaped its role. At the same time, Korean landlords, merchants, farmers, and workers were not passive. They borrowed, invested, organized, bargained, and moved, within a framework in which Japanese authorities held decisive political power. The term "colonial economy" in this chapter refers to that combination of structure and response.

Section 58.1

Colonial Korea as an Economy within the Japanese Empire

From 1910 to 1945 Korea's economic decisions were made inside an imperial framework. The Governor-General, appointed from Tokyo, controlled the colonial budget, issued ordinances with the force of law, and oversaw the police and courts that enforced contracts and property rights (Chapter 55). Korean residents had no representative role in setting tax policy, and the right to establish enterprises, borrow, and trade operated within rules written by the colonial authorities and shaped by Japanese economic priorities.

Economic integration with Japan deepened over time. Tariff barriers between Korea and Japan were largely removed by the early 1920s, Korean currency was tied to the yen through the Bank of Chōsen, and Korean exports flowed overwhelmingly to Japan. Trade, finance, and shipping were therefore not simply "foreign" or "domestic" matters. They were organized within an imperial market in which Japan was both the main buyer and the principal source of capital goods.

The chapter relies on a distinction that recurs in every section: the expansion of productive capacity is not the same as economic self-government, and neither is the same as the distribution of benefits. A colony can build dams and factories while its residents control little of what is built. It can also contain Korean-owned firms that prospered. Historians must therefore ask, for each development, who owned the assets, who made the decisions, who supplied the labor, who received the income, and who bore the costs.

The boundaries of this market were defined by more than tariffs. The Bank of Chōsen issued notes tied to the yen, Japanese shipping connected Korean ports with Osaka and Kobe, and railway freight schedules connected farms and mines to those ports. A merchant choosing a destination for a consignment therefore confronted prices, transport charges, available credit, and purchasing agents whose principal connections often ran toward Japan. Government-General controls over licensing, customs administration, and contracts established the enforceable framework of these private choices. This was an integrated market with very unequal political authority, not a self-contained Korean national economy simply exchanging goods with a foreign neighbor.

The consequences shifted across the period. During the 1920s the imperial demand for rice organized land improvement, merchant credit, and export logistics; during the 1930s northern power plants, coal, and chemical factories increasingly linked Korea to Japanese development in Manchuria. Wartime authorities then directed production and labor by controls that exceeded ordinary market incentives. These phases should not be flattened into a single theory of colonial extraction, nor described as unrelated episodes. Their continuity lay in who could define priorities and compel compliance. Their differences lay in the commodities, financing, workers, and institutions that carried those priorities out.

The appropriate unit of analysis also changes by question. An export series can describe Korea's aggregate trade without identifying which cultivators ate less rice; a factory inventory can show machinery installed without showing how much value stayed in the Korean economy. The sections that follow therefore trace transactions from institutional rules through firms and villages to household income. That chain allows an evaluation of economic transformation that neither dismisses the increase in productive capacity nor assumes that increased output was distributed fairly.

Section 58.2

Economic Institutions Inherited from the Korean Empire

Economic modernization in Korea did not begin in 1910. Between the 1880s and annexation, Korean governments, merchants, and foreign interests introduced telegraph and rail lines, modern banks, electric and streetcar service in Seoul, and cadastral survey work under the Korean Empire (Chapter 53). Korean-owned banks and commercial companies appeared, and merchants adapted to treaty-port trade. These initiatives were uneven, often underfunded, and increasingly contested by Japanese financial and political pressure.

The protectorate years after 1905 (Chapter 54) shifted control. Japanese-directed currency and fiscal reform, the expansion of Japanese-owned railways and banks, and the establishment of the Oriental Development Company in 1908 moved key economic levers toward Japanese hands before annexation was formalized. When the Government-General took over in 1910, it inherited existing markets, rural relationships, and some institutions, and it absorbed, replaced, or reorganized them.

This continuity matters for two reasons. It prevents the false impression that Korea was economically static until 1910, and it prevents the opposite mistake of treating all colonial institutions as unprecedented. Cadastral reform, rural credit, and commercial companies had precedents. What changed after 1910 was the political authority behind them and the degree to which Japanese capital and law framed their operation.

Late Joseon and Korean Empire development already connected economic change to foreign pressure. Treaty-port merchants dealt in imported manufactured cloth, agricultural commodities, and credit; the customs service generated an important stream of fiscal information; and modern transport projects competed with older coastal and inland circulation. Private Korean banks and companies experimented with corporate forms and new bookkeeping. These ventures, however, developed amid indemnities, military intervention, disputes over concessions, and an inability to control external political conditions. The colonial state did not find an economy lacking commerce or institutions; it absorbed an economy whose institutions were already being contested.

Continuity was especially important in agriculture. Korean landlords, tenant intermediaries, market brokers, village credit associations, and regional rice traders continued to operate under colonial law. The cadastral project made some claims easier to establish in court and some customary claims more difficult, but it did not recreate rural social relations overnight. A landlord whose family had collected rent in the nineteenth century could retain that position while acquiring new means of enforcing contracts. Conversely, a merchant could use a railway or bank account without endorsing the political conditions under which it was provided.

A sound comparison identifies both inherited and newly imposed features. Modern land registration had preannexation precedents, yet the Government-General imposed a more comprehensive register under colonial authority. Railways had begun earlier, yet the direction of new branch connections reflected imperial logistics. The issue is less whether a technology was new than who controlled its expansion and whose opportunities it enlarged.

Section 58.3

Colonial Government Expenditure, Taxation, and Public Finance

A government budget is evidence of priorities. In its early years the Government-General depended on subsidies from the Japanese treasury, which were phased out by the end of the 1910s, and on revenue from land taxes, customs, and later monopolies on items such as tobacco, ginseng, and salt. As the colonial economy expanded, a larger portion of revenue came from taxation on Korean residents and enterprises, and the colony's special-account budget became largely self-financing.

Expenditures were substantial on administration, policing, railways, ports, roads, and communications, which supported both governance and commercial or military movement. Education, public health, and welfare received smaller shares than those categories in most analyses, and investment in irrigation and agricultural finance rose with the rice programs of the 1920s. These patterns suggest priorities of control, infrastructure, and production.

Students should resist two shortcuts. A large infrastructure budget does not by itself show benefit to the Korean public, because the same railway could move rice, troops, and passengers. A large tax burden does not by itself show extraction, because taxation also funded services. The useful questions are comparative and structural: what was taxed, who paid, what was built, and who used it.

The special account of the Government-General is a useful starting point, but it cannot be read as the finances of a representative state. Japanese treasury support in the early years was one source of revenue; land and commercial taxes, monopoly earnings, fees, and borrowing were others. Railway accounts and other enterprise activities could be handled differently in different years, so budget categories need to be checked before comparing totals. The apparent rise or fall in an expenditure share may reflect a change in accounting boundaries as well as a real policy choice. A student assessing the financing of irrigation or schools should first ask whether the cost was carried by central accounts, local authorities, financial associations, or private borrowers.

Distribution matters. Land-tax assessments placed obligations on registered owners, but those owners could attempt to shift burdens toward tenants through rent or fees. Indirect taxes and monopoly prices reached households with few taxable assets. Railway capital could be financed through bonds whose interest had to be serviced later. Similarly, expenditure in a district did not ensure that Korean residents benefited in proportion to their contributions: a paved street serving administrative and Japanese commercial quarters might receive resources while crowded Korean neighborhoods lacked drainage.

For a defensible budget comparison, extract several benchmark years from an identifiable Government-General statistical yearbook, preserve the contemporary fiscal-year definition, separate nominal yen from price-adjusted values, and categorize spending consistently. Until such a table is assembled, it is more accurate to describe documented priorities than to manufacture exact welfare-to-police ratios. The chapter's governing distinction—public expenditure versus public benefit—rests on this accounting discipline.

Section 58.4

Banks, Currency, Credit, and Financial Dependence

The Bank of Chōsen, named in 1911 as the successor to an earlier Bank of Korea, issued the colony's currency and acted as its central financial institution, with currency tied to the Japanese yen. Alongside it, the Chōsen Industrial Bank (Chōsen Shokusan Ginkō, established in 1918 through the merger of six agricultural and industrial banks) provided long-term lending for agriculture and industry, and commercial banks served merchants and enterprises. Korean-owned banks existed but were merged or absorbed over the 1920s and 1930s, leaving the largest financial institutions under Japanese control.

At the village level, credit came from financial associations established from the 1900s, from rotating credit groups (kye), from landlords, and from private moneylenders. Access differed sharply. Japanese corporations and large landowners could obtain long-term bank credit on moderate terms. Korean businessmen depended more heavily on partnerships and personal networks, and small farmers and tenants borrowed on short terms at higher cost, a subject developed in Section 58.19.

The financial structure had two consequences for the rest of the chapter. It made capital cheaper for those already integrated into imperial finance, and it left the poorest cultivators exposed to the interest and timing of informal lending. Financial dependence was not only a matter of foreign ownership but of who had access to affordable credit.

The two institutions whose names are easily confused performed different jobs. The Bank of Chōsen functioned as a note-issuing and major settlement bank within the yen-linked monetary regime. The Chōsen Industrial Bank, established in 1918 through the consolidation of agricultural and industrial banking institutions, was designed for longer-term development lending. The first institution matters to currency and cross-border liquidity; the second to how plantations, irrigation works, and industrial concerns acquired investible funds. Neither should be characterized as an independent Korean central bank serving a sovereign Korean monetary policy.

A bank's location and charter tell only part of the story. Credit also depends on the banker's view of collateral, the borrower's language and commercial contacts, maturity dates, interest, and the ability to enforce a mortgage. Registered land offered security for loans, which sometimes made owners more creditworthy while excluding tenants who had no title to pledge. Financial associations and cooperatives helped spread formal credit into towns and villages, yet their reach varied and they could impose repayment schedules unsuitable for harvest-dependent income. Older rotating credit groups could bridge social trust and economic necessity but could not supply the scale of capital required by a hydroelectric plant.

An ideal case study would compare the financing of one large irrigation association with that of a land-poor household in the same district. The association could amortize major civil works over years through assessments, while a tenant might borrow for fertilizer with rent due at harvest. Both were participating in agricultural improvement, but the risks and returns were not symmetrical. The historical question is who could carry debt until an investment paid for itself.

Section 58.5

Economic Ownership and Control: Japanese Capital, Korean Capital, and Colonial Markets

Four questions help clarify the colonial economy: who owned assets, who managed operations, who controlled finance, and who received profits. These could point in different directions. A factory might be built in Korea with Japanese corporate capital and Japanese management, employ Korean labor, and send profits to Japan. A rice mill might be Korean-owned but financed by Japanese banks. A Korean landlord could be a major employer and political patron while dependent on colonial legal protection.

Japanese firms and state-supported companies generally had advantages in capital, technology, access to officials, and links to imperial markets. Korean entrepreneurs faced barriers in credit and regulation, and in some sectors direct competition with larger Japanese firms. Yet Korean capital was not absent: it was prominent in textiles, commerce, trading, landlordism, and some manufacturing, as examined in Division V.

Analytically, Chapter 58 treats ownership, management, finance, and profit distribution as separate variables. A table that lists factories by location says little until it identifies those variables. This approach also avoids simple categories. Korean-owned does not mean nationalist, and Japanese-owned does not mean uniform in practice, as later sections show.

Corporation registers, investment accounts, and balance sheets should be read as separate layers of evidence. Paid-in capital is not the same as a firm's total assets; the value of factory output is not identical with wages paid in Korea; a shareholder's nationality does not establish the nationality or working conditions of all employees. In some sectors Japanese firms held the largest installations while Korean businesses operated trading, servicing, food-processing, and light-manufacturing enterprises. An economy could therefore show an expanding Korean commercial class alongside increased control of its commanding industries by external investors.

The distinction can be seen by tracing a shipment of processed rice. Farmers supplied grain, local brokers financed collection, mills added value, transport companies moved sacks to a port, and wholesalers sold to Japanese markets. Korean and Japanese agents might participate at every stage, but not necessarily on equal terms. The mill owner could receive profit while the tenant's share of the crop remained inadequate for household consumption. Likewise, a factory could produce an export surplus without transferring technical decision-making to the local workforce.

This framework also avoids a misleading moral shorthand. Korean entrepreneurs sometimes invested to strengthen local economic capacity and at other times depended on official favors or supplied imperial projects. Japanese firms differed in scale and in the extent to which they hired or trained Korean personnel. Evaluating individuals requires dated business records and evidence of actual decisions, while evaluating the system requires analysis of ownership, law, capital, and market structure.

Section 58.6

Landownership after the Colonial Land Survey

Chapter 55 establishes the land survey as an administrative project of 1910–1918. This section is concerned with its economic consequences. The survey produced a registry of owners, boundaries, and land categories, which allowed taxation on a standardized basis and made land easier to sell, mortgage, and foreclose. For owners with clear documentation, registration secured title. For others, results varied.

Two cautions apply. The survey did not confiscate every Korean farmer's land, and it should not be described that way. Most cultivated land was registered to Korean owners. But the process shifted some state, crown, village, and unclaimed lands into government hands, and it recognized individual ownership more readily than layered customary rights. Cultivators who had held long-standing but unregistered claims to use land, and village or lineage holdings with ambiguous documentation, were more vulnerable.

The longer consequence was the creation of a legible land market. Over the following decades, indebtedness and price swings made sales and foreclosures more likely, and registered title facilitated the accumulation of land by landlords and companies. Registration was therefore not economically neutral, but neither was it a single act of seizure. It created conditions in which later pressures could transfer land.

The legal transformation of land becomes clearest when a family's access to a field involves several different claims. A household may cultivate a plot, draw water through a shared channel, collect fuel from nearby land, and hold a tenancy recognized locally but not recorded as ownership. A cadastral register primarily records parcel boundaries and legally recognized title; it does not automatically reproduce every use right. Colonial courts and taxation systems therefore favored claims expressed in the categories accepted by the new register. Some Korean owners secured valuable proof of title, while some groups found collective or customary claims harder to defend.

Registered ownership also changed the economics of debt. Once title could be mortgaged, a landholder could borrow against it, but failure to repay could convert a temporary cash shortage into permanent property loss. Larger landlords and corporate purchasers could acquire contiguous parcels when distressed owners sold. These transfers should be documented with land-register and transaction records, not assumed to follow mechanically from the survey itself. Differing village outcomes depended on the distribution of ownership, local prices, tenancy contracts, and access to formal credit.

For this reason, the long-run land question is not simply how many acres were declared public land in 1910–1918. It is how an enforceable property system affected bargaining power over the next three decades. A useful teaching comparison sets a registered landowner alongside a tenant with cultivation rights, an irrigation association claiming assessments, and a village relying on an uncatalogued common resource. The same administrative reform could improve clarity for one actor while exposing another to dispossession.

Section 58.7

Landlords, Owner-Cultivators, Tenants, and Landless Rural Households

Rural society was stratified. Landlords owned land and rented most or all of it out. Owner-cultivators farmed their own plots. Owner-tenants farmed some land they owned and rented more. Tenants cultivated rented land, and landless agricultural laborers worked for wages or in exchange for subsistence. Official figures from the 1920s and 1930s show that tenancy was widespread, and the share of farm households living purely on tenancy rose from under 40 percent at the start of colonial rule to roughly half, while owner-tenants declined. Exact shares depend on definitions and on the survey.

Tenure shaped economic life. A tenant who owed half the harvest or more as rent had little margin to invest in fertilizer or tools, and was exposed to eviction if rent was late or if a landlord found a higher bidder. Owner-cultivators could be vulnerable to debt, but held a more secure claim. Landless laborers had the weakest position, dependent on seasonal demand.

These categories overlap, and households moved between them. A family that lost land to debt might drop from owner-tenant to tenant. A landlord might also work some land. Treating categories as fixed boxes obscures that mobility, and mobility was generally downward for the poor during economic downturns, a subject examined in Division IV.

Tenancy proportions require an explicit denominator. A census may classify households as full owners, part owners and part tenants, or tenants alone; it may exclude families supported principally by casual labor, and the classification of a small plot-owner may change when definitions shift. The familiar statement that about half of farm households became full tenants toward the end of colonial rule should be presented as an estimate within a specified statistical series, not as an unchanging fact about every region. A high proportion of tenancy also does not reveal the share of cultivated acreage rented: large estate owners, small owner-cultivators, and tiny tenants produce very different acreage distributions.

Tenure had consequences for technological adoption. A farmer with secure possession might expect to benefit from an irrigation improvement over many seasons. A tenant facing annual contract uncertainty could be reluctant to pay for fertilizer or drainage if increased yields primarily raised the rent or improved the owner's property. The landlord might prefer more tenants competing for fewer leases; alternatively, a well-capitalized owner could support improvements and share some gains. These possibilities must be distinguished from actual contract practices.

Women and children worked within these categories even when the head of household was the person recorded as owner or tenant. Widows could face distinctive inheritance and leasing pressures; sons might leave for seasonal work, changing who maintained the fields. The household classification is therefore a starting point, not a complete social description. The result was a countryside differentiated by property rights, labor capacity, access to credit, and the power to survive a bad harvest.

Section 58.8

Japanese Agricultural Estates, Korean Landlords, and the Oriental Development Company

Japanese individuals and companies acquired agricultural land in Korea, especially in the rice-producing provinces of the south and west. The Oriental Development Company, chartered in Japan in 1908, became the single largest landowner and also engaged in settlement and investment. Other Japanese-owned estates and corporate farms operated alongside it, often with advantages in credit and access to officials.

Japanese ownership, however, was a minority share of cultivated land, and Korean landlords remained numerous and important. Some of them were large and politically connected, and some were small rent-collectors with a few plots. Their experiences differed. Some invested in irrigation, moved into banking or industry, or supported schools and newspapers. Others lived primarily on rents and relied on police and courts to enforce them.

The key analytical caution is not to treat the landlord class as monolithically Japanese or monolithically Korean, nor to treat Korean landlords as victims of colonialism in the same way as tenants. The Oriental Development Company was significant but not exclusive. The agrarian order was an alliance of convenience in some places and a site of competition in others, and tenants often faced landlords of either nationality in similar terms.

The Oriental Development Company was established under Japanese sponsorship before formal annexation and accumulated land through purchases, leasing, and other transactions. Its holdings and business practices must be situated among other Japanese corporate estates and a still significant Korean landlord class. The temptation to explain all rural change through a single notorious institution obscures the more extensive relationships among private landlords, smaller Japanese settlers, Korean tenants, village intermediaries, and lending institutions.

Settler agriculture differed by location. Japanese farming households could bring capital, machinery, or irrigation skills, but they also confronted soil and climate conditions, labor recruitment difficulties, and debt. Companies used hired managers, tenant contracts, and local rent collectors. Korean landowners with substantial property could operate similar systems, and some profited from higher rice prices or from policies that made irrigation investment attractive. Neither ethnic category maps perfectly onto the legal positions of landlord and cultivator.

To evaluate the economic influence of the company, compare the acreage it owned or controlled in specific years with total cultivated land and with Korean-owned estates in the same districts. Distinguish corporate ownership from settlement programs and from mortgages held by financial institutions. A lease contract, a land-purchase entry, and a settlement advertisement answer different questions. These documentary distinctions make it possible to analyze dispossession and advantage without repeating the demonstrably false claim that almost all Korean cultivated land passed automatically into Japanese corporate hands.

Section 58.9

Tenant Contracts, Rent Collection, Intermediaries, and Cultivation Rights

Rents were paid mainly in kind, as a share of the harvest, though cash rents existed in some areas. Reports from the 1920s and 1930s frequently place the landlord's share at half of the main crop or more, with tenants often responsible for seed, fertilizer, and sometimes taxes or assessments. Contracts were often oral or brief, of short duration, and renewable at the landlord's discretion.

Many large landlords relied on agents called mareum, who supervised tenants, collected rent, and sometimes controlled access to land. Mareum could be benign or exploitative. Because they stood between landlord and tenant, they could extract additional payments, favor kin, or threaten to cancel leases. Their position made tenant complaints difficult, since the landlord might not see conditions directly.

The central tenant problem was insecure cultivation rights. A tenant who invested labor and fertilizer in land could lose it at the end of the contract. Nominal contract terms differed from actual village practice, which depended on local norms, landlord reputation, and the availability of other tenants. This insecurity explains why tenancy disputes so often turned on contract length and eviction, as shown in Sections 58.21–58.22.

A tenancy bargain combined rights over land with obligations over the harvest. Rent might be set as a share of the crop, as a fixed amount of rice, or as cash; payment was affected by grading, measurement, storage, transport, seed provision, and deductions. A nominal fifty-percent share therefore did not always mean that the cultivator retained half of the crop in usable food. A landlord could demand payment in a particular grade or at a specified time, while the cultivator bore the risk of lower prices when selling grain to meet other obligations.

The mareum, a landlord's agent, occupies an important place in this relationship. Agents could inspect fields, manage rentals, collect dues, and report tenants to owners living elsewhere. They could also negotiate with villagers who possessed local knowledge and social connections that absentee landlords lacked. Their discretion created opportunities for favoritism, disputes over measurements, and eviction threats. The unequal balance of power was most visible when a tenant had invested labor in a field yet could be replaced by someone offering a higher rent.

A useful document exercise asks students to annotate the contract's length, renewal provisions, rent formula, costs paid by each party, sanctions for missed payment, and rights to transfer or inherit the tenancy. The later 1934 Agricultural Land Ordinance addressed some insecurity, but laws did not eliminate the need for evidence about enforcement. Tenant organizations demanded more than lower rent: they sought predictability and a voice in deciding who could remain on the land.

Historical Vocabulary
Tenancy, sharecropping, mareum, and irrigation associations

Definition. Tenancy is the cultivation of land owned by another in return for rent. Sharecropping is a form of tenancy in which rent is a fixed share of the crop. A mareum was a landlord's agent. An irrigation association (suri johap) was an organization created to build and manage irrigation works and to collect assessments from landholders.

Historical context. These terms describe relationships that varied by region, landlord, and period, and the same words could cover very different practices.

Why it matters. They explain who bore risk in agriculture. In sharecropping, landlords shared in the crop but not in the cost of inputs unless contracts said so, and tenants bore most of the risk of harvest failure. Irrigation associations transferred construction costs to landholders and often to tenants.

Potential misunderstanding. It is easy to read "tenant" as simply "poor farmer." Tenants varied in wealth, and some employed hired labor. Equally, "landlord" did not mean a single wealthy class, since landholdings ranged from small to large.

Section 58.10

Rural Household Labor: Men, Women, Children, and Seasonal Work

Farm production relied on household labor. Men typically did plowing, heavy field work, and much of the irrigation labor. Women worked in transplanting, weeding, harvesting, and processing, while also managing cooking, child care, spinning and weaving, raising poultry and small livestock, and producing goods for sale in local markets. Children contributed from an early age, herding, gathering fuel, and assisting in fieldwork.

Household work was often unpaid and therefore invisible in official statistics. Women's contributions to production, to food preservation, and to cash earnings from handicrafts are poorly captured by yearbooks, which count crops and not labor. When households fell into debt or when tenants lost land, women's earning from wage work, factory employment, or petty trade became more significant, a topic examined in Section 58.39.

Seasonal wage labor supplemented farm income. Households sent members to work on irrigation projects, in construction, in transport, or at mills in slack seasons. This pattern connected agricultural cycles to the growing wage economy and prepared the way for longer-distance migration. Understanding household labor is therefore essential to understanding why the same economic change could produce different outcomes for different members of a family.

Crop cultivation depended on household work that was rarely itemized in land or tax statistics. Women's labor could include transplanting, weeding, gathering fuel, preparing seed and food, raising small livestock, trading at markets, and producing textiles for home use or sale. Children might help with animal tending or seasonal tasks while also attending school where the family could afford it. Men could undertake plowing, irrigation repair, outside hauling, or wage work, but divisions changed with season, region, age, and household composition. A record naming one male cultivator should not be mistaken for a complete census of the workers behind a harvest.

Seasonality shaped both debt and migration. The harvest produced a concentrated moment of income, but expenses for fertilizer, seed, tax, rent, ceremonies, and household consumption arrived throughout the year. Households therefore borrowed between crops, sold labor away from the village, or sent family members to cities and Japan. When a harvest failed, obligations did not always diminish proportionately. A seemingly modest increase in irrigation fees could be decisive for a family whose cash surplus was near zero.

For an evidence-rich section, combine agricultural-calendar descriptions with women's oral histories, farm-family budget records, and household-register data, while noting the different dates and purposes of these sources. The economic history of rice cannot be explained adequately with hectares and yield alone; it must count the labor that made cultivation possible and the constraints that determined who retained its proceeds.

Section 58.11

Regional Differences in Korean Agriculture

Agricultural policy did not produce identical results across Korea. The southern and western plains, with warmer climate, deeper soils, and access to ports, were the principal rice regions, and the highest tenancy rates and the greatest concentration of landlord and company ownership were in this zone. These areas received the bulk of irrigation investment and were the principal sources of rice exports.

In the north and northeast, shorter growing seasons and mountainous terrain favored dry-field crops such as millet, barley, soybeans, and potatoes, with smaller rice acreage. Upland cultivation, including some shifting cultivation in mountainous districts, persisted. Owner-cultivation was more common in many northern districts, and the pressures of rice-oriented policy were different, although those regions later became the center of mining and heavy industry.

Islands and coastal communities, including Jeju, combined farming with fishing and gathering, and had distinct labor patterns, with women divers playing a prominent role in Jeju's coastal economy. Proximity to markets and transport also mattered. Villages near railways and ports could sell cash crops and send labor to nearby towns, while remote districts remained more dependent on subsistence. Regional comparison is therefore essential: statements about "Korean farmers" that ignore these differences risk misdescribing most of them.

The peninsula's agricultural geography produced distinct rural economies. Southwestern alluvial plains supported extensive paddy agriculture and shipment through ports such as Gunsan and Mokpo. Mountainous northern and northeastern districts devoted larger shares of land to dry-field crops, mixed cultivation, forestry, and livestock. Coastal settlements combined farming with fisheries, seasonal shipping, or salt-making. Even within one county, irrigated bottomland and rain-dependent upland fields could yield very different crops, rents, and risks.

These differences influenced how colonial projects operated. A reservoir or irrigation association could directly affect paddy productivity, but its benefits would be limited in a mountainous district with little suitable field area. A railway link might reduce transport costs for commercial rice farmers while expanding the extraction of timber or minerals elsewhere. Agricultural households near a port had access to cash markets but were exposed to fluctuations in Japanese demand and shipping prices. Remote communities might preserve more food for local use yet face difficulty purchasing fertilizer or finding cash income.

Regional comparisons should be concrete: distinguish a Jeolla rice-growing county, a Pyeongan or Hamgyeong upland district, and an island community such as Amtae-do. For each, identify main crops, tenures, transport connections, share of cash production, and household alternatives to farming. That comparative approach also prevents the later story of northern heavy industry and southern agriculture from becoming an absolute geographic binary.

Section 58.12

Japan's Food Demand and the Origins of the Rice Production Increase Plan

Japan's industrial and urban growth during and after the First World War raised demand for rice faster than domestic farmland could supply it. Rice prices rose sharply, and in 1918 popular protests known as the rice riots spread across Japan. The crisis made food supply a political priority and led Japanese policymakers to look to the empire, and especially Korea, as a source of rice.

Korea was attractive for several reasons. Its southern plains were suitable for paddy cultivation of types familiar to Japanese consumers, transport to Japan was short, Japanese-owned land and mills were already in place, and colonial authorities could direct irrigation finance and agricultural extension. Korean rice already moved through ports such as Gunsan and Mokpo, and the plan sought to expand that flow.

The origin was therefore as much a Japanese domestic issue as a Korean agricultural one. The policy responded to the needs of the metropolitan food market and the stability of its cities, even when it was presented in terms of Korean agricultural improvement. Historians still debate how the policy's agricultural and distributional effects should be weighed, a subject returned to in the Historical Debate below.

The 1918 rice disturbances in Japan reflected a crisis of prices, wages, urban food supply, and wartime market conditions. Japan's expanding industrial workforce depended on purchased grain, and political leaders sought supplies compatible with Japanese consumer preferences. Korean japonica rice could be marketed within the imperial economy, particularly when milling, shipping, and grading were coordinated. The rice-increase policy was therefore a response to metropolitan food insecurity as well as a program of agricultural infrastructure. It was not designed primarily through Korean representative debate over what households in Korea most needed to eat.

Early official plans mixed multiple techniques: extension of irrigated paddy area, reclamation, seed improvement, fertilizer use, and changes in cultivation. They also assumed large investments whose returns depended on future grain prices. The state expected private capital and landlords to participate, but landlords often found buying additional land preferable to funding long-gestation improvement projects. Contemporary inflation raised construction expenses. This mismatch between targets and incentives explains why announced schemes and completed irrigated acreage diverged.

Chronology matters in interpreting the figures. Policy began in 1920, but administrative implementation and fiscal year measurements may start in 1921 in particular reports. A planning target expressed in seok or koku is a volume quantity, not a verified metric-ton yield. Comparisons should preserve the original measurement and state whether a figure refers to planned increment, actual harvest, export shipment, or domestic consumption.

Section 58.13

The First Rice Production Increase Plan, 1920–1925

The first plan, begun in 1920, aimed to raise rice output through land improvement, irrigation expansion, and the spread of improved seed and fertilizer, with a target period of well over a decade. Financing combined government funds, loans through institutions such as the Chōsen Industrial Bank, and contributions by landholders and irrigation associations.

Implementation fell short of ambitions. Projects proceeded more slowly than planned, funding was uneven, and costs rose. Early results showed gains in some irrigated districts, but the plan's targets for acreage improvement and output were not met on schedule. Students should distinguish promised output from realized production, since announcements of targets are not evidence of achievement.

The plan also had a significant social dimension. Landholders were expected to share costs, and costs fell on cultivators in various ways. Large landlords and companies with access to credit participated more readily than small owners, and tenants often bore assessments indirectly. Even in its first phase, the plan raised questions that the later program and tenant disputes would make visible.

The plan's logic also rested on assumptions about who would invest. Because government funds were limited, officials expected landholders and associations to borrow and to repay through assessments, which favored those with collateral. Korean and Japanese landlords with registered title could obtain loans; tenants and small owners could not borrow on equal terms. The same project could therefore be an opportunity for one household and a burden for another, depending on where it stood in the tenure system.

Historians therefore read the first plan through three kinds of evidence: official targets, which show intentions; project and loan records, which show what was financed; and local reports of assessments and disputes, which show who paid. The three do not always agree, and discrepancies between them are themselves a finding.

The initial program's shortcomings are visible in the gap between proposed and completed improvement. In the National Institute of Korean History's account, land improvement was undertaken over roughly 97,500 jeongbo against a planned 165,000, about 59 percent of the intended area. This is a measure of project execution, not a direct estimate of the percentage change in rice output. A reservoir could increase stability for one set of fields even when aggregate acreage targets were missed, while another unfinished or poorly financed project could leave an association with substantial debt and few benefits.

The distribution of capital was as important as its volume. Large owners with titled land could obtain loans or provide collateral; tenants generally lacked that option and could nevertheless face rent increases or pass-through assessments. In a village, questions about who paid for earthworks, who supplied labor, who obtained reliable water, and who could raise rent after improvement determined the household outcome. A policy could generate a productive public asset while also strengthening the bargaining position of the owner of the improved property.

A defensible contemporary evaluation separates the ministry's planned construction schedule, the irrigation association's actual expenditures, bank loans, completed acreage, rainfall conditions, recorded yields, and tenant petitions. No single indicator establishes success. The government could rightly report an improved canal without that showing the typical tenant better off; an angry petition could document serious hardship without demonstrating that no field produced more rice. The distinction is especially important before comparing the revised program introduced in 1926.

Section 58.14

The Revised Rice Production Increase Plan, 1926–1934

In 1926 the program was reorganized into a revised plan, with changes in scale, financing, and implementation methods. The revised plan relied more on private and institutional lending and on the Chōsen Industrial Bank, and it expanded the involvement of landholders and irrigation associations. It continued the main objectives of rice increase and land improvement but with a different structure.

The revised program ended in 1934. The reasons combined economic and political factors. Rice prices in Japan fell sharply after 1929 as harvests were large and the Depression reduced demand, and Japanese farmers protested competition from colonial rice. Japanese policy turned toward controlling rice supply and protecting domestic prices, a change reflected in legislation regulating the rice market in Japan. Under these conditions, further expansion of Korean rice for export no longer suited metropolitan policy.

The National Institute of Korean History and other scholars distinguish three phases: the initial plan of 1920–1925, the revised program of 1926–1934, and the wartime program beginning in 1940. These must not be merged into one uninterrupted policy. Each responded to different circumstances, and the termination of 1934 shows that Korean agriculture was managed to serve the needs of the empire as defined in Tokyo.

In the renewed program the administration shifted the financing structure to reduce reliance on private investors who had not supplied the expected funds. A National Institute of Korean History synthesis reports that the projected private-capital share of development financing was reduced sharply, with greater government-arranged credit, subsidies, and institutional sponsorship. The Oriental Development Company established a land-improvement unit, and the Chōsen Land Improvement Company began operations during the renewed phase. Within the Government-General, specialized agricultural and irrigation offices were reorganized to manage the larger administrative task.

These changes did not remove economic risk; they relocated it. Publicly arranged credit could lower up-front borrowing costs for projects, while irrigation associations and landowners remained responsible for assessment payments over time. The benefits of improved fields accrued partly as higher land values and rents, which might favor owners more than cultivating tenants. The more ambitious bureaucracy also generated records—plans, budgets, engineering inspections, and performance reports—that allow historians to compare intentions with actual completed projects.

The second phase struggled under changing economic conditions. Rice prices and construction finance were affected by the late-1920s and early-1930s downturn, and by 1934 the administration terminated the program in its prevailing form. Students should not infer that every irrigation work closed or that rice output ceased to change after the termination. Existing assets remained, farms continued to adapt, and a new state-directed wartime rice policy began in 1940 under quite different conditions.

Section 58.15

Irrigation Associations, Reservoirs, Land Improvement, and Agricultural Finance

Irrigation associations were central to the program. They organized landholders to build and maintain reservoirs, canals, and embankments, and to reclaim land. Water rights, assessments, and construction costs were allocated among members, and loans for construction were repaid through assessments.

Irrigation could raise yields substantially, especially for rice, and could convert marginal land into productive paddy. It also raised land values, which benefited owners. But the costs fell on those within the association, including tenants who might pay assessments directly or indirectly through higher rent. Small owners could struggle to pay, and some lost land when assessments combined with other debts.

Associations were also administrative instruments. Boards were often dominated by large landlords and officials, and decisions about construction and allocation reflected their interests. Water supply was not evenly shared, and the maintenance of works required continuous payment. Irrigation therefore illustrates the central theme of the chapter: technical improvement and distributional burden could coexist.

An irrigation association was simultaneously a technical system and a financial institution. Its canals, embankments, sluices, reservoirs, and drainage works had to be constructed, maintained, and repaired; expenses were recovered through assessments and borrowing. A project could reduce the frequency of drought losses by making water delivery more predictable, but assessment payments could still burden a farmer in a poor harvest year. When a landlord owned the improved field and a tenant actually worked it, the crucial question was whether the legal payer of charges passed the costs into rent or other deductions.

There were also collective-action problems. Upstream and downstream fields had different interests in water schedules; an association might have authority to compel payments from members even when only some parcels experienced immediate benefits. Reservoir construction could affect land access and neighboring communities. Engineering reports emphasize measured water capacity and hectares supplied; petitions from residents reveal disputes about costs, drainage, rights of way, and village representation. Both types of record should be examined.

A complete assessment should compare preproject harvest variation with later variation, but control for rainfall, seed changes, fertilizer, prices, and cropping intensity. Land-improvement expense cannot automatically be classified as a loss, and a higher yield cannot automatically be classified as a net gain to the cultivator. The relevant unit is the farm household's benefit after capital costs, assessments, rent, and the opportunity cost of labor have been accounted for.

Section 58.16

Seeds, Fertilizers, Cultivation Techniques, and Agricultural Productivity

The rice programs promoted improved seed varieties, including Japanese cultivars adapted to Korean conditions, commercial fertilizers, and altered cultivation practices such as planting arrangements. Agricultural experiment stations and extension officers distributed seed and gave instruction. Yields in many districts rose, and productivity gains are well documented. One widely used estimate holds that rice yield per hectare stayed near 1.3 metric tons until the mid-1920s and rose to about 2.2 tons by the late 1930s, though the size and timing of gains are debated.

These inputs, however, had costs. Commercial fertilizer had to be purchased, often on credit, and improved varieties often required more fertilizer and water. Farmers who could not afford inputs gained less. Landlords sometimes encouraged or required new varieties because they were more marketable, and tenants adopted them on landlord terms.

Official reports frequently described pre-existing Korean practice as primitive. Historians should treat such statements as part of the justification of colonial improvement rather than as neutral description. Korean farmers had developed sophisticated knowledge of local conditions, and many improvements built on existing practice. The measurable gains and the rhetoric of backwardness must be separated.

Japanese and Korean experiment stations promoted selected seeds, improved nursery techniques, more systematic transplanting, fertilizer use, and pest control. Extension officers and agricultural societies attempted to disseminate these practices, but adoption depended on the ability to purchase inputs and control fields over more than one season. Increased fertilizer could raise output when water conditions were adequate, yet provide disappointing returns on rain-dependent land or under severe price declines. Thus the technology's potential and the cultivator's capacity to use it were separate problems.

Historical yield estimates should carry explicit definitions. A frequently cited academic series reports Korean rice yields per hectare around 1.3 metric tons through much of the earlier period and rising toward roughly 2.2 by the later 1930s. Those are estimates based on particular datasets, not a mechanically verified figure for every province and year. Yields vary with whether the calculation uses planted or harvested area, paddy versus milled rice, dry or irrigated fields, and climatic variation. A reliable graph must report the underlying series and any unit conversion.

Productivity gains can have distributional consequences that an agronomy report does not measure. A new seed might increase output, but if a landlord raised rent, the tenant's retained grain could rise much less or not at all. Greater commercial output could also expose households to market fluctuations. Students should therefore relate the technical history of varieties and fertilizers to the legal history of tenancy and the economic history of grain prices.

Section 58.17

Rice Markets, Exports to Japan, and Korean Food Consumption

Rice moved from villages through collectors and merchants to mills and ports, then to Japan. Prices responded to Japanese demand, and landlords, who received rent in kind, sold large quantities. Total output rose over the 1920s, and exports to Japan rose faster, so that exports came to take a large share of the harvest, in one published Korean historical series, 31.3 percent in 1924, 42.9 percent in 1928, 47.8 percent in 1932, and 53.2 percent in 1936. These are shares of recorded production shipped out of Korea, and the series and definitions must be stated when comparing years.

Over the same period, Korean per-capita rice consumption fell. Widely cited estimates put the 1932–1936 level at about half that of 1912–1916, and per-capita consumption of all food grains was reported to be about 35 percent lower by 1944, even though coarse grains were imported from Manchuria to supplement supply. Many households substituted barley, millet, and other cheaper grains. Because rent in kind was paid in rice, tenants had little rice to keep, and because landlords sold rice for cash, rice left villages. This pattern does not mean that no Korean household ate rice. Wealthier urban and landlord households could maintain rice consumption, and prices, income, and region mattered.

The relationship between production, exports, and consumption deserves its own analysis, not a slogan. Population growth, changes in other grain supplies, price trends, and debt all affected how much rice households kept. Students should be wary of simple claims in either direction: that exports show extraction by themselves, or that increased production shows improved nutrition.

Regional and class differences complicate any average. In rice-exporting districts of the southwest, where tenancy was high and rent was paid in rice, households kept the least rice. In northern districts where dry-field crops dominated, rice was less central to diet and the export trade touched fewer households. Urban salaried and landlord households could keep buying rice. A single national per-capita figure therefore blends very different experiences.

Substitution was not simply a change of taste. Barley, millet, and other grains were cheaper per unit, and households shifted to them as rice prices and cash needs rose. Some grain was also imported into Korea, notably from Manchuria, which partly offset the loss of rice in the aggregate supply of grains. A careful analysis therefore compares total grain availability, not rice alone, and adds price and income evidence. Even then, the average conceals who went short, which is why household budgets (Section 58.18) matter.

A directly attributed series makes the distinction between output and household consumption visible. A National Institute of Korean History table, based on a study by Tomita Akiko and collaborators, lists total rice production at 15.17 million seok in 1924, 17.29 million in 1928, and 17.88 million in 1936. Shipments out of Korea were recorded at 4.75 million, 7.42 million, and 9.51 million seok, respectively. The corresponding shares were 31.3, 42.9, and 53.2 percent. In that series, estimated annual per-person rice consumption decreased from 0.60 seok in 1924 to 0.38 in 1936. The table does not show that each family reduced consumption by precisely that amount; it describes an aggregate estimate across a changing population.

These figures must not be used as though shipments were a straightforward count of grain taken without payment. Much rice moved through commercial exchanges, but those exchanges were structured by rent, debt, price incentives, access to markets, and unequal bargaining power. A tenant could sell rice to meet cash obligations and then purchase lower-priced coarse grains for family consumption. Equally, a merchant or landlord might benefit from strong Japanese demand. The amount that remained physically in Korea was not identical with rice consumed by Korean households because seed, storage, spoilage, processing and other uses must be considered.

There are also competing datasets. A different National Institute source attributes annual figures to the Government-General's agriculture bureau, with distinctions between total production and exports specifically to Japan. These series may not align year by year with broader shipment figures. The chapter therefore should present one fully sourced table without silently mixing numerators, and note that divergent historical estimates require checking what each compiler counted. This precision strengthens, rather than weakens, the argument that increased market output could coexist with serious food insecurity.

Historical Debate I

Did the Rice-Production Programs Improve Productivity While Worsening Distribution?

The question: Did the rice-production programs raise agricultural productivity, and did they at the same time worsen distributional inequality and household food security?

Interpretation A — Productivity gains were real and significant: Some economists emphasize measurable increases in yields, irrigation, and output, and argue that these represent genuine agricultural development with long-run benefits.
Interpretation B — The programs primarily served imperial food supply: Other historians argue that export orientation, cost burdens on cultivators, tenancy insecurity, and declining per-capita consumption show that gains accrued mainly to landlords, companies, and Japan.
Interpretation C — Both were true, with outcomes varying by region and household: Many scholars hold that productivity and distribution must be analyzed separately, with outcomes depending on tenure and region.
Interpretation D — Imperial policy was only one cause of the consumption decline: Some economists argue that world price shocks and the global depression, not only imperial export policy, help explain interwar consumption trends, and that Korean outcomes compare less badly with other rice-producing regions than often claimed. Others reply that policy shaped how those shocks reached households.

Evidence: Colonial agricultural statistics, export records, consumption estimates, tenancy surveys, and dispute records.

Points of disagreement: Disagreement concerns the size of gains, the reliability of consumption estimates, and what counts as development.

Why it matters: The answer shapes whether colonial agricultural change is described as development, extraction, or both, and how later land reform is understood.

Student task: Evaluate which evidence best distinguishes productivity from welfare, and identify what additional data would help.

Section 58.18

Rural Household Budgets, Farm Income, and the Economics of Tenancy

Gross harvest is not household income. A tenant household must subtract rent, seed, fertilizer, assessments, loan interest, and often taxes, then convert what remains into food, clothing, and cash needs. Reconstructing such budgets from surveys shows how thin the margin was, especially for tenants paying half the crop or more, and how small changes in price or harvest could create deficits.

Surviving budget surveys, conducted by the colonial government and others, provide some evidence, though they were often compiled for administrative purposes and cover limited samples. Illustrative calculations can make the mechanics clear but must be labeled as illustrations, not as observed records.

The structure of tenancy meant that productivity gains did not automatically reach the household. If improved seed raised yields but also raised fertilizer costs and rents, the net effect depended on contract terms. Households responded through supplementary labor, reduced consumption, borrowing, and, in the worst cases, leaving the land.

A farm budget should be reconstructed as a sequence of obligations. After harvest the cultivator might surrender part of the crop in rent, repay the grain or cash advanced before planting, pay irrigation or financial-association charges, reserve seed for the next season, purchase clothing or fuel, and provide food for the household until the next harvest. The balance left after these obligations was the meaningful measure of security. A higher gross harvest could be absorbed by higher input costs or rent even if the family worked harder.

Contemporary household-budget surveys require skepticism about sampling. Officials could choose prosperous demonstration villages, and their classifications may omit unpaid labor or goods produced and consumed at home. A budget stated in yen also changes meaning when rice prices fall sharply: debt obligations fixed in nominal money can become heavier relative to agricultural income. A fuller measure of welfare considers how many months a household could feed itself, whether children stayed in school, and whether the family retained land and work equipment.

The chapter's numerical workshop should be clearly marked illustrative unless it uses a named historical household record. Its purpose is to teach the arithmetic of rent, debt, and retained grain, not to manufacture a representative farm from invented figures. Once a documented survey is selected, compare the model to the historical distribution of rent rates and to the share of households whose budgets actually recorded deficits.

Everyday Life and Social History I

A Tenant-Farming Household Facing Rent, Debt, and Food Requirements

This is a clearly identified hypothetical illustration built from the patterns described in this chapter, not a historical record or testimony.

Consider a household of five renting paddy land in a southwestern rice district. After the autumn harvest, the landlord's agent collects a share of the crop that leaves the household with a modest portion. Part of what remains is already committed to repay a spring loan for seed and fertilizer, and part is sold to buy salt, cloth, and tools.

By late winter the household's rice is nearly gone. It shifts to barley and millet mixed with whatever can be purchased, and members seek day labor on a nearby construction site or at a mill. The "barley hump" of late spring, the lean period before the barley harvest, is the point of greatest strain, and the household may borrow again against the next crop.

This pattern shows how debt, tenancy, and food shortage interlocked. It also shows variation: a household with a secure lease, an adult son in wage work, or a few owned fields would fare differently.

Section 58.19

Agricultural Debt, Moneylending, Mortgage, and Rural Economic Insecurity

Rural households borrowed for seed, fertilizer, irrigation assessments, rent arrears, taxes, ceremonial expenses, and food in lean months. Lenders included financial associations, landlords, merchants, and private moneylenders, and interest rates varied, with informal loans often much costlier than institutional ones.

Debt could be secured by land or by the next crop. Default led to distress sales or foreclosure, and registered title made transfer easier. For tenants, debt often became tied to the landlord relationship, since the landlord was also a creditor and could use arrears as grounds for eviction.

Debt was not only a symptom of poverty but a mechanism by which land and labor shifted. A household might remain on the land as a tenant after losing ownership, or leave for a town, mine, or foreign country. The link between debt, tenancy, and migration is developed in the Cause and Effect feature in Division VIII.

Debt accumulated because the calendar of payments often differed from the calendar of income. A borrower could need cash or seed in spring and owe rent immediately after autumn harvest; lenders could charge interest, demand collateral, or insist on payment in grain. In a year of weak prices, selling more grain might still raise too little money to repay a nominal debt. A farmer with registered title might mortgage land, while a tenant who could not offer land security might depend on a landlord, merchant, or personal guarantor. These were not interchangeable forms of credit.

The connection with agricultural improvement was paradoxical. Government promotion of fertilizer and irrigation encouraged capital outlays, but households with insecure leases and low retained shares had limited capacity to bear losses if the new input failed. A successful investment could increase land value and thereby attract a higher rent. Where debts mounted, a small owner might sell a plot and remain on the same land as a tenant. This was one route by which technical modernization and increased dependency could occur simultaneously.

Evidence should distinguish household consumption borrowing from productive loans, lenders' recorded interest rates from fees actually collected, and land foreclosure from voluntary sale. A local court file can reveal the legal terms of a mortgage but rarely the family's entire strategy for surviving the year. Farm surveys, landlord ledgers, and tenant testimony are complementary sources. The most useful question is not simply how much money was lent but who controlled the timing of repayment and the consequences of default.

Section 58.20

The Great Depression, Falling Agricultural Prices, and the Rural Crisis

After 1929 the global depression and large Japanese harvests drove rice prices down sharply. Farmers' incomes fell, since the value of what they sold dropped while rents often remained fixed in kind and debts fixed in money. The effect was especially severe for tenants and indebted owners.

Output could remain substantial while distress deepened. When prices fall, a larger quantity of rice must be sold to pay the same cash obligations. Many households ran short of food before the barley harvest, and the "barley hump" became a feature of rural hardship. Landlords also faced lower income, which led some to raise rents, shorten leases, or evict tenants in favor of better terms.

The crisis helps explain the rise of tenancy disputes in the early 1930s, the colonial regulations of 1932 and 1934, and the Rural Revitalization Movement, discussed below. It also contributed to the supply of labor available for northern mines and factories.

The Depression reached a largely agricultural Korean economy through commodity prices, the availability of credit, trade, and the incomes of people whose labor or products served Japanese markets. Rice prices could fall even when a family harvested a normal crop. For a tenant paying a fixed money rent or servicing a money loan, that price decline reduced the purchasing power of the harvest. For a household whose rent was a fixed share of grain, the immediate burden was different, but falling cash income still complicated school fees, fertilizer purchases, taxes, and debt repayment.

The shock was not distributed uniformly. Wealthier owners able to carry stock or obtain credit could sometimes wait for better prices; small cultivators often needed to sell immediately. Merchants with warehousing, transport connections, and bank relationships occupied a different position from producers selling from the field. Families might withdraw children from school, seek work in port cities, or migrate across borders. Such responses did not prove rural people lacked initiative; they represented difficult calculations under constrained conditions.

A historically rigorous analysis needs rice-price data in the same units over several years, preferably adjusted for changes in other goods. A fall in nominal prices may improve the position of urban consumers with stable wages even as it devastates indebted farmers. Rural policies launched in 1932 should be read against this unequal crisis: the Government-General sought to restore household solvency, maintain output, reduce disputes, and keep the countryside governable at the same time.

Section 58.21

The Amtae-do Tenant Dispute, 1923–1924

Amtae-do is an island in the southwest, now part of Sinan County. Its largest landowner, Mun Jae-cheol, is reported to have charged rents of 60 to 80 percent of the harvest, when a rent of about half the crop was common. Tenants organized the Amtae Tenants' Association in December 1923 and resolved that rent should not exceed 40 percent. Most other landowners on the island accepted this, but Mun refused, and tenants adopted collective nonpayment and demonstrations. Sources generally date the dispute from August 1923 to August 1924.

The conflict drew in police, who arrested tenants, and in solidarity campaigns by other tenant groups and social organizations on the mainland. Women and neighboring communities participated. The dispute became nationally known because of its length, the tenants' discipline, and its publicity in the press. After sustained organizing, an agreement was reached in August 1924, an outcome widely regarded as a significant, if limited, success for the tenants.

Amtae-do shows how economic grievances became organized action: shared rent terms, a visible landlord, an organizing association, and the support of outside groups. It also shows limits. The settlement addressed one estate, and structural tenancy problems remained. The political and ideological dimensions of tenant movements are treated in Chapter 59.

The dispute also shows how local conditions shaped tactics. On an island, tenants lived close together, depended on one dominant landlord, and could organize through existing village ties. Collective nonpayment worked as a bargaining tool only so long as tenants could sustain it without a harvest income, which depended on outside support in the form of food, funds, and publicity. Arrests tested that solidarity, and the eventual agreement reflected both tenant persistence and the cost to the landlord of continued conflict.

Later retellings often present Amtae-do as a decisive victory. A more careful reading treats it as a documented, significant but local settlement: it shows what organized tenants could achieve under particular circumstances, and it did not by itself change the legal or economic structure of tenancy in Korea.

The Amtae-do dispute can be reconstructed through identifiable persons and records rather than a generalized image of peasant protest. The Amtae Tenant Association was organized on December 4, 1923, and its demands differentiated between rice fields and dry fields; the commonly cited request was a 40-percent rent ceiling for paddies and 30 percent for upland fields. Landlord Mun Jae-cheol, whose family wealth included salt commerce and land, refused to agree to the reductions accepted by some other owners. That refusal became the focal point of a protracted conflict over economic power and recognition of a collective organization.

Island geography shaped tactics. Organizers relied on links to Mokpo for communication, markets, and public solidarity. When tenant leaders were arrested, family members and supporters mobilized outside the island, turning a dispute about rent into a more visible struggle over police authority and fair treatment. A June 6, 1924 Dong-A Ilbo account describes approximately 400 people supporting detainees being held under surveillance; this is one contemporary press report, not proof that exactly 400 participated in every phase of the campaign. The reported August 30 settlement was an important local result, not a colony-wide abolition of high rents.

The case shows both the effectiveness and vulnerability of organization. A landlord could ignore a single tenant and replace that household; a group refusing rents collectively could raise the cost of doing so. Yet arrest, confiscation, and the need to sustain families through a long dispute created countervailing pressures. Students should contrast the association's stated demands, the newspaper's narrative, police documents, and the actual settlement terms. That source comparison reveals how a contract dispute acquired public political meaning without ceasing to be about the economics of cultivation.

Section 58.22

Tenant Organizations, Agricultural Disputes, and the Colonial Land Regulations of 1932 and 1934

After Amtae-do, tenant associations and disputes spread, particularly in the 1920s and early 1930s. Disputes concerned rents, lease duration, evictions, and the conduct of agents, and tenants used negotiation, petitions, and collective nonpayment. Disputes increased in number as the depression deepened, and the nature of demands shifted from rent reductions toward protecting cultivation rights as landlords sought to change tenants.

The colonial government intervened. The Chōsen Tenancy Conciliation Ordinance was promulgated on December 10, 1932 and took effect on February 1, 1933; it provided a district-court process for conciliation of tenancy disputes. The Chōsen Agricultural Land Ordinance, promulgated on April 11, 1934, regulated aspects of tenancy contracts, lease terms, and the role of landlord agents. Its implementing rules were promulgated in September 1934. These measures aimed to stabilize agriculture and reduce conflict.

Their limits were significant. Enforcement was uneven, landlords retained strong influence, and the regulations did not alter the structure of ownership or rent levels substantially. They were also instruments of control, since conciliation channeled disputes into official procedures. Their effect was therefore mixed: some tenants gained greater security, while conflicts over cultivation rights continued.

Colonial intervention in tenant disputes expanded as conflicts became harder to contain. The Chōsen Tenancy Conciliation Ordinance was promulgated on December 10, 1932, and took effect on February 1, 1933. Its first article allowed parties to apply to district courts for conciliation in disagreements over rent and other tenancy relations. This mechanism was not the same as a tenant's enforceable right to receive a lower rent: it channelled disputes into supervised negotiation. The courts, local officials, and conciliation procedures could stabilize arrangements but could also displace independent collective bargaining.

The Agricultural Land Ordinance promulgated April 11, 1934, addressed aspects of agricultural leasing, including the security of cultivation and the regulation of landlord agents. Its implementing provisions appeared later that year. In the ordinance's framework, ordinary agricultural tenancies could be protected for three years and certain special uses for seven, subject to specified conditions; renewal and notice provisions mattered as much as the headline term. Legal protections should be read next to records of evictions and adjustments to rent, because written rules operated through a hierarchical administration and often unequal access to lawyers and officials.

Neither ordinance can be assessed through promulgation dates alone. One would need counts of conciliation applications, settlements, rejected complaints, subsequent evictions, and changes in tenant rents. A fall in reported disputes after a law might reflect improved conditions, intimidation, changed filing rules, or reduced collective organization. The historical significance of these regulations lies in the attempt to make an unstable agrarian order administratively manageable without fundamentally transferring control of land to cultivators.

Section 58.23

The Rural Revitalization Movement, 1932–1940

Under Governor-General Ugaki Kazushige the government launched the Rural Revitalization Movement (Nōson Shinkō Undō) in 1932. It encouraged village self-reliance through household reconstruction plans, farm guidance, debt management, thrift, and village organizations, with officials supervising activities. Selected model villages received attention and support.

The movement had economic aims, including reducing debt and increasing self-sufficiency, and social-control aims, including promoting loyalty and discouraging disputes and radical organizing. It placed much responsibility on households rather than altering landholding or rent structures. Results were mixed: some villages reduced debt and improved practices, while many structural problems persisted.

It can be compared with Korean-organized rural education and enlightenment campaigns discussed in Chapter 57, which operated independently and sometimes in tension with official efforts. The movement shows colonial authorities trying to stabilize rural society without redistributing resources.

The Rural Revitalization Movement developed through identifiable administrative stages. Ugaki Kazushige publicly advocated rural improvement in 1932, the Government-General established rural-revival machinery that year, and an October 8 administrative circular helped organize the campaign. In March 1933 another directive elaborated household economic-reconstruction plans. Officials selected model or guidance villages and asked households to record food production, cash receipts, expenditures, and outstanding debt. The stated objectives of food sufficiency, balanced cash accounts, and debt reduction made this an unusually close intervention into farm-family economics.

Implementation depended on village leaders, township offices, agricultural associations, and selected demonstration households. A model family's ledger could be used to teach budgeting, crop choice, and thrift, but the same procedures enabled local administrators to monitor indebtedness, labor, and compliance. The model-village system offered loans or public works in some contexts, yet its ability to transform unequal landholding was limited. Advice to reduce spending could not make a tenant's rent disappear; an official savings target did not solve the problem of collapsing grain prices.

The program's character changed as the war intensified. Before 1937 the language of household rehabilitation predominated; later directives increasingly tied village organization to production, donations, and mobilization for the imperial war effort. A historical source window should use an actual 1932 or 1933 circular and contrast it with a 1937 official instruction. This prevents a misleading description of the whole 1932–1940 period as either purely benign social work or nothing but wartime requisition from the start.

Section 58.24

Manufacturing before the Industrial Expansion of the 1930s

In the 1910s and 1920s Korean manufacturing consisted largely of rice milling, food processing, brewing, textiles, printing, ceramics, and small workshops. Household production persisted alongside small factories using hand or simple powered tools, and a smaller number of mechanized plants. Rice mills, tied to the export trade, were among the most numerous and economically significant factories.

Three categories should be distinguished. Household industry used family labor, often for subsistence or local sale. Small workshops employed a few workers with limited equipment. Mechanized factories used power machinery and wage labor. Korean-owned enterprises were concentrated in the first two categories and in light industry, while larger and more mechanized plants were more often Japanese-owned.

This early manufacturing was still small in scale compared with the heavy-industrial expansion of the 1930s. It matters because it produced the first factory workers, trained some Korean managers and technicians, and established the commercial networks that later industry used. The economy remained predominantly agricultural throughout the 1910s and 1920s.

Early colonial manufacture encompassed many different production forms. An urban workshop using hand tools, a rice mill with powered machinery, and a textile plant employing hundreds of wage workers all appear in histories of industry, but they cannot be counted as equivalent establishments. The 1910s and 1920s saw food processing, brewing, rice milling, tobacco-related activity, printing, and textiles develop around markets and transport links. Japanese and Korean capital participated at different scales and under different credit conditions. Small Korean workshops often depended on merchant finance or family labor, while larger plants required machinery, import connections, and reliable power.

Rice milling illustrates the relationship between agriculture and manufacturing. A mill added commercial value by cleaning and grading grain, but it depended on produce obtained through landlord, tenant, and broker networks. A change in the Japanese rice market could alter mill throughput even if the physical machinery remained unchanged. Textiles provide another contrast: mechanized cotton spinning and weaving competed with household production and with imported finished cloth. These developments reshaped the livelihoods of traders, craftspeople, and women engaged in home manufacture.

When describing industrial growth, use a consistent definition of factory and distinguish registered establishments from working machinery, gross output from value added, and a proprietor's nationality from the workforce's composition. Without these distinctions, a striking increase in the number of firms could say more about registration after 1920 than about how far mechanized production actually spread.

Section 58.25

The Repeal of the Company Ordinance in 1920 and the Growth of Registered Enterprises

The Company Ordinance of December 1910 required companies in Korea to obtain permission from the Government-General, which could refuse or revoke it. The ordinance gave the government a tool to shape which enterprises were established. It was repealed in April 1920, in the period of policy adjustment after the March First Movement (Chapter 56).

After repeal, the number of registered companies grew, and Korean and Japanese enterprises were formed more freely. But removing a permission requirement did not equalize conditions. Japanese firms continued to hold advantages in capital, banking access, technology, official connections, and links to the imperial market. Korean entrepreneurs faced higher costs of credit and, in many fields, competition from better-financed firms.

The repeal illustrates a general lesson: formal legal equality of access is different from equal capacity to use it. Enterprise formation increased, but the distribution of capital, finance, and market position changed more slowly.

The Company's Ordinance's initial regime required official permission for important forms of corporate establishment and conduct. The measure was promulgated in December 1910 and operated from the start of 1911. Its formal repeal came through Government-General Ordinance No. 7 effective April 1, 1920. The repeal notice is unusually useful primary evidence because it does not merely announce liberalization: it specifies how existing companies were to be treated under civil law and preserves exceptions for some regulated activities. Students should read the operative provision and supplementary rules rather than assume a completely unregulated market followed.

The shift coincided with a broader imperial strategy of attracting and facilitating commercial capital. Japanese corporations could establish or expand operations under less burdensome approval rules, and Korean investors gained formal opportunities as well. But reduced entry barriers did not equal equal access to loans, machinery, overseas purchasing agents, patents, managerial networks, or colonial officials. An already capitalized enterprise could exploit the change more readily than a group of merchants pooling limited local savings.

To evaluate the law's practical effect, compare company registrations before and after 1920 while controlling for changes in classification, survival rates, paid-in capital, and industry. The central counterfactual is not whether more names appeared in the register, but whether Korean ownership and productive opportunities expanded relative to the better-financed Japanese firms using the same legal opening.

Section 58.26

Korean Entrepreneurs, Business Partnerships, and the Limits of Indigenous Capital

A Korean business class existed and grew. It included merchants, landlords investing in commerce and industry, and manufacturers in textiles, rubber goods, brewing, and trade. Partnerships and joint-stock companies pooled capital, and some firms grew into large enterprises.

The best-known case is the Kyungsung Spinning and Weaving Company, founded in 1919 by the Kim family of Honam landlords, including Kim Seong-su and Kim Yeon-su, with investment from Korean shareholders. It became a leading Korean-owned textile enterprise, and its history has been studied closely, including by the historian Carter Eckert, who linked it to landlord capital and to the political choices of its owners. The firm's founding, financing, ownership, management, and later expansion, including activity outside Korea, should be distinguished rather than treated as one story.

Korean entrepreneurs faced constraints: limited access to long-term credit, competition from Japanese firms, and dependence on colonial regulation and favors. They also took part in campaigns to promote Korean-made goods in the early 1920s. It would be wrong to label all Korean businesspeople as nationalist or as collaborators. Behavior varied with firm, period, and opportunity, and each case requires evidence.

Korean capital did not disappear under colonial rule. Traders, landlords, and professional families pooled resources in joint-stock ventures, textile manufacture, retailing, warehousing, and banking. Kyungsung Spinning and Weaving, established in 1919 and associated with Kim Seong-su and Kim Yeon-su, became an important example of Korean enterprise in mechanized textiles. Its significance lies not in making it typical of all Korean business, but in showing what considerable capital, industrial machinery, technical training, and market strategy required. The company faced Japanese competition as well as changing import and domestic-demand conditions.

Business opportunities differed sharply by sector. Merchants could enter small-scale processing with relatively limited capital, while electric power, fertilizer production, mining, and chemical industry required sums and technical equipment far beyond most Korean investors' reach. A large Korean landlord might invest in urban property or mills rather than risk scarce funds on a long industrial project. Credit allocation, not only entrepreneurial inclination, affected the pattern of ownership visible by the 1930s.

Nationalist writers sometimes celebrated Korean-owned products and encouraged consumers to support domestic firms. Yet the politics of an entrepreneur cannot be inferred from the firm's ethnicity alone. Companies competed for colonial contracts, relied on Japanese banks, and operated under political constraints; owners also differed in their later wartime conduct. A responsible narrative analyzes individual decisions through dated records and separates economic achievement from political endorsement.

Section 58.27

Japanese Corporate Capital, Industrial Investment, and the Structure of Imperial Enterprise

Japanese corporate investment in Korea included major commercial and industrial groups, new industrial conglomerates, state-supported companies, and financial institutions. Firms located activities in Korea for access to raw materials, cheap labor, hydroelectric power, and favorable policy, and to serve Korean and Manchurian markets.

Management, technology, and profits typically flowed through Japanese corporate headquarters. Korean subsidiaries could be parts of integrated production chains, with materials shipped to Japan for further processing, or of regional systems linking Japan, Korea, and Manchuria. Investment decisions were made with reference to the imperial economy, not to Korean development goals.

This structure meant that factories located in Korea were not necessarily embedded in the Korean economy. They hired Korean workers and bought local goods, but their capital, management, and markets lay elsewhere. Later sections examine how this shaped the industrial economy of the 1930s.

The large Japanese enterprises that entered or expanded in Korea drew on financial, technical, and commercial systems spanning several territories. Corporate groups could raise money in Japan, hire specialized engineers, arrange imports of machinery, obtain power concessions, and sell products into markets connected by the imperial shipping and rail networks. Government encouragement, tax provisions, and the allocation of land or water-power rights could improve investment prospects. These conditions helped explain why capital-intensive sectors often developed under Japanese rather than Korean control.

A plant located in Korea may have contributed wages, transport demand, and technological learning locally while still sending dividends and executive decisions elsewhere. A mining company's gross value of output is therefore not an adequate measure of its contribution to Korean household incomes. Conversely, assuming that all value was immediately transferred out ignores local workers, suppliers, and service businesses. The appropriate empirical test separates payroll, purchased inputs, profits, depreciation, taxes, and retained reinvestment.

The meaning of zaibatsu also requires precision. It refers to particular corporate and family-group structures rather than every Japanese firm. Some industrial developments involved business combinations, affiliated utilities, colonial financial institutions, and state concessions without fitting a simplistic single-parent hierarchy. Ownership charts and annual reports can clarify those relationships. This matters especially in the northern chemical and hydroelectric complexes described in the next division.

Section 58.28

Textiles, Silk, Rubber, Paper, and Consumer-Goods Manufacturing

Textile production included cotton spinning and weaving, silk reeling, and hosiery. Large mechanized spinning mills, many Japanese-owned and some Korean-owned like Kyungsung, employed large numbers of women. Silk reeling developed alongside sericulture promotion among farm households, and rubber shoes became a prominent consumer product, with factories in Pyongyang and elsewhere.

Paper and other consumer goods were produced in mills and workshops of varying size. Raw materials were often imported or supplied from Korea or Manchuria, and machinery was frequently imported from Japan. Competition between large Japanese-owned plants and small Korean firms shaped market structure, and Korean-owned firms often concentrated in segments where small capital could compete.

These industries were important for employment and for consumer markets, and they were more locally embedded than heavy industry in that they used Korean labor and sold to Korean consumers. They also had significant differences between Japanese-owned and Korean-owned enterprises in scale, finance, and technology.

Textile manufacturing was not one industry with one labor regime. Cotton spinning and weaving required expensive machines and steady power, while some silk processes relied on smaller establishments and intensive female labor. Rubber goods and paper making had different raw-material requirements and consumer markets. Regional transport connections influenced where factories located; production for local consumption operated alongside output that competed with Japanese imports or served imperial demand. Each industry's technological choices created a distinct mix of skilled mechanics, supervisors, young operatives, and seasonal employees.

Textile factories illustrate the gendered labor economy. Women and girls often entered wage work under family financial pressure, and some lived in dormitories controlled by employers. Regular wages could give them money not available through unpaid household work, but hours, supervision, health conditions, and the deductions for food and lodging constrained that independence. Employer records tend to emphasize output and absenteeism; worker testimony and newspapers may reveal discipline and coercion that the company did not measure.

The industrial record also reflects market fluctuations. During downturns companies reduced hours or employment, cut wages, or altered product lines. Small Korean-owned mills were sometimes especially vulnerable because they could not obtain inexpensive long-term financing. Readers should examine production statistics by commodity rather than treat aggregate factory numbers as proof that every branch of manufacturing prospered throughout the period.

Section 58.29

Rice Mills, Fisheries, Food Processing, Forestry, and Commercial Trade

Rice mills and warehouses clustered at ports and rail junctions, processing and grading grain for export. Fisheries expanded, with Japanese and Korean fishers working coastal waters, and fish processing and canning developed in some areas. In Jeju, women divers' cooperative-based fishing was an important economic activity, and in the early 1930s divers protested against fishery association practices, an episode that shows how commercial fishing could generate local conflict.

Forestry in northern river valleys supplied timber, floated down the Amnok and other rivers to mills and ports, and paper and pulp enterprises used these resources. Food processing, brewing, and trade moved commodities among farms, factories, cities, Japan, and Manchuria through ports, shipping lines, and warehouses.

Resource extraction had regional consequences. Forest cutting, fishing intensification, and rail-linked commerce changed local economies and ecologies, and benefits were unevenly shared among local residents, Korean merchants, and Japanese firms.

Milling, fisheries, forestry, and food processing made up a substantial share of the commercial economy, but they connected to very different ecologies. Rice mills near port railheads processed crops from inland landlords and tenants, while seafood processing depended on fishing seasons, cold storage, coastal vessels, and access to wholesale markets. Forestry combined locally used timber and fuel with commercial extraction from wooded northern areas. Food-processing establishments ranged from small family businesses to larger mechanized plants, making aggregate statistics difficult to interpret without scale categories.

These activities carried environmental costs. Commercial timber cutting could alter watersheds and undermine customary collection rights; industrial fishing changed the pressure on stocks and could displace smaller fishing households; industrial mills and processing works created waste and demands for water and energy. Environmental consequences should be approached through documented concessions, catch or harvest records, and local complaints rather than assumed from contemporary norms.

An integrated commodity chain makes the social implications concrete. The value generated by an export shipment accrued successively to a producer, broker, processor, warehouse operator, railway, shipping firm, and importer. Different stages were financed in different ways and subject to different risks. Comparing the share of revenue retained at each stage offers a better account of colonial commerce than a simple assertion that the export value represented profit to Korea as a whole.

Section 58.30

The 1930s Industrialization Drive: Depression, Manchuria, and Imperial Economic Strategy

Several changes after 1929 reshaped Korea's economic role. The depression and the end of Korea's rice expansion for Japan, Japan's departure from the gold standard in 1931 and the weaker yen, and the occupation of Manchuria that year created conditions for new investment. Korea became positioned between Japan and a new imperial frontier.

Under Governor-General Ugaki Kazushige, from 1931 to 1936, the government encouraged industrial development, including support for power generation, mining, and manufacturing, and the idea of Korea as part of a regional economic system linking Japan, Korea, and Manchuria. Japanese corporations invested in northern Korea, where hydroelectric potential and mineral resources lay, and military planners valued Korea's location and resources.

Mining and manufacturing grew rapidly through the 1930s, and by some official measures the value of industrial output came to rival or exceed agricultural output by the end of the decade. Growth was concentrated in specific regions and sectors, and the chronological break from the 1920s should be kept clear: the rice program and the industrial drive had different logics.

Industrial priorities changed in the early 1930s because several processes converged. The Depression damaged commodity markets and businesses relying on agricultural exports. Japan's seizure of Manchuria in 1931 and establishment of Manchukuo in 1932 altered strategic transport routes and industrial investment calculations. The yen-bloc framework and Japanese military planning encouraged power generation, chemicals, metals, and transport infrastructure across Korea and Manchuria. Governor-General Ugaki Kazushige's approach was often described as parallel agricultural and industrial development, but the two sides did not receive equal political influence or benefits.

A refinery or fertilizer plant did not appear simply because officials pronounced an industrialization policy. It required engineering surveys, water or energy concessions, equipment purchases, imported expertise, finance, recruited workers, and a predictable buyer. Those conditions explain the concentration of investment in corridors with ports and rail lines, and in northern locations with hydropower and raw materials. The availability of colonial administrative support reduced some business risks while leaving others, particularly worker safety and community disruption, borne locally.

The chronology should distinguish pre-1937 industrial investment from the later war-directed economy. A factory constructed in the early 1930s might sell fertilizers for agriculture or chemicals for civilian use, then be drawn increasingly into strategic production after the outbreak of full-scale war in China. The same asset could change economic function without changing physical location. That shift is central to evaluating both industrial progress and wartime dependency.

Historical Vocabulary
Industrialization, capital formation, wages, and real income

Definition. Industrialization is the growth of factory and mechanized production relative to agriculture. Capital formation is the building of productive assets such as factories, power plants, and railways. Nominal wages are money amounts; real income adjusts for prices.

Historical context. Colonial statistics reported production and investment in current yen, so interpreting them requires attention to price changes, ownership, and who received income.

Why it matters. They allow precise statements about development: capital formation can rise while real wages stagnate, and industrial output can grow without Korean control of the assets.

Potential misunderstanding. "Industrialization" is not a synonym for "Korean prosperity" or "Korean-controlled development." It names a change in production structure that must be assessed separately from ownership and welfare.

Section 58.31

Hydroelectric Power, Energy Investment, and Industrial Geography

Northern Korea's steep rivers, including the Pujon, Changjin, Heochon, and later the Amnok at Supung, offered hydroelectric potential. Japanese companies obtained water-power concessions and built dams and transmission lines, creating large electricity supplies for energy-intensive industries such as chemicals, metals, and electrochemical manufacturing.

The geography of power determined the geography of industry. Chemical and metallurgical plants clustered in the northeast and along the northern coast where electricity, minerals, and ports were available, while the south remained more agricultural and commercial. Transmission lines also carried power south, so regions were interdependent.

Electrification served industry far more than households. Most rural Korean households had no electric service, and much power served industrial and military purposes. Dam construction also displaced local communities and required large labor forces, topics discussed in later sections.

Hydroelectric power turned particular river basins into major industrial investment sites. Developers had to acquire water rights, survey river gradients, finance reservoirs and tunnels, construct transmission lines, and negotiate the siting of chemical works that would use large amounts of electricity. The difference between installed generating capacity and actual electricity delivered matters: drought, reservoir management, transmission losses, and changing industrial demand affected utilization. An impressive installed-capacity number cannot by itself establish a continuous power supply.

The Changjin and Pujon river systems in northern Korea became important to the Hungnam-related industrial network, while later large projects extended the scale of generation. These names should be attached to dated company and engineering reports rather than loosely listed as examples of all power built in the 1920s. Electrical investment served energy-intensive fertilizer, chemical, and metallurgy plants especially well; nearby towns might gain power, but pricing and distribution could leave ordinary households less connected than industrial consumers.

Hydropower was not environmentally or socially costless. Reservoir construction changed river flows and land use, and transmission corridors required rights of way. Projects could move laborers temporarily or displace communities, with outcomes that require local evidence. Comparing the capital costs and industrial energy consumption with rural electrification or household access would reveal the economic priorities of colonial power development more clearly than celebrating the dams' engineering scale alone.

Section 58.32

Hungnam and the Chōsen Nitrogen Fertilizer Company

Hungnam on the northeastern coast became the site of the best-known industrial complex of colonial Korea. The industrialist Noguchi Shitagau, whose enterprises had begun in Japan, developed hydroelectric projects in the northeast, forming a hydroelectric venture in 1926 (with the name given variously in English sources), and established the Chōsen Nitrogen Fertilizer Company in May 1927.

The distinction between company formation and factory operation matters. The company's formation in 1927 preceded the construction and commissioning of its large facilities, and, according to company chronologies, ammonium sulfate production began only in January 1930, after the plant was built. The complex then expanded to include other chemical products over the following years, and its growth was tied to the cheap electricity of the northern dams.

Nitrogen fertilizer links industry to agriculture: it supplied products that Korean farmers bought, often on credit, as rice programs promoted commercial fertilizer, as described in Section 58.16. Hungnam shows the 1930s pattern of Japanese capital, northern power, and chemical production, with later expansion toward military-related output.

Hungnam's importance extends beyond one company. The complex combined hydroelectric supply, electrolytic and chemical processes, port facilities, and rail connections, so that fertilizer, and later other chemical products, could be made on a large scale and shipped. A planned industrial town grew around it, drawing workers from the surrounding provinces and from farther afield, and its housing, wages, and working conditions are part of the story told in Division VII.

Two cautions apply. First, the complex was integrated into Japanese corporate and imperial networks: capital, technology, and senior management were largely Japanese, and output served markets in Japan and Korea and, later, military needs. Second, its dependence on northern power and imports made it vulnerable when war and division disrupted those links after 1945, a theme taken up in Section 58.56. Hungnam therefore illustrates industrialization in Korea without an autonomous Korean-controlled industrial economy.

The Hungnam industrial complex illustrates the linkage of financial organization, water power, and electrochemical process. Company formation took place in May 1927, while substantial fertilizer production began around 1930 after plant construction, equipment installation, and the securing of electric power. The associated Japanese business network led by Noguchi Shitagau treated the location as a system: northern rivers supplied energy, the coastal industrial site permitted transport and expansion, and specialized electrical processes converted energy and chemical inputs into industrial products. The opening of a corporation and the first commercial output are distinct milestones and must be dated separately.

The scale of an electrochemical plant created a new local labor market. Construction workers assembled plant buildings and infrastructure; engineers and technicians installed machinery; operators maintained dangerous equipment; service workers handled transport, housing, supplies, and sanitation. Management often drew on Japanese expertise, while Korean workers entered varied positions under unequal conditions. Industrial employment could support urban growth without granting local residents control over finance, patents, or executive decisions. A factory-town history must include housing, labor discipline, and the costs of hazardous production, not only tons of fertilizer.

The case also tests the meaning of a colonial industrial legacy. Power stations, buildings, and industrial knowledge physically existed in northern Korea in 1945, yet their operation depended on spare parts, technicians, markets, financing, and imperial shipping connections that the end of Japanese rule disrupted. It would be misleading either to deny the existence of the industrial assets or to treat their presence as proof of an autonomous Korean industrial system ready to operate unchanged.

Section 58.33

Coal, Iron Ore, Gold, and the Expansion of Colonial Mining

Mining expanded under mining regulations favoring concession holders, with Japanese investment prominent. Coal, iron ore, gold, and other minerals each had different markets and conditions. Gold mining grew in the 1930s with the rise in gold prices after Japan left the gold standard, and the American-owned Unsan concession in the northwest, long operating, was sold to Japanese interests in 1939.

Coal supplied industry and railways, and iron ore from northern deposits served steelmaking in Korea and Japan. Raw-material extraction differed from manufacturing: much ore and coal left Korea for processing elsewhere. Underground work was dangerous, with accidents, poor ventilation, and long hours, and mining communities were often separate from farming regions, drawing migrants.

Mining shows both growth and extraction. It increased Korean wage employment and exports, while profits flowed largely to Japanese owners and workers faced hazardous conditions, as Division VII describes.

The geological and commercial character of each mineral determined the pattern of extraction. Coal mines supplied fuel for transport, cities, and industry; iron ore entered iron and steel supply chains; gold extraction involved different finance and refining arrangements. Mines required land or mineral concessions, underground development, pumping and ventilation, rail or cart transport, and access to buyers. Some locations could be worked seasonally or with limited mechanization, while major underground workings demanded much larger capital and organized labor.

The workers' environment was often dangerous. Flooding, poor ventilation, dust, blasting accidents, and long shifts threatened health. Recruitment networks brought laborers from farming households and sometimes from distant provinces. A mining village might combine wage income with agricultural sidelines, company housing, shops, and debt to merchants. A company's production report could omit the injury rate or the families supported by underground wages. Labour and safety records are needed beside geological statistics.

The meaning of mineral extraction also varied by ownership and geography. Japanese corporate firms could control concessions and sales even while local Koreans performed most work. But some Korean entrepreneurs and merchants supplied services, invested in smaller mines, or handled transport. A credible comparison uses a named mine's output, ownership, employment, and safety records over several years instead of applying one national narrative indiscriminately to coal, iron, and precious metals.

Section 58.34

Steel, Chemicals, Fertilizers, Cement, and the Structure of Heavy Industry

Capital-intensive manufacturing grew in the 1930s: chemicals and fertilizers, metallurgy and steel, cement, and related industries. Plants relied on imported equipment and technical specialists, mostly Japanese, and on Japanese capital. Military procurement increasingly shaped demand as war expanded.

These enterprises must be distinguished from raw-material extraction: they processed inputs into manufactured goods. They also employed large numbers of Korean workers, many in unskilled and semi-skilled positions, while supervisory and technical posts were more often held by Japanese. Over time some Koreans gained technical skills, though the opportunities were unequal.

Heavy industry created physical capacity, trained workers, and built infrastructure, and it also remained dependent on imperial supply chains, which affected its viability after 1945.

Heavy industry developed through linked production systems rather than isolated factory openings. The chemicals sector needed electricity, water, feedstocks, storage, specialized equipment, and trained operators; cement required quarrying, fuel, kilns, and bulk transport; metallurgy needed ore, coke or other fuel, and a market able to absorb substantial output. A port or rail spur therefore could determine whether a technically feasible facility was economically viable. The geographic concentration of these preconditions explains why southern commercial districts and northern heavy-industrial districts followed different trajectories.

Japanese investment and colonial support helped assemble the capital required. Such plants often acquired technological advantages over smaller Korean-owned enterprises, but they also depended on distant suppliers for machinery and on imperial markets for output. Corporate group structures determined where profits were booked and how management decisions were made. To evaluate industrialization properly, students should distinguish plant capacity, actual production, value added, employment, and paid-in capital; none of those variables can be inferred from the size of a factory building.

The consequences for local society were mixed and uneven. Industrial development brought wage jobs and technical occupations, attracted migrants, and increased demand for housing, water, and transport. It also brought industrial accidents, air and water pollution, and employer controls over work and sometimes housing. Since verified plant-level figures are scarce, this section should introduce the categories and documented patterns without inventing a production tonnage or a comparative salary. A named corporate account, inspection report, and local newspaper together would permit a more precise assessment than a celebratory or condemnatory generalization.

Section 58.35

Railways, Ports, Transport Networks, and the Integration of Industrial Regions

Railways were built from the turn of the century: the Gyeongin line in 1899 and the main north–south and northwest lines in the early 1900s, with the Honam and Gyeongwon lines in the 1910s, and the Hamgyeong line linking the northeast by the late 1920s. A rail bridge across the Amnok connected Korea to Manchuria. A ferry connected Busan with Japan.

Transport integrated markets. Rail carried rice to ports, ore and coal to plants and harbors, and manufactured goods to markets, and ports such as Busan, Incheon, Gunsan, Hungnam, and Cheongjin handled overseas trade. The system tied northeastern industrial areas, western transportation corridors, and southern ports to Japan and Manchuria.

Focus here is on economic flows, not urban architecture, discussed in Chapter 57. The network was designed largely to move goods and troops between Japan, Korea, and Manchuria, and fewer local roads served village needs.

Transport infrastructure had both physical and institutional components. Rail lines connected mines, factories, inland grain regions, and coastal ports; harbors needed dredging, cranes, storage, customs facilities, and regular vessels; road construction connected production sites to railheads. Freight rates, railway timetables, and access to bonded storage influenced which producers could profitably serve distant markets. The network made it possible for northern minerals or chemicals to move toward Japan and Manchuria while rice shipments departed from western and southern ports.

The economic value of a railway is not exhausted by traffic volume. A railway might carry locally useful passengers and freight while primarily serving the strategic movement of troops or imperial resources. A branch line could raise nearby land values and attract firms, yet bypass villages without sufficient purchasing power. Freight concessions or priority allocations could favor large shippers. During war, civilian access could be subordinated to military needs even when total tonnage remained high.

A map study should therefore overlay dated railway alignments, port capacities, major mines, power facilities, and industrial plants. Using a single undated map of the whole colonial period would conceal the changing network after 1931 and 1937. The important relationship is the co-development of energy, transport, finance, and industrial location—not simply the number of kilometers of track laid.

Section 58.36

Northern and Southern Industrial Development, Environmental Costs, and Economic Dependence

By the early 1940s much of Korea's electricity, mining, and heavy industry lay in the northern provinces, while the south remained centered on agriculture, commerce, and selected manufacturing, including textiles around Seoul and Busan. The division was not absolute: there were northern farms and southern factories. But the pattern shaped economic interdependence, as the north needed food and the south needed power and industrial inputs.

Industry had environmental costs, including pollution from chemical and metallurgical plants, forest cutting, mining waste, and river modification, and affected local communities. Large enterprises were also only partly integrated into local Korean economic activity, since much machinery, capital, and management came from Japan and output flowed outward.

The result was substantial industrial capacity without an autonomous Korean industrial economy. The distinction between industrialization occurring in Korea and Korean-controlled industrial development is central to the debate that follows.

The phrase “northern industry, southern agriculture” describes a broad tendency, not two self-contained economies. Northern districts contained important hydropower, mining, and heavy-chemical complexes, but also large agricultural and fishing populations. Southern districts included commercial factories, ports, mills, textile enterprises, and industrial labor. Both parts depended on networks crossing regional and international boundaries: northern factories required food and transport; southern exporters required shipping, credit, machinery, and markets.

Regional concentration mattered greatly in 1945. Large installations could be inherited by the state controlling their territory, but trade relations, spare parts, trained workers, and administrative systems did not necessarily survive political change. The location of generating stations relative to factories and transmission lines could produce powerful interregional dependencies. Later political division made those dependencies more difficult to manage; the wartime industrial geography did not, however, determine division or subsequent state policy by itself.

Environmental accounting should include downstream effects of dams, industrial effluents, air pollution, mine waste, and changes to coastal production systems where evidence survives. Those impacts were not evenly distributed among owners, managers, workers, and residents. Economic growth measured through gross factory output can coexist with unpriced environmental losses. This is one reason that a chapter about industrialization must retain a household and regional perspective alongside its company histories.

Historical Debate III

Did Japanese Industrialization Create an Independent Economic Foundation for Korea?

The question: Did the industrial expansion of the colonial period provide a foundation for later Korean economic development, or was it too dependent on Japanese capital and markets to do so?

Interpretation A — Foundation: Some scholars point to physical capital, infrastructure, and skills acquired by Korean workers and managers as inheritances that mattered later.
Interpretation B — Dependency: Others stress Japanese ownership and management, the integration of plants into imperial supply chains, and the disruption caused by 1945 and division.
Interpretation C — Both, depending on the sector and what is meant by foundation: Others hold that some assets and skills persisted while the structure collapsed, and that conclusions depend on definitions.

Evidence: Industrial statistics, ownership records, workforce data, and postliberation accounts.

Points of disagreement: Disagreement concerns definitions of foundation and the weight given to continuity versus rupture; it also concerns evidence about skills and management.

Why it matters: It affects how colonial industry is linked to later developments, discussed from Chapter 65 onward.

Section 58.37

The Formation of a Colonial Industrial Working Class

Wage employment expanded in factories, mines, railways, ports, construction, and processing industries, especially from the 1920s and rapidly in the 1930s. Many workers came from rural households squeezed by debt and tenancy, and some moved seasonally between farm and wage work, returning to villages in busy farming periods. Over time more became permanent wage earners.

Occupational specialization grew. Dockworkers, rail workers, miners, textile operatives, rubber workers, and construction laborers developed distinct working conditions and communities, often in company housing or in neighborhoods near plants and ports. Workers in port cities and mining towns formed networks that supported later organizing.

The working class remained a minority of the population, since the economy was still largely agricultural, but its significance exceeded its size, because it concentrated people in sites where collective action was possible and where colonial and corporate power was most visible.

The category “industrial worker” concealed several patterns of employment. A permanent mechanic in a large chemical plant, a young textile operative in a dormitory, a dock porter hired through an intermediary, and a miner recruited for a distant concession all depended on wages, but differed in skills, stability, bargaining power, housing, and exposure to risk. Korean economic change created opportunities to earn cash outside farming while often reproducing inequality between skilled and unskilled workers and between employees of different ethnic backgrounds.

Many workers retained ties to rural households. Men and women moved seasonally between fields and factories, sent remittances to parents, or returned home during unemployment. In ports and construction, hiring could be mediated through supervisors who organized crews and deducted fees. In larger factories, recruitment and dormitory life brought a different kind of discipline. A count of factory employees at the end of one year therefore does not capture the full population whose livelihood depended partly on wage labor.

Understanding class formation requires more than occupational totals. Workers shared crowded housing, confronted supervisors, joined education circles or mutual-aid groups, and sometimes participated in labor unions. Yet language, province of origin, gender, occupation, and job security could divide employees as well as unite them. The emergence of collective labor action, discussed below, was a historical development that organizers had to achieve rather than an automatic outcome of working beside machines.

Section 58.38

Recruitment, Wages, Working Hours, Contracts, and Employment Hierarchies

Workers were recruited through labor brokers, personal networks, company agents, and direct hiring at factory gates. Contracts were often informal, hours long, and dismissal easy. In construction, mining, and docks, labor bosses or contractors often stood between workers and employers, taking a share of pay.

Official wage surveys often report that Korean workers earned substantially less than Japanese workers. Interpreting these gaps requires care. Differences in skill, age, sex, industry, location, and length of service all affect wages, and Japanese workers were concentrated in skilled and supervisory jobs. Comparisons are most meaningful when occupationally matched. At the same time, institutional discrimination in hiring, promotion, and pay scales was real, and is documented in several sources. An aggregate gap alone cannot apportion causes among these factors.

Employment hierarchies placed Japanese managers and technicians above Korean skilled workers and above unskilled laborers, with gender adding another layer. Understanding these hierarchies requires disaggregated evidence, which the following feature illustrates.

Colonial wage comparisons must align comparable categories. An average Japanese employee might be disproportionately represented among engineers, supervisors, and skilled technicians, while Korean employees were concentrated in less secure or lower-paid positions. A raw difference between all Japanese and all Korean earnings thus combines discrimination with occupational segregation and differences in seniority, gender, and hours. Occupational segregation itself could reflect structural barriers to schooling and promotion. Equalizing the categories for statistical comparison should not erase those institutional causes.

Payment systems added further complexity. Some workers were paid by day, others by task, piece rate, or monthly salary; housing and food deductions could reduce cash received. Casual hiring meant that a high advertised daily rate did not guarantee a stable monthly income. Employers could require unpaid waiting time or prolong shifts during peak production. A real-wage calculation must compare pay to the price of staple food, lodging, fuel, and other necessities in the worker's actual town and year.

The National Institute of Korean History's labor source collection documents textile women earning far less than Japanese adults in some comparable settings, with long shifts and inadequate ventilation. Such examples are strong evidence of particular workplaces, not a universal ratio applicable to every colony-wide occupation and year. A final wage table should identify the source date, industry, gender, age, pay unit, and whether compensation before or after deductions was reported. The central historical issue is the structure that allocated skilled jobs and income, not simply one headline disparity.

Section 58.39

Women, Children, and Young Workers in the Industrial Economy

Women made up a large share of workers in textile and silk factories, rubber shoe plants, and food processing. Many were young, recruited from rural households facing debt, and some lived in company dormitories with limited freedom of movement. Wages for women were generally lower than for men.

Factory legislation and regulations existed but were unevenly enforced. Child labor appears in contemporary reports, and formal rules about age and hours differed from practice. Household economic pressure often shaped decisions to send daughters to work, and earnings sometimes supported siblings or paid family debts.

Women also acted collectively. A well-documented case is that of Kang Ju-ryong, a worker at the Pyongwon rubber factory in Pyongyang. In May 1931, after a strike against an announced wage cut was broken up by police, she climbed onto the roof of the Eulmildae pavilion and publicly protested on behalf of her fellow workers. Accounts report that the wage cut was withdrawn, although she herself lost her job. Women's labor was both essential to light industry and vulnerable, and an account that treats workers as male misses much of the industrial workforce.

Girls and young women entered textile and other factories through arrangements shaped by household need, recruiters, and employer housing. Some earned wages that could supplement a family's agricultural income or support their own living expenses, but the possibility of saving depended on regular pay, food and lodging deductions, and the freedom to change jobs. Dormitory rules regulated movement, visiting, and sometimes correspondence. Accounts of this work must distinguish formal contracts, actual supervision, and the voices of the employees themselves.

Age categories in colonial labor records may not match present-day definitions of child labor, and the distinction matters. A teenager could be classified separately from an adult while working a long industrial shift; younger children might help in family workshops without appearing in a factory survey. An increase in girls' schooling and an increase in female factory labor were not necessarily competing developments: different families, regions, and social groups had access to different options. The connection between schooling, early marriage, and wage work requires attention to class as well as gender.

Women's organizing and cultural visibility should not be reduced to the New Woman discourse treated in Chapter 57. Most wage-earning women did not possess the education or income of prominent magazine contributors. Their economic choices were shaped by parents, employers, household obligations, and sometimes collective workplace resistance. To reconstruct their position, compare payrolls, dormitory rules, factory inspection reports, letters, interviews, and press accounts of industrial disputes, with the limitations of each source identified.

Section 58.40

Factory Discipline, Industrial Accidents, Occupational Disease, and Worker Welfare

Work schedules were long, supervision strict, and fines and docked pay common. Machinery and poor ventilation produced accidents and occupational illness, such as respiratory disease in textile and mining work. Sanitation and access to medical care were limited, and compensation for injury was weak or absent.

Some companies provided housing, dormitories, schools, or welfare schemes. These could improve conditions, especially for workers who otherwise had no housing. They could also increase managerial control by tying residence and benefits to employment and by limiting workers' independent organizing.

Inspection and regulation existed on paper, but evidence of how often they were applied is limited. Accident and health data must be read with caution, since underreporting is likely and mortality in dangerous industries is hard to measure.

Industrial workplaces created hazards through machinery, chemicals, underground excavation, heavy lifting, and inadequate ventilation. Accidents could occur when employers reduced training, lengthened shifts, or neglected maintenance; chronic illness could result from dust, fumes, extreme heat, and crowding. The number of incidents recorded by an inspector depended on inspection coverage and on whether employers or workers reported injuries. A seemingly low official accident rate could reflect undercounting as much as superior safety.

Factory discipline encompassed fines, attendance rules, quotas, searching, dismissal, and restrictions on leaving company housing. Employers sometimes offered food, dormitories, clinics, or other welfare facilities. These provisions could improve living conditions for particular workers, yet also bind employees to company stores, deduct money from wages, or facilitate surveillance. Whether a dormitory was experienced as protection or control depended on its actual rules, the possibility of moving elsewhere, and the worker's household circumstances.

A grounded industrial-safety study should compare two different workplaces—such as a textile mill and a mine—because risks and reporting procedures were distinct. It should state the inspection year, workforce size, worker categories, and recorded injuries, then ask what remains invisible. Survivor accounts and compensation claims may reveal hardship absent from corporate production summaries, though their own retrospective and legal contexts must be considered. The economic cost of production includes injury and illness even when those costs are excluded from a firm's balance sheet.

Section 58.41

Labor Associations, Dockworkers, Transport Workers, and Collective Bargaining

From 1920, labor mutual aid societies and unions emerged, and federations were formed during the 1920s. Port and transport workers were prominent, since they were concentrated and their work was essential to commerce. The Busan dockworkers' strike of 1921 is among the best-known early large actions. Organizations demanded wage increases, shorter hours, and recognition, and used strike funds and mutual aid.

Employers responded with dismissals, replacement labor, and appeals to police, and the Peace Preservation Law, extended to Korea in 1925, gave authorities tools against organizations judged subversive. Labor organizations also had links to broader political movements, and ideological splits shaped their development. The party and organizational history of those links belongs to Chapter 59.

The economic point is that collective bargaining was difficult. Workers had limited legal protection, employers had strong backing, and the labor market was flooded by rural migrants, which weakened bargaining power. Yet workplace organization developed and left records through disputes.

Labor organization grew in a social landscape where workers were often recruited through intermediaries rather than hired directly by one employer. Dockhands, railway workers, construction crews, and other casual laborers confronted variable workloads and sometimes fees charged by crew supervisors. Mutual-aid groups and educational associations offered starting points for collective organization; unions developed committees, negotiated wages, organized membership dues, and sometimes coordinated disputes across workplaces. The legal tolerance for these activities remained conditional under colonial policing.

The 1921 Busan dock and transport workers' strike is a useful earlier case. Korean historical accounts describe thousands of participants and a dispute lasting roughly fifteen days, with wage increases among the reported results. The event demonstrates that organized labor action preceded the better-known Wonsan general strike and that commercial ports were important centers of bargaining. A precise reconstruction should distinguish a port-wide work stoppage from the number of workers enrolled in associations and verify which employers signed settlement terms.

Collective bargaining in such a setting was not purely a matter of negotiating with a private employer. Police intervention, restrictions on assemblies, the position of Japanese commercial interests, and racialized occupational hierarchies shaped each outcome. A strike could be economically motivated while also challenging unequal authority. The detailed ideological history of organized socialism belongs to Chapter 59; here the emphasis is on wages, hiring systems, employer strategy, and the costs of maintaining solidarity.

Section 58.42

The Wonsan General Strike of 1929

The strike began as the culmination of earlier conflict. In September 1928 a dispute at an oil company in the Munpyeong district of Wonsan involved a worker and a Japanese supervisor and escalated into a labor conflict. The Wonsan Labor Federation, an organization of dock, transport, and other workers in the port, became involved, and negotiations with employers failed to settle the demands.

In January 1929 the federation called a general strike. It lasted roughly eighty days and involved approximately two thousand workers, according to standard accounts (some sources give about 2,200), making it widely regarded as the largest strike of the colonial period. Employers used strikebreakers, police intervened, and arrests and pressure weakened the strike. Workers' demands concerned wages, treatment, and recognition of the union. Support came from other labor groups and from fundraising elsewhere in Korea. The workers' consumer cooperative, founded in 1922, had stored food in anticipation of a long strike, which helped sustain strikers' families for a time.

The strike ended in April 1929 without achieving its main demands, as the difficulties of sustaining a prolonged shutdown became overwhelming. It became a defining event in Korean labor history because of its scale and duration, the solidarity it generated, and the demonstration of both workers' organizational capacity and the power of employers and police. It should be reconstructed from its chronology and demands, not described as a spontaneous reaction to one incident.

The strike's organization deserves attention. The Wonsan Labor Federation brought together workers from several trades in a port where docks, transport, and warehouse work were closely linked, so stopping one group affected the others. This gave the federation leverage that a single workplace union would have lacked. It also meant that a prolonged strike put heavy pressure on the families of workers, who had little savings, and relief funds and donations from other labor groups became essential.

Employers and authorities had their own resources: substitute labor from other places, police arrests, and the ability to wait. As weeks passed, shortages of funds and the arrest or exhaustion of leaders made continuation harder. Reconstructing the strike therefore means following three interacting pressures, the union's demands and solidarity, the employers' replacement of labor, and police intervention, rather than treating its outcome as inevitable or as the result of any one decision. Its political and ideological significance for the labor movement is considered in Chapter 59.

The immediate trigger for the Wonsan dispute can be dated to September 7, 1928, when a Japanese supervisor at the Munpyeong Rising Sun Oil facility assaulted Korean workers, according to the National Institute of Korean History's reconstruction. Local stoppages followed. The Wonsan Labor Federation pressed for accountability and better treatment, but the promised resolution did not materialize. By January 1929 the conflict had broadened into a coordinated general strike by workers across Wonsan's port and allied industries. The underlying grievance was thus not only an act of violence: it included the credibility of collective bargaining and the employer's willingness to negotiate with an organized workforce.

The federation's organization mattered. Its affiliated unions could mobilize across occupations, gather contributions, maintain food stocks, distribute information, and seek support beyond Wonsan. A consumer cooperative helped sustain workers during a prolonged loss of wages. Employers and allied organizations responded with alternative labor recruitment, pressure to return to work, and support for rival bodies. Police arrests increased the cost of continuing the stoppage. These tactics produced a contest over who could survive the interruption of commerce longer.

The strike did not achieve all the union's central demands. Repression and exhaustion culminated in a decision to permit a return to work on April 6, 1929, with the federation's office closing later that month. Its immediate organizational defeat should be distinguished from its wider influence on labor politics and worker consciousness. Historians can evaluate the strike from employer correspondence, union demands, newspapers, and police files, each of which counted participants and justified actions differently. Describing it solely as either triumphant resistance or futile disruption would miss that dual economic and organizational history.

Section 58.43

Industrial Conflict during the Depression and the 1930s

During the depression, wage cuts and unemployment prompted strikes, including disputes among Pyongyang rubber workers and among mining workers in the north. The 1930s also brought greater industrial employment, particularly in the northern industrial regions, so the geography of labor conflict shifted northward.

Labor statistics must be read carefully. Strike counts and numbers of workers involved reflect what authorities recorded, and underground or suppressed organizing is less visible. Police repression, the Peace Preservation Law, and corporate power increased through the 1930s, and open organizing became more dangerous, so a decline in recorded strikes does not necessarily mean contentment.

By the late 1930s, wartime controls placed workplaces under tighter state supervision, ending most open independent labor activity, as Division IX describes.

Economic conflict in the early 1930s reflected both declining prices and the new concentrations of industrial labor. Workers faced wage reductions, layoffs, irregular hours, and employers seeking to contain costs. Some disputes focused on a single establishment while others involved wider occupational groups. In northern industrial towns, company control over housing and recruitment added to the power imbalance between management and employees. Official strike statistics can identify the number of recorded conflicts, but they cannot fully reveal informal stoppages, abandoned complaints, or negotiations held before police recorded an incident.

The 1931 rooftop protest of Kang Ju-ryong at Eulmildae in Pyongyang gives the history an identifiable individual case. Her action, connected with a rubber-workers' dispute, made workers' claims dramatically visible beyond the immediate factory. It should be read alongside the grievances and collective organization of the workers involved, rather than portrayed only as an isolated heroic gesture. The contemporary response also reveals the limits imposed on Korean women workers seeking public bargaining power.

National historical tables show changes in the number of strikes and participants during the later 1930s and early 1940s, but restrictions on organizing grew as wartime mobilization expanded. Lower official strike totals might therefore reflect an inability to organize openly rather than universal contentment. The chapter should distinguish disputes about wages, workplace treatment, and employment security and link them to the economic conditions of specific industries.

Section 58.44

Rural-to-Urban Migration and the Movement of Workers within Korea

Rural poverty, land insecurity, and the availability of wage work moved people toward Seoul (then Gyeongseong), Pyongyang, Hungnam, Wonsan, Busan, and other centers. Industrial recruiters, rail connections, and information from earlier migrants shaped movement. Decisions were often made at household level, with one or more members sent to work while others stayed.

Arrival brought difficulties. Housing was scarce, and many migrants lived in makeshift dwellings or crowded rooms at the edge of cities (see Chapter 57). Work was often irregular day labor, and wages did not always cover costs. Some returned to villages, others settled permanently.

Internal migration changed both sending and receiving places: it relieved some rural pressure while swelling urban poverty, and it supplied labor to northern mines and factories.

Rural-to-urban movement involved calculated decisions under conditions of insecurity. A land-poor family might send a son to a port city because rent and debt left too little income, while an educated youth sought clerical or technical employment unavailable in a village. Railways lowered some travel costs, and relatives already employed in a city could provide lodging and introductions to an employer. But arrival did not guarantee work. Migrants often entered crowded rented rooms, informal settlements, or company housing, where poor sanitation and insecure tenancy remained obstacles to stability.

The destination mattered. Seoul offered administrative, retail, and service jobs; Wonsan and Busan connected workers to shipping and transport; northern industrial towns drew construction laborers and later factory and mining workers. Labor demand could be cyclical, and a construction project might absorb workers briefly before dismissing them. Some migrants maintained agricultural claims at home and traveled back for planting or harvest, blurring the category of permanent urban resident.

Census comparisons should specify whether “urban” refers to municipal boundaries, built-up areas, or employment in nonagricultural occupations. Urban boundaries changed, so apparent population growth may partly reflect administrative reclassification. An individual migration case is most useful when it identifies origin, occupation, housing, wages, and return or onward movement, but regional household patterns also reveal how rural poverty and industrial expansion were connected.

Section 58.45

Korean Migration to Japan before the Wartime Mobilization System

Korean communities grew in Osaka, Kobe, Tokyo, Kyoto, and other industrial areas, with migrants working in factories, mines, and construction. Direct sailings, such as the Jeju–Osaka line from the early 1920s, helped link particular origins to particular destinations. Recruitment networks of relatives and fellow villagers guided newcomers, and colonial rules for departure permits and travel certificates sometimes limited movement.

Korean residents in Japan numbered about 419,000 in one 1930 census series and about 1.24 million in a 1940 census series (other administrative counts differ), rising to roughly two million by 1945. These figures use different collection methods and include people who arrived under very different conditions. They faced low-wage employment, crowded housing, discrimination, and violence, including the killings of Koreans after the Kantō earthquake of 1923, whose death toll is estimated from several hundred to several thousand.

Prewar labor migration, driven by poverty, family strategy, and employment opportunities, was constrained but not formally compulsory, and it must be distinguished from the coercive mobilization system introduced in 1939, discussed in Section 58.52.

Migration from Korea to Japan increased long before formal wartime labor conscription. Workers traveled to Osaka, Kobe, Kyoto, Tokyo, and other industrial centers through family and village contacts, recruiters, coastal shipping routes, and wage expectations. Employment opportunities included construction, textiles, ports, mining, and manufacturing. Korean migrants could find cash wages unavailable in their home districts but also faced housing discrimination, precarious work, language barriers, and exclusion from better positions. Some sent remittances home, and others established families and community institutions in Japan.

Careful population measurement is essential. A census-derived series reports approximately 419,009 Koreans in Japan in 1930 and 1,241,315 in 1940; police-registration and other administrative series may produce different totals because of coverage and classification. The roughly two million residents at the end of Japanese rule should not be equated with the number forcibly mobilized. It included earlier migrants, family members, children born in Japan, and people moved under increasingly coercive wartime systems.

The 1923 Kantō earthquake and ensuing anti-Korean mass violence exposed a danger unrelated to ordinary employment markets. False rumors, racial hostility, and failures of protection made Korean residents and visitors vulnerable to attack. The event belongs here as evidence of how insecurity shaped migrant life and community organization; its fuller political history should be treated elsewhere. A migration narrative that reports only wage differentials cannot explain why workers built associations, mutual-aid networks, and campaigns for protection.

Historical Vocabulary
Migration, remittances, recruitment, and compulsory mobilization

Definition. Migration is movement from one place to another for residence or work. Remittances are money sent to family at home. Recruitment is the organized seeking of workers. Compulsory mobilization is state-directed assignment of workers by legal obligation.

Historical context. Before 1939 most movement to Japan was economic, shaped by poverty and opportunity. Beginning in 1939 the state organized labor movements in stages of increasing compulsion.

Why it matters. Precise terms keep distinct experiences distinct and prevent both minimizing coercion and mislabeling earlier migrants.

Potential misunderstanding. Not all Koreans in Japan in 1945 were wartime conscripts, and not all wartime laborers were free to refuse.

Section 58.46

Korean Farmers and Migrant Communities in Manchuria

Korean farmers moved into southern and northeastern Manchuria from the late nineteenth century, and especially after annexation, in search of land and escape from poverty and political pressure. They cultivated rice on newly opened paddy and other crops, often as tenants of Chinese landlords or on newly reclaimed land, with the Jiandao region a major center.

Their position depended on changing political authority. Under Chinese local authorities they faced pressures over citizenship and land access, and conflicts with Chinese neighbors occasionally erupted, as at Wanbaoshan in 1931. After the establishment of Manchukuo in 1932, Japanese authorities promoted settlement and agricultural schemes involving Korean migrants. The number of Koreans in Manchuria reached well over a million by the early 1940s.

This section concerns agricultural migration and household strategies. Nationalist and armed independence organizations active in the same region are treated in Chapter 59, and Manchuria's place in imperial militarization in Chapter 61.

Korean households crossed into Manchuria for reasons that combined land hunger, kinship, commercial opportunity, and political insecurity. Migrants leased or purchased land where circumstances permitted, reclaimed fields, worked for established Korean residents, and joined markets connected to Korean border towns. Their legal status was complicated by competing Chinese, Japanese, and Korean claims, and by the changing authority of local governments. The occupation of Manchuria in 1931 altered these conditions without making migrants a single homogeneous political group.

The Wanbaoshan incident in 1931 demonstrates how land tenure and water rights could escalate into internationalized conflict. Korean cultivators involved in a disputed irrigation project near Changchun faced objections by local Chinese residents; Japanese officials and newspapers amplified the dispute within a wider imperial confrontation. Subsequent anti-Chinese violence in Korea was part of the crisis. The episode cannot be understood simply as a bilateral ethnic quarrel between two farming communities, because imperial intervention and media reporting changed its scale.

A sound migration study separates households farming for subsistence from merchants, laborers, soldiers, and armed political organizations. Settlement figures also require a geographic definition: “Manchuria,” “Jiandao/Gando,” and a particular province do not cover the same territory. Changes in census jurisdiction and refugee movement complicate long-term totals. Families' economic strategies should be reconstructed through land leases, local tax records, school registers, oral histories, and correspondence where available, not through a presumed single motive.

Section 58.47

Migration to the Russian Far East and the Changing Conditions of Borderland Communities

Korean communities in the Russian Maritime Province dated from the nineteenth century, and included farmers, traders, and laborers, with a Korean quarter in Vladivostok. They farmed rice and other crops and engaged in commerce, and their status changed as Russian and then Soviet policy shifted.

In 1937 Soviet authorities deported ethnic Koreans from the Far East to Central Asia, mainly Kazakhstan and Uzbekistan, a collective ethnic deportation carried out primarily by train in September–October 1937. An NKVD report of October 25 counted 171,781 people; another reported total, including subsequent movements, is 172,597. Soviet authorities justified the operation through fears of Japanese espionage, but the deportation targeted Koreans collectively rather than individuals proved to be spies. This Soviet policy must be clearly distinguished from Japanese-directed migration.

The deportees faced hardship on arrival and rebuilt communities, which later became part of the Korean diaspora in Central Asia. The episode shows that Korean displacement in these decades occurred in more than one empire.

The Russian Maritime Province had a Korean-speaking population with deep roots in farming, fishing, and commerce, and with family connections across the border. Revolution, civil war, and subsequent Soviet consolidation altered property rights and administrative citizenship, while collective agriculture and security policy reshaped the economic circumstances of Korean communities. Residents who had moved over decades should not be treated as recent migrants simply because a later government categorized them ethnically.

In 1937 Stalin's government forcibly deported ethnic Koreans from the Soviet Far East to Central Asia. An October NKVD accounting reported 171,781 people; other cumulative reconstructions give around 172,597. The figures count the implementation of a Soviet state action and must not be merged with the separate Japanese wartime recruitment system. Deportation by train severed farms, livelihoods, and local social networks, and resettlement in Kazakhstan and Uzbekistan imposed severe adaptation costs. The official security rationale did not establish individual wrongdoing.

The case reveals that Korean migration history crossed more than one imperial and state system. Japanese colonial authorities in Korea did not order this Soviet deportation, and migrants' choices before 1937 cannot be inferred from the forced removal that followed. The economic consequences included abandoned property, sudden loss of local markets, disrupted family networks, and the need to rebuild agricultural production in unfamiliar conditions. A fuller primary-source study would compare Soviet deportation instructions, transportation counts, and Korean family testimonies.

Section 58.48

Japanese Settlers, Administrators, Entrepreneurs, and Workers in Korea

Japanese residents in Korea numbered several hundred thousand by the 1930s; a reported May 1944 colonial-government figure was about 713,000, while a September 1945 estimate put the remaining population near 660,000. The wartime and post-surrender counts are not directly interchangeable. This was a small share of the total population, concentrated in cities, ports, and administrative centers. They included officials and police, merchants, farmers, technicians, factory managers, and workers.

Their experiences differed by occupation and class. Officials and managers held authority and high pay, and many settlers lived in separate commercial districts in cities such as Seoul and Busan. Others were small shopkeepers, tradesmen, or farmers with modest resources. Japanese settlement affected employment, as Japanese workers held many skilled and supervisory posts, land markets, where Japanese buyers acquired land, and commercial life.

Settlers should not be treated as homogeneous. Some were privileged agents of colonial rule, and some were ordinary people with limited means. Both facts matter for understanding colonial society and for the abrupt departure of the Japanese population after 1945.

Japanese settlers came to Korea in multiple capacities: officials, soldiers' families, merchants, teachers, financiers, small farmers, railway employees, managers, technicians, and workers. Their experience therefore varied by class and occupation, but colonial institutions generally granted Japanese citizens structural advantages in political authority and access to certain employment and services. Settlement shaped commercial neighborhoods, schooling systems, land markets, and managerial hierarchies in the ports and major cities.

Population figures are highly date-specific. One historical record puts the Japanese civilian resident population near 712,583 in May 1944, while an estimate of people remaining in September 1945 is lower, near 660,000. An older textbook series puts the 1940 resident count around three-quarters of a million, using its own definition. These should not be fused into one supposedly exact value for “1945.” Wartime evacuations, military personnel, administrative classification, and shifting census scopes affected the totals.

A distributional analysis asks where settlers worked and lived and how they interacted with Korean firms and households. A Japanese railway technician did not possess the same capital as a zaibatsu director, yet both might benefit from a higher likelihood of admission to certain institutions. Conversely, competition between Japanese and Korean retailers or between Japanese and Korean smallholders was economically real even within an unequal political system. The collapse of colonial rule in 1945 brought further displacement and property transfers that require their own evidence rather than a simplistic assumption that all Japanese assets passed smoothly to Korean hands.

Section 58.49

Migrant Households, Remittances, Family Separation, and Ethnic Inequality

Migrants faced housing shortages, informal savings arrangements, and community associations that provided mutual aid. Remittances sent home helped pay debts and support families, and letters and visits maintained ties, though distance and cost limited contact. Households were often split, with wives, children, and elders remaining in villages while men or young women worked elsewhere.

Ethnic inequality shaped migrant life. Koreans in Japan encountered discrimination in employment and housing, and in Korea itself Korean workers occupied lower positions in many firms than Japanese colleagues. Migration changed expectations: exposure to urban wages and new goods altered aspirations, and women's wage work shifted household roles.

Migration also changed sending communities, where remittances, absent men, and returnees brought new money and ideas, while some villages lost much of their working-age population.

Migration changed household economies even when only one family member moved. A worker could send cash to parents who used it to pay rent, repay a loan, or keep younger children at school. But remittances were irregular and subject to fees, job loss, illness, and exchange or transfer arrangements. A migrant earning more in Osaka than on a Korean farm might also face higher living costs, unsafe housing, and periods of unemployment. The household benefit therefore cannot be inferred by comparing nominal wages alone.

Family separation affected gender roles and care obligations. Women remaining in villages could assume more agricultural and financial responsibility, while children lived with grandparents or other relatives. Migrant communities developed associations, informal credit networks, religious congregations, shops, and schools to manage hardship and maintain ties. Discrimination in rental housing or employment also shaped where Koreans could live and whether a family could reunite. These were long-term social consequences of movements often initiated as short-term labor strategies.

The Jeju–Osaka corridor offers a valuable regional case because island communities had maritime connections and strong patterns of chain migration. Unless a named individual's oral history or family record is supplied, the feature should remain explicitly a regional/community case study, not a fictional first-person household biography. Students can compare a documented ferry route, census count, and migrant organization with oral histories, acknowledging that each source captures different aspects of movement.

Section 58.50

The Wartime Transformation of the Colonial Economy, 1937–1945

Japan's full-scale war in China from 1937 and the National Mobilization Law of 1938 transformed economic policy. The law, applied in Korea, authorized the government to direct labor, materials, finance, and production by administrative order, reducing the role of markets. Industrial priorities shifted to munitions, metals, chemicals, and transport, and factories were reoriented to military objectives.

Wartime finance relied on state borrowing, savings drives, and expanded note issue, and investment was channeled by allocation rather than by market returns. Capital goods, scarce imports, and fuel were allocated to favored sectors, and civilian consumer industries were curtailed. Korean light industry and small firms were often consolidated or closed.

The war economy deepened the integration of Korea into imperial production while eroding household consumption. It also placed new demands on labor and food, treated in the next sections.

After full-scale war began in China in 1937, colonial economic administration increasingly prioritized military supply. The National Mobilization Law of 1938 enabled the Japanese state to direct the use of labor, production, transport, finance, and essential resources. Its language was broad by design: Article 1 defined the mobilization of human and material capacities for war, and subsequent articles specified categories of goods and work. That framework was translated into many separate regulations, so the enactment of the basic law should not be confused with every later labor order or requisition.

Industrial firms faced changing quotas, procurement arrangements, raw-material allocations, and constraints on civilian output. Railways and ships were tasked with moving strategic goods, creating competition with ordinary grain and passenger transport. Farms supplied food under tighter procurement rules, while workers were increasingly allocated according to state priorities. These changes often accelerated output in selected strategic sectors while reducing the availability of consumer goods and worsening household insecurity.

The analysis should retain a chronological distinction between voluntary-looking recruitment drives, official mediation, compulsory requisition, and later military service. The broad history of forced labor and coercive wartime experience belongs to Chapter 62; Chapter 58 focuses on how state allocation of labor and materials changed economic organization. Economic mobilization was not a single switch thrown in 1938, but a cumulative process in which legal powers, administrative capacity, and military demand interacted.

Section 58.51

Wartime Agriculture, the 1940 Rice Program, and Food Procurement

From 1940 a renewed rice-production program sought to raise output under wartime conditions, distinct from the programs of 1920–1934. It was accompanied by growing government control of grain. Compulsory delivery quotas (gongchul) were assigned to villages and households, distribution was regulated, and rationing in cities limited consumption. Other requisitions, such as metal collection campaigns, also affected households.

Shortages of labor, as men were mobilized, and of fertilizer and equipment hurt agricultural production, even as demands rose. Official production statistics did not equal household food availability, since delivery quotas could leave farm families short, and shortages intensified in the final war years. Prices and black markets reflected scarcity.

Wartime agriculture shows the most direct subordination of rural households to state procurement, with consequences for nutrition, debt, and trust in officials.

The wartime rice-increase program beginning in 1940 should not be conflated with the earlier interwar plans. It sought greater production under conditions of material scarcity and increasingly coercive procurement. Officials promoted double cropping, seed changes, cultivation guidance, and the revival of selected land-improvement works, while fertilizer, machinery, and labor were diverted toward military use. Plans and actual harvests diverged when shortages, weather, and transport disrupted production.

Procurement obligations transformed the market relationship. The Government-General and local authorities assigned delivery requirements through layers of administration, and village-level allocation could make household subsistence contingent on official estimates of a crop. Later wartime rules covered rice and other foodstuffs, tightening controls over marketing and storage. A historical study of the 1943–1944 food regime must distinguish the total harvest, official allocation, delivered quantity, seed requirements, and grain retained by farms. The share requisitioned was not identical to the share exported to Japan.

One scholarly historical series compiled from colonial sources reports severe contraction in quantities retained by farming households in the final war years, though the units and categories of that table require checking before classroom calculation. The evidence also cautions against treating wartime harvest fluctuations as proof of policy success or failure in isolation: weather, labor shortages, collection pressure, and disrupted inputs all mattered. A dated 1943 food-policy directive and a 1944 delivery table would be more valuable than a fabricated “typical village quota.”

Section 58.52

Labor Recruitment, Official Mediation, Conscription, and Coercive Movement of Workers

Wartime labor mobilization proceeded in legally distinct stages. Beginning in 1939, labor for Japan was recruited under government supervision, with companies sending recruiters to Korean villages under official quotas and local authorities pressuring selection. From 1942 an official mediation system (kan-assen) placed the administration directly in charge of assigning workers. In 1944 compulsory labor conscription under the National Requisition Ordinance was broadly applied to Koreans.

These stages matter. Coercion existed before formal conscription: quotas, pressure from officials and police, and limited alternatives meant that many recruits had little real choice, and the early system must not be described as uniformly voluntary. At the same time, the stages differed in legal form and degree of compulsion, and should not be collapsed.

Workers were sent to Japan, Sakhalin, and other places, to mines, factories, and construction sites, and within Korea to similar work. Documented problems include forced savings, withheld wages, accidents, restrictions on movement, and long separation from families. Estimates of the numbers sent to Japan proper range from roughly 590,000 to 800,000 depending on the source and what is counted, and mobilizations within Korea were larger in aggregate, but the figures depend on what was counted: new recruits, border crossings, placements, repeated assignments, or persons employed at a given date. The National Institute of Korean History notes that the overall scale cannot be determined from surviving records. The broader human experience, including other forms of wartime coercion, is examined in Chapter 62.

The difference between the stages can be seen in who made the decisive choice. Under recruitment, company agents operated within quotas approved by the government, and village and police officials often selected or pressed individuals. Under official mediation, the administration itself allocated workers to employers and assumed responsibility for gathering them. Under conscription, refusal carried legal penalties. In each stage the worker's room to refuse narrowed, which is why historians treat the sequence as a progression of compulsion rather than a switch from free to forced.

Conditions at destination also varied. Mining and heavy construction were especially dangerous and typically involved barracks housing, long hours, and tight restriction of movement; some factory placements were less severe. Contracts that nominally promised wages and fixed terms were often extended, and savings were frequently held back or deposited in ways workers could not readily withdraw. Contemporary documents, company ledgers, and later testimony do not always agree, so statements about conditions should specify the site, date, and kind of source. Fuller treatment of individual experience, together with other forms of wartime coercion, belongs to Chapter 62.

The three principal wartime labor-allocation phases must be identified by who controlled recruitment and movement. Beginning in 1939 employers, often with state assistance or supervision, recruited Korean workers for mines, construction, and factories. From 1942 official mediation (kan-assen) placed greater authority in local and colonial administrative hands to fill assigned labor needs. The general application of compulsory requisition to Koreans in 1944 added further formal powers. A description of these categories should not imply that earlier recruiters always respected free consent, or that every worker in Japan had been formally requisitioned.

The National Mobilization Law's Article 4 provided authority to requisition labor under later regulations, but the law itself does not enumerate the entire chain of Korean village recruitment practices. Local officials, police, brokers, and employers could impose pressure through quotas, threats, misrepresentation, or control over travel. Workers at destination encountered wage withholding, compulsory savings, dangerous tasks, barriers to leaving, and difficulty contacting families. Conditions differed across mines, factories, construction sites, and Sakhalin; one testimony should not be treated as a complete census of every worksite.

Estimates of total movement require a careful ledger of scope. Counts of people recruited to Japan, mobilized within Korea, sent to Sakhalin, assigned more than once, or still working in a particular year are not directly additive. Published numerical ranges differ because administrative categories and surviving records differ. Chapter 58 should present the mechanisms and well-grounded examples while referring readers to Chapter 62 for the fuller history of coercion, human loss, return journeys, and postwar claims.

Section 58.53

Shortages, Resource Allocation, Industrial Disruption, and the End of Japanese Rule

In the last war years markets, transport, and civilian production deteriorated. Shipping losses, shortages of fuel and materials, labor drains, and requisition disrupted supply, and inflation rose as note issue expanded. Ordinary consumption fell, and food and cloth were scarce.

Defeat in August 1945 exposed dependence on Japanese imperial administration, capital, shipping, and procurement. Japanese managers and technicians, who held many senior and technical posts, left or were removed, and supply chains and markets collapsed. Electric power produced in the north supplied the south, so political division soon disrupted exchange.

The end of Japanese rule was therefore an economic as well as a political rupture. The immediate postliberation circumstances belong to Chapters 64 and 65.

The final war years subjected the colonial economy to simultaneous constraints. Military demand rose while shipping became more dangerous, machinery repairs grew difficult, and civilian consumption goods were restricted. Procurement and distribution systems had to function with shortages of transport, fuel, fertilizer, and workers. The fact that a factory remained on a production register did not show it was operating near capacity; a railway might have physical track but lack fuel, rolling stock, or available routes to move goods predictably.

Food insecurity varied among social groups. Those holding official allotments or secure employment might have access to controlled distribution, while farmers under delivery quotas, casual workers, and urban migrants confronted different obstacles. Informal markets and exchange could supplement ration systems, but purchasing power and police regulation shaped access. An economic history should examine price controls beside real transaction prices and distinguish official ration entitlements from actual goods received.

August 1945 ended Japanese political authority in Korea but did not instantly repair disrupted supply chains. The collapse of colonial financial and distribution arrangements, the departure of Japanese managers and settlers, and the division of administrative zones complicated business continuity. Equipment, land, and buildings remained, but production needed workers, inputs, credit, and customers. This distinction between physical inheritance and functioning economic institutions leads directly to the questions of the final division.

Section 58.54

Measuring Colonial Economic Change: Production, Prices, Wages, and Living Standards

Historians draw on statistical yearbooks, agricultural surveys, trade records, census reports, industrial statistics, wage data, and household accounts. Each has uses and limits. Several distinctions are essential: nominal versus real income, aggregate versus per-capita output, factory production versus profits, exports versus domestic consumption, and economic growth versus human welfare.

Data have gaps. Definitions and survey methods changed over time, coverage was uneven, and reliability varied. Official sources aimed to demonstrate administration success, and series for the poorest households are sparse. Units such as the seok require conversion assumptions about commodity and date.

Good practice states what a figure measures, who produced it, what it leaves out, and what would corroborate it. No single series can settle whether living standards rose.

Colonial economic statistics are not uniformly unreliable or uniformly transparent. Records of production, trade, investment, population, prices, and wages can be highly useful, but each has a unit of observation and administrative purpose. A factory census might omit small family shops; a labor table may record employees in registered plants but exclude casual porters; a census figure for Koreans in Japan may differ from police-registration totals. A rice table might mix seok, koku, thousand-seok totals, and metric-ton equivalents. Before calculating a percentage, the numerator and denominator must refer to the same series, territory, product, and period.

Consider rice as a worked example. If one source reports 1936 production of 17.88 million seok and outbound shipments of 9.51 million, then shipments equal approximately 53.2 percent of recorded output in that dataset. Another source may identify only shipments specifically to Japan, or use different adjustments for milled versus unmilled rice. The apparent numerical discrepancy may therefore reflect classification instead of a historical contradiction. The same discipline applies to real wages: nominal pay must be divided by an appropriately dated price index before claims about purchasing power can be made.

Distributional questions demand more than aggregates. The average income of a colony can rise while poorer households stagnate; large Japanese capital investment can increase production without transferring ownership to Koreans; and a factory's payroll can increase even if some of its workers' real wages fall. A strong economic textbook presents a figure, the source, the denominator, the uncertainty, and the conclusion that the figure does—and does not—support.

Section 58.55

Colonial Development, Economic Extraction, and the Historiographical Debate

Interpretations of colonial economic development divide broadly. Some emphasize capital formation, modern institutions, infrastructure, and industrial growth, an approach sometimes called colonial modernization. Others emphasize unequal ownership, coercion, exclusion, and the transfer of income abroad, often called extraction or exploitation. A related line of analysis, colonial modernity, examines how modern institutions and colonial power were intertwined.

Each interpretation must specify its evidence and what it means by development. A claim about output growth is different from a claim about Korean welfare or control. The evidence supports substantial growth in several sectors, especially heavy industry after 1930, and it also shows unequal ownership, coercive labor mobilization, and declining food consumption for many households.

This chapter rejects both extremes: the claim that colonialism produced no measurable development, and the claim that measurable development legitimized colonial rule. Legitimacy is a political and moral question that cannot be derived from output statistics.

The debate over colonial development is not resolved by deciding whether “growth” occurred. Some scholars emphasize measured increases in output, modern infrastructure, markets, educational capital, and industrial plant. Others stress unequal asset ownership, the subordination of economic priorities to imperial demands, land and labor coercion, and the limited welfare gains available to many Koreans. These positions may use different measures and time horizons; a statement about gross manufacturing output is not logically equivalent to a statement about household consumption or political legitimacy.

A useful comparison separates at least three questions. First, what happened to the economy's productive capacity? Here factory, electricity, and agricultural statistics are relevant. Second, how were benefits distributed and choices constrained? Here ownership records, land tenure, prices, wages, and the coercive labor systems matter. Third, what role did colonial rule itself play compared with initiatives already underway before annexation and with developments that might have occurred under other political conditions? That counterfactual is difficult to establish and cannot be answered solely by observing that a railroad existed in 1945.

Evaluating the research requires attention to selection and perspective. Colonial officials reported achievements to legitimate their work; nationalist critics highlighted dispossession and suffering; modern economists may reconstruct estimates from incomplete administrative series. Students should compare claims against the evidence each author actually uses, not reduce the controversy to competing declarations of patriotism or economic optimism. The chapter's conclusion should acknowledge both real structural change and the fundamental absence of Korean economic sovereignty.

Section 58.56

The Economic Legacies of Colonial Korea in 1945

By 1945 colonial Korea had an agrarian system marked by tenancy and landholding inequality, industrial installations concentrated in the north, power and transport networks, a wage-earning labor force, business institutions, and large migrant communities abroad. Each had been built within an imperial system.

The geographic unevenness mattered. Heavy industry and electricity were concentrated in the north, while the south held the main rice lands and much of the population, and the two were linked by exchange. Technical and managerial positions had been disproportionately Japanese, so liberation removed expertise as well as owners, and wartime destruction, inflation, and shortages added to difficulties.

These conditions entered a period of occupation and division, discussed from Chapter 64, but did not determine its outcome. The legacy of 1945 was a set of constraints and possibilities, not a predetermined path.

In 1945 Korea possessed irrigated fields, registered property, railways, mines, electrical installations, factories, trained workers, and commercial institutions that had changed substantially since 1910. The distribution of these assets was uneven across regions and social groups. Heavy industry and large generating installations were concentrated in particular northern districts, while many southern districts had extensive agricultural, commercial, and lighter-manufacturing activities. Yet neither half of the peninsula was economically self-sufficient: power, food, markets, transport, capital, and skilled labor crossed those boundaries.

Institutional continuity was more fragile than a map of physical assets suggests. Many large enterprises relied on Japanese management, outside shareholders, technical imports, or imperial procurement. When colonial authority collapsed, the control of banks, company property, trade accounts, and shipping had to be reorganized. Japanese residents and managers departed under different circumstances, while Korean workers and entrepreneurs sought new roles. Land tenure disputes and wartime food controls did not vanish immediately with liberation. The existing capital stock was real, but activating it within a new economic system was an enormous practical problem.

A responsible account does not claim that later division was mechanically caused by the industrial north and agricultural south. Postwar occupation policy, external power politics, institutions, war, and independent decisions shaped the eventual outcomes. The colonial economic inheritance constrained what governments and households could do after 1945, without predetermining every later result. Chapter 58 ends with that relationship between historical structure and contingency, leaving the detailed liberation-era narrative to subsequent chapters.

Chapter Summary

Chapter 58 has examined colonial Korea as an economy within the Japanese empire, governed by institutions in which authority over budgets, currency, property, and enterprise lay with the colonial state and Japanese capital. The chapter's central argument is that measurable economic transformation occurred and that it cannot be evaluated apart from ownership, distribution, and the welfare of Korean households.

The first division established the institutional framework: taxation and public finance, the Bank of Chōsen and credit institutions, and the distinction among ownership, management, finance, and profit. It also stressed that economic modernization had begun before 1910 under the Korean Empire.

The agrarian order rested on a land system created by registration after the survey of 1910–1918, a stratified structure of landlords, owner-cultivators, tenants, and landless laborers, and insecure cultivation rights. Japanese and Korean landlords and the Oriental Development Company all participated, and regions differed sharply.

The rice programs of 1920–1925, 1926–1934, and 1940 onward responded to imperial food needs, and they must be kept distinct. Irrigation, seeds, and fertilizers raised productivity in many districts, and costs and rents shaped who gained. Exports to Japan rose while per-capita consumption of rice fell for many households.

Rural poverty took the form of debt, falling prices after 1929, and tenancy disputes, of which Amtae-do is the best-documented case. The colonial government responded with conciliation and land ordinances in 1932 and 1934 and the Rural Revitalization Movement, which stabilized but did not restructure rural inequality.

Commercial capitalism and manufacturing developed before the 1930s through rice mills, textiles, and small firms, and Korean entrepreneurs such as the founders of Kyungsung Spinning and Weaving operated within real constraints. Repeal of the Company Ordinance in 1920 did not erase disparities of capital and access.

The 1930s brought industrialization tied to depression, Manchuria, hydroelectric power, mining, and chemicals, as at Hungnam. Industry grew substantially, largely under Japanese ownership and within imperial supply chains, which produced industrialization in Korea without an autonomous Korean-controlled industrial economy.

A colonial working class formed, with wage differentials, hazardous conditions, and organized action, including the Wonsan general strike of 1929. Workers, including many women, acted within narrow legal limits and increasing repression.

Migration moved households within Korea and to Japan, Manchuria, and the Russian Far East, shaped by poverty, family strategy, and policy. The Soviet deportation of 1937 was a separate forced displacement. From 1939 the Japanese state organized coercive labor movements in distinct stages.

Wartime mobilization subordinated production, food, and labor to military needs, and the end of Japanese rule in 1945 exposed the colony's dependence on imperial administration. The legacies were uneven, and debates about development and extraction require precision about definitions and evidence.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter shows why economic history cannot be reduced to totals. The same decade could add irrigation, dams, and factories while many households owed more, ate less rice, or left home. Keeping growth, ownership, distribution, labor conditions, and sovereignty apart is the main analytical habit it develops.

It also shows how agriculture, industry, labor, and migration formed one system. Rural debt supplied workers, industrial growth drew on northern power and imperial capital, and wartime controls drew all of these into military supply. The colonial economy was integrated within the empire more than within Korea.

The chapter prepares later discussion of movements, assimilation, and war. Chapter 59 takes up the political organizations that grew from tenant and labor conflict, Chapter 60 the cultural coercion of the wartime state, and Chapter 62 the wider experience of mobilization. The economic inheritance of 1945 enters the occupation and division examined from Chapter 64.

Finally, the chapter models how to argue about contested questions. Claims about development or extraction are strongest when they state their evidence, their definitions, and their limits, and when they recognize that people in the same economy experienced it very differently.
