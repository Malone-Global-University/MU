The Gabo Reforms and the Assassination of Queen Min

Korean History: Ancient Kingdoms to a Divided Peninsula

Chapter Opening

On the morning of October 8, 1895, a queen was killed inside her own palace by men who had entered with the help of soldiers, police, and the diplomatic establishment of a foreign power. Four months later, on February 11, 1896, her husband slipped out of that palace in a palanquin and took refuge in another country's legation, and the prime minister who had presided over Korea's most sweeping legal reforms was dead in the street. The same government had abolished hereditary legal rank, ended the civil service examinations, and declared that royal household and state were separate. How could a program of modernization end in such wreckage?

This chapter covers roughly nineteen months, from the Japanese seizure of Gyeongbokgung on July 23, 1894, to the king's flight on February 11, 1896. It is a period in which Korea's governing institutions were rewritten faster and more thoroughly than at any time since the founding of Joseon, and also a period in which the Korean monarchy lost control of the capital. These two facts are not separate stories. The reforms were drafted and debated by Korean officials, many of whom held serious convictions about what the state needed, but they were enacted in a capital occupied by Japanese troops, under the eye of Japanese diplomats, and eventually in the shadow of a political murder. That context shaped what was decided, how it was received, and how long it lasted.

The reforms are conventionally grouped into three phases: the First Gabo Reforms (July–December 1894), directed by the Gunguk Gimucheo under Kim Hong-jip; the Second Gabo Reforms (December 1894–mid-1895), carried out by a reconstituted cabinet in which the returned exile Pak Yeong-hyo was prominent; and the Eulmi Reforms (late 1895–early 1896), which followed the assassination and culminated in the controversial haircut order. "Gabo" and "Eulmi" are names of years in the sexagenary cycle (1894 and 1895), not names of programs, and historians often treat the three phases as a continuous but internally shifting effort. This chapter follows that convention while showing where the phases differ in personnel, aims, and degree of foreign pressure.

The central question of the chapter is how reforms meant to strengthen and modernize Korea could simultaneously weaken the political legitimacy of the government implementing them. Answering it requires rejecting two common oversimplifications. The first holds that the Gabo Reforms were Japanese inventions imposed on a passive Korea. Korean reformers had programs of their own, some dating to the Gapsin Coup of 1884 and earlier, and many of the measures echo Korean criticism of the old order. The second holds that the reforms were an uninterrupted Korean movement toward national independence. Japanese military and political intervention constrained the circumstances in which Korean programs operated, and some measures served Japanese interests in ways Korean ministers could not fully control.

The changes did not reach every part of society equally. A former bonded laborer, a young examination candidate, a provincial magistrate, a palace attendant, and a government minister experienced these years through very different institutions and vulnerabilities. The abolition of legal status distinctions could be momentous without providing a freed household with land or security. A new ministry could clarify administrative responsibility while leaving local clerks and old patronage relationships in place. The story of reform must therefore move between the documents promulgated in Seoul and the much slower alteration of social practices beyond the capital.

Nor was the court merely the setting for competing foreign ambitions. Korean rulers and ministers disagreed about the powers of the king, the staffing of the new state, the legal standing of established elites, and the value of cooperation with Japan or Russia. Those disputes could not be separated from the presence of foreign troops, but they were not entirely produced by it. This combination of independent political purposes and constrained choices is what makes the period difficult to evaluate. The chapter asks students to judge both the substance of the changes and the process by which they were put into effect.

The evidence is uneven. Reform decrees and council records survive in Korean official compilations, and the National Institute of Korean History preserves many of them; the Fourteen Articles of January 1895 are among the most important. Evidence for the assassination comes from foreign eyewitnesses, diplomatic dispatches, Japanese judicial proceedings, and Korean testimony, each with its own interests and limitations. This chapter builds on Chapter 51, which supplies the Donghak Peasant Revolution and the Sino-Japanese War, and it prepares for Chapter 53, which examines the Korean Empire proclaimed in 1897. Its lesson is that institutional modernization and political sovereignty are different things: a state may enact major legal and administrative change while losing the authority to govern itself.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  2. Distinguish the three phases of reform (First Gabo, Second Gabo, Eulmi) by personnel, institutions, and degree of foreign pressure.
  3. Analyze the abolition of hereditary status, the nobi system, and the civil service examinations, separating legislation from social effect.
  4. Describe the reorganization of central and local government, finance, law, policing, and education.
  5. Interpret the Hongbeom Fourteen Articles as a statement of constitutional and administrative aims, and distinguish declared independence from actual freedom of action.
  6. Explain how the Triple Intervention changed the balance of influence at the Korean court.
  7. Reconstruct the assassination of Queen Min using multiple kinds of evidence, distinguishing established responsibility from disputed questions of higher authorization.
  8. Evaluate how Korean officials, scholars, and communities responded to the assassination and to the haircut order.
  9. Assess the historiographical debate over the autonomy of Korean reformers under Japanese pressure.
  10. Trace the collapse of the Kim Hong-jip government on February 11, 1896 and explain why it did not end Korean state modernization.
  11. Connect the reform period to the preceding crisis of 1894 (Chapter 51) and the imperial project of 1897 (Chapter 53).

Historical Context

By 1894 Joseon had endured nearly two decades of treaty-port diplomacy and a series of political shocks: the Imo Mutiny of 1882 and the Gapsin Coup of 1884 (Chapter 50), the growth of Qing influence under Yuan Shikai, and persistent fiscal strain. The court around King Gojong and Queen Min had stabilized after 1884 through alliances with the Min clan and cautious foreign diplomacy, but the countryside was restless, and the Donghak movement (Chapter 46) had grown out of grievances about taxation, corruption, and religious persecution. The great peasant mobilization of 1894 and the intervention of Qing and Japanese forces, covered in Chapter 51, brought the political crisis to a head.

Joseon's formal institutions in 1894 still rested on a Confucian framework: a Council of State and Six Boards, a civil service examination system, a legally recognized hierarchy of yangban, jungin, commoners, and nobi, and a provincial administration of eight provinces with governors and magistrates who often combined fiscal, judicial, and police functions. Critics inside Korea, including those associated with Silhak thought (Chapter 38), the Gapsin reformers, and officials who had traveled to Japan, China, and the United States, had argued that these arrangements were inadequate. The crisis of 1894 gave those critics an opening, but it also gave a foreign army the power to choose who would speak for the Korean government.

The wider setting matters. Japan was a rising state that had reorganized its own government in the Meiji era and viewed Korea's independence from Qing as strategically essential. China considered Korea a tributary. Russia was extending its position in Northeast Asia, and Britain, the United States, France, and Germany had treaty interests. A student should therefore read the reforms on two levels at once: as an internal Korean argument about the shape of the state, and as an episode in a regional struggle over who would control Korea's future. Division I — The Crisis of Sovereignty and the Beginning of Reform

Section 52.1

Korea after the Donghak Uprising: Reform, War, and Political Crisis

Chapter 51 explains how the Jeonju armistice of June 11, 1894 ended the first phase of the Donghak campaign, and how Qing and Japanese forces came to Korea. What matters here is the political situation these events left behind. By mid-June, Japanese troops were landing at Incheon and moving toward Seoul. The Korean court had asked Qing for help against the rebels but now had no reason for foreign soldiers to remain, and it asked both powers to withdraw. Japan declined and proposed instead that China and Japan jointly press Korea to reform its internal administration. China rejected the idea, partly because it denied that outsiders had a role in Korea's domestic affairs. Japan's representatives then presented their own reform demands to the Korean government in early July.

The situation inside Korea was fluid. The court was divided between officials who distrusted Japan and those who thought reform inevitable. The peasantry had not been pacified by the truce: many of the grievances behind the uprising, including abusive tax collection, corrupt local officials, and hereditary privilege, were unresolved. Sources attribute to Donghak leaders a list of reform demands made at Jeonju, though the exact wording and authorship of such lists vary in surviving records and should be treated with care. What is clear is that a vocabulary of reform existed in rural Korea as well as at court, and Korean officials had reason to think that some visible change was necessary.

Japan's goals were mixed. Its government wanted Qing influence removed from Korea, a Korean administration willing to cooperate with Japan, and legal and economic conditions favorable to Japanese interests. Reform was also a justification for remaining. These aims were not identical to those of Korean reformers, and the resulting overlap, in which both sides favored some of the same measures for different reasons, is the key to understanding the entire period. This section therefore sets a limit on how much of the earlier story is retold here: the peasant campaigns, the operational history of the war, and the peace negotiations belong to Chapter 51, and the next section begins where the Korean government tried to answer the demand for reform on its own terms.

Section 52.2

Korean Reform Initiatives and the Gyojeongcheong

Faced with Japanese pressure, the Korean government attempted to take reform into its own hands. On July 13, 1894 (the eleventh day of the sixth lunar month), following a royal declaration of July 8 rejecting Japanese interference in Korea's internal affairs, it established the Gyojeongcheong, the Council for Correction, whose first reform measures were issued on July 18. Its stated purpose was to reform abuses in government and to respond to the grievances that had produced the rebellion. The council was staffed largely by established officials, and its creation allowed the court to argue to Japan that reform was already under way and that outside demands, especially under the pressure of foreign troops, were unnecessary.

Historians read the Gyojeongcheong in two ways. Some stress that it demonstrates the existence of a Korean reform impulse independent of Japan, and that the court was not simply waiting to be reformed. Others emphasize its limits: it was created in part as a tactical response to Japanese demands, its leadership came from the conservative establishment, and it achieved little before events overtook it. Both points hold. The council shows that Korean officials were willing to promise change; it also shows that the court hoped to control the pace and content of that change, and that it had little power to resist if Japan chose to act.

Japan did choose to act. On July 20 the Japanese minister, Ōtori Keisuke, presented what amounted to an ultimatum, demanding that Korea expel Qing troops and abrogate its agreements with China. The Korean reply on July 22 did not satisfy him. In the early hours of July 23, Japanese forces moved on the palace. The Gyojeongcheong, whose existence had been intended to forestall precisely this outcome, was soon overshadowed by a new body created under Japanese protection, and its place in the narrative of reform is largely that of an abortive Korean alternative.

The council's significance lies partly in what it reveals about the court's calculations. It was framed as a response to abuses of the kind the Donghak movement had denounced, including corrupt taxation and the sale of office, which meant that the court was borrowing part of the rebels' agenda in order to take the initiative away from both Japan and the peasantry. Whether it could have grown into a genuine alternative cannot be known, because it was displaced within days. The episode nevertheless shows that the idea of reform was not introduced to Korea by the occupation, and that the contest was as much over who would carry out reform as over whether it would happen.

Section 52.3

The July 23 Palace Seizure and the Restructuring of Royal Authority

On the morning of July 23, 1894, units of the Japanese forces in Seoul, commanded by Ōshima Yoshimasa, attacked and occupied Gyeongbokgung. Korean guards offered brief resistance. The details of the military action belong to the history of the war in Chapter 51; the constitutional and political consequences belong here. Unlike earlier wartime occupations, including the Japanese capture of Seoul during the Imjin War, this intervention placed a reigning Joseon monarch under direct foreign military pressure within his own capital. Every decision made by the Korean government afterward was therefore subject to the question of whether it had been made freely.

Gojong's position was radically compromised. He remained on the throne and retained his formal powers, and decrees continued to issue in his name, but he could not act independently. One of the first acts of the new situation was to bring the Daewongun, Gojong's father, out of semi-retirement to head the government. The Japanese expected the Daewongun, a longtime opponent of the Min clan, to be a pliable instrument, but his own aims were not identical to theirs. He wanted to restore his family's influence and, according to documents later found, communicated secretly with Qing commanders and Donghak leaders in the following months. Japan eventually forced him from political activity, and his role during 1894 is best understood as that of an ambitious Korean figure who used Japanese power for purposes of his own and was in turn constrained by it.

The seizure also reshaped the ministry. Members of the Min family and officials closely identified with the queen's circle, such as Min Yeong-jun, lost their positions or fled. New appointments were influenced, and in some cases directed, by Japanese representatives. The key point for students is not that Japan chose every minister, since Korean officials did participate in their own selection, but that Japan held a veto and could reconfigure the cabinet at will. This was the context in which the Gunguk Gimucheo was formed four days later.

Section 52.4

Kim Hong-jip and the Establishment of the Gunguk Gimucheo

Kim Hong-jip (1842–1896) was an experienced official who had served as an envoy to Japan in 1880 and returned with a copy of Huang Zunxian's Chaoxian celue (A Strategy for Korea), a text urging Korea to maintain close relations with China while building ties with Japan and the United States. He was a moderate by temperament, a believer in orderly institutional change rather than abrupt rupture, and a man respected across factional lines. After July 23 he became the most prominent figure in the new government, and on July 27, 1894 (the twenty-fifth day of the sixth lunar month) he took charge of the newly created Gunguk Gimucheo, the Deliberative Council.

The Gunguk Gimucheo was headed by a president, Kim Hong-jip, and a vice president, Pak Jeong-yang, with roughly sixteen to nineteen deliberative members and supporting secretarial staff; officials associated with its work included Kim Yun-sik, Eo Yun-jung, and Yu Gil-jun. It was empowered to deliberate and decide on reform measures, which the king would then approve. Its authority was ambiguous, and that ambiguity is crucial. Formally, it deliberated on behalf of the crown. In practice it acted as an organ of government that could override ordinary ministerial procedure, and the crown's approval was often a matter of form. Over the following months it adopted more than two hundred measures, an unusually dense program of lawmaking for so short a period.

The council's relationship to Japan was complicated. Japanese representatives pressed for specific measures, such as railroad and telegraph arrangements, and Japanese advisers were placed in several ministries. Yet the council's members also pursued their own agenda. Many of them had long favored changes in status law, fiscal administration, and examination policy, and they used the opportunity to enact those changes. The council thus embodies the central paradox of the chapter: Korean officials with real convictions were able to act only because a foreign army had cleared the way, and the foreign origin of their authority undermined the public standing of what they enacted.

The council's procedure shaped its output. Proposals were drafted, debated in session, and, once adopted, submitted for royal sanction; because Gojong was in no position to refuse, sanction was usually formal. The speed was possible because ordinary channels of remonstrance, consultation with the old boards, and negotiation among rival officials were bypassed. Reformers justified the shortcut as necessary to overcome entrenched resistance, while critics, then and since, argued that it deprived the reforms of the consultation that might have given them legitimacy. The distinction between a rapid process and a legitimate one runs through the rest of the chapter. Japanese advisers advised, and legation officials could press for particular items, but the surviving resolutions also show a body pursuing measures Korean reformers had long advocated. The council's record supports neither the view that it merely ratified Japanese dictation nor the view that it acted freely.

Historical Vocabulary
Gabo, Eulmi, Gunguk Gimucheo, and Gaeguk

Gabo (甲午) and Eulmi (乙未) are the names of the thirty-first and thirty-second years of the sixty-year sexagenary cycle. In East Asia they designate 1894 and 1895 respectively. The same cycle gave names to other events, such as the Gapsin Coup of 1884 and the Imjin War of 1592. "Gabo Reforms" therefore means "the reforms of the Gabo year," and "Eulmi Reforms" and "Eulmi Incident" similarly refer to 1895.

The Gunguk Gimucheo (軍國機務處) is usually translated "Deliberative Council" or "Council of Military and State Affairs." It was the reform body of July–December 1894. The Gaeguk era was a dating system counting years from the founding of Joseon in 1392; its use in 1894 signaled a break with Qing reign-era dating and was part of the assertion of independence.

Potential misunderstanding: "Gabo" is a calendar label, not an ideology. It does not tell us who authored the reforms, and using it as shorthand for "Japanese-imposed reform" or for "Korean modernization" would prejudge the questions this chapter examines.

Division II — The First Gabo Reforms: Reconstructing State and Society

Section 52.5

Reorganizing the Central Government and Royal Administration

The First Gabo Reforms began with the structure of government itself. The traditional arrangement of a Council of State (Uijeongbu) with Six Boards (Yukjo), each responsible for a broad category of administration such as personnel, rites, or punishments, was replaced by a Council of State presiding over eight ministries: Home Affairs, Foreign Affairs, Finance, Military Affairs, Justice, Education, Public Works, and Agriculture and Commerce. The new structure resembled the ministerial systems of Meiji Japan and of European states, with each ministry assigned a distinct portfolio and a minister responsible for it.

The reformers also tried to separate the royal household from the state. A new Royal Household Department (Gungnaebu) took over the administration of palace affairs, royal property, and court ritual, and the state administration was meant to be distinct from it. This was a significant change in principle. Under the old order the king's personal and public roles were intertwined, and officials who served the court often served the state, and vice versa. The reformers wanted to fix a boundary: the king would reign, the government would administer, and royal finances would not be indistinguishable from public revenue. Gojong and Queen Min viewed this separation with suspicion, because it reduced their ability to appoint and reward officials, and the question of who actually controlled the Gungnaebu became a political issue.

Other measures reached into the daily habits of officialdom: the structure of ranks and titles was revised, official dress was simplified, and Qing-era reign names were abandoned in favor of the Gaeguk era. These changes were visible, symbolic, and cheap, and they signaled that the government had changed. Whether the underlying bureaucracy was functionally different was another matter. The new ministries still depended on officials trained in the old system, on budgets that remained insufficient, and on local officers who might ignore Seoul's instructions. The reorganization of central offices was easier to decree than to administer, a pattern that recurs throughout the chapter.

Two mechanisms deserve emphasis. First, the new ministries gave each portfolio a minister and subordinate officials, creating lines of accountability the Six Boards had lacked; yet the Gunguk Gimucheo itself acted above the ministries, so that in 1894 the most important decisions were made outside the formal ministerial structure. Second, the separation of the Gungnaebu from state administration was a question of control as well as principle. Whoever controlled the household controlled royal property, palace appointments, and the flow of patronage. For reformers the separation prevented royal favorites from interfering in administration; for Gojong and Queen Min it risked reducing the crown to a ceremonial role. This is one reason the court's cooperation was grudging, and why disputes over household finance and appointments recur in the cabinet crises of 1895.

Section 52.6

Abolition of Hereditary Status and the Nobi System

Among the most consequential First Gabo measures were those addressing legal status. The Gunguk Gimucheo resolved that the distinction between yangban and commoners should be abolished, that talent rather than birth should govern appointment, and that public and private nobi (the legally unfree or bonded population) should be freed and the buying and selling of persons prohibited. These resolutions struck at the foundation of Joseon's social hierarchy, a system that had defined the legal and economic position of every Korean for centuries.

The measures did not appear from nothing. The Joseon state had already reduced the nobi population over time: most government-owned nobi had been freed in 1801, hereditary transmission of nobi status had been curtailed by legislation in the 1880s, and critics from Silhak writers to Donghak preachers had condemned hereditary servitude. Peasant demands associated with 1894 reportedly included the burning of nobi registers. The First Gabo resolution therefore ratified, and extended, a long erosion of the institution. Formal restrictions on other low-status groups, such as the butchers known as baekjeong, were also loosened in principle.

What students must keep distinct is legislation and effect. A decree could end legal ownership of persons, but it could not provide land, employment, or housing to those freed. Many former nobi continued to work for their former masters as tenants or household dependents, because they had no alternative; others left for towns or ports; some faced continued social stigma. Yangban status, too, was less a legal category than a matrix of kinship ties, landholding, education, and local prestige, none of which a decree could remove. The effect of the First Gabo status reforms was thus to remove legal sanction from the hierarchy while leaving its social and economic foundations largely in place. That gap between formal equality and lived inequality is a theme of the Continuity and Change feature below and of much of the history that follows.

Historians reconstruct the effects of these measures indirectly. Household registers, local petitions, missionary reports, and later memoirs show a mixed picture: some former nobi left their masters' households, while others negotiated new arrangements as tenants or hired workers. The decrees were generally not accompanied by land redistribution or a program to give former nobi the means of independent livelihood, so freedom of status did not by itself create economic independence. Former owners received no systematic compensation, so far as the record shows, which contributed to elite resentment, and some provincial conservatives could argue that the abolition had been decreed by an illegitimate government. The abolition of the nobi system, though legally decisive, was therefore completed in practice only gradually over the following decades, through economic change, migration, and the slow disappearance of old household forms.

Primary Source Window I

The Gunguk Gimucheo's Reform Resolutions of 1894

Creator: The Gunguk Gimucheo, with the approval of the crown · Date: July–December 1894; the first major set dated to July 30, 1894 · Type: Official legislative resolutions preserved in Korean government compilations and collections such as those of the National Institute of Korean History

Context: The resolutions were adopted in a capital occupied by Japanese troops, by a council whose members included both long-standing reform advocates and officials who accepted the new arrangement out of pragmatism. They cover status, marriage, appointment, examinations, and administration.

Paraphrase: The following is a paraphrase of content, not a quotation.

The resolutions declare that distinctions between noble and common birth are to be abolished; that officials are to be chosen for ability without regard to origin; that public and private bonded persons are to be released and the sale of persons forbidden; that boys and girls may not marry below stated ages; that widows may remarry; and that punishment of relatives for an offender's crime is to end.

The unusual circumstances of its adoption also mean it cannot by itself prove whether the measures reflected Korean consensus or Japanese preference.

Section 52.7

The Abolition of the Civil Service Examinations

The gwageo examinations were the central institution of Joseon's scholar-official culture. For centuries they had defined the path to office, structured education around the classics, and conferred prestige on families that produced successful candidates. In practice, the system had become entangled with factional politics, with the sale or manipulation of ranks, and with the dominance of a limited number of lineages, and Korean critics had long complained that it rewarded memorization and literary polish rather than administrative skill. Even so, it remained a powerful symbol of Confucian meritocracy.

The First Gabo Reforms ended the gwageo. Officials were to be selected under new rules, including examinations on subjects relevant to government, recommendation, and promotion within the new ministries. The Seonggyungwan, the traditional Confucian academy, was reorganized in 1895 around a more structured course. In theory, the change opened office to talent from any background and fitted recruitment to the needs of a modern administration.

The reality was more complicated. The new recruitment system was not fully built when the old one ended, and the transition created uncertainty for the many young men who had spent years preparing for exams that no longer existed. Appointments continued to depend on patronage, factional ties, and foreign preferences. At the same time, the abolition struck at the self-understanding of the scholar-official class. For Confucian scholars, the examination was not merely a hiring device but a moral institution linking learning to office, and its end contributed to the alienation of rural literati, many of whom would later lead the righteous armies. A merit system that is announced before it is operational may produce resentment without producing merit.

The institutional change also altered the relationship between learning and political power. Under the examination system, knowledge of the canonical texts had been one recognized justification for entrance into official life, even though access was unequal and the process was far from purely meritocratic. New ministries instead required people able to handle budgets, correspondence, foreign languages, policing, engineering, and unfamiliar legal procedures. No single new qualification could supply all those skills. Ministries consequently had to depend on a combination of officials educated under the old order, men who had studied abroad or traveled in treaty-port networks, and new technical specialists. Their practical competence varied, and their political connections continued to matter.

In rural communities, the consequences were especially uneven. The end of examinations did not automatically create schools where prospective officials could learn the new subjects, while the reputation attached to generations of classical study did not disappear at the stroke of a decree. Some families adjusted their educational investments and sought alternatives; others defended the older ideal that learning should cultivate moral judgment before it prepared a person for administrative work. This was not a simple contest between education and ignorance, but a dispute over what kind of knowledge could legitimately authorize the exercise of state power.

Section 52.8

Fiscal Reform, Taxation, Currency, and Government Finance

Fiscal reform sought to address the fundamental weakness of the Joseon state: its inability to raise and spend revenue predictably and fairly. The reformers moved to concentrate revenue under the Finance Ministry, to regularize taxation by standard rules, and to separate the royal household's expenses from state expenditure. Taxes traditionally paid in grain or goods were increasingly to be paid in money, a shift intended to reduce opportunities for corruption in collection and storage. Weights and measures were to be standardized, and a new monetary regulation introduced a silver standard.

The measures confronted structural obstacles. Local officials and clerks who had profited from the old system had little incentive to cooperate, and the central government lacked an administrative apparatus to monitor collection. Monetary reform ran into the reality that Japanese coins and banknotes were permitted to circulate in Korea, which diluted the intended standard and gave Japanese financial interests an advantage. Government finances remained fragile, and Japan extended a loan of three million yen in 1895, which relieved immediate pressure but increased Korean dependence on Japanese credit.

Fiscal reform thus illustrates the tension at the center of the period. Rationalizing revenue and ending the entanglement of palace and state were aims many Korean officials shared, and some elements, such as the principle that royal expenses should be budgeted, would persist. But a state that depends on foreign loans and shares its currency with a foreign power does not control its own finances. Reform of the form of fiscal administration advanced faster than the substance of fiscal independence.

The cash-tax reform illustrates the difficulty. Converting taxes from grain to money was meant to reduce the opportunities for officials to profit from storage, transport, and conversion at arbitrary rates. But cash taxation also required peasants to sell produce for coin before the tax date, exposing them to merchants and to fluctuations in grain prices, and it depended on a supply of coin the government could not guarantee. Where the old system had allowed officials to exploit peasants through unfair conversion rates, the new one risked shifting the burden to market forces beyond the peasants' control. Centralizing revenue in the Finance Ministry likewise assumed that the center could audit local accounts, a capacity that did not yet exist. The fiscal program is therefore best described as a statement of principles whose practical realization lay largely in the future, and whose most immediate concrete effect was to deepen reliance on external credit.

Section 52.9

Local Administration and the Reorganization of Provincial Government

Under the old system, the eight provinces were governed by governors, with county magistrates responsible for tax collection, courts, and local order. Magistrates often combined fiscal, judicial, and police authority and were frequently accused of abuse, as the Donghak grievances had shown. The reformers wanted to curtail those powers, separate judicial and administrative functions, and place local officials under clearer central supervision.

The most dramatic statutory change came in 1895. In late May, a royal ordinance replaced the eight provinces with twenty-three districts (bu), each headed by an official appointed by the center, with counties beneath them. The new districts included Hanseong (Seoul), Incheon, Chuncheon, Gangneung, Chungju, Gongju, Jeonju, Naju, Jeju, Andong, Daegu, Dongnae, Jinju, Haeju, Pyongyang, Uiju, and Hamheung, among others. The structure was meant to break the power of long-established provincial networks and to make local government more uniform.

The distinction between statute and implementation is again essential. The twenty-three-district system was announced in 1895 and applied unevenly. Appointees had to be found, offices staffed, and boundaries drawn, all while the countryside was unsettled and the central government lacked authority. The new structure lasted only until August 1896, when the government of the post-flight period replaced it with thirteen provinces (a subject for Chapter 53). The episode shows how a rational administrative design could be undermined by political instability, and how reform legislation could be quickly reversed when the regime that created it fell.

The reorganization also standardized the lower tier. The Academy of Korean Studies dates the ordinance to May 26, 1895 and records that 337 counties were placed under the new districts with uniform status, which was itself a reform, since earlier counties had varied in rank and privilege. The chief officials of the new districts were appointed from the center, to prevent local networks from entrenching themselves, but this meant that the system depended on Seoul's ability to find competent appointees and pay them. In a government whose cabinet changed repeatedly, that ability was limited, and the structure was revisited as soon as the political balance shifted.

Section 52.10

Marriage, Gender, Family Law, and the Reform of Social Practices

The Gabo Reforms reached into family life. Marriage below stated ages (twenty for men and sixteen for women, according to the standard accounts) was prohibited, widows were permitted to remarry, and restrictions based on hereditary discrimination were loosened, including rules affecting the children of concubines. Collective punishment of relatives for an offender's crime was abolished, a significant legal change discussed further in Section 52.15. These measures drew on a critique, expressed by Silhak scholars and by some missionaries and foreign observers, that early marriage and the prohibition of widow remarriage harmed individuals and families.

For women, the changes were meaningful but limited. The ban on widow remarriage had been an ideal enforced by social pressure and by the status consequences for the children of remarried women, and removing its legal basis did not remove the stigma. The state had not changed property law or political rights for women, and the age limits on marriage depended on local enforcement that was inconsistent. In many rural households, practice continued as before. Elite families who had built their standing on lineage purity and on the chastity of widows had reason to resist or ignore the change.

Students should avoid two errors: dismissing the reforms as irrelevant because they did not immediately transform social life, and imagining that legal change created equality. The marriage and family provisions are better understood as setting a new legal standard against which later reformers, educators, and women themselves could argue. They shifted the burden of justification, even where they did not change behavior.

Legal Equality and the Persistence of Social Hierarchy

What Changed

  • Legal distinctions between yangban and commoners were abolished in the 1894 resolutions
  • Ownership of persons and the sale of persons were prohibited
  • Birth was formally replaced by ability as the principle of appointment, and the gwageo ended
  • Widow remarriage and age limits on marriage altered the legal framework of family life

What Stayed the Same

  • Landholding, lineage networks, and local prestige continued to advantage former yangban
  • Many former nobi remained dependent on former masters for land and shelter
  • Stigma against groups such as the baekjeong persisted well beyond the decrees
  • Patronage and factional ties continued to shape appointments

Timing and experience: Legal change was abrupt, concentrated in 1894–1895, while social change was gradual, uneven, and contested for decades. Former nobi and baekjeong experienced the change most directly in principle but often experienced continuity in daily dependence; former yangban lost legal privilege but retained many informal advantages.

Why the pattern matters: It shows that legal revolution and social revolution are distinct processes, and that a reform may be historically significant without achieving its stated aims quickly.

Division III — The Second Gabo Reforms and the Struggle to Reconstruct Government

Section 52.11

Reform Implementation and the Resistance of Established Interests

By autumn 1894 the distance between decree and administration was already apparent. Provincial officials who lost judicial and fiscal powers had reason to delay or ignore instructions. Clerks and runners, whose income depended on customary fees and the manipulation of tax records, saw reform as a threat. Hereditary elites objected to the loss of legal privilege, and the court itself, under the strain of palace occupation, was often reluctant to approve measures that reduced its patronage. Decrees were issued faster than officials could be appointed to carry them out.

Reform also unfolded amid continuing violence. Chapter 51 describes the second Donghak mobilization of the autumn, which was suppressed by Japanese and Korean government forces by the end of the year, and the later execution of its leader Jeon Bong-jun in 1895. The result was a striking contradiction: a government that adopted measures answering some peasant grievances, including the end of status distinctions, was at the same time using force, with Japanese assistance, against the movement that had made those grievances politically urgent. Rural Koreans could hardly be expected to view the reform program as a gift from a government that was killing the people who had demanded it.

For historians the lesson is to distinguish four levels: laws announced, institutions established, reforms administered, and changes experienced. The Gabo program was rich at the first level, partial at the second, uneven at the third, and often invisible at the fourth. The gap did not mean the reforms were meaningless. It meant that their historical significance lay as much in the precedents they set and the opposition they provoked as in immediate effects.

A central government can alter the titles and responsibilities of its officers without immediately changing the relationships through which authority operates in a county. Magistrates and their clerks knew local households, tax burdens, disputes, and patronage networks in ways that newly appointed reform administrators did not. That knowledge could be used constructively, but it could also be withheld or manipulated. Where the capital could neither audit local accounts consistently nor pay its officials reliably, it struggled to distinguish genuine administrative obstacles from deliberate obstruction. Even a decree promising relief could be turned into another demand for a processing fee or another occasion for discretionary punishment.

Reformers therefore confronted a dilemma of their own making. To reduce the power of provincial intermediaries, they needed officers capable of enforcing central instructions; yet building those capacities required cooperation from the same intermediaries whose prerogatives they were trying to curtail. The continuing military crisis sharpened the problem by diverting attention, funds, and personnel away from civilian administration. This helps explain why a program capable of changing the formal legal order so quickly could remain uncertain in its effects on everyday government.

Section 52.12

The Return of Exiled Reformers and Conflicts within the Reform Coalition

A second wave of reform leadership arrived in the second half of 1894. Pak Yeong-hyo, a leader of the failed Gapsin Coup of 1884 who had lived for a decade in Japan, was pardoned and returned to Seoul, and Seo Gwang-beom, another Gapsin participant who had lived in the United States, also entered the government in late 1894. Their return reconnected the Gabo era with the earlier reform movement, and it reminded contemporaries that the aims of 1894 had a Korean genealogy reaching back at least to 1884.

The returning radicals were not natural allies of Kim Hong-jip and his associates. Pak and Seo had a more ambitious conception of institutional change, a stronger commitment to constitutional limits on royal power, and, in Pak's case, closer ties to Japanese officials and a reputation among conservatives as the author of a violent coup. Kim Hong-jip's circle favored gradual and consensual reform and preferred to retain court support. These differences were real and became sharper as the stakes grew. The reform coalition was never a single party but a shifting alliance of moderates, radicals, and pragmatists with differing views of Japan, the monarchy, and the pace of change.

This disunity weakened reform in two ways. It made the government vulnerable to royal and conservative efforts to exploit divisions, and it made foreign advisers more influential, because rival ministers sought Japanese backing against one another. A coalition united only by the circumstances of its formation could not easily build lasting authority.

The disagreements were not only personal. They concerned the role of the monarchy, with Pak and Seo inclined toward a cabinet system in which royal initiative would be constrained and Kim Hong-jip's circle inclined to a more cooperative relationship with the crown; the degree to which Japanese advice should be accepted; and the pace at which measures could be carried out without provoking revolt. These questions reflected different experiences. Pak and Seo had spent years abroad, in Japan and the United States, and tended to see Korea's institutions through comparison, while Kim Hong-jip had spent a career in the existing administration and sought to reform it from within. Neither view was inherently more Korean than the other, and both contributed to the reform program.

Section 52.13

The Second Reform Phase and the Reorganization of Executive Power

The arrival of Inoue Kaoru as Japanese minister in October 1894 marked a change in Japanese method. Inoue, a senior statesman, favored a more systematic and less openly coercive approach than his predecessor, and he presented proposals for reform to Gojong that emphasized cabinet responsibility, royal withdrawal from day-to-day administration, and financial control. Japanese policy was to build a reformed Korean government dependent on Japanese advice, rather than to govern directly.

In December 1894 the Gunguk Gimucheo was dissolved. Its functions passed to a reorganized government, a cabinet in which Kim Hong-jip served as prime minister and Pak Yeong-hyo as Home Minister, in what is often called the Kim Hong-jip–Pak Yeong-hyo coalition. The State Council was recast as a cabinet (naegak) of ministers collectively responsible for administration, and the principle that the king should reign through ministers rather than command directly was stated more clearly. Japanese advisers took positions in ministries, and Inoue's influence over the king and ministers was substantial.

The reorganization raised the question that dominates the rest of the chapter: who held executive power? In theory, ministers did. In practice, the king retained powers of appointment and, through his access to foreign diplomats, an independent political standing; Japanese advisers shaped policy; and ministers competed for foreign and royal favor. The attempt to define executive authority clearly was a genuine constitutional experiment, but it took place in conditions where authority was contested at every level.

Japanese pressure on the court was also personal. The Daewongun, returned to power in July 1894, was gradually pushed aside after Japanese authorities came to suspect that he had contacted Qing commanders and Donghak leaders, and Inoue worked to reduce his influence in the autumn. The removal of one royal figure did not strengthen the cabinet, since it left Gojong and Queen Min, who had their own base at court, to compete with ministers for influence. Inoue's strategy relied on the expectation that a government dependent on Japanese funds and advice would remain cooperative, and the loan of three million yen extended in 1895 reflected that expectation. Financial dependence did not guarantee political loyalty, however, and the court's reaction to the Triple Intervention would show how thin Japanese leverage could be.

Section 52.14

The Hongbeom Fourteen Articles and the Declaration of Political Independence

On January 7, 1895 (the twelfth day of the twelfth lunar month), Gojong went to the royal ancestral shrine, the Jongmyo, and swore a set of principles to his ancestors, together with a declaration of Korea's independence. The principles, known as the Hongbeom Fourteen Articles (Great Plan), were the most important single statement of the reformers' constitutional aims. They were promulgated in a ceremony intended to give them the solemnity of a dynastic oath, and they were published in mixed script, in an early use of Korean vernacular elements in an official document.

The articles can be read thematically. A first group concerned sovereignty: the end of dependence on Qing China and the establishment of the foundations of independence. A second concerned the monarchy: rules for the royal household and succession, the king's exercise of government through his ministers and consultation with them, and the separation of palace affairs from state administration. A third concerned finance: the regulation of taxation by law, the central control of revenue and expenditure, and the fixing of royal household expenses within a budget. A fourth concerned administration and law: reform of local government, the drafting of civil and criminal codes protecting life and property, and the appointment of officials without regard to origin. A final group addressed the sending of talented young men abroad to study and the creation of conscription-based armed forces. The articles amounted to an outline of a constitutional monarchy without using that term.

The declaration of independence needs careful interpretation. Korean officials had already repudiated Qing suzerainty in the summer of 1894, and Japan's Treaty of Shimonoseki with China (April 1895) would recognize Korea as fully independent. Independence from Qing was a real change, and for many Koreans a desirable one. But formal independence from one power occurred under the guns of another. A government whose capital was garrisoned by Japanese troops and whose finances depended on Japanese loans was independent in the legal sense and constrained in the practical sense. The Fourteen Articles therefore document both a genuine program of reform and the paradox of declaring sovereignty while exercising it under pressure.

Section 52.15

Courts, Policing, Legal Administration, and the Making of a Modern State

The reformers sought a new relationship between state and subject, in which law, rather than the discretion of magistrates, governed punishment and procedure. Collective punishment of relatives for one person's crime was abolished, restrictions were placed on judicial torture, and in 1895 a court organization law began to separate judicial functions from general administration, creating courts in Seoul and the treaty ports with defined procedures. These changes responded to long-standing criticism of arbitrary imprisonment and abuse by local officials.

The reformers also built a national police organization. A police bureau created in 1894 was meant to maintain order, replace the scattered runners and guards of the old system, and serve as an instrument of central government. This reveals the double character of modern state-building. The same institutions that offered legal protection also expanded the state's capacity for surveillance and coercion, and Japanese advisers played a significant role in organizing and training the police. In the context of 1895, a more efficient police force could enforce decrees against popular resistance, and it did.

The legal reforms therefore illustrate a tension central to the period: protections introduced on paper coexisted with coercive mechanisms that the reformers could not fully restrain. The new courts and police would be tested during the crises of late 1895, and their reputation suffered from association with the same government that failed to prevent, and was later forced to respond to, the murder of the queen.

The tension is visible in the details. The abolition of collective punishment recognized that individuals, not their kin, should answer for offenses, a break with an old and widely criticized practice. The new police, however, were organized to enforce government decrees, including unpopular ones such as the later haircut order, and soldiers and police in the capital took part in forcibly cutting topknots in December 1895. Judicial reform was also incomplete in practice, since the new courts were few and located mainly in Seoul and the treaty ports, while most disputes continued to be handled by local officials. The legal reforms thus created the outline of a rule-governed state without yet creating the personnel and facilities to apply it, and the coercive element developed faster than the protective one.

Division IV — Reform, Diplomacy, and the Struggle for Control of Korea

Section 52.16

Education Reform and the Formation of Modern Schools

Education was among the most durable reform areas. On February 26, 1895 (the second day of the second lunar month, as dated in the contemporary official gazette), Gojong issued the Education Rescript, which declared education the foundation of national strength and called for a new learning aimed at moral, physical, and intellectual development in place of empty literary study. It presented schooling as a responsibility of the state, a notable departure from a tradition in which education depended mainly on private academies, lineage schools, and local village teachers.

The government followed with institutions. A teachers' school, the Hanseong Normal School, was established in the spring of 1895, regulations for primary schools were issued in the summer, and the first public primary schools opened in Seoul in autumn. A foreign-language school and other specialized institutions were created. Missionary schools, such as those founded by American Protestants, continued to operate alongside the state system and provided an alternative model.

The difference between a national framework and actual schools was large. A rescript could declare a policy, but it could not supply teachers, textbooks, buildings, or funding, and in 1895 the new schools reached only a small number of children, mostly in the capital. Nonetheless, the idea that the state should educate its subjects, and that education was linked to national survival, proved lasting. Later nationalist movements and the Korean Empire built on it (Chapter 53).

Education reform was linked to reform of language. In late 1894 the government decreed that official documents should be based on the Korean script, with Chinese characters permitted as a supplement, a step in the history of Hangeul (Chapter 27) that gave the vernacular a formal place in administration. The new primary-school and teacher-training regulations reflected the same impulse: instruction was to be in language students could understand, and the curriculum was meant to add subjects such as arithmetic and geography to reading and ethics, in place of an exclusive focus on the classics. Missionary schools, which had begun operating in Seoul in the 1880s, provided both a model and a competitor, and families chose among state schools, mission schools, and traditional village academies according to cost, religion, and expectations about future careers. Through 1895 and 1896 the traditional academies remained far more numerous than the new schools.

Section 52.17

Military Reform, Palace Security, and Foreign Advisers

Control of armed force in Seoul was a political question. The reform government and Japanese advisers sought to build a modern military and police under central command. A unit known as the Hullyeondae (Training Unit) was organized with Japanese instructors, and in 1895 the government reorganized its forces, including a capital guard. These measures reflected the Fourteen Articles' pledge of a trained, conscripted force.

The royal palace guard, the Siwidae, remained under court influence and had been trained with the help of American advisers, notably William McEntyre Dye. The coexistence of two sets of armed forces, one tied to Japanese instruction and the other to the court, meant that the palace itself was a site of rivalry. The Japanese garrison in Seoul, which remained after the war with China, added a third armed element.

For Gojong and Queen Min, the military question was personal as well as political. A palace guard loyal to the court was their only security against the possibility of a repeat of July 1894. For Japanese officials, the existence of a guard unfriendly to their influence was a problem. This conflict over who would guard the palace would help set the stage for October 1895.

The balance among these forces was unstable for specific reasons. The Hullyeondae's officers and men were trained under Japanese instruction, and its commanders owed their positions to the cabinet and its advisers rather than to the court. The Siwidae, by contrast, was the king's guard, and its loyalty was personal. A government that did not control the palace guard could not guarantee the king's cooperation, and a king who distrusted the units trained by Japan could not feel secure in his own residence. The conscript army promised in the Fourteen Articles was not realized in this period, because it required revenue, recruitment machinery, and legitimacy that the government lacked. What existed was a collection of small units whose commanders had competing allegiances, conditions that help explain the confusion and internal betrayal visible in October and November 1895.

Section 52.18

The Triple Intervention and the Weakening of Japanese Influence

Japan's victory over Qing China, concluded by the Treaty of Shimonoseki on April 17, 1895, recognized Korea as independent and ceded the Liaodong Peninsula to Japan. Within days, on about April 23, Russia, France, and Germany advised Japan to return the peninsula to China. Unable to resist the combined pressure, Japan acquiesced in May. The episode is known as the Triple Intervention, and its effects reached Korea.

For Korean observers, the Triple Intervention showed that Japan's power was not unlimited and that other great powers, especially Russia, were willing to constrain it. The Korean court, and Queen Min in particular, drew the conclusion that Japanese pressure could be balanced by cultivating other powers. At the same time, Japan's leaders concluded that their position in Korea might erode unless they acted, a concern that would shape policy in the following months. Japanese financial support, including the loan of 1895, aimed to reinforce influence even as prestige declined.

The point for this chapter is the shift in the balance of influence. Before April 1895 Japan was the dominant external force in Seoul. After the intervention the court had more room to maneuver, ministers who favored Japan faced greater suspicion, and the opportunity for a reassertion of royal authority increased. The next sections examine how Gojong and Queen Min used that opportunity.

The Treaty of Shimonoseki's first article, which recognized Korea as fully independent and ended its tributary obligations to China, was in one sense a vindication of the reformers' declaration of independence. The Triple Intervention revealed the paradox: the powers that checked Japan did so to protect their own interests, not Korea's, and Korean officials had no seat at the table. Ministers who had cooperated with Japan found their positions weakened, while those who had resisted felt vindicated. The court responded cautiously. It did not break with the cabinet, but it slowed measures it disliked, protected officials it trusted, and increased contact with the Russian representative and others. Inoue's attempt to hold influence through the loan and through direct appeals to the king had less effect than he hoped, and he was replaced in September 1895 by a successor with a different view of how Japan should act.

Section 52.19

Gojong, Queen Min, and the Search for Alternative Foreign Support

Gojong and Queen Min were not passive objects of foreign policy. They were experienced political actors who had managed factions, foreign representatives, and rivals for two decades. After July 1894 their aim was to restore royal authority and security, and they used the instruments available: appointments, personal contacts with foreign diplomats and missionaries, and the exploitation of differences among the powers.

Russia was an important counterweight. The Russian representative, Karl Waeber, had long-standing relations with the court, and the queen and king contacted the Russian legation as Japan's prestige fell. Americans and others in the Jeongdong district, where several legations and missionary institutions were located, also served as contacts, and a group of officials associated with them, sometimes called the Jeongdong faction, developed ties to the court. These were political strategies, not declarations of loyalty to one country. The court sought whichever foreign support would reduce its dependence on Japan, and it preferred to keep several options open.

The queen's role was controversial then and since. Reformers and Japanese officials saw her as an obstacle, a skilled politician who could undermine their plans by working through the court and by cultivating foreign diplomats. Admirers and later Korean nationalists saw her as a defender of the dynasty's independence. Historians generally emphasize that she operated within the logic of a weak state: she looked for balance, and her choices carried risk. The next section shows how the cabinet's divisions gave her opportunities, and how those opportunities increased the danger to her.

The queen's personal network was a political resource. Members of the Min clan and officials she trusted had been removed in 1894, and the court's effort to bring some of them back was part of the contest over the cabinet. Foreign contacts, including legation families and missionaries who attended members of the court, provided channels of information and social access that formal diplomacy did not. Historians caution that these contacts did not amount to a settled pro-Russian policy. Gojong and Queen Min wanted a counterweight, and Russia, which had no garrison in Seoul in 1895, appeared a plausible one. The risk was that every move toward a counterweight looked to Japanese officials like a threat to Japan's position, and that some Japanese observers concluded the queen was the center of the resistance.

Section 52.20

Pak Yeong-hyo, Reform Factions, and the Crisis of the Cabinet

By spring 1895 the coalition cabinet was under strain. Pak Yeong-hyo, as Home Minister, was the most powerful figure in the government and a strong advocate of centralized reform, but his rise alarmed conservatives and the court, and his disagreements with other ministers grew. Questions of royal finance, control of the palace, and the extent of Japanese guidance divided the cabinet, and the court worked to recover authority by bringing in ministers it trusted and sidelining Pak.

In July 1895 the crisis broke. Pak was accused of plotting against the queen, an allegation whose truth is disputed, and an order for his arrest was issued; he fled to Japan. His departure removed the most prominent radical from the government and left Kim Hong-jip's more moderate group, together with officials the court favored, in control. Japanese influence in the cabinet was reduced, and the court's position improved.

The effect was to erode trust between the monarchy and the reform coalition. The court believed that reformers sought to reduce royal power, and reformers believed the court sought to undo reform. Japanese officials, seeing their position weaken, began to consider more drastic options. This breakdown in confidence is essential to understanding how the events of October became possible.

Pak's fall also had a documentary dimension. The accusation that he had plotted against the queen was publicized, an arrest order was issued, and associates were dismissed; Pak himself left Korea for Japan in July. Historians remain divided about how much substance the charge had. Some see it as an effective instrument for removing a rival when the court regained confidence; others note that Pak's relationship with the court had indeed become adversarial and that the queen had reason to fear his plans. In either case the cabinet was weaker and the court emboldened, and reform ministers who remained, including Kim Hong-jip, faced a court that no longer trusted them and a Japanese legation that no longer trusted the court.

Historical Debate

How Autonomous Were Korean Reformers under Japanese Military Pressure?

The question: Were the Gabo Reforms primarily a Korean initiative that Japan facilitated, or a program driven by Japanese interests and carried out by Korean officials with limited choice?

Interpretation A — Korean agency emphasized: Some historians stress that many reform measures echo Korean proposals from the Gapsin period and earlier, that Korean officials drafted and debated the resolutions, and that the abolition of status and the examinations answered domestic criticism. On this view, describing the reforms as Japanese impositions understates Korean initiative.
Interpretation B — Japanese constraint emphasized: Others stress that the reforms were enacted under military occupation, that Japanese advisers shaped many measures, and that some provisions, such as those on railways, currency, and finance, served Japanese interests. On this view, Korean agency operated within limits set by a foreign power.
Interpretation C — Differentiated by measure and phase: A third approach, increasingly common, holds that the question cannot be answered globally. Social-status and legal reforms may have had stronger Korean roots, while fiscal, military, and diplomatic measures reflected greater Japanese influence, and the balance shifted among the three phases.

Evidence: Debates draw on the reform resolutions, Korean and Japanese diplomatic records, memoirs, and comparison with earlier Korean reform proposals. Disagreement concerns interpretation and emphasis more than the basic facts of occupation and advice.

Why it matters: The answer shapes how later generations view the legacy of the reforms: as part of Korean modernization, as an episode of imperial interference, or as both.

Division V — The Assassination of Queen Min and the Crisis of the Monarchy

Section 52.21

Miura Gorō, Japanese Policy, and the Decision to Remove the Queen

In September 1895 Miura Gorō, a retired army lieutenant general, arrived in Seoul to replace Inoue Kaoru as Japanese minister. Inoue had pursued influence through financial assistance and cooperation with reform ministers; the situation after the Triple Intervention and the fall of Pak Yeong-hyo suggested to many Japanese observers that this approach was failing. Miura's tenure was brief, and the operation that bears his name occurred barely five weeks after his arrival.

The documented facts of the planning are fairly well established. Miura and members of his legation, including the chancery secretary Sugimura Fukashi and the adviser Okamoto Ryūnosuke, planned an operation to seize the palace, place the Daewongun at the head of a new arrangement, and remove the queen from politics. Japanese military units from the Seoul garrison, Korean troops of the Japanese-trained Hullyeondae, Japanese police, and a group of Japanese civilians, including journalists and adventurers, were to participate. Accounts indicate that the court's decision to move against the Hullyeondae in early October, by disbanding or disarming it, led planners to act sooner than they had intended.

What remains disputed is the role of higher authorities in Tokyo. Some historians believe that senior officials knew of or tolerated a plan to remove the queen; others argue that Miura and his circle acted on their own initiative in interpreting broad policy goals. The surviving evidence does not conclusively resolve the question. What is not disputed is that the Japanese minister and members of his legation planned and participated in the operation, a point that Japanese authorities themselves implicitly accepted by recalling Miura and his staff.

The planning drew on several channels. Okamoto Ryūnosuke is generally identified as an intermediary to the Daewongun, whose cooperation was sought because his presence could give the operation a Korean and royal-family face; Lieutenant Colonel Umayabara Muhon, commanding the Japanese battalion in Seoul, is reported to have helped organize the military element; and civilians connected to the Japanese-language newspaper Hanseong Sinbo and other adventurers were recruited. These details come from the later Japanese inquiry, diplomatic reports, memoirs, and testimony, whose reliability varies. Participation by legation staff in recruiting and directing the operation is the most solidly documented element, while the details of who gave which order are less certain. The decision to proceed appears to have been driven by the Hullyeondae's anticipated disbandment, the court's growing independence, and Japanese fears of losing the influence for which the war had been fought.

Section 52.22

October 8, 1895: The Assault on Gyeongbokgung

In the early hours of October 8, 1895 (the twentieth day of the eighth lunar month), the Daewongun was brought from his residence at Gongdeok-ri, outside the city, by Japanese and Korean escorts, and a column moved toward Gyeongbokgung. The force comprised units of the Japanese garrison, Korean Hullyeondae troops, Japanese police, and armed Japanese civilians, several dozen of them, wearing ordinary dress and carrying swords. The accounts of the participants and witnesses differ in details, and the exact numbers remain uncertain.

Near dawn the column reached the palace gates. The palace guard, the Siwidae, offered resistance. Its commander, Hong Gye-hun, was killed in the fighting. Two foreign witnesses inside the palace, the American military adviser William McEntyre Dye and the Russian architect Afanasy Seredin-Sabatin, observed the entry of troops and armed men and later testified to what they saw. By the time the intruders reached the inner buildings, the guard's resistance had collapsed.

The attackers searched the buildings where the king and queen resided. Gojong and the crown prince were confronted, and members of the intruding party pressed the king and his entourage. The queen, who was living in the Geoncheonggung complex, was found in or near the Okhoru pavilion and killed by the attackers, along with other women of the palace. Her body was removed and, according to accounts, partly burned in a nearby grove. The identification of the queen was made by people who knew her, including court women. The killing occurred in the presence of the palace's inhabitants, and the intruders left only after the deed was done and the Daewongun and a supportive cabinet were in place.

The assault was a political killing carried out inside the dynasty's principal palace by agents who included members of a foreign diplomatic mission. Its implications went beyond the death of one person. It violated the physical sanctity of the monarchy, demonstrated that Japanese influence could be exercised by force even after the Triple Intervention, and made the position of the reform government impossible to defend. The next section examines the evidence.

Several features of the event should be stressed. The operation was timed for the hours before dawn and relied on surprise and on Korean units as well as Japanese ones, so that the Japanese role could later be minimized; the presence of the Daewongun was intended to support the claim that the action was a Korean palace change. Foreign eyewitnesses undermined that claim by describing Japanese soldiers and men in civilian dress moving through the palace. Accounts place Miura at the palace later in the morning, and the Japanese legation quickly circulated a version attributing the violence to a Korean dispute. The palace's Korean inhabitants, including the women who served the queen, were both the primary victims and primary witnesses, and their testimony, recorded under difficult conditions, is among the most important evidence for what happened inside the buildings.

Section 52.23

Witnesses, Documentary Evidence, and the Investigation of the Assassination

The assassination was investigated almost at once, by several parties with differing motives. Foreign representatives in Seoul, including the Russian, American, and British diplomats, collected statements and reported to their governments. The Japanese government recalled Miura and members of his legation and sent an official, Komura Jutarō, to investigate. Japanese authorities in Hiroshima then conducted a preliminary judicial inquiry into the civilian suspects, Miura among them, while military personnel faced separate proceedings under military justice. The preliminary inquiry's decision of January 20, 1896 released the civilian suspects on the ground that the evidence was insufficient to proceed to trial for the killing, and the military proceedings likewise ended without conviction. As scholarly treatments emphasize, this was not a full criminal trial that tested the evidence and established innocence; it was a screening process whose own documentation recorded substantial involvement in the operation while declining to send anyone forward for the killing itself.

The Korean government, under cabinet pressure, also held proceedings. A Korean court judgment sentenced three Korean defendants, Pak Seon, Yi Ju-hoe, and Yun Seok-u, to hang for involvement in the October 8 assassination; the judgment's reasoning survives in the National Institute of Korean History's database, and a French diplomatic report of December 31, 1895 records the three death sentences and their publication in the official gazette. Many contemporaries and later historians have read these proceedings as an attempt to place responsibility on Koreans and so to protect the Japanese participants, a reading supported by the contradiction between the judgment's account and the testimony of foreign eyewitnesses. Students should treat the sentencing record carefully: it documents what the court found and published, but it is not independent proof of each allegation in the judgment, and by itself it does not verify that each sentence was carried out.

Students should distinguish legal outcome from historical evidence. A finding that evidence is insufficient to prosecute named individuals is not a finding that the event did not occur, and it does not establish innocence. The historical record, drawn from independent foreign eyewitnesses, diplomatic reports, the Japanese inquiry's own documentation, and Korean testimony, establishes that the queen was murdered on October 8, 1895 in an operation involving Japanese legation personnel and military and civilian participants, with Miura Gorō as the senior Japanese official directing events locally. Questions about individual responsibility for the fatal act and about authorization from Tokyo remain matters of evidentiary limits, not grounds to treat the assassination as an unproven allegation.

Section 52.24

The Monarchy after the Assassination

Gojong's position after October 8 was precarious. His wife had been killed in front of his household, his father had been brought to the palace by those responsible, and a cabinet under Kim Hong-jip, whose members had to decide how to respond, remained in office. In the first days, under pressure, edicts were issued in the king's name that reduced the queen to the status of a commoner, an action that foreign diplomats and the court soon pressed to reverse; her rank was restored within weeks, and a full funeral would wait until 1897 (Chapter 53).

Reports indicate that the king feared for his own life and took precautions with food, relying on sealed supplies provided by foreign residents. Whether or not every anecdote is accurate, the pattern shows a monarch effectively confined. The Daewongun withdrew from politics, and Kim Hong-jip and his ministers faced an impossible position. They were committed to reform, but were identified with a government that had come to power through Japanese force and survived the murder of the queen. Those who remained in office have been criticized for failing to resign, and defended for trying to preserve the reform institutions and protect the king.

The consequence was a collapse in the legitimacy of reform. Whatever its merits, reform was now associated in many Koreans' minds with foreign violence against the dynasty.

The cabinet's dilemma was acute. Resignation would have left the king without ministers he could rely on and might have opened the way to a government composed of the operation's sponsors; remaining in office meant presiding over a government whose legitimacy was destroyed. Kim Hong-jip chose to remain, and the foreign representatives, who pressed for the queen's rank to be restored and for the guilty to be punished, dealt with his government as the available interlocutor. Historians judge the choice differently, as political courage in difficult circumstances or misplaced reliance on a position that could not be saved. The king's isolation increased the importance of the foreign legations, where members of the court could speak more freely and where plans for a rescue or escape were discussed in the following months.

Section 52.25

Korean and International Reactions to the Assassination

Reactions were swift and varied. Foreign diplomats in Seoul protested; their governments, including Russia, the United States, and Britain, pressed Japan for an explanation, and Japan, facing international criticism, recalled Miura and investigated. The diplomatic cost to Japan was substantial, and Russia's standing at the Korean court rose.

Among Koreans, officials in the Jeongdong faction, scholars, and provincial elites reacted with horror and anger. Confucian scholars submitted memorials and circulated letters demanding vengeance and the end of Japanese influence. Local communities, already unsettled by the reforms, heard accounts of the murder and of the king's captivity. The event transformed existing resentment into a moral cause: avenging the queen and rescuing the king became grounds for armed action. When the haircut order arrived two months later, these grievances combined.

Reactions were not uniform. Some reform officials, shocked by the killing, nonetheless continued to serve in the hope of preserving reforms; others turned against the government. Some foreign residents expressed sympathy for the queen, others considered her a political obstacle. The common effect was to widen the gap between the reformers and the society they sought to transform.

The effect on Japan's international position was considerable. Western diplomats and press reports condemned the killing, and the Japanese government, anxious about its standing, disavowed the operation, recalled its minister, and ordered the inquiry discussed above. Within Japan there was public criticism of the affair as well as sympathy for the accused in some circles. In Korea, memorials from scholars and officials called for punishment of those responsible and restoration of the queen's rank, and linked the murder to broader complaints about the reforms. The Jeongdong group and others at court used the outrage to build support for the king's rescue. The murder thus became a political resource for opposition movements with different aims, from loyalist Confucians to officials who wished to reverse Japanese influence.

Intervention, Reform, Assassination, Resistance, and Collapse

Long-term causes

Fiscal weakness, factional politics, status-based grievances, an earlier tradition of Korean reform thinking, and regional rivalry over Korea's international position.

Short-term causes

The Donghak crisis, the arrival of Qing and Japanese troops, Japanese demands for reform, and the court's attempt to forestall them through the Gyojeongcheong.

Immediate trigger

The July 1894 palace seizure created a reform government under occupation; the Triple Intervention and Pak Yeong-hyo's fall in 1895 then shifted the balance and prompted Japanese planners to act.

Event and immediate effects

The October 1895 assassination destroyed the legitimacy of reform; the haircut order of December sparked widespread armed resistance.

Long-term consequences

Gojong's flight in February 1896, the collapse of the cabinet, the rise of Russian influence, and a lasting association of reform with foreign coercion, alongside durable institutional change.

Historical complexity

No single actor or decision explains the outcome. Domestic opposition, great-power rivalry, factional conflict, and individual choices interacted.

Division VI — The Eulmi Reforms, Popular Resistance, and the Collapse of the Reform Government

Section 52.26

The Eulmi Reforms and the Accelerated Transformation of Government

After October 1895 the Kim Hong-jip cabinet continued to issue reforms, often at greater speed and under heavier Japanese influence. Historians call these measures the Eulmi Reforms. They included the adoption of the solar calendar, with January 1, 1896 replacing the lunar date of the seventeenth day of the eleventh month, and a new era name, Geonyang, replacing the Gaeguk reckoning. Other measures included smallpox vaccination regulations, a reintroduced postal service, the reorganization of the army, including a capital guard, and further educational and administrative regulation.

Several of these measures had been prepared before the assassination, and it is important to distinguish those from ones introduced or accelerated afterward. Postal reform, educational regulation, and aspects of military reorganization belonged to the earlier program. The calendar change and the haircut order were new, and they were politically explosive. The cabinet may have believed that rapid action would consolidate reform before it could be reversed; its opponents saw an illegitimate government imposing changes on a captive king. The result was to link practical reforms, such as vaccination and postal service, with the most contested cultural impositions, and to damage the credibility of both.

Several Eulmi measures were practical in character. Smallpox vaccination regulations responded to a recurrent public-health threat, and the reintroduced postal service addressed the need for reliable communication. Military reorganization created a capital guard and provincial garrisons under central command, and the educational system continued to expand. Some of these measures persisted after the cabinet fell. Yet they were introduced by a government widely seen as illegitimate, at the moment it demanded visible obedience from the population. The calendar change likewise had practical logic, since it aligned Korea with the international system of diplomacy and trade, but it disrupted ritual observances and household practices tied to the lunar calendar. The combination of useful reform and abrupt imposition made it hard for the population to distinguish measures worth accepting from measures worth resisting.

Section 52.27

The Chunsaengmun Incident and the Failed Attempt to Rescue Gojong

On November 28, 1895 (the twelfth day of the tenth lunar month), a group of Korean officials and military officers attempted to rescue Gojong from the palace and overthrow the cabinet. The Academy of Korean Studies' reconstruction identifies Yi Do-cheol, a major, and Yi Min-goeng (李敏宏), a captain, among the officers involved in the conspiracy, while civil officials such as Yi Jae-sun, Im Choe-su, and Ahn Gyeong-su were connected to its organization. Company commanders Nam Man-ri and Yi Gyu-hong were also drawn into leading soldiers, although a contemporary report depicts their participation as compelled under threat rather than freely volunteered. The sources therefore require a distinction between committed planners and officers whose cooperation was obtained under pressure. Sources also link figures associated with the Jeongdong group, among them Yi Beom-jin and Yi Wan-yong, to the plot's aftermath, when several participants sought refuge in foreign legations, and foreign residents, including American missionaries, are reported to have given some support. The plan was to approach the palace through Chunsaengmun and other northern access points, reach the king, and form a new government; the attempted entry and the disposition of participating units differed among contemporary accounts.

The attempt failed, and a decisive factor was betrayal from inside the military. Yi Jin-ho, a battalion commander, disclosed the operation, and the defenders were prepared; participating troops were repulsed and the plot collapsed. The episode is a reminder that the armed forces in Seoul were divided: not all officers sympathized with the conspirators, and some owed their careers to the cabinet, so the participants cannot be treated as a single loyalist force. After the failure some plotters fled to foreign legations and others were arrested.

The consequence was to deepen royal insecurity. Gojong, who may have known of the plan, was more closely watched, and confidence in an internal rescue diminished, leaving escape with foreign assistance as the more plausible option. The incident also shows that opposition to the cabinet came from within the Korean elite as well as from provincial scholars, and that the court's friends were prepared to risk violence.

The surviving accounts require particular caution in assigning political loyalties. The reform government had an interest in portraying the episode as a criminal attempt to seize the sovereign, while the participants and their supporters might present it as obedience to the king. Contemporary reports differ over the number of men involved and the degree of advance agreement among officers. The French diplomatic reporting associated with the affair, for example, describes commanders pressured to mobilize soldiers at sword point. Such testimony does not erase the planning of the operation, but it warns against assuming that every man in an advancing column had independently committed himself to a shared program.

Section 52.28

The Haircut Decree, Confucian Resistance, and the Eulmi Righteous Armies

On December 30, 1895, Gojong cut his hair and issued the danbalnyeong, an order requiring adult men to cut their topknots. Officials, including Home Minister Yu Gil-jun, were sent to enforce it, and in Seoul soldiers and police cut topknots at city gates. The decree was presented as hygienic and practical, consistent with the adoption of Western-style dress and administration, and the king's own haircut was meant as an example.

The topknot had deep meaning. Confucian teaching, particularly the classic on filial piety, taught that one's body, hair, and skin were received from one's parents and should not be harmed. To cut the hair was to violate filial duty and, in the eyes of many, to abandon civilization for the practices of foreigners. Coming two months after the queen's murder and under a government identified with Japan, the order appeared to confirm that reform was an assault on Korean moral order. Resistance came from Confucian scholars and local elites who combined this cultural grievance with demands to avenge the queen and restore the king.

In the winter of 1895–1896 the uibyeong, or righteous armies, rose in several regions: in Gangwon around Chuncheon, in Chungcheong around Jecheon, in North Gyeongsang around Andong, and later in the southwest. Leaders often came from the scholar class, and many fighters were peasants. They attacked local officials who enforced the decree and clashed with government troops. The movement cannot be reduced to opposition to modernization. Many participants accepted or tolerated some reforms, and the issue was less change itself than the circumstances and the government that imposed it. The distinction between opposing a particular reform, opposing a regime, and opposing modernization altogether is essential, and the righteous armies reflected primarily the first two.

Leadership and organization varied by region. In Chuncheon the movement was led by Yi So-eung and in Jecheon by Yu In-seok, among others, drawing on networks of scholars, local notables, and former soldiers. Their manifestos combined three themes: vengeance for the queen, restoration of the king's freedom, and rejection of the haircut order, and they often stressed loyalty to the throne rather than rebellion against it. Government forces sent against them were drawn partly from the Seoul garrison, which reduced the force available to guard the capital. Local officials who had enforced the haircut order were attacked in some districts, while others quietly cooperated with the resistance. The movement thus affected the cabinet's fall directly, by drawing away troops, and indirectly, by demonstrating the extent of public hostility.

Historical Vocabulary
Danbalnyeong and Uibyeong

Danbalnyeong (斷髮令) was the haircut order of December 30, 1895 requiring the cutting of the topknot (sangtu). Uibyeong (義兵), "righteous armies," were local militias raised on moral grounds, with a tradition reaching back at least to the Imjin War (Chapter 34). The Eulmi uibyeong of 1895–1896 were the first major wave of such forces in the modern crisis, and later waves followed after 1905 (Chapter 54).

Potential misunderstanding: The haircut order is sometimes described as a trivial issue of fashion. For contemporaries it concerned filial duty, social identity, and the authority of a government many regarded as illegitimate.

Section 52.29

February 11, 1896: Gojong's Flight to the Russian Legation and the Fall of the Cabinet

By early February 1896 the capital's defenses were thin: government troops had been sent against the righteous armies, and the king's allies saw an opening. Russian sailors had been brought to Seoul at the Russian representative's request. In the early morning of February 11 (the twenty-eighth day of the twelfth lunar month), Gojong and the crown prince left Gyeongbokgung, according to later accounts concealed in palanquins of the type used by palace women, and proceeded to the Russian legation in Jeongdong. The success of the escape depended on arrangements with palace officials and guards, and the cabinet learned of it only after the king was safely in the legation.

Russian participation was a precondition of the flight. A detachment of Russian sailors had been brought to Seoul a few days earlier at the representative's request, and the legation's willingness to receive the king had evidently been arranged in advance through court intermediaries. Japanese officials, who learned of the flight only afterward, could not reverse it without confronting Russia. In the months that followed, Japan and Russia negotiated understandings about their positions in Korea, but the immediate result was that the king governed by decree from foreign premises while the ministers of the fallen cabinet were hunted in the streets. For about a year the legation was the political center of Korea, an arrangement many Koreans found humiliating even as they welcomed the end of the cabinet.

From the legation, Gojong issued edicts dismissing the Kim Hong-jip government, denouncing Kim and several other ministers as traitors and ordering their arrest. Kim Hong-jip was seized and killed by a crowd in the street, as was Jeong Byeong-ha; Eo Yun-jung was killed while fleeing the capital. Yu Gil-jun and Jo Hui-yeon escaped to Japan. A new government was announced in which Kim Byeong-si was named prime minister, with Pak Jeong-yang serving as acting prime minister and interior minister, and officials associated with the Jeongdong group took prominent positions. The king also asked the righteous armies to disband and moved to reverse the haircut order, although the solar calendar was retained.

The consequences were large. The Japanese position in Korea was set back, Russian influence rose sharply, and the king governed from foreign premises for about a year. The killing of Kim Hong-jip, a moderate and incorruptible official, stands as one of the period's tragedies. Yet the fall of the cabinet did not end state modernization. Many institutions survived, and the Korean Empire of 1897 would pursue reform under royal direction. What collapsed was a particular regime, compromised by its origins in foreign force. The unresolved question was whether a monarch sheltered in a foreign legation could recover effective sovereignty, which Chapter 53 addresses.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the nineteen months between the Japanese seizure of Gyeongbokgung on July 23, 1894 and Gojong's flight to the Russian legation on February 11, 1896. Its central argument is that institutional modernization and political sovereignty are different things. Korea's governing institutions were rewritten more extensively than at any time since the dynasty's founding, yet the monarchy lost control of its own capital, and the government that carried out the reforms lost legitimacy in the process.

The reforms began in a situation of crisis. After the Donghak uprising and the arrival of Qing and Japanese forces, Japan demanded reform, and the Korean court responded with the Gyojeongcheong in July 1894. When Japanese troops seized the palace, the Daewongun was installed and the Gunguk Gimucheo, led by Kim Hong-jip, became the engine of the First Gabo Reforms. Korean reformers had programs of their own, and Japan had interests of its own, and the two overlapped without being identical.

The First Gabo Reforms reorganized central government into a Council of State and eight ministries, separated royal household from state, abolished legal distinctions between yangban and commoners, freed the nobi and prohibited trafficking in persons, ended the gwageo, regularized taxation, and altered marriage and family law. Their significance lay in setting new legal principles, even though implementation was partial and social hierarchies persisted, as the distinction between laws announced, institutions established, reforms administered, and changes experienced makes clear.

The Second Gabo phase, under a coalition cabinet with the returned exile Pak Yeong-hyo, produced the Hongbeom Fourteen Articles of January 7, 1895, a constitutional-style statement of aims, together with judicial, police, and educational measures, including the Education Rescript and the first modern schools. The reform coalition was never united, and the declaration of independence from Qing coexisted with Japanese military presence and financial influence.

The Triple Intervention of April 1895 weakened Japan's prestige, and Gojong and Queen Min sought to use the opening to recover authority by courting Russia and others. Pak Yeong-hyo's fall in July eroded the reform coalition and the confidence between court and cabinet. Japanese planners under the new minister, Miura Gorō, decided on drastic action.

On October 8, 1895, Queen Min was assassinated at Gyeongbokgung in an operation involving Japanese legation personnel, military units, police, Korean troops, and civilians. Foreign eyewitnesses and diplomatic reports establish this, though the Hiroshima preliminary inquiry of January 20, 1896 released the civilian suspects for insufficient evidence and separate military proceedings ended without conviction, and the question of authorization from Tokyo remains debated. The murder destroyed the legitimacy of the reform government and intensified opposition at all levels of Korean society.

The Eulmi phase accelerated reform under heavy pressure, adopting the solar calendar and a new era name and reorganizing the army, while the Chunsaengmun attempt to rescue the king failed. The haircut order of December 30, 1895, touching filial duty and identity, combined with outrage over the queen's murder to produce the Eulmi righteous armies, whose opposition was directed more at the regime and its methods than at all modernization.

On February 11, 1896, Gojong escaped to the Russian legation, the cabinet fell, and Kim Hong-jip and other ministers were killed. The king reversed the haircut order and urged the righteous armies to disband, while the solar calendar and many institutions survived. The fall of the cabinet ended one compromised regime but not the effort to build a modern state, and the question of how a monarch sheltering in a foreign legation could recover sovereignty leads directly to the Korean Empire of Chapter 53.

Chapter Reflection

Chapter 52 matters because it shows, in compressed form, a problem that recurs across modern Korean history: how a society can transform its institutions while its political independence is under threat. The Gabo and Eulmi reforms are not easily summarized as success or failure. Many of their principles, including legal equality, a separation between household and state, and education as a public responsibility, became part of the standard vocabulary of Korean politics. Yet the circumstances of their adoption linked reform in many minds to foreign coercion.

The chapter also reveals the limits of narratives that make Korea either a passive object or an unproblematic protagonist. Korean reformers, courtiers, scholars, and commoners made real choices in constrained conditions, and their disagreements shaped outcomes as much as foreign policy did. The assassination of Queen Min was a deliberate act by a foreign mission, but its consequences passed through Korean institutions, factions, and communities, producing the Chunsaengmun plot, the righteous armies, and the king's flight.

The events of 1894–1896 shaped what followed. The year in the Russian legation, the proclamation of empire in 1897, and the intensifying rivalry among Japan, Russia, and other powers all grew from this period. The memory of the queen's murder remained a touchstone of Korean resentment, and the righteous-army tradition continued after 1905 (Chapter 54). Chapter 53 asks whether a state that had learned the difference between modernization and sovereignty could build both.
