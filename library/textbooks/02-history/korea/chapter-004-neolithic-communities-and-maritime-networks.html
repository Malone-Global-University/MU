Neolithic Communities and Maritime Networks

Korean History: Ancient Kingdoms to a Divided Peninsula

Chapter Opening

On Jeju Island, at a coastal site called Gosan-ri, archaeologists have recovered plain, coarse, plant-fiber-tempered pottery that current dating places at approximately 7800 to 7500 BCE, the earliest securely dated pottery yet found anywhere on the Korean peninsula. No substantial village architecture has been securely associated with this earliest pottery, a detail that itself carries an important lesson: pottery, permanent settlement, and farming did not arrive together as a single "Neolithic package," but developed unevenly, at different times, in different combinations, across many centuries and many regions.

Chapter 3 established that the Holocene, the current geological epoch, began roughly 11,700 years ago, close to 9700 BCE, and that postglacial warming and rising seas were already reshaping Northeast Asian coastlines before and after that boundary. It also established that Korea's archaeologically documented Neolithic begins somewhat later than the Holocene boundary itself, with Gosan-ri-type pottery on Jeju marking the earliest secure evidence. This chapter picks up that handoff directly, tracing the long, regionally uneven process by which peninsular communities adopted pottery, developed increasingly persistent patterns of settlement, intensified their exploitation of coastal, riverine, and terrestrial resources, and eventually incorporated cultivated plants into diets that remained, for most of the period, built on fishing, hunting, shellfish collection, and gathering.

This chapter's subject spans roughly four and a half thousand years, from the appearance of Gosan-ri-type pottery around 7800 BCE to the gradual transition, beginning around 1500 BCE and continuing at different paces in different regions, toward the more intensively agricultural, bronze-using, and socially stratified societies examined in Chapter 5. None of the communities described in this chapter constituted a unified political society, and none would have recognized any of the later names historians and Koreans themselves would eventually apply to the peninsula. This chapter studies them on their own terms, using archaeological evidence rather than later national narrative.

A central theme of this chapter, signaled by its title, is that the Korean peninsula was never a sealed or isolated space. Even during the Neolithic, before any writing system, any centralized state, or any well-documented overland trade route connected the peninsula to the Eurasian continental interior, its communities were linked by sea to communities living along the opposite shores of the Yellow Sea, across the Korea Strait in the Japanese archipelago, and northward along the coasts of Manchuria, the Russian Far East, and the northeastern peninsula itself. This chapter treats the surviving physical evidence for that connection, including a remarkably preserved wooden logboat recovered at Bibong-ri, obsidian sourced through geochemical analysis to specific volcanic origins in Kyushu, and shell ornaments whose possible movement in the opposite direction remains debated, as some of its most important material.

Archaeologists commonly divide the Korean Neolithic using pottery styles and regional sequences as primary markers, since pottery survives well in the archaeological record, changes recognizably over time, and often varies meaningfully by region. This chapter introduces Gosan-ri-type plant-fiber-tempered pottery, subsequent raised-design pottery traditions, the better-known Jeulmun comb-pattern traditions that became especially prominent in several regions during the fourth millennium BCE, and the plainer, double-rimmed styles associated with the Neolithic-to-Bronze-Age transition, while cautioning students against treating "Jeulmun" and "comb-pattern pottery" as simple synonyms for the entire period, or against assuming that any single pottery sequence adequately represents the whole peninsula.

This chapter also introduces a range of major sites reaching across the peninsula rather than concentrating narrowly on its southern half: Gosan-ri on Jeju; Amsa-dong on the Han River; Osan-ni on the eastern coast; Dongsam-dong and the Gadeokdo/Janghang cemetery site near modern Busan; Bibong-ri at Changnyeong in the southeastern interior; and Seopohang in the northeastern Rason region, whose multiple cultural layers have long been important for comparing Korean Neolithic chronology with neighboring Primorye and northeastern China. Each contributes different kinds of evidence about diet, technology, settlement, exchange, and mortuary practice, and together they resist the tendency, common in some popular accounts, to let a handful of well-known southern sites stand in for the entire peninsula.

Students should approach this chapter with the same historical caution introduced in Chapters 1 and 3. Neolithic communities on the Korean peninsula were not "ancient Koreans" in any national sense, and applying that label to them would be anachronistic. At the same time, their technologies, settlement patterns, and maritime connections established long-term patterns, sustained interaction with continental and maritime Northeast Asia, intensifying exploitation of the peninsula's varied coastal and riverine environments, and gradually broadening subsistence strategies, that would continue, in transformed ways, through the Bronze Age societies examined in Chapter 5 and into the early states examined in Chapter 6 and Part II.

Finally, this chapter asks students to consider, once again, what kind of evidence allows historians to make claims about a period with no writing. Archaeological excavation, radiocarbon dating, archaeobotanical and faunal analysis, geochemical sourcing of materials such as obsidian, and comparative study of pottery and stone tools across regions together allow careful, if always provisional, reconstruction of Neolithic life. This chapter models that evidentiary reasoning throughout, distinguishing well-supported conclusions from more tentative or contested interpretations, and paying particular attention to where the archaeological record is uneven, incomplete, or shaped by the differing excavation and publication histories of South Korea and North Korea.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how pottery, settlement, resource use, and cultivation combined with, rather than instantly replaced, older Paleolithic foraging practices during the Korean Neolithic.
  2. Identify Gosan-ri-type pottery as Korea's earliest securely dated pottery tradition and explain its significance for understanding the pace of Neolithic change.
  3. Describe the broader sequence of Neolithic pottery traditions, including raised-design pottery, classic Jeulmun comb-pattern pottery, and later plainer styles, without treating them as a single uniform tradition.
  4. Identify major Neolithic sites across the peninsula, including Gosan-ri, Amsa-dong, Osan-ni, Dongsam-dong, Bibong-ri, Gadeokdo/Janghang, and Seopohang, and explain what each contributes to historical understanding.
  5. Analyze the role of shell middens as evidence for diet, seasonal settlement, and regional variation in coastal subsistence.
  6. Trace the gradual, regionally variable emergence of cultivation, particularly of millet, alongside continued fishing, hunting, and gathering.
  7. Explain why the Yellow Sea and Korea Strait functioned as maritime corridors connecting Neolithic communities, using evidence such as the Bibong-ri logboat, sourced obsidian, cross-strait pottery movement, and debated shell-ornament connections.
  8. Distinguish archaeological evidence of contact and exchange from claims about shared ethnic or political identity, direct voyages, or a single trade route.
  9. Describe Neolithic dwellings, tools, and technology, and explain what they reveal about daily life without overstating what they reveal about kinship or social ranking.
  10. Evaluate competing scholarly framings of the timing, mechanisms, and relative importance of early cultivation on the peninsula.
  11. Assess the strengths and limitations of archaeological evidence, including the uneven publication and access history separating North Korean and South Korean excavations.
  12. Connect Neolithic settlement, exchange, and technological patterns to the Bronze Age transformations examined in Chapter 5.

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  2. How do archaeologists use regional pottery sequences to reconstruct chronology without assuming one sequence applies uniformly to the whole peninsula?
  3. What can shell middens and cemetery sites such as Gadeokdo/Janghang reveal about diet, seasonal movement, and community organization?

Historical Context

Chapter 3 examined the Paleolithic peninsula, tracing evidence of human occupation extending back tens of thousands of years, the development of stone tool technologies, and the environmental changes accompanying the end of the last glacial period. It established that the conventional Pleistocene-to-Holocene boundary falls at roughly 11,700 years ago, close to 9700 BCE, but that postglacial warming and sea-level rise were already underway before that boundary and continued through the Early Holocene. It also noted that during the Last Glacial Maximum the maritime gap across what is now the Korea/Tsushima Strait had narrowed considerably compared to the present day, without treating a complete dry land connection as securely established. This chapter maintains that same careful language rather than reintroducing a firmer land-bridge claim.

The term "Neolithic," literally "New Stone Age," is a periodization label developed by nineteenth-century European archaeologists and subsequently adapted, with varying degrees of fit, to archaeological sequences around the world. On the Korean peninsula, as in much of Northeast Asia, the label is most usefully understood not as marking a single sharp transition to agriculture, as it originally did in some Near Eastern contexts, but as describing a long period characterized by the adoption of pottery, increasingly persistent habitation, polished stone tools, and, in its later phases, the gradual incorporation of cultivated plants into a subsistence base that remained heavily oriented toward fishing, hunting, shellfish collection, and gathering. Students should treat "Neolithic," like "Jeulmun," the term Korean archaeologists commonly use for the same broad period, as an analytical convenience rather than a label the peninsula's inhabitants would have recognized or applied to themselves.

The Korean Neolithic is generally dated from approximately 8000 to 7500 BCE, when the earliest pottery-bearing sites appear, through roughly 1500 BCE, when bronze technology and more intensive, settled agriculture began to appear in the archaeological record, developments examined in Chapter 5. These dates remain approximate and vary somewhat by region and by the specific archaeological criteria different scholars apply; students should treat them as a general framework rather than as precise boundaries. As with the Paleolithic sequence discussed in Chapter 3, the archaeological record for this period comes disproportionately from South Korea, reflecting decades of difference in excavation opportunity, international collaboration, and publication access between South Korea and North Korea; this chapter draws attention to that imbalance directly rather than allowing it to pass unremarked.

Geographically, this chapter's subject extends across the entire Korean peninsula, including Jeju and other islands, with particular attention to its extensive coastlines along the Yellow Sea to the west, the Korea Strait and East Sea to the south and east, and its major river systems, especially the Han, the Nakdong, and the Amnok and Duman in the north, introduced in Chapter 2. Neolithic settlement concentrated disproportionately along coasts and river valleys, reflecting the importance of aquatic resources, transportation, and, in the later Neolithic, suitable land for early cultivation.

Section 4.1

From Paleolithic Foraging to New Combinations

Chapter 3 closed with an explicit warning against imagining a sharp break between the Paleolithic and what followed it: hunting, gathering, mobility, and stone tool traditions developed over the preceding millennia did not disappear at some fixed date but persisted, in modified form, well into the Neolithic and, in some respects, throughout the entire span this chapter covers. Rather than describing Neolithic life as "fundamentally different" from what came before, this chapter frames the transition as a gradual layering of new elements, pottery, more persistent or repeated occupation of favored locations, intensified aquatic-resource use, polished stone technologies, storage, wider exchange, and, eventually, cultivation, onto older foraging practices that never fully disappeared.

The environmental backdrop for this layering process was the continued warming and sea-level rise of the Early Holocene, already introduced in Chapter 3. As temperatures rose and previously exposed continental shelves became submerged, the Yellow Sea and the Korea Strait widened and deepened relative to their glacial-era extent, and the peninsula's coastline gradually approached its modern configuration, a process not fully complete until approximately 4000 BCE. These changes created extensive new estuarine and coastal environments rich in shellfish, fish, and marine mammals, and archaeologists generally connect the adoption of pottery, one of the Neolithic period's defining technological markers, to the opportunities and demands created by this widening range of exploitable foods, since pottery vessels are particularly useful for cooking, storing, and processing shellfish, nuts, and, later, cultivated plants.

Sedentism, meaning settled, non-mobile habitation, is often treated as a defining feature of the Neolithic, but the concept requires careful handling on the Korean peninsula. Many Neolithic communities probably combined a degree of residential stability, repeated or extended use of favored locations such as productive shell midden sites, with continued seasonal mobility tied to the changing availability of fish, shellfish, game, and wild plants. Archaeologists therefore increasingly describe Neolithic settlement in terms of degrees of residential persistence rather than a simple binary between mobile foragers and settled farmers, a framing this chapter maintains throughout.

The archaeological evidence for these gradual changes comes primarily from stratified sites, locations where successive layers of occupation debris allow archaeologists to reconstruct sequences of change over time, calibrated wherever possible through radiocarbon dating. As with the Paleolithic evidence discussed in Chapter 3, dates for the earlier Korean Neolithic remain subject to some revision as dating techniques and sample quality improve, and this chapter flags dates as approximate wherever appropriate.

Section 4.2

Gosan-ri, Early Pottery, and the Development of Jeulmun Traditions

Korea's earliest securely dated pottery comes not from the peninsula's better-known river-valley or southeastern coastal sites but from Gosan-ri on Jeju Island, where archaeologists have recovered coarse, plain pottery tempered with plant fiber rather than the mineral tempering typical of later traditions. Recent chronological work places the earliest Gosan-ri-type pottery at approximately 7800 to 7500 BCE, making it several centuries older than pottery traditions long treated as archetypally "Neolithic" elsewhere on the peninsula. Gosan-ri-type pottery is technically and stylistically distinct from the classic comb-pattern Jeulmun pottery examined later in this section, and it should not be described as an early form of comb-pattern pottery.

Just as significant as its early date is what has not been found alongside it: Gosan-ri's earliest pottery-bearing layers are not securely associated with the kind of substantial pit-house architecture documented at later sites such as Amsa-dong. This absence is strong evidence against a simplified model in which pottery, sedentism, and eventually farming arrived together as a single interconnected "Neolithic package." Instead, it suggests that different elements of what archaeologists broadly label "Neolithic" developed on independent, only loosely related timelines, with technological innovations such as pottery sometimes preceding, by a considerable margin, the more settled residential patterns with which they are often popularly associated.

Following Gosan-ri-type pottery, the archaeological record documents a distinct raised-design pottery tradition, appearing broadly across southern and eastern coastal regions from roughly the sixth through the fifth millennium BCE, characterized by applied or raised clay decoration rather than the incised or combed patterns of later Jeulmun pottery. This tradition represents a further, still regionally variable stage in the Korean Neolithic pottery sequence, distinct from both Gosan-ri-type pottery and classic comb-pattern pottery.

The term Jeulmun, meaning "comb-pattern" or "comb-decorated," is most precisely applied to pottery decorated with geometric designs, typically composed of short parallel or angled lines, impressed or incised into the vessel's surface using a comb-like tool or similar implement before firing. This classic comb-pattern style became especially prominent in several regions of the peninsula during the fourth millennium BCE, the period best represented by extensive excavated assemblages at sites such as Amsa-dong. Archaeologists sometimes use "Jeulmun" or "Chulmun" more broadly to label the entire Korean Neolithic period, encompassing Gosan-ri-type pottery, raised-design pottery, classic comb-pattern pottery, and the plainer, often double-rimmed pottery styles that appear toward the Neolithic's end. Students should keep these two uses of the term distinct: the Jeulmun/Chulmun period as a whole chronological label, and classic comb-pattern pottery as one specific, chronologically and regionally bounded tradition within that period.

Archaeologists use these successive pottery traditions, and their many regional and chronological variations, to construct relative chronologies, sequencing sites and assemblages by comparing their pottery styles to one another, a method supplemented and calibrated wherever possible by radiocarbon dating of associated organic material. No single pottery sequence adequately represents the entire peninsula; the sequence sketched here, from Gosan-ri-type pottery through raised-design pottery to classic comb-pattern pottery and later plainer styles, describes a generalized southern and central pattern, and northern sites such as Seopohang, discussed in Section 4.7, show related but not identical sequences of their own.

The broader question of how Jeulmun and related comb-pattern pottery traditions on the peninsula relate to similarly decorated pottery found in Manchuria, the Russian Far East, and parts of the Chinese mainland has generated substantial archaeological discussion, examined further in Section 4.7. This textbook treats these traditions as evidence of both regional distinctiveness and wider Northeast Asian technical and cultural connection, without assuming that shared pottery decoration implies a single shared ethnic identity or political community across the very large geographic area in which broadly related decorated pottery traditions appear.

Historical Vocabulary
Jeulmun, Gosan-ri, and Comb-Pattern Pottery

Definition: "Jeulmun" or "Chulmun," literally "comb-pattern," is used both as a broad label for the entire Korean Neolithic archaeological period and, more narrowly, for classic comb-decorated pottery that became especially prominent during the fourth millennium BCE. "Gosan-ri-type pottery" refers specifically to Korea's earliest known pottery tradition, coarse and plant-fiber-tempered, dated to approximately 7800–7500 BCE and technically distinct from comb-pattern pottery.

Historical context: The Korean Neolithic pottery sequence moves, in a generalized southern and central pattern, from Gosan-ri-type pottery through raised-design pottery to classic Jeulmun comb-pattern pottery and finally to plainer, double-rimmed styles near the Neolithic's end, with meaningful regional variation at every stage, including in the northern peninsula.

Why it matters: Because pottery survives well archaeologically and changes recognizably over time and space, this sequence provides archaeologists with one of their most important tools for constructing chronology and tracing regional variation and contact.

Potential misunderstanding: Students should not treat "Jeulmun" as a synonym for comb-pattern pottery alone, should not assume Gosan-ri-type pottery is an early form of comb-pattern pottery, and should not assume that shared or similar pottery decoration across a wide region automatically indicates a single shared people, language, or political community.

Section 4.3

Coastal Settlement and Shell Midden Communities

Some of the richest evidence for Korean Neolithic life comes from shell middens, accumulated deposits built up over years or generations of discarded shellfish remains, fish bones, animal bones, broken tools, ash, and occasionally human burials, typically located near estuaries, bays, and other productive coastal environments. Shell middens form gradually as successive occupations of a site deposit refuse in roughly the same location, creating stratified layers that archaeologists can excavate and analyze to reconstruct changing patterns of diet, technology, and settlement intensity over time.

Coastal subsistence, however, was not a single uniform system repeated identically around the peninsula's perimeter. Southern-coast sites such as Dongsam-dong, near modern Busan, preserve evidence of a subsistence strategy heavily oriented toward shellfish collection, deep-water and large marine fish, and the hunting of marine mammals, reflecting the relatively open, current-influenced waters of the southeastern coast. Western coastal sites, by contrast, exploited the extensive tidal mudflats characteristic of the Yellow Sea coastline, producing different shellfish assemblages and different opportunities for tidal fishing and shellfish gathering tied to the rhythms of tidal exposure rather than open-water fishing. Eastern coastal and river-mouth communities, including those represented at Osan-ni, drew on still different combinations of marine, estuarine, and freshwater resources shaped by the East Sea's distinct coastal geography. Students should therefore avoid treating any single site, including well-known southern shell middens, as representative of "coastal Neolithic life" across the entire peninsula.

Dongsam-dong remains among the most thoroughly studied shell midden sites, yielding not only extensive shellfish and fish remains but also symbolic and ornamental artifacts, discussed further in Section 4.8, including a well-known carved shell mask and shell ornaments. Analysis of the shellfish species represented in the midden's various layers allows archaeologists to draw inferences about seasonal patterns of occupation, since many shellfish species have distinct seasonal availability and different growth characteristics that leave traces detectable through careful analysis of shell size and structure.

Shell midden sites along the southern and western coasts also provide evidence of fishing technology, including bone and antler fishhooks, net weights, and, at some sites, harpoon points suggesting the hunting of larger marine animals. Stone and bone tools recovered from these contexts indicate a technology adapted specifically to coastal and maritime subsistence, distinct in important respects from the terrestrial hunting toolkits that dominated the preceding Paleolithic and that continued to be used, in modified form, for inland hunting throughout the Neolithic.

The density and continuity of occupation evident at major shell midden sites suggest that at least some Neolithic coastal communities achieved a significant degree of residential persistence, though, as Section 4.1 emphasized, the extent to which any given community remained in one place year-round versus following a seasonal pattern of movement between coastal and inland locations likely varied by region and by period. This question of seasonal mobility versus more persistent settlement remains an active area of archaeological investigation, and definitive conclusions often depend on site-specific evidence rather than broad generalization across the entire Neolithic peninsula.

Section 4.4

Fishing, Gathering, and the Emergence of Cultivation

For most of the Korean Neolithic's several-thousand-year span, communities relied on a broad-based subsistence strategy combining fishing, shellfish collection, hunting of terrestrial game, and the gathering of wild plant foods, including nuts, acorns, and various edible roots and greens. This diversified strategy allowed communities to draw on multiple food sources across different seasons, reducing vulnerability to the failure of any single resource and supporting, at productive coastal and riverine locations, relatively persistent settlement even without agriculture.

Archaeobotanical research, the study of ancient plant remains, typically recovered through flotation techniques that separate carbonized plant material from surrounding soil, indicates that domesticated foxtail millet and broomcorn millet had become archaeologically well established in southern Korea by the Middle Chulmun, roughly the mid-fourth millennium BCE on a calibrated radiocarbon timescale, considerably earlier and more securely than older, more cautious accounts of Korean Neolithic agriculture once suggested. Evidence from several sites indicates what archaeologists describe as mixed resource-producing economies, meaning subsistence systems that combined cultivated crops with continued, often still dominant, reliance on fishing, hunting, shellfish, and wild plant gathering, rather than economies in which cultivation quickly displaced older subsistence strategies.

This chapter frames Neolithic subsistence as a spectrum rather than a single transition. In the Early Neolithic, communities relied overwhelmingly on fishing, hunting, shellfish, nuts, and gathered plants, with little or no evidence of cultivation. By the Middle and Late Neolithic, cultivation of millet, along with the use and possible management of legumes such as soybean and azuki, had become significant in some regions, particularly in parts of the south. Even so, intensive agriculture did not yet displace the diverse, mixed subsistence system characteristic of the period; that further intensification, together with more extensive land clearance and increasingly permanent agricultural villages, is a development more clearly associated with the Bronze Age societies examined in Chapter 5.

Foxtail millet and broomcorn millet were domesticated earlier on the East Asian mainland, and their appearance on the Korean peninsula is best understood within the context of wider Northeast Asian crop dispersal, spreading through interaction networks connecting the peninsula to continental Northeast Asia rather than through an independent, separate process of domestication occurring in isolation on the peninsula itself. Some researchers have proposed that legumes such as soybean and azuki bean may have undergone local processes of use, tending, and possible management on or near the peninsula, a more open question than the mainland origin of millet, and one this chapter treats as a genuine area of ongoing research rather than an established conclusion.

Archaeobotanical analysis, alongside the complementary faunal analysis of animal remains, including bones, shells, and, in some cases, residues preserved on tools, provides the primary evidence for reconstructing Neolithic diet and subsistence. Both forms of analysis require careful, technical interpretation, and conclusions about diet often depend heavily on the preservation conditions and recovery methods specific to individual excavations, meaning that the archaeological record likely underrepresents some food sources, particularly plant foods that preserve less well than shell or bone, and that apparent regional differences in the importance of cultivation may partly reflect differences in how thoroughly sites have been sampled for plant remains rather than differences in ancient diet alone.

Section 4.5

Dwellings, Tools, and Daily Life

Neolithic communities on the peninsula typically constructed pit-houses, dwellings built around a shallow excavated floor, often roughly circular or square in plan, with a superstructure of wooden posts and organic roofing materials that rarely survive archaeologically but can be inferred from surviving post-holes and structural evidence. Excavated pit-houses at sites such as Amsa-dong, where more than twenty Neolithic dwellings have been identified across successive excavation campaigns dating the site's principal occupation to roughly the fourth millennium BCE, reveal interior hearths used for cooking and heating, storage pits for food and materials, and, at some sites, evidence suggesting spatial organization of activities within the dwelling. Students should note that Amsa-dong's well-documented village represents a relatively later and unusually well-preserved phase of Neolithic settlement, not the earliest phase; that chronological distinction belongs to Gosan-ri, examined in Section 4.2.

The clustering of multiple pit-houses at major sites suggests that Neolithic communities often lived in settlements comprising multiple households, though the size, permanence, and internal social organization of these settlements likely varied considerably by region, period, and local environmental productivity. Archaeologists use the number, size, and arrangement of excavated dwellings, along with associated storage and refuse features, to make inferences about population size and settlement organization, while acknowledging that such inferences remain approximate given the incomplete nature of the archaeological record and the possibility that not all structures at a given period of occupation have been excavated or have survived.

Neolithic stone tool technology included both flaked tools, continuing techniques developed during the Paleolithic, and increasingly prominent polished stone tools, ground and smoothed through abrasion to produce durable, sharp-edged implements suited to tasks such as woodworking, plant processing, and, in later phases, agricultural activity. Bone and antler tools, including fishhooks, harpoon points, needles, and awls, are particularly well preserved at shell midden sites, where the surrounding shell deposits create chemical conditions favorable to bone preservation, and provide valuable evidence for craft activities such as basketry, netmaking, and the working of hides and textiles that leave little direct archaeological trace.

Woodworking technology deserves particular attention in a chapter concerned with maritime networks, since the polished stone adzes and axes recovered from many Neolithic sites are consistent with the kind of timber-working required to hollow and shape a logboat such as the one recovered at Bibong-ri, examined further in Section 4.6. Ornamental objects, including shell beads, animal tooth pendants, and, at some sites, worked jade or similar stones, appear in Neolithic contexts and suggest attention to personal adornment and, potentially, the communication of social identity, though the specific social meanings of such objects cannot be recovered with certainty from archaeological evidence alone.

Section 4.6

The Yellow Sea and Korea Strait as Maritime Corridors

Modern viewers accustomed to thinking of seas as barriers should reconsider that assumption when studying Neolithic Northeast Asia. For coastal and island-dwelling communities equipped with watercraft, open water often functioned as a more efficient medium for transportation and communication than overland travel through forested, mountainous, or otherwise difficult terrain. The Yellow Sea and the Korea Strait both functioned during the Neolithic as maritime corridors facilitating the movement of people, materials, and cultural practices rather than as impassable divides, though the strength and character of the surviving evidence differs considerably between the two.

Korea's clearest direct evidence for Neolithic watercraft comes not from an exchanged artifact but from a boat itself: a preserved wooden logboat recovered at Bibong-ri, an inland wetland site in Changnyeong, radiocarbon dated to approximately 5700 BCE and currently the oldest boat yet discovered on the Korean peninsula. This single vessel should not be overstated. It does not by itself establish long-distance open-sea capability, precise construction methods used elsewhere on the peninsula, or the frequency with which such craft were built and used. What it does establish, concretely rather than by inference alone, is that Neolithic communities on the peninsula possessed the woodworking technology and practical knowledge necessary to build and use substantial wooden watercraft on inland waterways from a very early date in the sequence discussed in this chapter, materially strengthening the broader argument that rivers, wetlands, and coastal waters were actively used as transportation routes rather than merely inferred as such from the movement of exchanged objects.

The strongest evidence for contact across open water specifically comes from the Korea Strait, connecting the peninsula's southern coast to Kyushu in the Japanese archipelago. Geochemical sourcing techniques, which identify the specific volcanic origin of obsidian samples by analyzing their trace chemical composition, have demonstrated that obsidian recovered at southern Korean coastal sites derives from sources in Kyushu, while some obsidian recovered from inland or northern Korean contexts can be traced to sources associated with Mount Paektu, demonstrating that obsidian moved along more than one route and in more than one direction across the wider region. This provenance evidence establishes, with considerable confidence, that Kyushu-sourced material reached the Korean peninsula. It does not, by itself, establish whether every such movement resulted from a direct voyage across the strait, from island-hopping through intermediate points such as Tsushima, or from a longer chain of successive short-distance exchanges between neighboring communities; the evidence is compatible with any of these mechanisms, and this chapter does not claim more certainty than the sourcing data supports.

Some archaeologists have also proposed that exchange across the strait moved in the opposite direction as well. Shell ornaments and bracelets of a type manufactured from marine shells available on the southern Korean coast resemble ornaments found at sites in Tsushima and Kyushu, and some researchers have suggested that Korean-made shell ornaments, or the raw shell used to make them, circulated toward the Japanese archipelago. This proposal remains debated rather than established: stylistic resemblance between ornaments on both sides of the strait does not by itself prove that specific objects, or the raw shell from which they were made, physically crossed from the peninsula to Tsushima or Kyushu, since parallel manufacture from locally available shell using shared techniques is also possible. What is more securely documented is that Jōmon-period pottery, discussed further in Section 4.7, has been recovered at southern Korean coastal sites alongside Kyushu obsidian, demonstrating that material reached the peninsula from the Japanese archipelago. Taken together, the evidence supports describing Korea Strait exchange as sustained and, in general character, likely bidirectional, while treating the specific claim that Korean shell ornaments were physically exported to Japan as an open question rather than a settled fact.

Evidence for exchange across the Yellow Sea, connecting the peninsula to the Shandong Peninsula and other parts of the Chinese coast, is harder to document with the same directness as the Korea Strait evidence. Nonlocal materials, including Chinese jade recovered at inland sites such as Huam-ri, provide indirect evidence that materials ultimately originating across or around the Yellow Sea reached the peninsula during parts of the Neolithic, and broad technical similarities in pottery and stone tool traditions across the Yellow Sea region suggest that some degree of contact, whether direct or mediated through intermediate communities, likely occurred. Students should distinguish clearly, however, between evidence that an object ultimately originated across the sea and proof of a direct voyage connecting its point of origin to its findspot; for the Yellow Sea in particular, indirect, multi-stage movement through intermediary communities remains at least as plausible as direct crossing, and the evidence does not currently allow confident selection between these possibilities.

Section 4.7

Connections to Manchuria, the Russian Far East, and the Japanese Archipelago

Beyond maritime connections to the south and west, the Korean peninsula's Neolithic communities maintained meaningful, if less precisely documented, relationships with communities in Manchuria and the Russian Far East to the north, a region that would remain historically significant to the peninsula's political history for millennia afterward, as later chapters on Gojoseon, Goguryeo, and Balhae will demonstrate. Comb-pattern pottery traditions broadly related in technique to peninsular Jeulmun pottery appear across a wide swath of Northeast Asia, including parts of Manchuria and the Amur River basin in the Russian Far East, suggesting a loosely connected regional pattern of technological and cultural exchange extending well beyond the peninsula's modern political boundaries.

Archaeologists generally interpret these broad Northeast Asian similarities as evidence of a shared technological horizon, meaning a widely distributed set of related techniques and styles that spread through a combination of population movement, trade contact, and independent local adaptation, rather than as evidence of a single unified culture or people spanning the entire region. This interpretation is consistent with the broader methodological caution this textbook applies throughout: technological and stylistic similarity across a wide area does not, by itself, establish shared political or ethnic identity, particularly across a period as long and a region as large as Neolithic Northeast Asia.

A peninsula-wide account of the Korean Neolithic must also draw on evidence from the northern peninsula, which is too often absent from popular treatments that focus almost exclusively on well-known southern sites. Seopohang, in the northeastern Rason region, preserves multiple superimposed Neolithic cultural layers and has long been an important reference point for archaeologists comparing Korean Neolithic chronology with that of neighboring Primorye in the Russian Far East and parts of northeastern China. Other northern sites, including Gungsan-ri and Jitap-ri, have contributed evidence relevant to settlement patterns and, at some sites, early cultivation in the northern peninsula. North Korean excavations, many conducted decades ago, made foundational contributions to the discipline of Korean Neolithic archaeology; however, differences in publication history, restricted international access, and limited opportunities for modern re-dating and reanalysis have created an uneven evidentiary record between north and south. This imbalance is a historiographical limitation students should keep in view rather than an indication that the northern peninsula was historically less significant during the Neolithic; it means, rather, that the peninsula's Neolithic archaeology, like so much else discussed in this textbook, cannot be allowed to functionally mean only South Korea.

The relationship between Korean peninsular Neolithic communities and those of the Japanese archipelago deserves particular attention because of its later historical significance and because of the strong cross-strait evidence discussed in Section 4.6. The Japanese archipelago during this broad period was home to Jōmon-period pottery-using hunter-gatherer and fishing societies, a long and internally diverse tradition whose chronological sequence overlaps substantially with much of the Korean Neolithic/Jeulmun period without being identical to it in either timing or character. Students should avoid describing the Jōmon sequence simply as "Japan's Neolithic," since applying Korean or European periodization automatically to another region's archaeological sequence risks obscuring the distinct trajectory of Jōmon societies, which combined long-term, in places highly persistent, settlement with a subsistence base that remained centered on fishing, hunting, and gathering for a considerably longer span than developments on the peninsula. The direct evidence of obsidian and pottery movement, together with related shell-ornament traditions whose precise movement across the strait remains debated, demonstrates that these two Neolithic worlds were not developing in complete isolation from one another, providing useful context for later, far better documented connections between the peninsula and the Japanese archipelago during the Three Kingdoms period, examined in Part II, though students should avoid drawing a direct historical line from Neolithic exchange to later Baekje-Wa diplomatic and cultural relationships, since many thousands of years and profound social transformations separate the two periods.

Section 4.8

Belief, Burial, and Social Organization

Evidence for Neolithic belief systems and social organization on the Korean peninsula is considerably more limited and more difficult to interpret than the evidence for subsistence, technology, and settlement, since beliefs and social structures leave far less direct material trace than pottery, tools, or food remains. The most substantial body of evidence for burial practice comes from the Gadeokdo (Janghang) site on an island near Busan, where archaeologists have investigated 48 Neolithic graves, making it the largest known Neolithic cemetery yet identified in Korea and a valuable concentration of evidence rather than a single isolated burial.

The Gadeokdo graves show variation in body position and in the presence, absence, and character of accompanying grave goods, including ornaments made from shell and other materials. Stable-isotope analysis, a technique that examines the chemical signatures of particular elements preserved in human bone and teeth to reconstruct aspects of diet, applied to sampled human remains from the site indicates a diet strongly weighted toward marine resources, consistent with the coastal and island setting, while not excluding a meaningful terrestrial component. This combination of burial, ornament, and dietary evidence allows the discussion of Neolithic death and community organization to move well beyond abstraction, though students should note that even a sample of 48 graves represents only a fraction of the individuals who lived and died in the communities that used the cemetery, and that the site's evidence, however substantial by Korean Neolithic standards, cannot be assumed to represent burial practice everywhere on the peninsula.

Regarding social organization more broadly, the relatively small and, in most cases, not sharply differentiated character of Neolithic dwellings and burial goods provides only limited evidence for institutionalized social ranking during this period; it does not amount to proof of complete equality within these communities, since forms of status or differentiation that leave little durable material trace, based on age, skill, ritual role, or other factors, could well have existed without being archaeologically visible in the way this chapter's evidence allows. Similarly, while household-scale and small-community organization is a reasonable inference from the size and clustering of excavated dwellings, kinship structure itself, meaning the specific rules by which these communities reckoned relatedness, descent, or marriage, is not directly demonstrated by the archaeological record and should not be asserted with more confidence than the evidence supports. Most archaeologists therefore describe Neolithic peninsular society in general terms as organized around small, likely kin-connected communities without the pronounced, materially visible social hierarchy that becomes more archaeologically evident during the Bronze Age, examined in Chapter 5, when larger fortified settlements, more elaborate burial monuments, and greater differentiation in grave goods suggest the emergence of more pronounced social ranking.

Evidence for symbolic and artistic life, while it cannot recover specific beliefs, rituals, or the exact social meanings such objects carried, demonstrates that Neolithic communities invested real labor and skill in adornment and symbolic expression. Dongsam-dong has yielded a well-known carved shell mask alongside shell bracelets and other ornaments; other sites have produced animal-shaped clay objects and additional worked ornamental materials. This material evidence establishes that symbolic behavior, broadly defined as the deliberate production of objects carrying meaning beyond immediate practical function, was a genuine feature of Neolithic life on the peninsula. It establishes that behavior far more securely than it can establish what any particular object meant to the people who made and used it, and this chapter does not claim to reconstruct specific Neolithic beliefs, deities, or ritual practices from these objects alone.

Section 4.9

Toward the Bronze Age: Continuity and Transformation

The final centuries of the Korean Neolithic, conventionally dated to roughly 2000 to 1500 BCE, though the precise timing varies by region, saw important changes that anticipate the more dramatic transformations of the Bronze Age examined in Chapter 5. Classic Jeulmun comb-pattern pottery gradually gave way, in many regions, to plainer, often double-rimmed pottery styles, a shift that some archaeologists associate with broader changes in social organization, subsistence, and possibly population movement, though the causes of this pottery transition remain incompletely understood and are the subject of continuing archaeological research.

Evidence for cultivation, discussed in Section 4.4, appears to become somewhat more prominent and more widespread in the archaeological record during this transitional period at some sites, though, as noted, the pace and extent of this shift varied considerably by region, and full agricultural intensification, together with the appearance of bronze metallurgy, dolmen construction, fortified settlement, and more pronounced social stratification, more clearly defines the Bronze Age societies examined in the following chapter rather than the Neolithic period covered here. This chapter deliberately stops short of that transformation: intensive agriculture, bronze technology, fortifications, dolmens, and sustained warfare remain the substantive responsibility of Chapter 5.

This chapter's central historical argument is that the Korean Neolithic, though it left no written record and involved no state, nation, or unified political community, nonetheless established patterns of profound and lasting significance: increasingly persistent habitation exploiting the peninsula's rich and regionally varied coastal, riverine, and, on Jeju, island environments; technological innovation reflected in an evolving pottery sequence beginning with Gosan-ri-type pottery; direct, physically documented use of watercraft; and sustained maritime and continental connections, likely bidirectional in character, with neighboring Northeast Asian communities. These patterns did not disappear with the Bronze Age transition; rather, they were transformed, intensified, and built upon by the societies examined in Chapter 5, which in turn provided the archaeological and social foundation from which the earliest Korean states, examined beginning in Chapter 6, eventually emerged.

Historical Debate

How and When Did Cultivation Take Hold on the Korean Peninsula?

The question: Because foxtail and broomcorn millet were already domesticated earlier on the East Asian continental mainland, the genuine historical question for the Korean peninsula is not whether Koreans independently invented millet agriculture, but when cultivated crops became established in different regions, through which interaction networks domesticated millet arrived, how important crops were relative to aquatic resources, hunting, and wild plants at different times and places, and how much regional differences and uneven archaeological sampling distort the resulting picture.

Interpretation A — Cultivation as an early and integral component of mixed economies: Some archaeologists, pointing to archaeobotanical evidence of domesticated millet well established in southern Korea by the Middle Chulmun, argue that cultivation became a genuinely significant, if not dominant, part of subsistence earlier and more securely than older, more cautious accounts suggested, and that some communities may have actively tended or managed local legumes such as soybean and azuki alongside imported millet cultivation.
Interpretation B — Cultivation as a persistently minor supplement until later intensification: Other archaeologists, while accepting the same archaeobotanical evidence for millet's presence, emphasize that its contribution to overall diet, as measured against the continuing dominance of fish, shellfish, and wild plant remains at many sites, appears to have remained modest through much of the Late Neolithic, with genuine agricultural intensification occurring only with the Bronze Age transition examined in Chapter 5.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on archaeobotanical remains recovered through flotation and related techniques, radiocarbon dating of plant material, and comparison with better-documented agricultural sequences in neighboring regions of continental Northeast Asia and China.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns less the timing of millet's arrival, now reasonably well dated to the Middle Chulmun in southern Korea, than the interpretation of its dietary and social significance relative to continuing aquatic and gathered resources, and the extent to which uneven sampling and preservation across sites might understate or overstate cultivation's true importance.

Why it matters: Resolving or clarifying this debate affects how historians characterize the pace and character of the Neolithic-to-Bronze-Age transition and how much continuity versus change should be emphasized between the two periods.

Student task: Considering the evidence and interpretations described above, explain what kind of additional archaeobotanical or isotopic evidence would help historians decide between these interpretations, and explain why regional variation may mean that both interpretations are partly correct for different parts of the peninsula.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From Neolithic Networks to Later Korean Maritime History

What Changed

  • Subsistence strategies shifted gradually from an exclusive reliance on fishing, hunting, and gathering, as at Gosan-ri, toward the incorporation of cultivated millet in southern regions by the Middle Chulmun, a process that intensified significantly in the Bronze Age examined in Chapter 5
  • Pottery styles evolved from Gosan-ri-type plant-fiber-tempered pottery through raised-design pottery, classic Jeulmun comb-pattern decoration, and finally the plainer styles associated with the Neolithic-Bronze Age transition
  • Settlement patterns developed from more dispersed and less architecturally visible occupation toward the larger, denser, multi-household villages documented at sites such as Amsa-dong
  • The technology and purposes of maritime contact changed enormously between the Neolithic movement of obsidian and pottery and the related, but partly debated, shell-ornament traditions, and the later diplomatic, religious, and commercial maritime networks of the Three Kingdoms and Unified Silla periods examined in Part II and Part III

What Stayed the Same

  • The peninsula's coasts, rivers, and surrounding seas remained central to settlement, subsistence, and connection across the Neolithic, the Bronze Age, and every subsequent period this textbook examines
  • The Yellow Sea and the Korea Strait functioned as maritime corridors of exchange rather than sealed barriers throughout Korean history, from Neolithic obsidian, pottery, and debated shell-ornament connections to later historically documented maritime exchange and diplomacy with Japan
  • Communities on the peninsula consistently maintained meaningful contact, at times likely bidirectional, with neighboring Northeast Asian societies rather than developing in isolation

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing that maritime connection long predates any Korean state, and that it was sustained and at times likely bidirectional rather than simply one-directional, helps students avoid treating peninsular history as a story of isolation interrupted only by later, better documented contact, and instead understand connection across the Yellow Sea and Korea Strait as a deep and continuous historical pattern.

Cause and Effect

Why Communities Settled Where They Did

Long-term causes

Post-glacial warming and rising sea levels created extensive, resource-rich coastal and estuarine environments by roughly 4000 BCE, while the peninsula's river systems and wetlands, including the inland waterway environment at Bibong-ri, provided freshwater fish, transportation, and access to land suitable for later cultivation.

Short-term and enabling factors

Pottery, first attested at Gosan-ri without associated village architecture, functioned as one enabling technology within a larger bundle that also included more efficient fishing and shellfish-collection tools, storage capacity, repeated or extended use of favored locations, and, eventually, cultivation; no single technology by itself caused settlement to intensify.

Effect

Settlement concentrated disproportionately along coasts, river valleys, and productive wetlands, producing the dense shell midden accumulations, the multi-household villages documented at Amsa-dong, and direct evidence of watercraft construction at Bibong-ri that provide most of the archaeological evidence for this chapter.

This textbook's response

Treats settlement location and intensity as the product of interacting environmental opportunity, a bundle of enabling technologies, and human choice, rather than as a simple or inevitable outcome of geography or any single technology such as pottery alone, avoiding both environmental determinism and technological determinism.

Historical complexity: The gap between Gosan-ri's early pottery and its apparent lack of substantial architecture reminds students that these enabling factors did not always appear together, and that human choice, local circumstance, and factors not fully recoverable from the archaeological record also shaped settlement patterns.

Everyday Life and Social History

The Rhythms of Neolithic Daily Life

For the ordinary members of a Neolithic community on the Korean peninsula, daily life likely revolved around a seasonal rhythm of gathering, fishing, hunting, food processing, and, in later periods and particularly in the south, small-scale cultivation, organized around the household and the small local community rather than any larger political structure. Pottery vessels, made and used within the household or the local community, served essential functions in cooking and storing food across seasons of differing abundance, while stone, bone, and antler tools, crafted through skills passed down within the community, equipped daily subsistence tasks ranging from fishing and hunting to plant processing, hide working, and, at sites such as Bibong-ri, the construction of substantial wooden watercraft.

Archaeological evidence suggests that labor within these communities likely involved some degree of division by task, though the extent to which such division followed lines of age, gender, or other social distinctions cannot be established with confidence from material remains alone, and historians must be cautious about projecting later or modern assumptions about gendered labor onto Neolithic society without direct supporting evidence. What can be said is that these communities' survival depended on a broad base of practical ecological knowledge, understanding of seasonal resource availability, fish and shellfish behavior, plant growth cycles, and, eventually, the requirements of early cultivation, knowledge developed and transmitted across generations through direct experience and observation rather than through any written record.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the Neolithic period of the Korean peninsula, a span of roughly four and a half thousand years beginning with Korea's earliest securely dated pottery at Gosan-ri on Jeju Island, approximately 7800 to 7500 BCE, and extending to the gradual transition toward the Bronze Age societies examined in Chapter 5, beginning around 2000 to 1500 BCE. Gosan-ri's plant-fiber-tempered pottery, notably not securely associated with substantial village architecture, provides direct evidence against a simplified model in which pottery, settled village life, and farming arrived together as a single interconnected "Neolithic package," a central interpretive theme carried throughout the chapter.

The chapter traced a regionally variable pottery sequence moving from Gosan-ri-type pottery through raised-design pottery traditions to classic Jeulmun comb-pattern pottery, especially prominent in several regions during the fourth millennium BCE, and finally to plainer, double-rimmed styles associated with the approach of the Bronze Age. It emphasized that "Jeulmun" functions both as a broad period label and as the name of one specific pottery tradition within that period, and that no single pottery sequence adequately represents the entire peninsula.

The chapter examined coastal and riverine subsistence as a genuinely regional phenomenon rather than a single uniform system, contrasting the shellfish- and marine-mammal-oriented economy documented at southern shell midden sites such as Dongsam-dong with the different resource regimes of western tidal environments and eastern coastal and river-mouth communities such as Osan-ni.

On cultivation, the chapter presented a spectrum rather than a single transition: overwhelming reliance on fishing, hunting, shellfish, and gathering in the Early Neolithic, giving way in the Middle and Late Neolithic to the well-documented establishment of foxtail and broomcorn millet cultivation in southern Korea, alongside possible local use and management of legumes such as soybean and azuki, all understood within the context of wider Northeast Asian crop dispersal rather than independent Korean domestication of millet itself.

The chapter described Neolithic settlement and daily technology through excavated dwellings, tools, and ornaments, with particular attention to the well-documented fourth-millennium-BCE village at Amsa-dong, while cautioning that Amsa-dong represents a relatively late and unusually well-preserved phase of the Neolithic rather than its earliest chapter, a chronological role reserved for Gosan-ri.

A central argument of the chapter concerned direct maritime evidence. The Bibong-ri logboat, dated to approximately 5700 BCE, provides concrete physical evidence of substantial wooden watercraft construction used on rivers and wetlands, independent of any exchanged artifact. Geochemical sourcing of obsidian demonstrates that material from Kyushu reached southern Korean sites, and Jōmon-period pottery appears at Korean coastal sites, together documenting sustained contact moving from the Japanese archipelago toward the peninsula. Shell ornaments resembling Korean types found in Tsushima and Kyushu raise the possibility of movement in the opposite direction as well, though the chapter treated the physical export of specific Korean shell ornaments as an open question rather than an established fact, describing the overall exchange network as sustained and likely bidirectional in character rather than definitively reciprocal in every particular. Evidence for Yellow Sea contact, including nonlocal jade recovered at sites such as Huam-ri, remains real but less direct, and the chapter distinguished evidence of an object's distant origin from proof of a direct voyage.

The chapter also insisted on a peninsula-wide, rather than a southern-only, account of the Korean Neolithic, drawing attention to northern sites such as Seopohang in the Rason region, whose multilayered sequence has long been compared to Primorye and northeastern China, while acknowledging the uneven publication and access history that has left North Korean Neolithic archaeology less thoroughly integrated into international scholarship than South Korean archaeology.

On burial and social organization, the chapter drew on the Gadeokdo (Janghang) cemetery, with 48 excavated graves the largest known Neolithic cemetery in Korea, and on stable-isotope evidence indicating a strongly marine-based diet among the individuals buried there. It cautioned that limited evidence for social ranking should not be mistaken for proof of complete equality, and that household-scale community organization, while a reasonable inference, does not by itself demonstrate specific kinship structures. Evidence for symbolic and artistic life, including the Dongsam-dong shell mask and various ornaments, was treated as securely establishing symbolic behavior while leaving the specific meanings of that behavior beyond secure archaeological reach.

Finally, the chapter connected these developments to the Bronze Age transformations examined in Chapter 5, emphasizing that the patterns of settlement, technological innovation, and sustained, likely bidirectional maritime and continental connection established during the Neolithic did not disappear but were transformed and intensified by subsequent Bronze Age societies, which in turn provided the foundation from which the earliest Korean states, examined beginning in Chapter 6, eventually emerged. Throughout, the chapter modeled the evidentiary caution required when studying a period with no written record, distinguishing well-supported archaeological conclusions from more tentative or actively debated interpretations, and resisting the anachronistic application of modern national or ethnic categories to communities that lived many thousands of years before any such categories existed.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Neolithic
The "New Stone Age" period, marked on the Korean peninsula by the adoption of pottery, increasingly persistent habitation, and, in later phases, cultivation alongside continued fishing, hunting, and gathering.
Jeulmun/Chulmun period
The broad archaeological period label for the Korean Neolithic, encompassing Gosan-ri-type pottery, raised-design pottery, classic comb-pattern pottery, and later plainer styles; not synonymous with comb-pattern pottery alone.
Gosan-ri-type pottery
Korea's earliest securely dated pottery tradition, coarse and plant-fiber-tempered, found on Jeju Island and dated to approximately 7800–7500 BCE, not associated with substantial village architecture.
Raised-design pottery
A Neolithic pottery tradition featuring applied or raised clay decoration, appearing in southern and eastern coastal regions between Gosan-ri-type pottery and classic comb-pattern pottery.
Comb-pattern pottery
Classic Jeulmun pottery decorated with combed or incised geometric patterns, especially prominent in several regions during the fourth millennium BCE; one specific tradition within the broader Jeulmun period rather than a synonym for the whole period.
Shell midden
An accumulated deposit of discarded shellfish remains and other refuse, providing rich, regionally variable archaeological evidence for coastal Neolithic diet and settlement.
Pit-house
A dwelling built around a shallow excavated floor with a wooden and organic superstructure, the typical Neolithic house form on the peninsula.
Holocene
The current geological epoch, beginning approximately 11,700 years ago, encompassing the entire span of the Korean Neolithic.
Radiocarbon dating
A scientific technique using the decay of a radioactive carbon isotope in organic material to estimate the age of archaeological remains.
Cultivation
The deliberate planting and tending of plants for food, becoming archaeologically well established for millet in southern Korea by the Middle Chulmun.
Domestication
The process by which plants or animals become genetically and behaviorally adapted to human cultivation or husbandry over successive generations; Korean millet reflects mainland domestication and subsequent dispersal.
Foxtail millet and broomcorn millet
Cultivated grain crops, domesticated earlier on the East Asian mainland, archaeologically well established in southern Korea by the Middle Chulmun.
Archaeobotany
The archaeological study of ancient plant remains, typically recovered through flotation, used to reconstruct diet and cultivation.
Logboat / dugout canoe
A boat hollowed from a single log; the Bibong-ri logboat, dated to approximately 5700 BCE, is the oldest such vessel yet found on the Korean peninsula.
Polished stone tool
A stone implement shaped and smoothed through abrasion, increasingly prominent in Neolithic technology alongside older flaked stone tool traditions.
Obsidian sourcing
Geochemical analysis identifying the specific volcanic origin of obsidian artifacts, used to trace Neolithic exchange between southern Korea and Kyushu.
Stable-isotope analysis
A technique examining chemical signatures preserved in bone and teeth to reconstruct aspects of ancient diet, applied to remains from the Gadeokdo cemetery.
Seasonal settlement
A pattern of occupation in which communities move between locations according to the seasonal availability of resources, often combined with more persistent residential use of favored sites.
Maritime corridor
A sea route understood as a connective zone of transportation and exchange rather than a barrier, exemplified by the Korea Strait and, with less direct evidence, the Yellow Sea.
Exchange network
A system through which materials, techniques, or styles move between communities, whether through direct trade, island-hopping, or successive short-distance transfers.
Regional variation
Meaningful differences in pottery, subsistence, or settlement patterns across different areas of the peninsula during the same general period, including differences between southern, western, eastern, and northern regions.
Archaeological culture
A scholarly grouping of sites sharing similar material remains, used as an analytical tool rather than necessarily indicating a single people or political community.
Sedentism
A pattern of settled, residentially persistent habitation; on the Korean peninsula, often combined with continued seasonal mobility rather than becoming wholly nonmobile.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has shown that long before any Korean state existed, the peninsula's inhabitants, from Jeju in the south to the Rason region in the northeast, were already developing pottery, increasingly persistent communities, sophisticated technologies including substantial wooden watercraft used on rivers and wetlands, and sustained connections, at times bidirectional, with their Northeast Asian neighbors. The Neolithic period examined here left no written record, no named ruler, and no political institution that later Korean states would directly inherit, yet it established patterns, intensive and regionally varied use of coastal, riverine, and island environments, technological innovation beginning with Gosan-ri's early pottery, and maritime and continental exchange running in more than one direction, that would recur, in transformed and intensified ways, throughout the many centuries this textbook goes on to examine.

Understanding the Neolithic peninsula also reinforces a methodological lesson introduced in Chapters 1 and 3 and essential to the study of any long historical civilization: the absence of written sources does not mean the absence of meaningful history, but it does demand particular discipline. Archaeological evidence, interpreted carefully and with appropriate acknowledgment of its limits, allows historians to reconstruct genuine, if incomplete, knowledge about societies that left no texts of their own. The Gosan-ri evidence, in particular, warns against assuming that technological, residential, and subsistence changes must have arrived together as a single package; the same evidentiary caution applies to distinguishing well-supported archaeological conclusions from more speculative interpretations throughout this chapter.

The maritime connections traced in this chapter, particularly the Bibong-ri logboat and the documented movement of obsidian and pottery across the Korea Strait, together with the debated but plausible movement of shell ornaments in the opposite direction, anticipate a theme that will recur throughout this textbook: the Korean peninsula's seas have functioned across many different periods as connective corridors rather than isolating barriers, and that connection has often run in more than one direction. Students carrying this insight forward will be better prepared to understand later chapters examining Three Kingdoms-era maritime diplomacy, Unified Silla's international networks, and the complex, never simply isolated, character of Joseon-era foreign relations.

This chapter has also modeled a commitment to peninsula-wide coverage that resists letting a handful of well-known southern sites stand for the whole, incorporating northern evidence from Seopohang alongside an honest acknowledgment of the uneven historiographical record that separates North Korean and South Korean archaeology, a caution that will recur when this textbook turns, in Volume IV, to the parallel and equally serious treatment of North Korea and South Korea as modern states.

The Neolithic communities examined in this chapter did not yet constitute anything resembling a Korean nation, and describing them as such would misrepresent the historical record. But their technological achievements, their adaptation to the peninsula's varied coastal, riverine, and island environments, and their participation in a wider interconnected Northeast Asian world of exchange and contact provided the deep, long-term foundation upon which the Bronze Age societies of Chapter 5, and eventually the earliest Korean states examined in Chapter 6, would be built.
