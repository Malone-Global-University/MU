What Is Korea?

Korean History: Ancient Kingdoms to a Divided Peninsula

Chapter Opening

Two capitals sit on the same historical peninsula, separated by a border established by the 1953 armistice and, at the Demilitarized Zone itself, only about four kilometers wide, yet divided by radically different states, economies, and even the words their citizens use for their own country. Understanding how one long historical civilization produced two such different Koreas requires first asking a more basic question: across more than four millennia of historical tradition and change, what has "Korea" actually meant?

Seoul and Pyongyang lie less than two hundred kilometers apart. Their residents speak closely related varieties of the same language and share a deep pre-1945 cultural and historical inheritance; for long periods, especially under Goryeo and Joseon, the regions surrounding both cities belonged to the same political states, even though earlier Korean history also included substantial periods of political division. And yet by the early twenty first century, these two cities anchor two of the most different states on earth: one a globally connected, densely urbanized, democratic, capitalist society whose popular culture circulates worldwide, the other a state-directed economy formally organized around a dominant party and smaller subordinate parties, increasingly shaped in practice by informal markets alongside central planning, and built around hereditary leadership, strict information control, and a nuclear arsenal. Citizens of the Republic of Korea generally call their country Hanguk. Citizens of the Democratic People's Republic of Korea generally call their country Chosŏn. Even the ordinary word for "Korea" no longer means quite the same thing on the two sides of the Demilitarized Zone.

This divergence did not emerge from ancient or inevitable differences between a northern people and a southern people. It resulted from a specific, comparatively recent sequence of twentieth century events, examined at length in Volume IV of this textbook: Japanese colonial rule, the sudden arrival of Soviet and American occupation armies in 1945, the establishment of two rival governments in 1948, and a catastrophic civil war between 1950 and 1953 that hardened a wartime armistice line into one of the most heavily fortified borders on earth. Before any of that happened, the peninsula's history was Korean history in a continuous, if never simple or uniform, sense stretching back through the Joseon dynasty, the Goryeo dynasty, the kingdoms of Silla and Balhae, the earlier Three Kingdoms and Gaya, and further still into the prehistoric societies that inhabited the peninsula and neighboring Manchuria for tens of thousands of years before any state called itself Korean at all.

This chapter does not attempt to tell that story yet. Chapter 3 begins the narrative proper with the Paleolithic peninsula, and Chapters 3 through 96, ninety four chapters in all, carry it forward to the year 2026. Chapter 1 instead does something more basic and, in its own way, more difficult: it defines the historical subject. What do historians mean when they write "Korea," "Korean," or "the Korean peninsula"? Where does that subject begin and end geographically? Which peoples, states, and regions belong within it, and on what basis? Why must a responsible historian resist projecting the modern Republic of Korea and Democratic People's Republic of Korea backward onto ancient Gojoseon, medieval Goryeo, or early modern Joseon, none of whose inhabitants understood themselves as citizens of either modern state?

These are not merely academic housekeeping questions. Loose or careless use of the word "Korea" has shaped, and sometimes distorted, both popular and scholarly understanding of this region's history. Treating Korea as though it were always a single unified nation-state flattens centuries of political plurality, including the Three Kingdoms period, the Later Three Kingdoms period, and the divided peninsula of the present day. Treating Korean history as simply a smaller version of Chinese history erases the many ways Korean states selected, adapted, resisted, and transformed continental institutions on their own terms. Treating the peninsula as historically isolated, an assumption reinforced by the much later nickname "Hermit Kingdom," obscures a long history of maritime trade, diplomatic exchange, migration, warfare, and cultural connection across the Yellow Sea, the Korea Strait, and the Eurasian steppe.

This textbook studies a region defined chiefly by an evolving geographic space, the Korean peninsula and its immediate Manchurian and maritime surroundings, by the political communities that have governed and inhabited that space, and by the shifting historical meanings that "Korea" has carried across time. Before any state calling itself Korean existed, the peninsula was home to Paleolithic hunter-gatherers, Neolithic fishing and farming communities, and Bronze and Iron Age societies whose material remains this textbook examines on their own terms, not as an anachronistic "ancient Korea" understood in the modern national sense. Only later, through the long historical process beginning with early state formation and continuing through the Three Kingdoms, Silla, Balhae, Goryeo, and Joseon, did a recognizable Korean political and cultural community emerge, one that itself repeatedly changed its own name, borders, and self-understanding.

Understanding how "Korea" came to mean what it now means, and understanding what it has not always meant, requires the same historical toolkit historians use to study any long-lived civilizational region: careful attention to chronology, geography, language, evidence, and the political purposes behind competing national narratives, whether produced by medieval court chroniclers, modern nationalist historians, or the rival governments of a divided peninsula. This chapter establishes that vocabulary, geographic scope, chronological framework, and methodological commitment for the ninety five chapters that follow.

Finally, this chapter asks students to consider why any of this matters beyond the classroom. Confusing modern national categories with ancient or medieval political communities, assuming that political unity has always been Korea's default condition, or assuming that Korean civilization developed in isolation from its neighbors, has real consequences for how the peninsula's past, present, and possible futures are understood, both inside and outside Korea. Learning to define this historical subject carefully is the necessary first step toward understanding, accurately and without anachronism, one of the most historically continuous and, in the twentieth and twenty first centuries, most dramatically divided civilizations on earth.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Define "Korea" as a geographic, historical-cultural, political, and linguistic term, and explain why these meanings are related but distinct.
  2. Distinguish Korea as a peninsula from Korea as a succession of historical states, and explain why modern political borders cannot simply be projected backward in time.
  3. Explain why Manchuria, the Yellow Sea, and the Korea Strait are historically essential to Korean history rather than external to it.
  4. Identify the major names historically applied to Korea and Korean states, including Gojoseon, Goryeo, Joseon, Hanguk, and Daehan, and explain how the modern division of the peninsula produced two different everyday names for "Korea."
  5. Describe the basic chronological framework this textbook uses, from prehistoric Korea through the contemporary divided peninsula.
  6. Analyze why applying the label "Korean" uncritically to ancient or medieval populations, or projecting modern Korean nationalism backward in time, would be anachronistic.
  7. Evaluate the strengths and limitations of geographic, linguistic, political, and cultural definitions of Korean identity.
  8. Distinguish foundation mythology, later written tradition, and archaeological evidence as different kinds of historical source material.
  9. Explain why political plurality, rather than continuous unity, has repeatedly characterized the peninsula's history.
  10. Assess common misconceptions about Korean history, including isolation, cultural dependency on China, and the supposed inevitability of the current division.
  11. Apply careful historical and geographic terminology when discussing states, periods, and peoples introduced in later chapters.
  12. Contextualize the study of Korea within wider Northeast Asian and maritime East Asian historical processes.

Historical Context

Before students can engage with the specific content of this textbook, the ancient kingdoms and Goryeo of Volume I, the Joseon dynasty of Volume II, the imperial crisis, colonization, and war of Volume III, and the two Koreas of Volume IV, they need a shared, carefully defined vocabulary.

The Korean peninsula extends southward from the Eurasian continental landmass into the seas separating it from the Japanese archipelago, bordered by Manchuria and the Russian Far East to the north, the Yellow Sea to the west, the Korea Strait and the East Sea (Sea of Japan) to the south and east. No single ancient state, language, or political authority unified the entire peninsula, still less the peninsula together with its Manchurian hinterland, before the seventh century CE, and even that consolidation, discussed in Part III, covered only part of the territory earlier associated with Goguryeo and was itself soon joined by a second northern state, Balhae. Long before any of these kingdoms existed, the peninsula and neighboring Manchuria were home to varied prehistoric societies: Paleolithic hunter-gatherers, Neolithic fishing and farming communities along the coasts and river valleys, and Bronze and Iron Age peoples whose material remains, including distinctive dolmen burial monuments, are examined on their own terms in the chapters that follow.

Historians studying this region must therefore resist two opposite errors. The first error treats "Korea" as a timeless, unchanging national category stretching back to remote antiquity, projecting a modern national identity onto Paleolithic hunters, Bronze Age dolmen builders, or the inhabitants of Gojoseon who would not have recognized it in anything like its current sense. The second error, a reaction against the first, sometimes minimizes the genuine historical continuity that does connect ancient Gojoseon, the Three Kingdoms, Goryeo, and Joseon, treating each as an unrelated society with no meaningful historical relationship to the others. This textbook adopts neither error. It describes the pre-state and early societies of the peninsula honestly and on their own terms, and it treats the long historical process by which successive Korean states, peoples, and cultural traditions developed as one of the most significant and continuous developments in Northeast Asian history, without claiming that a unified Korean political identity was always present or that political unity has been the peninsula's normal historical condition.

A further complication concerns geography itself. Korean history has never been confined neatly to the territory of either modern state, or even to the peninsula as a whole. Goguryeo and, later, Balhae governed extensive territories in Manchuria as well as the northern peninsula, making the Manchurian frontier essential rather than peripheral to Korean history, discussed further in Chapters 8 and 16. Maritime connections across the Yellow Sea and the Korea Strait linked the peninsula to China and the Japanese archipelago from at least the Three Kingdoms period onward, discussed in Chapter 12 and Chapter 17. In the twentieth century, Korean migration and diaspora communities extended Korean history into Manchuria, Japan, Russia's Far East, China, the United States, and beyond, developments examined throughout Volumes III and IV. This textbook treats Korean history as the history of a peninsula-centered civilization in continuous interaction with its neighbors, not as the history of a sealed and isolated territory.

Section 1.1

Korea as a Historical Subject

Every regional history textbook must, at some point, explain what its subject actually is. For a history of France, Japan, or Brazil, the geographic and political referent, while not without its own complications, is at least anchored to a single, currently existing state. A history of "Korea" faces a different and in some ways more difficult problem: the historical subject is anchored to a single peninsula and a long continuous cultural and linguistic tradition, but that tradition has, at different times, been organized into one state, several competing states, and, since 1948, two rival states that each claim, in different ways, to represent the whole.

This opening section establishes the central interpretive commitment of this textbook: Korea, understood as the Korean peninsula together with its historical Manchurian and maritime connections, and the political, linguistic, and cultural communities that have inhabited that space, is the organizing subject of this book, not any single modern state, dynasty, or period treated as though it were the only legitimate "real" Korea. This commitment has several consequences that will recur throughout the following chapters. First, this textbook does not treat Goguryeo, Baekje, Silla, and Gaya as though they were simply four provinces of one preexisting Korean nation waiting to unify, because doing so would misrepresent the genuine political competition, cultural variation, and historical contingency of the period, examined fully in Part II. Second, this textbook does not treat premodern Korean history as merely a preface to the modern Republic of Korea, nor does it treat North Korea as merely a foreign-policy complication in South Korea's history; Volume IV treats both postwar states as parallel and equally legitimate subjects of historical analysis. Third, this textbook does not treat "Korean" as a fixed ethnic or biological category projected uniformly across the whole of this span, because language, political identity, and cultural practice have all changed substantially over time, and because the peninsula's population has never been entirely homogeneous.

None of these clarifications is intended to diminish the real historical continuity that connects ancient Gojoseon to the Three Kingdoms, Silla and Balhae, Goryeo, Joseon, and the modern Korean states. Language, geography, agricultural practice, religious tradition, and, eventually, shared political memory link these societies in ways that justify treating them as a single continuous historical subject. But treating that continuity as though it meant an unchanging, uniform national essence stretching back to prehistoric times would misrepresent the actual historical record this textbook sets out to explain.

Historical Vocabulary
Korea, Hanguk, and Chosŏn

Korea is the English-language name for the peninsula and its historical civilization, derived from Goryeo (also transliterated Koryŏ), the dynasty that governed the peninsula from 918 to 1392 and whose name reached medieval Europe through Middle Eastern and Central Asian trade networks. The English name therefore reflects only one dynasty out of many, chosen because it happened to be the dynasty ruling Korea when sustained contact with distant traders and, eventually, European travelers developed.

Koreans themselves have used other names at other times. Joseon (also transliterated Chosŏn) was the name of both the earliest documented Korean state, Gojoseon ("Old Joseon"), and the dynasty founded in 1392 that governed the peninsula until the early twentieth century. Daehan ("Great Han") draws its "Han" element from the ancient Samhan peoples and confederacies of the southern peninsula, examined in Chapter 7, and is unrelated to the Han dynasty of China, despite the identical English spelling. This name was adopted as part of the title of the short-lived Korean Empire proclaimed in 1897, examined in Chapter 53, and survives in the modern South Korean state's official name, Daehan Minguk, the Republic of Korea. South Koreans today commonly call their country Hanguk, while North Koreans commonly call their country Chosŏn, reflecting the two governments' different choices about which historical name to emphasize after the peninsula's division in 1948.

This linguistic divergence is not a trivial curiosity. It reflects a genuine historical process in which two governments, each claiming legitimate continuity with the whole of Korean history, selected different historical names to anchor their own national identity. This textbook uses "Korea" as its general English-language term for the peninsula and its historical civilization across all periods, while using the specific historical names, Gojoseon, Goryeo, Joseon, Republic of Korea, and Democratic People's Republic of Korea, when referring to particular states and periods.

Section 1.2

The Korean Peninsula

Students encountering this textbook for the first time should have a basic orientation to the physical setting of Korean history, developed in full in Chapter 2 but introduced briefly here.

The Korean peninsula extends roughly one thousand kilometers from its northern land border with Manchuria and the Russian Far East to its southern coast facing the Korea Strait and the Japanese archipelago. Two mountain systems dominate its interior geography: the Taebaek range running along the eastern coast and the Sobaek range branching through the southern interior, together shaping settlement patterns, agriculture, and internal communication for millennia. Major river systems, including the Amnok (Yalu), the Duman (Tumen), the Han, and the Nakdong, have historically supplied irrigation, transportation, and strategic frontiers. The western and southern coastal plains have long supported the peninsula's densest agricultural populations, while the mountainous north and east supported different economic and settlement patterns.

The peninsula's northern land border, along the Amnok and Duman rivers, connects directly to Manchuria, a region essential to the history of Gojoseon, Goguryeo, and Balhae in particular. Its western coast faces the Yellow Sea and, beyond it, the Chinese mainland, a maritime connection central to trade, diplomacy, and cultural exchange throughout Korean history. Its southern and eastern coasts face the Korea Strait and the East Sea (Sea of Japan), connecting the peninsula to the Japanese archipelago. Numerous islands, including Jeju, Ganghwa, and Ulleungdo, have played distinct historical roles as refuges, defensive positions, and sites of regional culture, examined at several points in later chapters.

This textbook treats the peninsula not as a sealed or isolated territory but as a distinct geographic space positioned at the intersection of continental and maritime East Asia, a position that has shaped Korean history through both connection and, at times, vulnerability to larger continental and maritime powers.

Section 1.3

Korea and Northeast Asia

No error is more common in popular understanding of Korean history than the assumption that the peninsula developed in isolation, sealed off from its neighbors until Western and Japanese ships forced it open in the nineteenth century. This assumption is inaccurate for nearly every period this textbook examines, and correcting it is one of this chapter's central purposes.

From the era of Gojoseon onward, the peninsula maintained continuous, if fluctuating, relationships with the Chinese mainland, ranging from tributary diplomacy and trade to direct military conflict and, in the case of the Han dynasty commanderies discussed in Chapter 7, direct administrative control over parts of the peninsula. Buddhism, Confucian statecraft, historical writing, administrative models, and, eventually, the Chinese script itself arrived in Korea through these continental connections and were then adapted, modified, and in some cases fundamentally transformed by Korean states and thinkers, a pattern examined throughout Volumes I and II.

Manchuria was not merely an external backdrop to early Korean history but, for long stretches of ancient and early medieval history, a central theater of Korean political life. Goguryeo governed extensive Manchurian territory alongside northern portions of the peninsula, and Balhae, following Goguryeo's fall, governed a state whose core territory lay substantially within Manchuria. This textbook accordingly treats Manchuria as integral to early Korean history rather than as an external region that Korean history merely bordered.

Maritime connections proved equally important. Baekje maintained close cultural and diplomatic relationships with polities in the Japanese archipelago, transmitting Buddhism, administrative techniques, and craft technologies. Later, Unified Silla's monk-pilgrims, merchants such as Jang Bogo, examined in Chapter 17, and diplomatic missions connected the peninsula to Tang China and Japan across a genuinely international maritime world. Even the much later Joseon dynasty, despite policies restricting private foreign trade that later observers would characterize, somewhat misleadingly, as a "Hermit Kingdom" posture, maintained active tributary diplomacy with Ming and Qing China and structured relations with Japan through the Tsushima domain, examined in Chapter 30.

This textbook's guiding principle, consistent with the wider MGU History Series' treatment of connected regional histories, is that Korea should be understood as a distinct historical civilization that developed through continuous interaction with continental and maritime East Asia, not as an isolated civilization that occasionally and reluctantly opened itself to outside contact.

Section 1.4

What Does "Korean" Mean?

Popular writing about Korean history frequently assumes that "Korean" describes a single, continuous ethnic and national community stretching back without interruption to Gojoseon or even earlier. Historians must examine this assumption carefully rather than accept it uncritically.

In one direction: the early historical peninsula was home to multiple distinct polities and, in some cases, distinct peoples, including the Samhan confederacies of Mahan, Jinhan, and Byeonhan in the south, Buyeo and Okjeo in the north, and the various peoples later incorporated into Goguryeo, Baekje, Silla, and Gaya, examined in Chapter 7. These populations shared broad cultural and, in many cases, linguistic affinities, but they were not organized as a single people with a shared political identity, and their inhabitants would not have described themselves as "Korean" in anything resembling the modern national sense.

In another direction: language itself provides an important, but not perfectly clean, marker of continuity. Silla's language is generally considered ancestral, in some meaningful sense, to later and modern Korean, but the evidence for Baekje's and especially Goguryeo's languages is far more fragmentary, consisting largely of place names and scattered glosses preserved in later sources, and scholars remain divided over how closely these languages were related to one another and to Silla's. This textbook treats linguistic continuity as an important thread connecting ancient and modern Korea while avoiding overstated claims about the Three Kingdoms' linguistic relationships that the surviving evidence cannot support, and while avoiding the assumption that shared language, where it existed, automatically implied shared political identity in the ancient or medieval periods, when most people's most meaningful loyalties were local, dynastic, or religious rather than national in the modern sense.

Modern Korean nationalism, examined in later chapters including those on the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, developed the powerful and historically significant idea of a single, continuous Korean people (a concept often expressed through the term minjok) stretching from Dangun to the present. This idea has real cultural and political importance and has shaped modern Korean identity, resistance to Japanese colonial rule, and the self-understanding of both postwar Korean states. This textbook treats that nationalist narrative itself as a significant historical phenomenon, discussed further in Part IX and Part X, while distinguishing it from a claim that ancient and medieval Koreans understood their own identity in the same terms.

Section 1.5

Names for Korea

Students will encounter a range of names for Korea and Korean states throughout this textbook, and confusion about these names is one of the most common obstacles for new students of Korean history.

Gojoseon ("Old Joseon") is the name used to distinguish the earliest Korean state from the much later Joseon dynasty founded in 1392. The prefix "Go-" ("old") is already attested in medieval Korean historiography, including the Samguk Yusa, and is not a purely modern scholarly invention, though sources from Gojoseon's own era use the name Joseon rather than Gojoseon; the "Go-" distinction belongs to later historiography. Goguryeo, Baekje, Silla, and Gaya name the major early historical states, examined fully in Part II. Balhae names the northern successor state to Goguryeo, examined in Chapter 16. Goryeo names the dynasty founded in 918, from which the English word "Korea" derives. Joseon names both Gojoseon in abbreviated later reference and, far more commonly in ordinary usage, the dynasty founded in 1392. The same royal house continued to rule until Japanese annexation in 1910, but in October 1897 King Gojong formally changed the state's name from Joseon to Daehan (the Korean Empire) and assumed the imperial title, a redesignation examined further in Chapter 53.

In the twentieth century, the Korean Empire, proclaimed in 1897 and examined in Chapter 53, used the name Daehan Jeguk. After national division in 1948, the two new states adopted different official names reflecting different historical emphases: the Republic of Korea (Daehan Minguk), commonly called Hanguk by its citizens, drew on the "Daehan" name associated with the Korean Empire and Korean nationalist resistance to Japanese rule; the Democratic People's Republic of Korea (Chosŏn Minjujuŭi Inmin Konghwaguk), commonly called Chosŏn by its citizens, drew on the older dynastic name Joseon. This naming divergence, while easy to treat as a minor linguistic curiosity, in fact reflects the deeper historical and ideological divide examined throughout Volume IV: two governments, each constructing a distinct national narrative while claiming continuity with the whole of Korean history.

This textbook uses "Korea" as a general English umbrella term throughout, while using the specific historical name appropriate to each period and state when precision matters, and while explicitly noting, as this section has done, that these names are not interchangeable synonyms but carry distinct historical associations.

Section 1.6

Language and Writing

Korean is a language with a long independent history and, for much of that history, a complicated relationship to the writing systems used to record it. This section provides only a brief orientation; Chapter 27 treats the creation of Hangeul in full.

For many centuries, Korean states used Classical Chinese as their primary written language for government, scholarship, and formal literature, even though spoken Korean and spoken Chinese are unrelated languages with different grammar and vocabulary. This arrangement, common across much of premodern East Asia, meant that a literate Korean scholar could read and write fluently in Classical Chinese while using a different, unwritten language, Korean, in daily speech. Korean scribes gradually developed adaptive systems, including idu and related systems, using Chinese characters to represent Korean grammar and sounds, in order to record spoken Korean more directly, particularly for administrative and legal purposes.

This situation changed decisively with the creation of Hangeul, a featural alphabet designed specifically for the sounds of the Korean language, developed under King Sejong and promulgated in 1446, examined fully in Chapter 27. Chinese characters, known in Korean as hanja, continued to be used alongside Hangeul for centuries afterward, particularly among elite and male literati, and were not fully displaced in general use until the twentieth century, with somewhat different trajectories in North and South Korea after division. This textbook introduces this linguistic and writing history here so that later references to hanja, idu, and Hangeul make sense in context.

Section 1.7

How Korean History Is Periodized

This textbook's ninety-six chapters divide into four broad chronological Volumes, summarized here and developed fully in the chapters that follow.

Chapters 1 through 24, forming Volume I, examine the peninsula's prehistoric societies, the emergence of Gojoseon and other early states, the Three Kingdoms and Gaya, Silla and Balhae, the Later Three Kingdoms period, and the Goryeo dynasty through its fall in 1392. Chapters 25 through 48, forming Volume II, examine the founding and consolidation of the Joseon dynasty, the creation of Hangeul, Sejong-era science, the Imjin War and Manchu invasions, later Joseon society and culture, and the nineteenth-century crisis culminating in the forced opening of 1876. Chapters 49 through 72, forming Volume III, examine reform, the Korean Empire, Japanese colonization, resistance and collaboration, total war, liberation, occupation, and the Korean War through the 1953 armistice. Chapters 73 through 96, forming Volume IV, examine the parallel development of two rival Korean states from postwar reconstruction through the fixed contemporary endpoint of 2026.

This periodization reflects genuine historical turning points: the founding of Joseon in 1392, the Treaty of Ganghwa in 1876, and the Korean armistice of 1953. Students should nonetheless treat these Volume divisions, and the smaller Part and chapter divisions within them, as organizational and analytical tools rather than as natural or self-evident boundaries in the historical record itself. Individuals living through 1392 or 1876 did not experience their own era as the start of a new textbook volume; they experienced gradual, often contested, political and social change whose long-term significance became clearer only in retrospect.

Section 1.8

Myth, Archaeology, and Written History

Students of Korean history must learn early to distinguish among several different kinds of evidence, a skill this section introduces because the very next chapters discuss the legendary founding of Gojoseon.

Foundation mythology refers to traditional stories explaining the origins of a state or people, often centered on divine or semi-divine founders. The story of Dangun, the legendary founder of Gojoseon traditionally dated to 2333 BCE, is Korea's most significant foundation myth, examined further in Chapter 6 and in this chapter's Primary Source Window. Such stories carry genuine historical and cultural significance, shaping later Korean identity and providing a shared point of national memory, but they are not, by themselves, archaeological or contemporary documentary evidence for the events they describe.

Later written tradition refers to accounts of earlier periods recorded by later historians, sometimes centuries after the events described. The Dangun narrative survives in Korean sources compiled in the thirteenth century, more than three thousand years after the traditional founding date, during the Goryeo dynasty's own period of crisis under Mongol pressure, a context that shaped why and how the story was recorded, examined further in this chapter's Primary Source Window.

Archaeological evidence, including settlement remains, burial practices, tools, ceramics, and, for later periods, inscriptions, provides evidence independent of later written tradition, though it must be interpreted carefully and rarely confirms or refutes specific narrative details such as a named individual founder.

Contemporary documentary evidence, meaning textual records produced at or very near the time of the events they describe, becomes available for Korean history mainly from the centuries when Gojoseon appears in Chinese historical records, and becomes progressively richer with the Three Kingdoms, Goryeo, and especially Joseon periods, examined throughout later chapters.

This textbook treats the traditional 2333 BCE founding date and the Dangun narrative as historically and culturally significant traditions worth studying in their own right, while clearly distinguishing them from the archaeologically and documentarily supported history of Gojoseon as an organized state, which is more securely dated to several centuries later. Holding both of these facts in view, without collapsing one into the other, is a core historical skill this textbook expects students to develop.

Section 1.9

One Peninsula, Many Political Centers

A second major misconception this textbook addresses directly is the assumption that political unity has been the Korean peninsula's normal or default historical condition, interrupted only by occasional and temporary episodes of division. The historical record suggests a more complicated pattern.

The earliest historical period examined in this textbook features multiple competing polities: Gojoseon in the north, the Samhan confederacies in the south, and various other groups including Buyeo and Okjeo, none unified under a single government. The Three Kingdoms period that followed featured, as its name suggests, three major competing states, Goguryeo, Baekje, and Silla, joined for centuries by the smaller but historically significant Gaya confederacy, examined in Chapter 11. Silla's victory in 668 established Silla rule over much of, but importantly not all of, the former Goguryeo territory, and within decades a new northern state, Balhae, arose to govern much of that remaining territory alongside Silla, a genuine two-state period historians sometimes describe using the term "North and South States," examined in Chapter 16.

The end of unified Silla rule in the ninth and early tenth centuries produced the Later Three Kingdoms period, another episode of multi-state competition, before Goryeo's reunification in 936, examined in Chapter 18 and Chapter 19. Goryeo and Joseon then governed the peninsula as single, if internally regionally diverse, states for extended periods, but this long era of unified peninsular government, roughly from the tenth century to the early twentieth century, should be understood as one significant pattern within Korean history rather than as the only "true" or "normal" pattern against which all other periods are measured as aberrations.

The peninsula's current division since 1948, examined throughout Volume IV, is therefore not without historical precedent as a general pattern of political plurality, even though the specific circumstances, ideological character, and international context of the present division differ fundamentally from earlier periods of multi-state competition. This textbook asks students to hold both of these observations together: division is not historically unprecedented, but the current division is also not simply a repetition of earlier patterns and must be explained on its own twentieth-century terms.

Section 1.10

Continuity and Change in Korean History

This textbook will return repeatedly to a set of recurring themes that connect its ninety-six chapters into a single, coherent historical narrative rather than a sequence of disconnected episodes.

State formation and political institutions recur from Gojoseon through the contemporary two Korean states, each period developing distinct governing structures, from early chiefdoms and confederacies through centralized dynastic bureaucracies to twentieth-century developmental and single-party states. Relations with continental and maritime neighbors, particularly China and Japan, but also Manchuria, the Mongols, and, from the modern period, Russia and the United States, shape nearly every era examined in this textbook. Cultural adaptation and transformation, including the Korean reception and reworking of Buddhism, Confucianism, and, in the modern period, Christianity, capitalism, and communism, recurs as a pattern of selective adoption rather than passive imitation.

Social hierarchy, including the bone-rank system of Silla, the yangban aristocracy of Joseon, and modern class structures in both postwar Korean states, provides another recurring thread, as does technological innovation, from Bronze and Iron Age metallurgy through Goryeo printing and celadon, Joseon-era science, and modern industrial and digital technology. Resistance and accommodation under pressure from stronger neighboring powers appears repeatedly, from the Goguryeo-Sui and Goguryeo-Tang wars through resistance to Mongol, Manchu, and Japanese domination. Regionalism, including persistent differences among the peninsula's northern, southern, eastern, and western regions and its islands, recurs as a counterweight to any assumption of automatic national uniformity.

Students should watch for these themes as they progress through the textbook, since they provide the connective tissue linking chapters that might otherwise appear to describe entirely separate historical worlds.

Section 1.11

From Ancient Kingdoms to a Divided Peninsula

This textbook is titled Korean History: Ancient Kingdoms to a Divided Peninsula. That title identifies both the starting point and the ending point of the narrative, but it should not be read as suggesting that division was the inevitable destination toward which all of Korean history was always moving.

The Republic of Korea and the Democratic People's Republic of Korea emerged from a specific and contingent sequence of twentieth-century events: thirty-five years of Japanese colonial rule that eliminated Korean political sovereignty and reorganized Korean economy and society; Japan's sudden defeat in 1945; the equally sudden arrival of Soviet and American occupation forces, which divided the peninsula at the 38th parallel as a temporary military line marking where each army would accept the Japanese surrender; the hardening of that surrender line into two separate governments in 1948 amid the emerging global Cold War; and a catastrophic three-year civil war, examined in Part XII, that killed enormous numbers of Koreans, involved international combatants on both sides, and ended in 1953 not with a peace treaty but with an armistice and a heavily fortified Demilitarized Zone that remains in place today.

None of this was predetermined by ancient regional rivalries, ethnic differences, or supposed timeless northern and southern Korean characters. Students should resist any narrative suggesting that a "naturally" divided Korea simply reasserted an old pattern in 1948. The specific ideological, military, and international circumstances of division belong to the twentieth century and are examined in detail in Volume IV, not read back into ancient or medieval Korean history.

By the book's final chapter, students will have followed one continuous historical civilization through prehistoric settlement, ancient state formation, the Three Kingdoms and Gaya, Silla and Balhae, Goryeo, Joseon, imperial crisis, colonization, liberation, civil war, and finally two distinct twenty-first century Korean states, each having developed its own political system, economy, and international relationships while sharing an inherited history stretching back thousands of years. Understanding that shared inheritance, and understanding how and why it eventually produced two very different states, is the task the rest of this textbook undertakes.

Historical Vocabulary
Periodization and Anachronism

Periodization is the historical practice of dividing the past into named eras for analytical convenience. Terms such as "Three Kingdoms period," "Later Three Kingdoms period," and "Joseon period" are scholarly conventions that help organize a long and complicated history into comprehensible units. They are useful tools, not natural or self-evident divisions that historical participants themselves would necessarily have recognized in the same way.

Anachronism is the error of applying a later concept, term, or identity to an earlier period in which it did not yet exist or apply in the same way. Describing a Bronze Age dolmen-building community as "ancient Korean" in the modern national sense, or describing a Baekje official as a "Korean citizen," would both be anachronistic, since neither the modern nation-state nor the modern national identity existed at that time. This textbook uses geographic and period-specific terms, such as "peninsula," "Gojoseon," or "Three Kingdoms period," when describing early societies, reserving fuller national and cultural claims about "Korea" and "Korean identity" for periods when the evidence supports them.

Primary Source Window

The Samguk Yusa on Dangun and the Founding of Gojoseon, 13th Century CE

Creator: traditionally attributed to the Buddhist monk Iryeon and associated compilers of the late Goryeo period · Date: compiled in the thirteenth century CE, describing events traditionally dated many centuries earlier · Type: Historical and legendary compilation, the Samguk Yusa ("Memorabilia of the Three Kingdoms"), assembled under Mongol-era pressure on Goryeo

Context: Korea's best-known account of Dangun and the founding of Gojoseon survives in the Samguk Yusa, a compilation produced during the thirteenth century, a period of severe crisis for Goryeo under sustained Mongol military pressure, examined in Chapter 23. Many historians consider the timing suggestive: a foundation narrative describing a long, divinely sanctioned Korean political tradition survives in a text assembled around the same period when Goryeo's independence faced serious external threat, though the surviving evidence does not allow historians to establish the compiler's specific intentions with certainty.

Paraphrase: The following is a paraphrase, not a direct quotation, of the narrative's content.

The narrative describes a heavenly figure who descended to a sacred mountain, a bear and a tiger who sought to become human through trial and endurance, and the eventual birth of Dangun, who is said to have founded a state named Joseon and to have ruled for an extraordinarily long reign before the account transitions toward more historically grounded rulers of the northwestern peninsula.

Analysis: This passage is valuable to students of this chapter because it illustrates the difference between a foundation tradition, preserved and shaped by later compilers, and a contemporary historical record. The narrative's supernatural elements, its extraordinarily long reign lengths, and the many centuries separating its recording from its traditional setting all indicate that it functions as a foundation myth carrying deep cultural significance rather than as a documentary account of Bronze Age political events.

Limitations: The narrative cannot establish the actual date, extent, or governmental structure of the earliest Korean political community, and it should not be treated as equivalent to the later, better documented references to Gojoseon found in contemporary Chinese historical records. At the same time, the story's endurance and its preservation in a compilation produced during a period of political crisis reveal a great deal about medieval Korean historical consciousness and the cultural work such origin narratives perform.

Questions for students:

  1. Why might a national foundation narrative be recorded and emphasized during a period of political crisis rather than during a period of stability?
  2. What does the presence of clearly legendary elements suggest about how this source should be used as historical evidence?
  3. Why is it important to distinguish this thirteenth-century compilation from a contemporary record of events traditionally dated to the third millennium BCE?
  4. How does the existence of a documented, later Gojoseon in Chinese historical sources relate to, but also differ from, the Dangun tradition?
Historical Debate

When Does "Korean" History Properly Begin?

The question: Should a history of Korea begin with the documented rise of Gojoseon as a political power in Chinese historical records, treated as the first secure evidence of organized Korean statehood, or should it begin earlier, with the prehistoric human societies of the peninsula and Manchuria whose material remains long predate any documented state and even predate the traditional Dangun founding date? Historians differ on how to weight these two possible starting points and on how much continuity should be assumed between prehistoric peninsular societies and the later states that arose on the same territory.

Interpretation A — Korean history begins meaningfully with documented statehood: Some historians argue that "Korean history" as a coherent political and cultural narrative properly begins with Gojoseon's appearance in the documentary record, treating earlier prehistoric societies as important background but not yet "Korean" in any politically or culturally specific sense.
Interpretation B — Korean history must include its longer prehistoric foundation: Other historians emphasize that the peninsula's Paleolithic, Neolithic, and Bronze Age societies developed sophisticated technologies, settlement patterns, burial traditions, and regional networks over many thousands of years, and that beginning the story only with documented statehood risks reducing this long span of human habitation to a mere prelude, even though doing so requires care not to project any unified "Korean" identity onto these earlier and regionally varied populations.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the same body of evidence, archaeological remains, later Korean and Chinese textual sources, and modern scholarly reconstruction, but differ in how much historical weight and independent significance to give the prehistoric material relative to the political innovations that began with organized statehood.

Why it matters: This textbook resolves the debate practically rather than doctrinally: it devotes Chapters 3 through 5 to the peninsula's Paleolithic, Neolithic, and Bronze Age societies before treating Gojoseon in Chapter 6, treating prehistoric Korean history as historically significant in its own right while still organizing Volume I's overall narrative around the emergence of documented statehood as a foundational, though not exclusive, point of departure.

Student task: Based on the evidence described above, construct an argument for each interpretation, and then explain which starting point you find more persuasive and why. Consider what is gained and what is lost by each choice of starting point.

Multiple Perspectives

Who Gets to Define "Korea"? Perspectives on Naming a Peninsula

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns historians have identified across many sources. They are not quotations from any single named historical actor.

From the perspective of a thirteenth-century Goryeo Buddhist chronicler under Mongol pressure: Recording the Dangun tradition affirms that Korea possesses an ancient, divinely sanctioned political community stretching back long before the current crisis, providing cultural continuity and dignity at a moment when Goryeo's political independence is under severe external threat.
From the perspective of a modern South Korean historian: "Korea" names a continuous civilization whose modern expression, the Republic of Korea, carries forward the political and cultural inheritance of Joseon, the Korean Empire, and Korean nationalist resistance to colonial rule, a narrative examined further in Part IX and Volume IV.
From the perspective of a modern North Korean historian: "Chosŏn" names the same continuous civilization, but its legitimate contemporary expression lies with the Democratic People's Republic of Korea, whose founding narrative emphasizes anti-colonial resistance, revolutionary struggle, and a distinct political and economic path, examined further in Chapter 77 and throughout Volume IV.
From the perspective of an international historian studying the peninsula comparatively: "Korea" is best understood as a long historical civilization that has, at different times, taken the form of one state, several competing states, and, since 1948, two rival states, each claiming the same historical inheritance while developing in sharply different directions, a pattern this textbook treats as requiring careful, even-handed historical explanation rather than allegiance to either state's official narrative.
Continuity and Change Over Time

The Idea of "Korea" from Antiquity to the Present

What Changed

  • The peninsula's political organization shifted repeatedly between unity and plurality, from Gojoseon and the Samhan through the Three Kingdoms and Gaya, Silla and Balhae, unified Goryeo and Joseon, and the modern division
  • The name most commonly used for Korea, and by whom, changed across periods, from Joseon and Goryeo to Daehan, and eventually to the divergent Hanguk and Chosŏn of the two modern states
  • The writing system used to record the Korean language changed from exclusive reliance on Classical Chinese and adaptive systems such as idu to the widespread use of Hangeul following its fifteenth-century creation
  • Korea's relationship with neighboring powers evolved from ancient tributary and military relationships with Chinese dynasties through complex early modern diplomacy to colonization by, and later renewed cultural exchange with, Japan, and finally to Cold War and post-Cold War relationships involving the United States, China, Russia, and Japan

What Stayed the Same

  • The Korean peninsula and its immediate Manchurian and maritime surroundings have remained the geographic core of Korean history throughout its documented existence
  • From at least the era of documented states onward, a recognizably continuous Korean language tradition has persisted, despite significant change, though linguistic evidence for the peninsula's prehistoric populations remains too limited to support similar claims about that earlier period
  • Sustained interaction, connection, and periodic conflict with Chinese and Japanese neighbors has remained a constant feature rather than an occasional exception
  • Debates over what "Korea" means, who belongs within it, and which political authority legitimately represents it have recurred across many centuries, from the Three Kingdoms' competing claims to the present division

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both change and continuity prevents two opposite errors: treating "Korea" as an ancient, unchanging essence extending unbroken from Dangun to the present, or treating it as nothing more than a collection of unrelated states with no genuine historical connection.

Cause and Effect

Why Definitional Confusion about Korea Persists

Long-term causes

The long historical overlap between Korean statehood and shifting names, Gojoseon, Goryeo, Joseon, Daehan, and the deep cultural importance of foundation narratives such as Dangun, which blend historical memory with legendary tradition.

Short-term causes

The sudden twentieth-century division of the peninsula in 1948 produced two governments using different historical names, Hanguk and Chosŏn, for ordinary reference to the same peninsula, reinforcing public confusion about what "Korea" means today.

Effect

Popular understanding of Korean history often flattens genuine political plurality into an assumed continuous national unity, or conversely treats North and South Korea as entirely unrelated societies with no shared historical inheritance.

This textbook's response

Consistent, careful use of period-specific and state-specific names throughout all ninety-six chapters, with explicit attention to political plurality, regional diversity, and the parallel, equally legitimate treatment of both modern Korean states.

Historical complexity: Imprecise terminology is not merely a modern journalistic problem; it has roots in centuries of Korean court chroniclers, and later nationalist historians on both sides of the modern division, using historical and mythological categories for their own political and cultural purposes.

Everyday Life and Social History

Why Ordinary Identity Belongs in This Chapter

Definitions of "Korea," "Joseon," and "Hanguk" are not only matters for scholars and governments; they shape how ordinary people have experienced daily life, legal status, and belonging across the centuries this textbook studies. A Silla farmer working land under the bone-rank system, a Goryeo Buddhist monk copying sutras at a mountain monastery, a Joseon-era yangban scholar preparing for the state examinations, a nineteenth-century peasant caught up in Donghak religious enthusiasm, and a present-day office worker in Seoul or a farmer in a North Korean cooperative each navigated daily social, legal, and economic life shaped by their status, region, occupation, and the particular state under which they lived, categories that often mattered more in practice than any single overarching label such as "Korean."

Throughout this textbook, chapters devoted to society and everyday life examine how ordinary populations across the peninsula experienced agriculture, markets, family and community obligation, taxation, religious practice, and, in the modern era, citizenship and national identity under two different political systems. Because most surviving premodern written sources were produced by a literate aristocratic or religious elite, historians must read these sources carefully for indirect evidence about groups who left few records of their own, including women, farmers, fishers, artisans, enslaved and unfree people (nobi), and regional and island communities such as those of Jeju, while also drawing on archaeology, material culture, and, for the modern period, oral history and government records to reconstruct their experiences more fully.

Chapter Summary

This first chapter has introduced "Korea" as this textbook's organizing subject and has carefully distinguished it from several related but distinct concepts with which it is often, and mistakenly, conflated: a single continuous nation-state, a subsidiary chapter of Chinese history, and an isolated civilization. Korea, as this textbook defines and studies it, is a peninsula-centered historical, cultural, and political space whose political organization, dominant names, and self-understanding have all changed substantially across millennia of documented history and a much deeper archaeological past.

The chapter has emphasized that geographic, linguistic, cultural, and political definitions of Korea are related but distinct, and that careful use of these categories is essential to accurate historical understanding. It has explained why Manchuria and the surrounding seas are integral rather than peripheral to Korean history, and why political plurality, not continuous unity, has repeatedly characterized the peninsula's political condition, from the Samhan and Three Kingdoms period through the Later Three Kingdoms period to the current division between the Republic of Korea and the Democratic People's Republic of Korea.

Students have been introduced to the major names historically applied to Korea and Korean states: Gojoseon, the Three Kingdoms and Gaya, Balhae, Goryeo, from which the English name "Korea" derives, Joseon, Daehan, and the modern Hanguk and Chosŏn, each carrying distinct historical associations rather than functioning as interchangeable synonyms.

The chapter distinguished foundation mythology, later written tradition, archaeological evidence, and contemporary documentary evidence as different kinds of historical source material, using the Dangun tradition and the documented rise of Gojoseon as a worked example of why these distinctions matter. It also introduced the basic linguistic history of Korean, including the roles of Classical Chinese, idu, hanja, and Hangeul, developed fully in Chapter 27.

The chapter established the textbook's four-volume chronological framework, from prehistoric Korea and the ancient kingdoms of Volume I through the Joseon dynasty of Volume II, the imperial crisis, colonization, and war of Volume III, to the two Koreas of Volume IV, concluding at the fixed contemporary endpoint of 2026. It explained why this periodization reflects genuine historical turning points while remaining an analytical tool rather than a set of natural divisions.

Finally, the chapter directly addressed why the current division of the peninsula requires historical explanation rather than being treated as an inevitable culmination of ancient regional differences. The Historical Debate feature on when Korean history properly begins illustrated a related methodological challenge: how much independent weight should be given to prehistoric peninsular societies relative to the emergence of documented statehood that later observers have often treated as Korean civilization's true origin?

These definitions, distinctions, and commitments are not abstract housekeeping. They are the interpretive foundation on which the following ninety-five chapters are built, and they will recur, explicitly and implicitly, throughout the textbook's treatment of ancient states, the Three Kingdoms and Gaya, Silla and Balhae, Goryeo, Joseon, the imperial crisis and colonial period, the Korean War, and the divergent development of two Korean states into the twenty-first century.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has laid the intellectual groundwork for the entire textbook. The habits of mind it introduces, distinguishing Korea as a peninsula from Korea as a succession of states, separating foundation mythology from documented history, keeping political plurality visible rather than assuming continuous unity, and treating both modern Korean states as equally serious historical subjects, are not isolated skills for a single assignment. They are ways of thinking that should inform the study of every subsequent chapter in this book.

Korean history is one of the most continuous, and in its modern chapter one of the most dramatically divided, historical subjects any textbook can undertake. It has included prehistoric societies that came long before any concept of "Korea"; the documented rise of Gojoseon and the political plurality of the Three Kingdoms and Gaya; the coexistence of Silla and Balhae; the reunification and cultural achievement of Goryeo; the long Neo-Confucian order of Joseon; a catastrophic loss of sovereignty under Japanese colonial rule; and a modern era shaped by liberation, civil war, and the divergent development of two Korean states. Understanding this diversity and discontinuity is not a challenge to Korea's historical significance; it is what makes that significance historically accurate rather than flattened into a simplified or politically convenient story.

Students who understand that "Korea" is a peninsula-centered historical, cultural, and political idea rather than a synonym for any single modern state, that political plurality has repeatedly characterized the peninsula's history, and that both North and South Korea deserve serious, even-handed historical treatment, are well equipped to read the rest of this textbook, from the prehistoric foundations of Part I through the ancient kingdoms and Goryeo of Volume I, the Joseon dynasty of Volume II, the imperial crisis, colonization, and war of Volume III, and the two Koreas of Volume IV, with the same careful, evidence-based attention that professional historians bring to the subject.

The story this textbook tells extends from the Paleolithic peninsula of Chapter 3 through the unresolved questions of the divided peninsula examined in Chapter 96. It is a story of one long historical civilization organized, at different times, into one state, several competing states, and finally two rival states, joined by a shared but never simple inheritance of language, geography, and historical memory. Understanding that story, with all its complexity, continuity, and continuing relevance, is what the rest of this textbook is for.
