The Imo Mutiny, Gapsin Coup, and Competing Reform Movements

Korean History: Ancient Kingdoms to a Divided Peninsula

Chapter Opening

In July 1882 soldiers in Seoul who had gone months without full pay were handed rice mixed with sand and spoilage. Within days an argument over rations had become an assault on a royal palace and a foreign legation. Two and a half years later, on a December night in 1884, a small group of reform-minded officials used the opening banquet of a new postal bureau as cover for an attempt to seize the Korean government. Both crises failed. Together they reshaped who was able to govern Joseon.

The two events at the center of this chapter are conventionally named for the years in which they occurred, using the traditional sixty-year cycle of stems and branches. The Imo Mutiny belongs to the year 1882, and the Gapsin Coup to the year 1884. The names are convenient, but they can mislead, because they suggest two episodes of the same kind. They were not. The Imo Mutiny began as a protest by underpaid and displaced soldiers about concrete grievances, and it widened into an uprising that drew in the urban poor, political opponents of the Min family, and anti-foreign sentiment. The Gapsin Coup was the opposite in character: a planned operation, organized by a small circle of officials, aimed at seizing the king and the palace and imposing a program of institutional change.

What connects them is not a common plan or a common social base but a common problem. After the Treaty of Ganghwa of 1876 (Chapter 48), the Joseon state was trying to strengthen itself while its revenues were thin, its institutions were contested, and its neighbors were increasingly willing to intervene. Different groups drew different conclusions. Some court officials favored adopting foreign technology while preserving the existing political and moral order. Others wanted rapid institutional change. Many scholar-officials and provincial literati opposed any accommodation with Japan or the West. Soldiers, merchants, and ordinary residents of the capital had their own interests, which did not always align with any of these programs.

The political crises that resulted also invited foreign armies into the capital. Qing troops entered Seoul in 1882 and remained. Japanese guards were stationed at the Japanese legation under the settlement of that year. In December 1884 Japanese soldiers were drawn into the reformers' plan, and Qing troops ended it. Korean political disputes had become entangled with a rivalry between Qing China and Meiji Japan over influence on the peninsula. That rivalry was only provisionally settled by the Tianjin Convention of 1885, to which Joseon was not a party, and it would return in 1894 (Chapter 51).

Historians reconstruct these events from several kinds of evidence, each with its own limitations. Korean court records, including the annals of King Gojong's reign and the daily records of the royal secretariat, preserve official versions of events and the memorials and edicts that followed them. Diplomatic correspondence and treaty texts survive in Korean, Chinese, and Japanese archives. Kim Ok-gyun's Gapsin Ilrok, the best-known account of the coup from the reformers' side, was written in exile and is both indispensable and self-interested. Later chronicles such as Hwang Hyeon's Maecheon Yarok offer vivid but retrospective and politically opinionated narratives. Foreign diplomats and residents left their own accounts. No single source is neutral, and the chapter treats disagreement among them as part of the history.

The chapter also attends to the people who rarely write the documents. The soldiers of the old camps and their households lived in particular neighborhoods and depended on a salary that had become unreliable. Seoul residents experienced street fighting, arrests, and reprisals. After the coup, relatives of the reformers suffered punishments for acts they had not committed. These experiences are less fully documented than the maneuvers of ministers and envoys, but they are necessary to explain why the crises unfolded as they did.

Finally, the chapter resists two tempting simplifications. The first treats 1882 and 1884 as rehearsals for 1894, as though later events were already contained in earlier ones. The second reads the history of these years as a contest between progressive reformers and reactionary conservatives. The record shows more complicated alignments: reformers who opposed the coup, conservatives with practical concerns, a king and queen pursuing their own strategies, and foreign officials who changed their minds. The chapter's central question is why competing attempts to reform and control the Joseon state culminated in these two crises, and how their outcomes altered Korea's capacity to govern independently.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain the military, fiscal, and political conditions in Seoul that made the Imo Mutiny possible, including the reorganization of the army and the arrears in soldiers' pay.
  2. Trace the course of the mutiny from the ration dispute of July 1882 to the Daewongun's return to power and his removal by Qing forces.
  3. Distinguish the soldiers' material grievances from the political and anti-foreign dimensions of the revolt, and assess how historians weigh them.
  4. Analyze how Qing and Japanese responses to the mutiny, including the Treaty of Jemulpo, changed the conditions under which the Korean court governed.
  5. Compare the moderate, radical, and conservative positions on reform in the early 1880s, and explain why these labels are useful but imperfect.
  6. Describe the formation of the reformist network around Kim Ok-gyun and Park Yeong-hyo and the obstacles it faced.
  7. Reconstruct the events of December 4–6, 1884, and explain why the Gapsin Coup collapsed so quickly.
  8. Evaluate the Fourteen Reform Decrees as evidence, distinguishing proposals from measures actually implemented.
  9. Assess the Hanseong Treaty and the Tianjin Convention as settlements negotiated largely without Korean participation in their decisive terms.
  10. Interpret competing historical judgments of the Gapsin Coup, weighing its reform content against its methods and its dependence on foreign force.
  11. Contextualize both crises within the wider Qing–Japanese rivalry without treating Korean actors as passive.
  12. Connect the two crises to the struggle over Korea's political future examined in later chapters, while avoiding teleological readings.

Historical Context

Students should arrive at this chapter with the following background, developed in earlier chapters. King Gojong, who came to the throne as a boy in 1863, governed under the regency of his father, the Heungseon Daewongun, until the early 1870s. The Daewongun's reforms and his anti-foreign policies are treated in Chapter 47, as is the rise of the family of Queen Min, whose relatives came to hold many senior offices. The Ganghwa Treaty of 1876 (Chapter 48) challenged the older diplomatic framework by inaugurating unequal treaty relations with Japan; it did not end Joseon's continuing tributary relationship with Qing China. The treaties, treaty ports, and commercial arrangements that followed are the subject of Chapter 49. This chapter refers to those developments but does not repeat them.

Three structural conditions matter especially. The first is fiscal. The Joseon government depended on land taxes and related levies that had been strained by the corruption and abuses described in Chapter 44, and it now faced new expenses: diplomatic missions, new offices, new military units, and indemnities. Revenue did not grow in step with these demands. The second is military. The army inherited from the seventeenth century consisted of the five camps created in the aftermath of the Imjin War and the Manchu invasions (Chapters 35 and 36). By 1881 these camps were poorly paid, unevenly supplied, and dependent on a salary system that had been disrupted by corruption and shortage. Their soldiers lived with their families in particular districts of the capital and were bound to the camps by long-standing hereditary or semi-hereditary service.

The third condition is political. In the capital, power was concentrated in a small circle around the king and queen, and in the Min family and its allies. Access to office, revenue, and the king was contested. The Daewongun, though out of formal office, retained supporters, and in 1881 a plot to replace Gojong with the Daewongun's elder son was uncovered and punished. Outside the capital, Confucian scholars mobilized against the opening to Japan and the West; in 1881 thousands of provincial literati petitioned the throne against the adoption of foreign ideas. Other officials and younger aristocrats, impressed by what they had learned from missions to Japan and Qing China, argued for more thorough institutional change. These camps overlapped and divided in ways that the labels used later in this chapter capture only partly.

Finally, the international situation was shifting. Qing China, which had long maintained a tributary relationship with Joseon in which the Korean court accepted Chinese investiture while managing its own internal affairs, began under Li Hongzhang's direction to redefine that relationship in more active terms. Japan, newly modernized and eager to establish itself on the continent, sought influence through diplomacy, commerce, and military instruction. Western powers had begun to negotiate treaties, beginning with the United States in 1882. Korean officials of all political tendencies had to decide how far to depend on any outside power, and which one.

Division I · Sections 50.1–50.4

Military, Political, and Social Divisions before 1882

Why the political system was becoming unstable

Section 50.1

Joseon after the Opening: Reform and Political Instability

After 1876 Gojong's government did not simply react to foreign pressure. It also began, tentatively, to experiment. In 1880 the court created an office for the management of state affairs, the Tongnigimuamun, modeled in part on the Qing office that handled foreign relations and related modernizing measures. In 1881 it sent a large observation mission to Japan, whose members, drawn from several ministries, toured Japanese government offices, factories, and military facilities and returned with reports. That same year a smaller group of Koreans traveled to Tianjin to study Qing arsenals and manufacturing. These initiatives show a court willing to learn from abroad, and they also show how little the effort rested on a stable fiscal or administrative base. The new offices competed for funds with existing ones, and their authority over the older ministries was ill defined.

Gojong himself occupied an unusual position. He had been enthroned as a child and, after his father's regency ended, he exercised personal authority in a political environment in which the queen's relatives held many of the most important posts. The Min family's influence grew with the king's reliance on it, and it generated resentment among officials excluded from its patronage, among supporters of the Daewongun, and among those who regarded the family's dominance as a distortion of proper government. Historians use the term "sedo politics" for the pattern in which a monarch's in-laws monopolize power, and Chapter 43 traces its earlier forms. It would be too simple to treat the Min family as a unified faction; its members held different views on foreign policy and reform, and some of them proved among the most receptive to new ideas.

Opposition was real and varied. The Daewongun's supporters wanted him restored, or at least wanted a different arrangement of power. The conspiracy of 1881 was punished severely, but it showed that a disaffected circle of officials was willing to risk an attempt on the throne. Provincial scholars, defending what they called orthodoxy against heterodoxy, regarded the treaties and the adoption of foreign methods as a betrayal of the moral foundations of the kingdom. Their petitions, such as the large memorial presented from the southeastern provinces in 1881, were not mere reaction. They articulated an alternative understanding of what the state was for, and they commanded the respect of many local elites.

Government capacity was the underlying constraint. Revenue still depended on land taxes and the grain-tribute system that had been under strain for decades. New expenses arrived faster than new income. Reformers who wanted to build modern institutions had to find money, and the officials who controlled existing revenue were rarely willing to surrender it. The commercial changes that followed the opening, described in Chapter 49, also altered prices and supply in ways that burdened some urban households, notably through the growing export of grain. The combined effect was a state with greater ambitions and lower reserves, in which any disruption to payments to the capital's soldiers could become a political crisis.

Section 50.2

Military Reorganization and the Creation of the Byeolgigun

The most consequential of the court's new initiatives was military. Korean officials who had visited Japan and Qing China concluded that the army's weapons and drill were outdated. In 1881 the court created a new unit, the Byeolgigun ("Special Skills Force"), and engaged a Japanese army officer, Lieutenant Horimoto Reizō, to train it. The unit was initially small, composed of a few score of young men selected largely from aristocratic families, and was given modern uniforms, rifles, and drill. Its recruits were paid and provisioned better than the older soldiers and enjoyed the court's favor. For its supporters it was a first step toward a modern army. For the older soldiers it was a visible sign that the state valued them less.

In December 1881 the court also reorganized the existing five camps, inherited from the seventeenth century, into two, called the Muwiyeong and the Jangeoyeong. Consolidation was a plausible rationalization of overlapping commands, and many Korean and foreign observers agreed that the old arrangement was inefficient. But reorganization has human consequences. Positions were eliminated or merged, some soldiers were discharged or reassigned, and the households that depended on military salaries lost income or security. Sources differ on how many were affected, and the figure often repeated for those discharged should be treated as an approximation. What is clear is that reorganization added displacement to existing grievances.

The presence of a Japanese instructor aggravated matters. Anti-Japanese feeling in Seoul had already been strong before 1876, and the opening had exposed the capital to Japanese merchants and diplomats. Training Korean soldiers in Japanese methods under a Japanese officer was, to many observers, particularly provocative. Older soldiers could see that the new unit, trained by a foreigner, received what they had been denied. Resentment of the unit, resentment of Japanese influence, and resentment of unpaid pay accumulated together.

Historical Vocabulary
Byeolgigun, the Five Camps, and the Two Camps

Definition. The Byeolgigun ("Special Skills Force") was a small, Japanese-trained military unit created in 1881. The five camps (ogunyeong) were the capital's main military commands, established in the seventeenth century after the Imjin War and the Manchu invasions. In December 1881 they were consolidated into two, the Muwiyeong and the Jangeoyeong.

Historical context. The reorganization was meant to modernize and economize. It did so by merging offices, reducing posts, and favoring a new unit with modern training and equipment, while older soldiers' salaries remained in arrears.

Why it matters. The difference in treatment between the Byeolgigun and the older soldiers made a financial dispute into a question of status and state priorities.

Potential misunderstanding. The Byeolgigun was not a national army. It was a small unit, and its significance was symbolic and political as much as military.

Section 50.3

Unpaid Soldiers, Defective Rations, and Urban Hardship

Soldiers of the capital camps were paid largely in grain. Their salaries were drawn from stores managed by state grain offices, and the system depended on regular shipments of tax grain to the capital and on honest administration of the storehouses. By 1882 the system had broken down. Scholarship based on the court's own records and on the Academy of Korean Studies reconstruction documents a backlog of salary payments amounting to roughly thirteen months for many soldiers. A soldier's household that had expected regular grain was instead forced to borrow, sell possessions, or find casual labor.

The problem was not only shortage but corruption and mismanagement. Officials who handled the grain stood to profit from diverting stores, mixing inferior grain with good, or delaying issue. Contemporary accounts, which vary in detail, agree that when rice was finally distributed in 1882 it was of shockingly poor quality. Because officials associated with the Min family controlled the relevant offices, including the Seonhyecheong, the bureau that administered grain taxation and military provisioning, the grievances acquired a political edge. Soldiers who might have blamed individual clerks came to blame the court's leading family.

The soldiers did not live in isolation. They resided with families in particular districts of the capital, notably the neighborhoods to the east of the main city, and they shared those neighborhoods with laborers, porters, petty merchants, and other poor households. When a family's income failed, its neighbors often suffered too, because the soldiers' purchases supported local trade. Grain prices in the capital, affected by supply and by exports through the new treaty ports, mattered to everyone. A protest by soldiers could therefore draw sympathy and participation from a wider community of urban poor with grievances of their own.

It is important not to reduce the soldiers' action to manipulation by court politicians. Political actors, including supporters of the Daewongun, undoubtedly saw opportunity in the unrest. But the grievances over pay and rations were real, long-standing, and documented independently of any political interest. Soldiers had good reason to protest, and the evidence for their motivation does not depend on any claim about a hidden organizer.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Soldier's Household in Early 1880s Seoul

For a soldier of the older camps, military service was not only a duty but a livelihood and a family inheritance. The salary, paid in grain, fed a household that might include a spouse, children, and elderly relatives. When payments fell months behind, the household's options narrowed. Family members might take in sewing or piecework, children might be sent to relatives, and possessions might be pawned. Debt to local lenders and shopkeepers accumulated, and those creditors in turn depended on the soldiers' eventual payment.

Women in these households carried much of the burden of managing scarcity, though the records rarely name them. Neighbors, including laborers and porters who worked near the grain wharves and storehouses, shared the same markets and the same prices. When a soldier was arrested for protesting, as happened in 1882, the loss of the household's wage earner and the risk of execution affected an entire network of kin and neighbors. Historians reconstruct this world from court records, official petitions, later memoirs, and the circumstantial evidence of where soldiers lived and what they were owed; the testimony of soldiers' wives and children is largely absent from the surviving sources, and their experience must be inferred.

Section 50.4

The Court, the Daewongun, Conservative Officials, and Reformers

The political networks that would shape both crises were already visible. At the center was the king, who relied on the queen and on officials from her family but who also, at various moments, sought to balance them. The queen's relatives held offices that gave them control of money and military appointments. Their critics included officials excluded from patronage and anyone who objected to the family's dominance on principle. Within the Min family itself, views diverged: some members supported cautious reform and some resisted it, and treating the family as a single bloc obscures actual disagreements.

The Daewongun stood outside this circle but had not disappeared. He retained a network of former subordinates and sympathizers, and his sons were integral to the court's family politics. After his retirement from the regency he remained a potential alternative center of authority, and the conspiracy of 1881 showed that some were willing to act on that possibility. His own ideological position was conservative in several respects: he had pursued a hostile policy toward Western powers and had strengthened royal authority through restoration of the palace and fiscal measures (Chapter 47). But his motivations are better explained by interest and by his conception of dynastic order than by simple opposition to every kind of change.

Outside the court, provincial scholar-officials and literati regarded the opening and the new offices as threats to Confucian order. The 1881 petitions from the provinces urged the king to reject the Japanese and the new learning. Many conservative officials drew a distinction between technology and principle. They were willing, in some cases, to accept limited technical borrowing but rejected the political and moral implications of the reforms and the treaty relationships that accompanied them. This is why historians distinguish ideological conservatism, a principled defense of a moral and political order, from the institutional or personal interests that also motivated opposition to particular reforms.

Reformers formed a third cluster, though one internally divided. Some, such as Kim Hong-jip and Kim Yun-sik, favored selective adoption of Western techniques within a framework of continuing Confucian government and cooperation with Qing China. Others, younger and more critical of the existing order, wanted faster and deeper institutional change and were more inclined to look to Japan. Both groups saw themselves as defenders of the dynasty's survival, and their disagreement concerned means, pace, and foreign partners. Section 50.16 develops this distinction, and it will matter again when the reformers' paths diverged after 1882.

Division II · Sections 50.5–50.10

The Imo Mutiny and the Crisis of July 1882

Reconstructing the uprising and its political consequences

Section 50.5

The Rice-Ration Dispute and the Outbreak of Mutiny

The immediate crisis began in the summer of 1882. After many months without full pay, soldiers of the older camps were finally issued a portion of their back pay in rice. According to the accounts that survive, the grain was spoiled and adulterated, mixed with sand and bran, and the quantity was short. The soldiers' anger was directed first at the storehouse officials who had distributed it. In the dispute that followed, soldiers confronted a clerk, and the matter was reported to the authorities. The senior official responsible for grain and military finance, Min Gyeom-ho, ordered the arrest of soldiers who had taken part, and several were condemned to death, among them leaders identified in the sources as Kim Chun-yeong and Yu Bok-man.

The arrests converted a complaint into a danger. Soldiers and their families realized that protest could lead to execution. Contemporary accounts report that relatives of the condemned men sought help and that word of the arrests spread among soldiers of the older camps. Some accounts assert that the soldiers appealed to the Daewongun or his circle for advice or support, but these claims come largely from later narratives hostile or favorable to particular factions, and what the Daewongun actually said or did at this stage is not securely documented. What the records do show is that the soldiers organized with remarkable speed once the arrests occurred.

The date usually given for the main outbreak is July 23, 1882, corresponding to the ninth day of the sixth lunar month. Soldiers gathered and went first to the residence of the official they held responsible, then freed their imprisoned comrades and seized weapons from armories. The step from complaint to armed rebellion was short and driven by circumstances: the soldiers had been told that those who had protested would die, they possessed weapons or could seize them, and the authorities in the capital had little ability to restrain them. Historians therefore describe the sequence as a progression from grievance to protest to defense of comrades to open revolt, rather than as the execution of a prepared plan.

Section 50.6

The Uprising Spreads through Seoul

Once weapons were in the soldiers' hands, the revolt expanded. Soldiers from other older units joined, and the target of their anger widened from the officials who had arrested their comrades to the apparatus of the Min family and of the new reforms. Houses and offices belonging to leading officials were attacked. Government facilities were stormed. Weapons from storehouses and arsenals were distributed, further arming participants. The speed with which the rebels spread through the city shows how thin the state's defenses were: the capital's armed forces were precisely the group in revolt.

The uprising did not remain a purely military affair. Residents of the poorer districts of the capital, including laborers, porters, and small traders who shared the soldiers' neighborhoods and hardships, joined in. Their participation is described in contemporary accounts as significant, though precise numbers are not available. Their motivations likely overlapped with the soldiers' but were not identical: grain prices, the exactions of local officials, and resentment of the wealthy and well-connected families who controlled the capital's offices all offered reasons to join. The presence of civilians made the revolt harder to control and harder for later narrators to categorize as either a military mutiny or a popular uprising. It was both.

Breakdown of authority in the capital was swift but incomplete. The royal court, concentrated in the palace, had few reliable forces at hand. Officials either fled, hid, or were attacked. The rebels did not, at first, have a clear political program beyond punishing those responsible for their suffering and the people they associated with the reforms. This absence of a program is part of what made the rebellion vulnerable, but it also made it difficult for the court to negotiate: there was no organized leadership to approach with concessions.

Section 50.7

The Attack on the Japanese Legation

Among the targets of the revolt was the Japanese legation. Hostility toward Japanese influence had several sources: the humiliating circumstances of the 1876 treaty, the growing Japanese commercial presence, and especially the training of the Byeolgigun by a Japanese officer. Horimoto Reizō was killed during the uprising, and rebels attacked the legation compound, where Minister Hanabusa Yoshitada (also read Yoshimoto) and his staff were present. The legation was set on fire. Accounts differ on the number of Japanese fatalities. Horimoto and several other Japanese personnel were killed, but a precise total should not be asserted without specifying which victims and records are being counted.

Hanabusa and the surviving members of his party escaped from the burning legation and fought their way toward the port at Incheon. They were able to take refuge there and later left the coast aboard a British survey vessel, reaching Japan. The escape mattered politically. It meant that the Japanese government would learn of the attack quickly and would be able to respond with force and demands, rather than being left uncertain about what had happened. The attack gave Tokyo both a grievance and an opportunity.

Historians distinguish the anti-Japanese violence from the soldiers' other grievances while recognizing that the two were linked. The soldiers had not been mistreated by Japan; their pay had been withheld by Korean officials. But Japan was visibly associated with the changes that had disadvantaged them and with the favoritism shown to the Byeolgigun. The legation attack therefore combined anger at a specific foreign instructor, a broader hostility toward Japan's growing presence, and the logic of a rebellion that had already turned against the symbols of the reform program.

Section 50.8

The Palace Crisis and Queen Min's Escape

The rebels also turned toward the palace, where the king resided at Changdeokgung. In the violence that followed, several senior officials were killed, among them Min Gyeom-ho, the official who had ordered the arrests, as well as other prominent figures identified in the sources, including Kim Bo-hyeon and Yi Choe-eung, the latter a member of the Daewongun's own family. The killing of officials in or near the palace shows that the revolt had crossed from protest into a direct assault on the structure of government.

Queen Min was the object of intense hostility. Rebels searched the palace for her. According to accounts that agree on the main outline, she escaped disguised, aided by a palace guard, and made her way out of the capital to shelter in the countryside. Her whereabouts were uncertain for some time. The court later announced her death and prepared funeral rites, though whether this was an expression of genuine belief, a political maneuver by the Daewongun, or something else is among the questions that scholars have debated. Her survival would prove decisive: when she returned, her political strength had not been destroyed.

The danger to Gojong's government was acute but not final. The king was in the palace, the rebels had not seized the state apparatus in an organized way, and there was no alternative government prepared to replace his. But the court could not restore order using the forces at hand. Its military had mutinied, its senior officials were dead or in hiding, and its foreign supporters were far away. The situation required a figure who could command some obedience from the soldiers and who commanded the king's trust. That figure was the Daewongun.

Section 50.9

The Daewongun's Return to Political Power

King Gojong, facing the collapse of order, turned to his father. The Daewongun was summoned to the palace and entrusted with restoring authority. The soldiers, many of whom regarded him as an opponent of the Min family and a defender of older ways, were more willing to obey him than any other figure. In the days that followed, he took effective control of the government.

His actions were substantial and reflect a deliberate reversal of recent policy. He abolished the Tongnigimuamun, the new office for managing state affairs, and the Byeolgigun, the Japanese-trained unit. He restored the older five-camp military system, undoing the consolidation of December 1881, and made appointments favorable to his own supporters. He also moved to resolve the immediate military grievances. The soldiers, whose pay and status were the proximate cause of the crisis, were given at least partial satisfaction. These measures show that the Daewongun had both the authority and the intent to reverse the program of the preceding two years.

His restoration lasted about a month. It faced obstacles from the start. The treasury remained short of funds. The king and queen had not become reconciled to his authority, and the queen, whose death he had formally announced, was alive. Most importantly, the neighboring powers would not accept the removal of the reform program or the attack on the Japanese legation. Qing officials, in particular, had strong reasons to want to reassert influence in Seoul.

Historians distinguish what the Daewongun did from what has been claimed about his earlier role. His political advantage and his intervention after the outbreak are documented. Whether he planned the revolt from the beginning, directed it from behind the scenes, or merely exploited it once it began is far less certain. Accounts that assert a detailed prior conspiracy tend to come from sources, Qing, Japanese, or Korean, with their own political motives for assigning him responsibility. The responsible conclusion is that his role in the crisis after July 23 is clear, while his role in its origins remains contested.

Section 50.10

Interpreting the Imo Mutiny: Soldiers, Citizens, and Political Interests

Interpreting the mutiny requires recognizing that different participants acted for different reasons. The soldiers' grievances over pay and rations were direct and material. The urban poor who joined the revolt had overlapping but distinct concerns, including prices, local abuses, and hostility to the wealthy families they associated with the court. Supporters of the Daewongun saw opportunity to reverse the Min family's dominance and the reforms that accompanied it. Hostility toward Japanese and other foreigners, though real, was not the only or necessarily the principal motivation for most participants.

Historians have emphasized different aspects. Some describe the mutiny primarily as a military revolt driven by material hardship. Others stress its anti-foreign and conservative character, seeing it as resistance to the opening and to the reforms that followed. Still others emphasize the political struggle between court factions. These interpretations are not mutually exclusive, and the strongest accounts hold them together: a revolt whose origin lay in economic grievance, whose targets were shaped by the political and diplomatic environment, and whose outcome was determined by the maneuvering of those with power to exploit it.

Identifying a single organizer or ideological program is difficult and perhaps misguided. The evidence does not show a coordinated plan from the outset. It shows rapid improvisation by soldiers who had little to lose, followed by attempts by various actors to direct the consequences. Contemporary accounts, including official court records, later chronicles, and foreign reports, differ in emphasis, and each reflects the perspective of its author. Official accounts tended to emphasize disorder and the punishment of offenders; later narratives sympathetic to the Min family or hostile to the Daewongun assigned blame accordingly; foreign accounts concentrated on the attack on their own nationals.

Division III · Sections 50.11–50.15

Qing Intervention, Japanese Demands, and Political Realignment

How a domestic uprising became an international crisis

Section 50.11

Qing Military Intervention and the Removal of the Daewongun

News of the uprising reached Qing officials quickly, in part through Korean officials then in Tianjin, who urged Qing help in restoring order. Li Hongzhang, the Qing statesman who managed relations with Korea and much of China's modernizing diplomacy, concluded that intervention served several purposes. It would restore a government friendly to Qing interests, forestall a unilateral Japanese response that might establish Japanese predominance, and demonstrate that Qing China intended to give its relationship with Korea a more active character. Ma Jianzhong, a Qing official who had recently taken part in Korea's treaty negotiations with the United States (Chapter 49), and the naval commander Ding Ruchang went to Korea with warships. They were followed by an expeditionary force under General Wu Changqing, which included the young officer Yuan Shikai. Figures for the force vary in sources, but it numbered in the low thousands.

Qing officers took action against the Daewongun. In late August they invited him to a meeting at their camp and then, instead of releasing him, seized him and transported him to China, where he was held for several years. The operation removed the one figure whom the soldiers had been willing to obey and eliminated the principal alternative to the king's own government. It also demonstrated that Qing officers were prepared to remove a prominent member of the Korean royal family without the formal consent of the Korean court. Later Korean and foreign observers regarded this as a decisive precedent.

The Qing troops then moved against the remaining rebels. Soldiers and civilians in the districts where the revolt had been strongest were arrested, and a smaller number were executed. Contemporary accounts suggest that Qing troops also engaged in looting and harassment of residents, though the extent is difficult to document. Order was restored, and the king's government was returned to authority, but its restoration depended on foreign force. The government that emerged was therefore more secure against domestic rebellion and more dependent on Qing support.

Historical Vocabulary
Suzerainty and the Tributary Relationship

Definition. A tributary relationship in the premodern East Asian order involved a ruler who accepted investiture and ritual precedence from the Chinese court, sent periodic missions, and used its calendar, while generally managing internal affairs independently. Suzerainty is a modern European term for a relationship in which a stronger power exercises overlordship, sometimes including control over foreign relations.

Historical context. Before the 1880s the Joseon court governed itself while observing tributary forms toward the Qing. After 1882 Qing officials sought to give the relationship more concrete political and military content, while treating Joseon as a dependent state in matters of foreign policy.

Why it matters. The Qing intervention changed the practical meaning of a long-standing relationship. Joseon officials, Japanese diplomats, and Western observers disagreed about what the relationship permitted.

Potential misunderstanding. The pre-1882 tributary relationship should not be read as simple subordination, nor should the Qing policy after 1882 be read as a continuation of unchanged practice. Both continuity and innovation were involved.

Section 50.12

Japanese Military Pressure and the Treaty of Jemulpo

Japan responded with force. Hanabusa returned to Korea in mid-August with warships and soldiers, commonly stated to number over a thousand, and demanded satisfaction for the attack on the legation. Tokyo had several aims: to obtain redress and compensation, to secure protection for its representatives, and to ensure that Qing intervention would not exclude Japan from influence in Korea. The Joseon court, restored to authority under Qing protection and without the Daewongun, was in a poor position to refuse.

The resulting agreement, the Treaty of Jemulpo, was signed on August 30, 1882. Its provisions, as summarized in the source window below, included punishment of those responsible for the attack, relief for victims commonly reported as 50,000 yen, a separate indemnity of 500,000 yen payable in installments, and the right of Japan to station guards at its legation. The payment schedule for the indemnity was subsequently extended from five to ten years. A supplementary agreement expanded the zones in which Japanese merchants and diplomats could travel and trade. The court also agreed to send a mission to Tokyo to express regret. Park Yeong-hyo, later a central figure in the Gapsin Coup, headed that mission, accompanied by Kim Ok-gyun and Seo Gwang-beom. Park adapted and used a Taegeukgi design on the voyage; accounts also identify an earlier design prepared by Yi Eung-jun in connection with the 1882 United States treaty, so Park should not be credited as its sole inventor.

The stationing of Japanese guards had long consequences. It meant that Japanese armed personnel would be permanently present in Seoul, a fact that would matter in December 1884. The indemnity strained the court's already limited funds. At the same time, the mission to Japan brought Korean reformers into direct contact with Japanese statesmen and intellectuals, including the educator Fukuzawa Yukichi, whose influence on Kim Ok-gyun and others is discussed in Section 50.17. The settlement thus both constrained the Korean government and opened new channels for the reformist network.

Section 50.13

Restoration, Reprisals, and the Reorganization of Government

With the Daewongun removed, Gojong's authority was restored, and Queen Min returned to the palace in the autumn. Her return was a political event as well as a personal one: it confirmed that the family network around her had survived the crisis. The court proceeded to punish those held responsible for the revolt. Soldiers and others were arrested and some executed, and the officials killed in the uprising were commemorated. Reprisals therefore fell on those who had acted in the streets, while the question of higher responsibility, including the Daewongun's, was resolved by his removal abroad rather than by Korean process.

Reorganization followed. The measures the Daewongun had reversed were largely reinstated in modified form, and the court created new offices for foreign affairs and customs. It also employed foreign advisers. Paul Georg von Möllendorff, a German who had served in Qing employment and was recommended by Li Hongzhang, arrived at the end of 1882 to advise on foreign relations and customs administration. The appointment illustrates how the court sought expertise from outside while Qing officials sought to guide its choices. Military reorganization proceeded under Qing influence, and new units were trained with Qing assistance.

The restored government was thus not simply the pre-crisis government returned. It was more closely supervised by Qing officials, more constrained by Japanese demands, and more aware of the vulnerability of its own army. It had also learned that unpaid soldiers could threaten the throne. This lesson shaped efforts to provide more reliably for the capital's troops, though the underlying fiscal weakness remained.

Section 50.14

The Expansion of Qing Power after 1882

After 1882 Qing influence in Korea became more systematic. A Qing garrison remained in the capital under officers including Wu Changqing, and Yuan Shikai, then a young officer, gained responsibility for training Korean troops and became increasingly prominent in Korean affairs. Qing advisers sat near the court. In October 1882 China and Korea concluded trade regulations that gave Chinese merchants privileges and expressed the relationship in terms that treated Korea as a dependency; the commercial details belong to Chapter 49 and are not repeated here. Chinese merchants established themselves in Seoul and the treaty ports, supported by Qing officials.

These developments did not amount to annexation or simple colonial rule, and it would be inaccurate to treat them that way. Joseon remained a state with its own king, court, and administration, and its officials negotiated, resisted, and sometimes used Qing support for their own purposes. Queen Min and her circle, in particular, accepted Qing assistance while also exploring other partners. But the changes did alter the balance. Qing officials intervened in appointments, supervised foreign relations, and maintained troops that could decide a political contest in the capital.

Qing policy was itself not unified. Li Hongzhang preferred to keep Korea stable and to prevent Japanese or Russian dominance, and he was cautious about overcommitting. Others favored a more assertive line. The Qing garrison was reduced during the war with France in 1884, as discussed in Section 50.19, which made Qing power in Seoul appear smaller to those watching it. This miscalculation about Qing readiness to respond would matter in December 1884.

Section 50.15

The Changing Political Balance, 1882–1884

The mutiny and its aftermath redistributed political advantage. Moderate reformers, such as Kim Hong-jip, Kim Yun-sik, and Eo Yun-jung, who favored gradual change and worked with Qing officials, gained from the restoration and from Qing support. Conservative officials who had opposed the reforms were weakened by the Daewongun's removal but retained their moral arguments and provincial following. The royal court gained security against domestic rebellion but became dependent on the Qing garrison. The Min family consolidated its position.

Radical reformers occupied an ambiguous position. They had gained prestige from the mission to Japan and from contact with Japanese statesmen, and they continued to hold office. But Qing predominance in Seoul limited their influence, and they were suspect to officials who regarded dependence on Japan as dangerous. Their proposals met resistance from the Min family and from moderate reformers who preferred a different path. In 1883 Kim Ok-gyun traveled to Japan seeking a substantial loan to finance reforms, commonly given as about three million yen. He returned without it. Japanese leaders were unwilling to commit funds on that scale, and the failure damaged his standing at home and weakened the case that Japan would be a reliable partner.

New constraints thus shaped the political landscape. Qing troops in the capital meant that any attempt to seize power would have to deal with them or evade them. The Japanese presence at the legation meant that a small armed force sympathetic to some Korean reformers was available. The king and queen's political strategy depended on Qing protection but not on Qing control. And reformers who despaired of persuasion were looking for other methods. The constraints did not make the coup inevitable, but they defined the situation in which some chose it.

Division IV · Sections 50.16–50.20

Competing Reform Movements and Preparations for the Gapsin Coup

Political ideas, reform networks, foreign support, and the decision to seize power

Section 50.16

Moderate Reformers, Radical Reformers, and Conservative Opposition

Historians usually divide the officials who debated reform into three groups. Moderate reformers, represented by Kim Hong-jip, Kim Yun-sik, and Eo Yun-jung, advocated adopting Western technology and institutions selectively while preserving the Confucian political and moral order, and they generally preferred cooperation with Qing China. Kim Yun-sik is associated with the formula often rendered "Eastern ways, Western tools," meaning that technology could be borrowed without altering fundamental principles. Radical reformers, including Kim Ok-gyun and Park Yeong-hyo, wanted deeper and faster institutional change, were more critical of the status order and of Qing influence, and looked to Japan as a model and partner. Conservative opponents, including many provincial literati, rejected the opening and the borrowing of foreign methods on principled grounds, defending what they called orthodoxy against heterodoxy.

Historical Vocabulary
Gaehwa and Wijeong Cheoksa

Definition. Gaehwa ("enlightenment" or "opening to civilization") was the term used for the movement favoring adoption of new ideas and institutions. Wijeong cheoksa ("defend the correct, reject the heterodox") was the slogan of those who opposed foreign influence in defense of Confucian orthodoxy.

Historical context. Both terms were used by contemporaries to describe positions in an argument that was not settled in the 1880s. The petitions of 1881 from provincial scholars are typical of wijeong cheoksa. The reformers around Kim Ok-gyun are associated with gaehwa.

Why it matters. The terms describe real tendencies but obscure the range of views within each camp.

Potential misunderstanding. "Gaehwa" did not mean a single program. Moderate and radical reformers both used it, and they disagreed fundamentally about pace, partners, and means.

The labels are useful but imperfect. Officials who favored adopting foreign technology could oppose a coup, and officials who supported a strong monarchy could advocate institutional reform. Moderate reformers worked with Qing officials but also wanted a state able to resist foreign domination. Radical reformers cooperated with Japanese diplomats and military personnel, but their aim, as they stated it, was Korean independence. Conservative critics included people who objected to specific policies or to corruption as well as those who rejected every form of borrowing. The chapter therefore uses the labels as shorthand for tendencies, not as a map of fixed parties.

Section 50.17

Kim Ok-gyun, Park Yeong-hyo, and the Formation of the Reformist Network

The central figure of the radical group was Kim Ok-gyun, born in 1851, who passed the civil service examination at the top of his class and moved in a circle of young aristocrats who read works on the outside world and discussed the opening of Korea. Intellectual mentors from the capital's educated circles, among them figures associated with the salon of Park Gyu-su and with interpreters and Buddhist and physician mentors who had access to foreign books, shaped the group's outlook. Kim traveled to Japan in 1881–1882 and again in 1883–1884, met Japanese statesmen and intellectuals, and was strongly influenced by Fukuzawa Yukichi's writings on civilization and national independence.

Park Yeong-hyo, born in 1861, was married into the royal house and enjoyed high social standing. He headed the 1882 mission to Japan, served in 1883 as governor of the capital district, where he attempted to introduce measures such as a newspaper and policing, and then trained troops in a county near the capital. Conflict with conservative officials and with the Min family led to his removal. His experience taught him that reform from within the existing structure faced strong resistance.

Other participants brought different experience. Hong Yeong-sik, who had traveled to the United States as part of a Korean mission in 1883, became the head of the new postal bureau. Seo Gwang-beom had also traveled abroad. Seo Jae-pil, very young, had studied military training in Japan and returned to train a group of young men. Together the circle combined aristocratic social standing, foreign exposure, access to a few military trainees, and a belief that incremental persuasion had failed.

The reformers' relationships with Japanese figures were real but ambiguous. Fukuzawa and others offered sympathy and some practical help, while the Japanese government, which had its own priorities, was cautious. Korean reformers sometimes assumed more commitment than Japanese officials intended. The network also had little base beyond a small circle of young officials, which made it dependent on the king's favor and on external force, and which is a central reason its proposals could be imposed only by a sudden seizure of power.

Section 50.18

The Obstacles to Reform: Finance, Court Resistance, and Foreign Dependence

The reformers faced several obstacles that explain their increasing radicalization. The first was money. Government revenue was limited, and the large loan Kim Ok-gyun sought in Japan was not granted. Korean historical research describes negotiations over a proposed loan of approximately three million yen, but promises and discussions did not amount to a disbursed loan. The court's own attempts to raise funds, including debased coinage issued in 1883, produced inflation and criticism. Without a financial base, new institutions could not be created or sustained.

The second was court resistance. The Min family and established officials controlled appointments and revenue and had little interest in reforms that would diminish their influence. Moderate reformers, who held office and enjoyed Qing support, favored slower change and did not share the radicals' sense of urgency. Reformers who tried to act within the system, as Park Yeong-hyo did, were removed.

The third was foreign dependence. Each reform group relied on a foreign partner for credibility or protection, and each partner had its own priorities. Qing officials supported measured change that preserved Qing influence. Japanese officials were willing to offer encouragement but not unlimited resources. The reformers who favored Japan hoped for more assistance than Tokyo would give. Dependence on a foreign power created the risk that reform would be seen as serving that power's interests, which opponents could use to discredit it.

Disagreements among reformers about cooperating with Japan and Qing China were therefore not peripheral but central. Radical reformers resented Qing supervision and wished to end Korea's dependent status. Moderates regarded Qing support as a necessary protection against Japan and Russia. Both sides spoke of independence, but they envisioned different paths to it.

Section 50.19

The Sino-French War and the Opportunity for Political Action

In 1884 Qing China became engaged in a conflict with France over Vietnam and the south China coast. The Sino-French War, which ran through 1884 and into 1885, drew Qing resources toward the south, and the Qing garrison in Seoul was reduced, with roughly half the troops withdrawn according to commonly cited figures. The National Institute of Korean History identifies the reduction in Qing troop strength during the war, together with Japanese diplomatic cooperation, as significant circumstances behind the decision to launch the coup in December 1884.

Japanese minister Takezoe Shin'ichirō, who had been absent from Seoul, returned in the autumn of 1884 and, according to the reformers' accounts, conveyed that Japan would support a move against the Min-led government and against Qing influence. What Takezoe actually promised, and whether he spoke with authorization from Tokyo, is uncertain. The principal source for the assurance is Kim Ok-gyun's account, written in exile, and Japanese official records present the matter differently. The episode illustrates a recurring problem: the reformers believed they had a commitment that the Japanese government did not clearly endorse.

The reformers' calculation rested on three assumptions: that Qing forces were too weak to intervene decisively, that Japanese troops would support them, and that the king, once in their hands, would back their program. Each assumption was at least partly wrong. The Qing garrison, though reduced, was still large enough, and its officers acted without waiting for instructions from Beijing. Japanese support was contingent and limited. The king's cooperation was uncertain and, in the view of many accounts, was not freely given.

Section 50.20

Planning the Coup: Strategy, Supporters, and the New Postal Service

The conspirators planned to seize the king, eliminate leading officials of the Min-associated government, and then proclaim a new administration. They drew on a small group of followers, including the young men trained by Seo Jae-pil, and they arranged for the support of the Japanese legation guard. The plan required controlling the king, since his authority would give legitimacy to the new government and his presence would protect against counterattack.

The new postal bureau, the Uijeongchongguk, provided the occasion. A banquet to celebrate its opening, to which senior officials and foreign diplomats were invited, would gather many of the officials the conspirators intended to remove, and the bureau's director was Hong Yeong-sik, one of the conspirators. A fire set as a diversion would draw attention and create a pretext to enter the palace. The scheme depended on timing, discretion, and the assumption that the Qing garrison would not respond quickly.

The plan combined proposals for reform with the willingness to use assassination and foreign armed support, and these aspects should be evaluated together. The reformers stated their goals in terms of strengthening Korea and ending dependence on Qing China. But their methods, killing officials and relying on foreign troops to hold the capital, undermined their claim to represent a broad program of national renewal and exposed them to the charge that they were serving a foreign power.

Cause and Effect

From the Imo Mutiny to the Gapsin Coup

Long-term causes

Fiscal weakness of the Joseon state; competing visions of reform; dominance of the Min family; the Qing–Japanese rivalry over influence on the peninsula.

Short-term causes

The failure of the 1883 loan effort; Qing supervision of the court after 1882; frustration of radical reformers excluded from power; reduction of the Qing garrison in 1884.

Immediate trigger

The opening of the postal bureau on December 4, 1884, and the belief that Japanese support would be available.

Immediate effects

A three-day government, Qing counterintervention, the flight of the reformers, and a new crisis with Japan.

Long-term consequences: The Hanseong Treaty and the Tianjin Convention; deeper Qing involvement under Yuan Shikai; discrediting of radical reform for a time; and a continuing rivalry that returns in Chapter 51.

Historical complexity: The Imo Mutiny did not cause the coup in a linear sense. It created Qing predominance in Seoul, and radical reformers reacted to that predominance. Without other conditions, including the Sino-French War and the failed loan, no coup might have been attempted.

Division V · Sections 50.21–50.26

The Gapsin Coup and the Three-Day Government

Reconstructing December 4–6, 1884

Section 50.21

December 4, 1884: The Postal Banquet and the Beginning of the Coup

On the evening of December 4, 1884 (lunar 10/17), guests gathered at the Uijeongchongguk for the banquet celebrating the postal bureau's opening. Senior officials and foreign diplomats were present, including figures later targeted in the coup. Among the officials was Min Yeong-ik, a nephew of the queen who had led the Korean mission to the United States the previous year. The banquet offered the conspirators the gathering they had wanted.

The plan called for a fire to draw attention. An initial attempt reportedly failed, and the conspirators set a fire at a nearby building. When the alarm was raised and Min Yeong-ik rushed out, he was attacked and severely wounded. He survived, treated by a Western medical missionary, and the episode later contributed to the establishment of a Western-style hospital under royal patronage, an outcome unrelated to the conspirators' aims. The wounding of a leading member of the queen's family marked the conspiracy's first decisive act and the beginning of violence against officials.

Kim Ok-gyun and his associates then went to the palace, where they informed the king that disorder had broken out and urged him to move to a safer location. Accounts differ about how the conspirators presented the situation, and the reformers' own account differs from those that stress deception. What is clear is that the conspirators exploited the confusion created by the fire and used their access to the palace to gain control of the king's person.

Section 50.22

The King, the Palace, and Japanese Military Assistance

The conspirators moved the king and queen to a smaller palace residence that was easier to defend. They then called for Japanese troops. The Japanese legation guard, numbering on the order of a hundred and fifty, entered the palace compound. The reformers claimed that the king himself had asked for Japanese protection, and Kim Ok-gyun's account includes a note attributed to Gojong. Others contended that the king acted under duress and that the document was obtained or manipulated by the conspirators. The question of the king's actual intentions and freedom of action is one of the central disputes of the coup.

With the king in their hands and Japanese soldiers securing the approaches, the conspirators proceeded against officials. Several senior figures associated with the Min-led government and with the Qing connection were killed, including Min Tae-ho, Jo Yeong-ha, and Min Yeong-mok. The killings of officials who were summoned to the palace and then attacked were not incidents of battle but political executions, and they would shape how contemporaries and later historians judged the coup.

The queen, who opposed the conspirators, used her position to seek help from the Qing. Sources report that messages were conveyed to Qing officers urging intervention. The conspirators, aware that their position depended on speed, moved quickly to establish a government. But the king's presence in their custody did not ensure his cooperation, and many accounts suggest that he and the queen waited for the Qing to act.

Section 50.23

The Formation of the New Government

On December 5 the conspirators announced new appointments under royal authority. A royal kinsman, Yi Jae-won, was named chief state councillor, while Hong Yeong-sik became left state councillor. Kim Ok-gyun was appointed vice minister of the Board of Taxation (Hojo), a post relevant to the government's finances. Park Yeong-hyo and Seo Gwang-beom received military commands, with Seo also assigned foreign-affairs responsibilities; Seo Jae-pil became vice minister of the Board of War. The appointments changed rapidly and different records give variant lists, but these assignments are documented in Korean historical research.

The new government relied on royal authority for its legitimacy. Decrees were issued in the king's name, and the conspirators presented their program as the king's will. But the arrangement was fragile. The government had a narrow political base, although the conspirators were able temporarily to recruit or command several Korean military detachments as well as military trainees; the extent and reliability of that support varied over the three days. The officials removed or killed had many allies in the bureaucracy and among the troops. Control over Korean soldiers was uneven, and important units—especially those trained under Qing influence—remained outside their dependable command or shifted allegiance. Foreign diplomats in Seoul observed events with concern, and the Japanese minister was careful about how far to commit.

Maintaining control beyond a small political circle proved impossible. The conspirators could hold the king and the immediate palace area, but they could not command the loyalty of the wider government or the city. The three-day government survived only as long as no significant force opposed it, and within a day the Qing garrison would act.

Section 50.24

The Fourteen Reform Decrees: Political, Social, and Economic Transformation

The reform program announced on December 6 is preserved in Kim Ok-gyun's Gapsin Ilrok as fourteen articles. The National Institute of Korean History explains that these fourteen recorded measures may represent an abbreviated selection of a larger program, and that Kim wrote the account in exile after the coup, with his own purposes in mind. The decrees were promulgated shortly before the government collapsed. They are best read as a statement of intent rather than a record of accomplished reform.

The measures fall into several groups. Relations with Qing China: the program called for ending the tributary obligations to the Qing, a direct challenge to Qing supervision, and for the return of the Daewongun, who was held in China. Social and political status: it proposed weakening hereditary privilege and appointing officials on the basis of talent rather than family. Finance and taxation: it proposed reform of the land tax and centralization of government finance, so that revenue would be managed by a single financial office, and it called for ending or reforming the grain-loan system that had burdened peasants. Institutions: it proposed abolishing offices regarded as redundant, reorganizing the roles of the court and the eunuchs, curbing corruption, and establishing a police force. Military: it proposed consolidating military units and reorganizing recruitment. Some articles also dealt with the Council of State and the conduct of ministers.

These are significant proposals. Several anticipate measures later adopted in the Gabo Reforms of 1894–1896 (Chapter 52), and the emphasis on equality of talent over birth, centralized finance, and independence from Qing tributary obligations marks a distinctive political vision. But it would be a serious error to claim that the reformers abolished the hereditary status system or completed land-tax reform in three days. The decrees were issued in the midst of a seizure of power, with no administrative apparatus to implement them, and the government fell before most could be put into practice. The chapter therefore distinguishes proposals from reforms actually carried out, and it treats the decrees as evidence of what the reformers wanted and of how they understood Joseon's problems.

The decrees also need to be read alongside the methods used to obtain power. The reformers' program called for national independence and good government, yet it was announced by a government installed through assassinations and sustained by foreign troops. Historians therefore evaluate the program and the means together: the content points toward a coherent vision of institutional modernization, while the method limited its legitimacy and made its adoption depend on a foreign military presence it could not control.

Section 50.25

Qing Counterintervention and the Collapse of the Coup

On December 6 Qing troops under officers including Wu Zhaoyou and Yuan Shikai, with Korean forces, moved against the palace. The Qing commanders acted in response to appeals from the court and on their own judgment that the situation threatened Qing interests; they did not wait for authorization from Beijing. The Qing garrison, though reduced, outnumbered the Japanese guard and the conspirators' own followers, and some Korean troops joined the counterattack.

Fighting around the palace was brief. The Japanese minister, assessing that his small force could not hold against the Qing and the Korean troops joining them, withdrew the Japanese guard. The withdrawal removed the conspirators' only armed support. Their own followers were few. The government that had been declared on December 5 and had issued its decrees on the morning of December 6 collapsed that afternoon. Hong Yeong-sik and other participants who remained with the king were killed. The king passed back into the hands of those who supported the Qing connection.

Kim Ok-gyun, Park Yeong-hyo, Seo Gwang-beom, and Seo Jae-pil escaped with Japanese assistance. They made their way toward Incheon and boarded a Japanese ship, reaching Japan. The collapse of the coup in about three days showed how thoroughly the reformers had miscalculated: they had underestimated Qing readiness, overestimated Japanese commitment, and misjudged the extent of their own support.

Section 50.26

Repression, Exile, and the Political Aftermath

Violence did not end with the fighting. Crowds in Seoul attacked the Japanese legation and Japanese residents, and the legation was burned. Several Japanese were killed. The Korean government pursued the conspirators' supporters and punished participants. Relatives of the leading conspirators, who had taken no part in the events, suffered severe punishment, including executions and suicides. The harshness of the reprisals reflected both the court's fear of further plots and the traditional principle of collective liability for family members.

The exiles in Japan became a political problem for the Korean government for years. Kim Ok-gyun lived abroad and was a target of Korean efforts to secure his return or removal; in 1894 he was assassinated in Shanghai, an episode that belongs to the background of Chapter 51. Park Yeong-hyo and Seo Jae-pil traveled and lived in different places and later returned to Korean politics in different circumstances, as later chapters show. The exiles carried with them an account of the coup that their supporters would cultivate and their enemies would contest.

The consequences for subsequent reform efforts were mixed. In the short term, radical reform was discredited among many officials, who associated it with assassination and dependence on Japan. Moderate reformers continued their work under Qing supervision. The court's relationship with Japan, damaged by the coup and its aftermath, required settlement. And the idea of institutional reform did not disappear. Many of the proposals in the Fourteen Decrees would return, in changed form and under different political conditions, in the following decade.

Historical Debate

Was the Gapsin Coup a Failed Modernization Movement, a Struggle for Power, or a Reform Attempt Compromised by Foreign Dependence?

The question: Historians evaluate the Gapsin Coup in very different ways. The disagreement concerns how to weigh the reformers' program against their methods and relationships, and which of these best explains the event.

Interpretation A — a pioneering but premature modernization movement: Some historians, particularly in nationalist traditions of Korean historiography that sought the origins of modern reform, emphasize the content of the Fourteen Decrees: independence from Qing tributary obligations, equality of opportunity, fiscal centralization, and institutional rationalization. On this reading, the coup was an early attempt to modernize Korea whose defeat reflects the strength of conservative and foreign opposition rather than the weakness of its ideas.
Interpretation B — a struggle for power by a narrow elite: Other historians stress that the coup was carried out by a small group of aristocratic officials, that it relied on assassination, and that it had no popular base. On this reading, the coup was an elite contest for control of the court that used the language of reform, and its failure demonstrates the absence of social support for its program.
Interpretation C — a reform attempt compromised by foreign dependence: A third approach holds that the reformers' goals were serious but that their reliance on Japanese military support undermined both their legitimacy and their chances. It treats the dependence as an essential feature, because it tied the program to the interests of a foreign power and made it vulnerable to the charge of serving it.

Evidence: The interpretations draw on the same evidence: the decrees, Kim Ok-gyun's account, Korean court records, and Chinese and Japanese diplomatic documents. They differ in how they weigh them. The decrees support interpretation A; the assassinations and the narrow base support B; the role of the Japanese guard supports C. Because the main reformist account was written by a participant in exile, its credibility is itself part of the debate.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement is primarily one of interpretation and emphasis, though it also touches on evidence, notably the extent of Japanese commitment and the king's freedom of action, and on terminology, notably what counts as "modernization."

Why it matters: The judgment shapes how later reform movements are understood. If the coup was a legitimate if flawed reform attempt, its defeat marks a lost opportunity. If it was an elite power struggle, its defeat is less significant for the history of reform. The three readings are not mutually exclusive, and many historians combine elements.

Student task: Using the decrees, the account of events in this chapter, and the limitations of the sources, evaluate each interpretation. Identify evidence that would help decide among them, such as documents showing what Japanese officials promised or what the king intended.

Multiple Perspectives

The Crises of 1882 and 1884 as Experienced by Different Groups

Older soldiers and their families: For soldiers whose pay had been withheld for many months and whose posts were threatened by reorganization, the issue in 1882 was survival and status. Arrests of their comrades made protest a matter of life and death. Many then faced reprisals once Qing troops restored order.
Royal officials of the Min circle: For officials who controlled revenue and appointments, the revolt of 1882 was a threat to life and position, and the coup of 1884 was an attack in which senior colleagues were killed. They sought Qing protection as the means of survival.
Radical reformers: They regarded the dominance of the Min circle, Qing supervision, and hereditary privilege as obstacles to a strong, independent Korea. After peaceful efforts failed, they judged a seizure of power necessary, and they saw Japanese help as a means rather than an end.
Seoul civilians: Residents experienced fighting, looting, and arrests in 1882 and again during the coup, and many suffered when crowds attacked Japanese residents or when troops searched for rebels. Their own views are poorly recorded.
Qing commanders: Qing officers saw intervention as a way to restore a friendly government, forestall Japanese predominance, and give substance to Qing influence. In December 1884 they acted quickly, without waiting for instructions.
Japanese diplomats: After 1882 Japanese officials sought compensation and security. In 1884 some, notably Takezoe, encouraged the reformers, while Tokyo remained cautious about the risks. When the coup failed, Japan used the incident to demand a settlement.

Section 50.27

The Hanseong Treaty of January 1885

Japan responded to the burning of its legation and the deaths of its nationals by sending Inoue Kaoru to Korea with troops and warships. Negotiations followed with Korean officials led by Kim Hong-jip, and the Hanseong Treaty was concluded on January 9, 1885. Korea expressed regret for the attack on the Japanese legation and for the deaths of Japanese, agreed to compensate families of victims and to pay for losses and for the reconstruction of the legation, and undertook to punish those responsible for the violence against Japanese. The surviving English diplomatic text specifies 110,000 yen for compensation to victims and merchants, and a further 20,000 yen for repairs and expansion of the Japanese legation premises.

The treaty is notable for what it did not do. It did not assign responsibility to the Japanese guard or to Takezoe for participating in the seizure of the palace, and it treated the coup as an attack on Japan by Koreans. This framing allowed the Japanese government to distance itself from the reformers' conspiracy while securing compensation. Korean officials, constrained by the circumstances and by Qing preoccupation with its own conflict with France, accepted terms that largely reflected Japanese demands.

The consequences for Joseon's relationship with Japan were mixed. The settlement restored formal diplomatic relations and avoided war, but it left resentment, and it reinforced the pattern of Korean governments settling disputes with Japan under pressure. It also meant that Japan and Qing China, each of which had troops in Seoul, now needed to settle their own rivalry, which they did in the Tianjin Convention.

Section 50.28

The Tianjin Convention of April 1885 and the Qing–Japanese Rivalry

Itō Hirobumi and Li Hongzhang negotiated the convention, signed on April 18, 1885. Both governments agreed to withdraw their troops from Korea within four months, to refrain from sending their own military instructors to train Korean forces (instead encouraging instructors from a third country), and to notify the other party in advance if either found it necessary to send troops in the future. The agreement reflected each side's wish to avoid war while neither was prepared to accept the other's predominance. For Qing China, then still concluding its war with France, a settlement with Japan was valuable. For Japan, it kept open the possibility of future involvement.

Joseon was not a party to the convention. Korean officials were not consulted in its negotiation, and the agreement determined the conditions under which foreign armies might enter Korea without establishing any Korean guarantee against such intervention. The notification clause, in particular, was designed to preserve each power's freedom to intervene, provided it informed the other. Korean sovereignty was treated as a matter to be managed between the two powers.

The convention did not end Qing influence. Yuan Shikai returned to Seoul later in 1885 in a role of increased authority, and Qing officials continued to supervise Korean affairs. The Korean court, wary of both powers, also explored other options, including approaches to Russia, which belong to the wider story of the period rather than to this chapter. The convention provided a framework that held until 1894, when a Korean uprising led each side to claim the right to send troops (Chapter 51).

Section 50.29

Comparing the Imo Mutiny and the Gapsin Coup

The two crises differed in nearly every respect. In participants and social base, the Imo Mutiny involved soldiers of the older camps, their families, and urban poor, with court factions seeking to use it; the Gapsin Coup was organized by a small circle of aristocratic officials with a handful of military trainees. In objectives, the mutiny began as a demand for pay and just treatment and became a reaction against the Min family, the new unit, and Japanese influence; the coup aimed at rapid institutional reform and independence from Qing supervision. In methods, the mutiny was improvised and spread through direct action; the coup was planned and depended on surprise, assassination, and control of the king. In foreign involvement, the mutiny attacked Japanese targets and drew in Qing and Japanese forces afterward; the coup enlisted Japanese support in advance and was defeated by Qing intervention.

Their outcomes also diverged but shared a pattern. Each ended in intervention by Qing troops, and each produced a diplomatic settlement with Japan. Each strengthened the position of the king and the court's moderate leadership in the short run while increasing dependence on outside force. Neither achieved durable political transformation: the Daewongun's reversal lasted a month, and the reformers' government lasted three days. In both cases, foreign armies determined the result, a fact that limited the capacity of any Korean group, whether conservative, moderate, or radical, to settle the question of political direction by itself.

Yet they also reveal something common. Both exposed a government without reliable revenue, without a loyal army, and without agreement on how to reform. The soldiers' revolt showed what happened when the state failed to pay those on whom it depended. The coup showed how a small circle that despaired of persuasion would risk violence and foreign assistance. Both show that the question of reform could not be separated from the question of who controlled the court and who could call on foreign support.

Two cautions apply. First, these events should not be treated as rehearsals for 1894. Each had its own actors, circumstances, and possible outcomes. The rivalry between Qing China and Japan would intensify, but nothing in 1882 or 1884 determined the war of 1894, still less the annexation of 1910 (Chapter 54). Second, the failure of both crises should not be read as proof that reform was impossible. It indicates the constraints of fiscal weakness, court rivalry, and foreign intervention operating together, and it leaves open that other choices at various points might have produced different results.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined two political crises that followed the opening of Korea, the Imo Mutiny of 1882 and the Gapsin Coup of 1884, and the competing reform movements and foreign interventions that linked them. Its central argument is that both crises exposed the difficulty of reforming Joseon's institutions when military grievances, conflicting political programs, court rivalries, and expanding foreign intervention reinforced one another.

Before 1882 the court had begun to experiment with new offices, missions abroad, and a modern military unit. These initiatives rested on a fragile fiscal base and on contested authority. The creation of the Japanese-trained Byeolgigun and the consolidation of the five camps into two displaced and disadvantaged the older soldiers, whose pay was many months in arrears and whose rations were corrupted by mismanagement. Their grievances were material and well documented.

The mutiny began in July 1882 when defective rice and the arrest of protesting soldiers turned a complaint into rebellion. It spread through Seoul, drew in the urban poor, attacked officials, the palace, and the Japanese legation, and led to the killing of leading officials. Queen Min escaped, and the king turned to the Daewongun, who briefly resumed power and reversed recent reforms. His role after the outbreak is documented; his role in its origins is disputed.

Qing intervention restored the government, removed the Daewongun to China, and inaugurated a period of more direct Qing involvement under officials including Ma Jianzhong, Wu Changqing, and Yuan Shikai. Japan used the attack on its legation to secure the Treaty of Jemulpo, which provided indemnity, an apology mission, and legation guards. The settlement left the court more secure against domestic rebellion but more dependent on foreign power.

Between 1882 and 1884 reformers divided into moderate and radical tendencies, with conservative opposition continuing from the provinces. The moderate reformers worked with Qing officials. The radical group around Kim Ok-gyun and Park Yeong-hyo, frustrated by the failure of the 1883 loan effort and by court resistance, looked to Japan. The labels are useful but imperfect, because the actors shared aims and differed on pace, partners, and means.

In December 1884, taking advantage of the reduction of the Qing garrison during the Sino-French War and of encouragement from the Japanese minister, the radical reformers used a postal-bureau banquet to launch a coup. They killed leading officials, took control of the king, and formed a government. The Fourteen Reform Decrees, preserved in Kim Ok-gyun's account, expressed a program of independence from Qing tribute, equality of talent over birth, fiscal centralization, and institutional reorganization, but they were proposals and were not implemented.

Qing troops, with Korean forces, defeated the coup within three days, the Japanese withdrew, and the leaders fled to Japan. Repression followed, falling harshly on participants and their relatives. The reformers' dependence on assassination and on foreign troops limited their legitimacy, and historians continue to debate whether the coup is best understood as an early modernization movement, an elite power struggle, or a reform attempt compromised by dependence.

The diplomatic settlements of 1885 completed the sequence. The Hanseong Treaty resolved Japanese claims arising from the coup, and the Tianjin Convention arranged for Qing and Japanese withdrawals and mutual notification before future intervention. Joseon was not a party to the convention, which regulated foreign armies' access to Korea without a Korean guarantee.

The two crises differed in participants, objectives, and methods, yet each ended with Qing intervention, a settlement with Japan, and greater dependence on outside force. Neither produced durable political change. They should not be read as rehearsals for 1894 or as steps toward annexation, which was not inevitable. They reveal the constraints within which Korean actors operated and the contested question of the state's future that continues into the chapters that follow.

Constructing a Source Dossier on the Gapsin Coup

This project asks you to assemble and evaluate sources on a single episode of the coup, comparing accounts produced by different participants and observers.

  1. Choose an episode. Select one, such as the postal banquet, the movement of the king to a smaller palace, the promulgation of the decrees, or the Qing counterattack.

Chapter Reflection

The crises of 1882 and 1884 matter because they show the state of the late Joseon polity when its problems were most exposed. A government that could not reliably pay its capital troops faced a rebellion in its own streets. A group of reformers who despaired of persuasion tried to impose change by force. In each case, the outcome was decided not by Korean actors alone but by the armies of neighboring powers, and in each case the settlement was negotiated under conditions Korea could not control.

The chapter also shows why simple categories fail. Soldiers were not merely conservative opponents of reform, and reformers were not simply progressive heroes. The people involved acted from interest, conviction, fear, and calculation, and many held more than one motive. Understanding the period requires holding together the material grievances of soldiers, the political maneuvering of court factions, the ideas of competing reformers, and the strategies of Qing and Japanese officials.

What the crises shaped is clear but not predetermined. They deepened Qing involvement in Seoul, established the Qing–Japanese framework of 1885, discredited radical reform for a time, and left unresolved the question of who would direct Korea's future. Chapter 51 examines how that rivalry reached open war in 1894, Chapter 52 how many of the reformers' proposals returned in the Gabo Reforms, and Chapter 53 how a different state-building project followed. Those later developments were not contained in the events of the 1880s. They depended on further choices and circumstances.

The enduring lesson of this chapter is methodological as much as historical. To understand why reform failed, one must ask who held power, who paid for change, who could call on foreign support, and whose perspective each source preserves. Students who can answer those questions for 1882 and 1884 are well prepared to read the more complex struggles over sovereignty that follow in Volume III.
