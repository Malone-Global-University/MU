Jeju, Yeosu–Suncheon, and the Founding of Two Koreas

Korean History: Ancient Kingdoms to a Divided Peninsula

Chapter Opening

In the summer and autumn of 1948 two governments were proclaimed on one peninsula, each claiming to speak for all Koreans. In the same months, villages on Jeju Island were being emptied and burned, and soldiers in the southern port of Yeosu refused an order to sail. The founding of two states and the violence that accompanied it were not separate stories. They were the same story seen from different places.

On August 15, 1948, the third anniversary of Japan's surrender, the Republic of Korea was formally established in Seoul, in a ceremony attended by General Douglas MacArthur and by the members of the new government. Less than four weeks later, on September 9, the Democratic People's Republic of Korea was proclaimed in the north. Each state was built on the administrative framework left by an occupation army, the American in the south and the Soviet in the north. Each declared a constitution, a legislature, a cabinet, and a claim to represent the whole Korean nation. Neither accepted the other's right to exist as the government of Korea.

Chapter 65 followed the political and institutional divergence that began in 1945: the 38th parallel as an occupation line, the trusteeship controversy, polarization between left and right, the failure of the Joint Commission, and the transfer of the Korean question to the United Nations in the autumn of 1947. This chapter picks up the story at that point of diplomatic breakdown. Its central problem is how an administrative division became a contest between two sovereign claimants, and why that transformation generated such severe conflict inside Korean society itself.

The chapter has two interwoven concerns. The first is constitutional and institutional: the United Nations commission, the negotiations among Korean leaders, the May 10 election in the south, the drafting of two constitutions, and the proclamation of two governments. The second is the human cost of that process. On Jeju Island, an armed uprising that began on April 3, 1948, and the counterinsurgency that followed devastated the island's population. In October, soldiers of the 14th Regiment at Yeosu rebelled, seized Yeosu and Suncheon, and were suppressed in operations that again killed many civilians. The historically recognized Jeju period runs from March 1, 1947, to September 21, 1954, and the official truth investigation estimated roughly 25,000 to 30,000 deaths across that entire span.

These episodes must be treated as historical events in their own right. They should not be reduced to footnotes of Cold War diplomacy, nor to a simple story of Communist rebellion or of government atrocity. They involved armed insurgents, police and constabulary forces, rightist youth organizations, peasants, fishers, divers, students, teachers, soldiers, and local officials, each acting within a particular local setting. A responsible account must recognize both the armed attacks that opened the Jeju uprising and the far larger civilian catastrophe that followed, and must distinguish what is documented from what remains contested.

The evidence is uneven and has itself a history. For the founding of the two states, historians have constitutional texts, legislative records, United Nations reports, American diplomatic and military documents, and, for the north, Soviet materials made available after 1991. For Jeju and Yeosu–Suncheon, the picture is harder. Contemporary police and military reports were written by the forces conducting the operations. American intelligence summaries reflect American assumptions. Testimony by survivors was suppressed for decades under anticommunist rule and was gathered only after democratization, culminating in the 2003 Jeju investigation report and later legislation. This chapter therefore pays continuing attention to who produced each kind of evidence and for what purpose.

The chapter's argument is that the founding of the Republic of Korea and the Democratic People's Republic of Korea in 1948 turned an occupation-era division into a contest between two states, each claiming authority over the Korean nation. Jeju and Yeosu–Suncheon exposed the coercive methods accompanying that process and showed that many Koreans opposed separate statehood or particular forms of government. Chapter 67 examines how these rival states then moved toward armed confrontation; this chapter ends before that escalation.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain the diplomatic decisions of 1947 and 1948, including the role of the United Nations Temporary Commission on Korea, that led to separate elections in the south.
  2. Analyze the positions of Syngman Rhee, Kim Ku, Kim Kyu-sik, and the northern leadership in the debate over separate governments and the April 1948 Pyongyang conferences.
  3. Assess the May 10, 1948 election, including its achievements, geographic limits, coercive environment, and the failure of two Jeju constituencies to return members.
  4. Trace the drafting of the Republic of Korea's constitution and the establishment of its government between May 31 and August 15, 1948.
  5. Reconstruct the northern constitutional process from the August 25 elections to the proclamation of the Democratic People's Republic of Korea on September 9, and distinguish formal representation from political control.
  6. Compare the constitutional foundations, institutions, and rival nationwide claims of the two governments without treating either document as a complete description of political practice.
  7. Explain the origins, armed conflict, repression, and civilian consequences of the Jeju 4·3 events, distinguishing insurgents, security forces, and the island's population.
  8. Evaluate varied kinds of evidence about Jeju, including American reports, official records, oral testimony, and the findings of the truth investigation, and explain why casualty estimates differ.
  9. Analyze the October 1948 Yeosu–Suncheon rebellion and its suppression as a development distinct from, though connected to, Jeju.
  10. Explain how the rebellion contributed to military purges, the National Security Law, and the growth of the ROK security state.
  11. Interpret United Nations General Assembly Resolution 195 (III) and the withdrawal of occupation forces with precision about territorial and evidentiary limits.
  12. Connect the events of 1948 to the later history of memory, silence, and truth investigation in both Koreas.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did decisions made by foreign powers, international organizations, and Korean political leaders combine to produce two states in 1948, and how much room for alternatives existed?
  2. What did "representation" mean in the elections of 1948, and how should historians weigh an election observed in part of a country against one whose results could not be independently verified?
  3. Why did Korean leaders who agreed that national division was undesirable reach opposing conclusions about how to prevent or manage it?
  4. How should historians distinguish an armed insurgency, a wider political movement against separate elections, and the civilian population of a region in which both operated?
  5. Why did the Jeju conflict move from limited armed attacks and a brief negotiation to mass civilian death, and who bore responsibility at which stages?
  6. How was the Yeosu–Suncheon rebellion different from the Jeju uprising in origin, social base, and consequence?
  7. How did the experience of rebellion shape the ROK's security institutions, and what did the National Security Law change in the relationship between state and citizen?
  8. How do truth commissions, oral testimony, and official records each help and limit the writing of history about state violence?
  9. In what sense were the two states of 1948 comparable, and in what ways did they differ in constitutional foundation, international recognition, and political practice?
  10. Why did the creation of two governments not settle the question of Korea's national future?

Historical Context

The division of Korea at the 38th parallel in August 1945 was a military arrangement. American and Soviet commanders agreed on a line to organize the Japanese surrender, and each occupation then governed its zone through its own methods. In the north, the Soviet Civil Administration worked through a Korean People's Committee system led by Kim Il Sung and the Workers' Party, and implemented a land reform in 1946 and the nationalization of major industries. In the south, the United States Army Military Government in Korea (USAMGIK), under Lieutenant General John R. Hodge, retained much of the Japanese-era administrative and police apparatus, suppressed the People's Republic and the People's Committees, and relied on conservative Korean politicians. Chapter 65 examined these processes and the trusteeship controversy that polarized Korean politics.

Two features of the preceding years matter here. First, the Joint American-Soviet Commission, charged with forming a provisional Korean government, collapsed in 1947 over the question of which Korean organizations should be consulted. The United States then placed Korea on the agenda of the United Nations General Assembly in September 1947. Second, southern society had been badly strained. Returning migrants from Japan and Manchuria, rice shortages, inflation, cholera, labor unrest, and the autumn 1946 uprisings, together with the police's visible continuity with colonial-era personnel, produced a political environment in which accusations of communism and of collaboration were weapons in daily conflict. The assassination of the moderate leftist Yeo Un-hyeong in July 1947 had removed one of the most prominent advocates of a coalition between left and right.

The Korean political field in 1947–1948 was not a simple contest between two camps. Syngman Rhee advocated establishing a government in the south if no unified settlement was possible. The Korea Democratic Party, representing many landlords and professionals, generally supported elections. Kim Ku, president of the Provisional Government during exile, insisted on a unified Korea and rejected separate elections. Kim Kyu-sik, a moderate, sought a coalition with left and right participation. Communists and leftists in the south, organized in the South Korean Workers' Party, opposed the election but themselves disagreed about tactics. In the north, leaders like Kim Il Sung, Kim Tu-bong, and Pak Hon-yong worked within a political order supervised by the Soviet occupation authorities.

Geography and demography framed the choices. The south was more populous, with roughly two-thirds of Korea's people, and had most of the peninsula's rice-growing areas, while the north held most of the heavy industry and hydroelectric power developed under Japanese rule. Jeju, a volcanic island about 90 kilometers off the southwestern coast, had a distinct local economy of agriculture, fishing, and women divers (haenyeo), and a record of local organization after 1945. Yeosu and Suncheon, in South Jeolla Province, were a port and a rail-junction town in a region with a long history of agrarian conflict. Understanding why these particular places became sites of rebellion and repression requires attention both to national politics and to local history.

Finally, the terms in which events were later described are themselves contested. South Korean anticommunist governments long labeled Jeju and Yeosu–Suncheon as communist riots or rebellions. North Korean accounts celebrated them as people's uprisings against American imperialism. Democratic-era scholarship and truth commissions have produced more differentiated accounts, reaching beyond both official narratives. This chapter uses the names "Jeju 4·3" and "Yeosu–Suncheon" for the events and examines the competing characterizations as part of the historical record.

Section 66.1

The Failure of a Unified Settlement and the United Nations, 1947–1948

The diplomatic path to 1948 began with the wartime and immediate postwar expectation that Korea would be placed under a period of international trusteeship before independence. At Moscow in December 1945 the United States, the Soviet Union, and Britain agreed that a Joint Commission of the two occupation commands would consult Korean political organizations and help form a provisional government. As Chapter 65 showed, that plan collapsed in controversy. Koreans of the right denounced trusteeship as a denial of independence, while the left, after initial hesitation, largely supported the Moscow Agreement. The American and Soviet delegations then disagreed over which Korean groups were entitled to consultation, and especially over whether organizations that had opposed trusteeship should be included. The second session of the Joint Commission, which met in 1947, ended without agreement.

In September 1947 the United States placed the Korean question before the General Assembly of the United Nations. Washington argued that a Korean government should be formed through elections held under UN observation, with the occupation forces then withdrawing. The Soviet Union objected that the matter belonged to the occupying powers under the Moscow Agreement and proposed instead the simultaneous withdrawal of both armies, leaving Koreans to form a government themselves. The General Assembly rejected that approach. On November 14, 1947, it adopted Resolution 112 (II), which established the United Nations Temporary Commission on Korea (UNTCOK) to observe elections for a national assembly and to consult with Korean representatives. The resolution passed without opposing votes, but the Soviet bloc did not take part in the vote, and one state named to the commission, the Ukrainian Soviet Socialist Republic, declined to participate.

UNTCOK arrived in Seoul in January 1948 with delegates from several member states, including Australia, Canada, China, El Salvador, France, India, the Philippines, and Syria. K. P. S. Menon of India served as its chair. The commission immediately encountered the problem that defined the entire effort: the Soviet occupation authorities refused to allow it to enter the northern zone. Without access to the north, UNTCOK could not carry out the mandate as written in Resolution 112 (II). The commission referred the problem to the Interim Committee of the General Assembly, sometimes called the "Little Assembly," which was the body the Assembly had charged with working between sessions. On February 26, 1948, the Interim Committee voted to proceed with elections in those parts of Korea accessible to the commission. The vote was far from unanimous, with a substantial number of abstentions.

The interpretation of this decision has been contested from the start. Supporters argued that the General Assembly's purpose was to establish a representative Korean government and that the Soviet refusal could not be allowed to veto it. The resolution, they noted, had also been framed to give the commission flexibility. Critics, among them the Soviet Union and North Korea at the time and some UN delegations as well, argued that Resolution 112 (II) envisioned a national election and that an election confined to the south could only create a separate southern government and make division permanent. Some members of the commission itself, including those from Canada and Australia, expressed reservations about whether the commission had authority to supervise a southern-only election. Their concerns concerned both legal authority and political consequences.

Historians have drawn different conclusions about responsibility. Interpretations that emphasize the United States stress Washington's decision to move the question to the UN at a moment when it expected a favorable General Assembly majority, and its preference for a southern government if negotiation with Moscow failed. Interpretations that emphasize the Soviet Union point to its refusal to admit the commission and to the political order it was building in the north in the same months. Interpretations that emphasize Korean agency note that Rhee and the Korea Democratic Party actively sought a southern election, while other Korean leaders tried to prevent it. Most scholars treat all three levels as necessary: the great-power breakdown made separate governments possible, but Korean political choices determined their form.

The practical effect of the February 26 decision was to commit the occupation authorities and the commission to an election date in the spring. The USAMGIK issued an election law in the following weeks, and the commission began observing registration. By early March the question for Korean political actors was no longer whether a southern election would occur, but whether to take part in it, to boycott it, or to try to prevent it. The next section examines the last major Korean effort to find another path.

Section 66.2

Kim Ku, Kim Kyu-sik, and the Last Major Efforts at National Negotiation

The proposed southern election divided the southern political right as sharply as it divided right from left. Syngman Rhee had argued since 1946 that if a unified government could not be formed, one should be formed in the south. He and the Korea Democratic Party, led by Kim Seong-su and others, supported the election as the only realistic route to independence and as a means of establishing a legitimate Korean government under which the occupation would end. Kim Ku, the president of the Provisional Government who had returned from China in 1945, took the opposite position. In early 1948 he issued statements declaring that he could not support the establishment of a separate government and that national unity had to take priority even over rapid statehood. Kim Kyu-sik, a veteran moderate who had led the Left-Right Coalition Committee, shared the objection to a separate election and also sought to bring northern leaders into talks.

In February 1948 Kim Ku and Kim Kyu-sik sent a letter to Kim Il Sung and Kim Tu-bong proposing a meeting of leaders from north and south. The northern leaders did not answer immediately, and in late March a Pyongyang broadcast proposed a joint conference of political and social organizations. The conference took place in April 1948. A broad meeting of delegates from a large number of political and social organizations met from about April 19 to 23, and a more restricted consultation among the principal leaders took place in the following days, ending around April 30. Kim Ku arrived in Pyongyang on April 19, and Kim Kyu-sik a few days later. They did not take part in every session in the same way, and the distinction between the larger conference and the leaders' talks should be kept in view.

The meetings produced documents calling for the withdrawal of American and Soviet forces, the rejection of separate elections in the south, and the establishment of a unified provisional government through an all-Korean political conference followed by general elections. The leaders' statement of April 30 also stressed that Koreans themselves could prevent civil conflict after the withdrawal of foreign troops. In practical terms, however, no mechanism emerged for creating a government that both the northern leadership and the southern conservatives would accept. The northern leaders retained the existing northern institutions. Participants from the south who had taken part in the broad conference were largely drawn from leftist and centrist organizations; several prominent southern leftists remained in the north. When Kim Ku and Kim Kyu-sik returned to Seoul in early May, they found the election preparations unchanged, and the American authorities and the Rhee camp treated the Pyongyang talks with suspicion.

It is important to avoid two easy conclusions. One is to dismiss the Pyongyang meetings as a propaganda exercise with no historical significance. The statements reflected a real and widely shared Korean desire to avoid permanent division, and Kim Ku and Kim Kyu-sik took serious political risk in traveling north. The other is to treat the talks as having produced a viable alternative. The northern leadership entered the talks from a position of institutional strength: it already governed a territory with a People's Committee, an established party, and Soviet backing. It stood to benefit from a settlement that withdrew American troops and prevented a southern government, while preserving the northern order. The Soviet position, which supported the northern leaders' proposals, also served Soviet strategic interests.

The participants likewise had specific interests of their own. Kim Ku sought to preserve the legacy of the Provisional Government and his own claim to national leadership against Rhee's more successful organizational position. Kim Kyu-sik sought a role for moderates in a government that neither extreme could provide. Rhee sought a government he could lead. The northern leaders sought to prevent a state in the south hostile to them and to strengthen their own claim to the nation. None of this means that the calls for unity were insincere. It does mean that calls for unity and the political interests of those making them were not separable, which is why historians treat the Pyongyang meetings as both a sincere expression of nationalist feeling and a political maneuver.

The limited practical effect was soon apparent. The northern authorities cut the supply of electricity to the south in mid-May 1948, an act that underlined the economic stakes of division. The northern leadership later cited the April meetings as evidence of broad popular support for its own constitutional plan. Kim Ku continued to oppose the southern government and was assassinated in June 1949. Kim Kyu-sik, who remained in the south, was taken north during the war that began in 1950.

Primary Source Window I

The North–South Leaders' Statement, Pyongyang, April 30, 1948

Creators and endorsement: A joint statement publicly issued under the names of participating northern and southern political parties and social organizations, following consultations involving Kim Il Sung, Kim Tu-bong, Kim Ku, and Kim Kyu-sik; a related four-leader communiqué is documented in United Nations records. These linked texts and levels of endorsement should not be treated as identical. · Date: April 30, 1948 · Source type: Political communiqué and joint organizational statement; consult the National Institute of Korean History and UNTCOK document A/575 for the documentary distinctions

Context: The statement concluded the Pyongyang meetings during the weeks when the southern election was being prepared and when armed conflict had begun on Jeju. It was issued at the end of a process organized under northern auspices, in a city controlled by the northern authorities and under Soviet occupation.

Paraphrase: The following is an editorial paraphrase of the statement's main points as commonly summarized. It is not a verbatim quotation, and students should consult a published translation of the full text.

The statement affirms that Korea must be unified and independent and that foreign troops should withdraw simultaneously. It declares opposition to the separate southern election and to any government formed through it. It proposes that after the withdrawal a political conference of representatives of the northern and southern parties and organizations draw up a provisional government, which would then prepare general elections and a constitution for a unified Korea. It also asserts that Koreans would not fight a civil war after the foreign armies left.

Analysis: The document records a program that combined a widely shared nationalist demand, namely independence without division, with a particular sequence of steps: troop withdrawal first, then a negotiated provisional government. That sequence mattered. In practice, it would have left the northern institutions largely intact and the southern order, which had no equivalent institutional structure, exposed.

Limitations: The statement cannot show what Kim Ku and Kim Kyu-sik personally expected from it, nor whether the northern leaders intended to honor it. It was produced in a controlled setting, and the extent to which each participant freely chose its wording is not established by the document itself.

Perspective: The statement reflects the positions of its organizational endorsers, the four principal leaders involved in consultation, and the northern authorities hosting the meetings; the precise relation among the organizational text and the four-leader communiqué requires source collation. It does not represent the southern conservatives who supported the election, the American occupation authorities, the Soviet command, or the ordinary Koreans for whom the choice between unity and separate government had very different practical consequences.

Questions for students:

  1. What does the statement say it wants, and in what order does it propose to achieve it?
  2. Who created it, and in what setting was it produced?
  3. What purposes might each participant have had in signing?
  4. What can historians learn from it about Korean attitudes toward division?
  5. What cannot be established from this statement alone about the sincerity or feasibility of its proposals?
Section 66.3

The May 10, 1948 Election and the Contested Meaning of Representation

The election law, issued by the American military government in March 1948, provided for a Constituent National Assembly of 300 seats, of which 100 were reserved for representatives from the north and left vacant, and 200 were to be filled from single-member districts in the south. The ratio reflected the roughly two-to-one population difference between south and north. Suffrage was extended to men and women aged 21 and over, which gave women the vote for the first time in Korean history. Candidates were elected by plurality in each constituency. The law required that voters register in advance, and registration took place in April under the observation of UNTCOK delegates.

The preparation of the election took place in an atmosphere of strikes, violence, and mutual intimidation. In February 1948, shortly before the Interim Committee's decision, a wave of strikes and demonstrations organized by the left and by labor unions had resisted the plan for separate elections, and the strike produced deaths and arrests. In the spring, the South Korean Workers' Party and allied organizations attacked police stations and election offices in a number of localities and urged voters to stay away. The police and rightist youth organizations, for their part, were accused of pressuring registrants and of threatening those suspected of boycotting. Contemporary accounts also describe local officials and organizations making registration appear to be a condition for receiving ration cards or other benefits, although how widespread such pressure was cannot be established from the surviving evidence with confidence. Dozens of people died in election-related violence in the weeks before the vote.

Major political groups made different choices. Rhee's National Association for the Rapid Realization of Korean Independence and the Korea Democratic Party fielded candidates. Kim Ku's group and Kim Kyu-sik's moderates boycotted, as did most leftist organizations. A large number of independent candidates, many of them local notables, also ran. Official figures reported very high registration and turnout, with more than 90 percent of registered voters casting ballots, a figure that reflects both the genuine political excitement of a first democratic election and the pressure to participate in a context where abstention could be dangerous. The results gave Rhee's association and the Korea Democratic Party substantial blocs, but independents won the largest single share of seats, a result that left the assembly less tightly controlled by any single party than Rhee wished.

UNTCOK observed the election, but the scale of observation was limited by the number of delegates and the geography of the south. Observers visited polling places in many districts and the commission reported on the conditions it saw. Its conclusions were not uniformly enthusiastic. The commission found that the election results were a valid expression of the will of the electorate in the areas it could observe, while some delegates remained skeptical of the conditions in which the election was held. For the Rhee government and the United States, the commission's report would become the basis of the claim to legitimacy that the General Assembly later endorsed in Resolution 195 (III).

Only 198 of the 200 allocated southern constituencies returned members. The two constituencies on Jeju Island, the northern and southern halves of the island's northern district, could not complete valid elections. Insurgents had attacked election officials and polling places, and many residents had fled to the mountains or stayed home, so that the turnout in those two constituencies fell below the level required for validity. The elections were eventually held in May 1949. The failure of the Jeju constituencies was thus not a trivial administrative detail but a direct consequence of the armed conflict described in Division III, and a measure of how contested the election was in at least one part of the country.

Historical Debate

Was the May 10 Election a Step toward Independence, a Consolidation of Division, or Both?

The question: Historians agree that the May 10 election occurred, that it was held only in the south, and that it produced the assembly that created the Republic of Korea. They disagree about how to characterize its significance: as a genuine, if flawed, democratic founding, as the decisive act that made division permanent, or as something that cannot be reduced to either.

Interpretation A: A meaningful step toward independence. Supporters emphasize the extension of suffrage to women, the high participation, the competition among many candidates including numerous independents, the presence of international observers, and the creation of an elected assembly that produced a constitution and established a government recognized by the United Nations. They argue that waiting for a unified settlement that the Soviet Union would not permit would have left Korea under indefinite occupation.
Interpretation B: A consolidation of division. Critics emphasize that the election excluded the north, that major nationalist leaders boycotted it, that the electoral environment included police and rightist pressure, that leftist opposition was suppressed or driven underground, and that the election's logic made a southern state the primary outcome. They note that the decision to proceed was taken when alternatives such as continued negotiation or a broader coalition had not been exhausted.
Interpretation C: Both at once. Many historians avoid choosing, arguing that the election was simultaneously a genuine exercise in participation for millions of Koreans and a step that closed off the possibility of a unified government by negotiation. On this view, the two characterizations answer different questions: whether the election gave voters a real choice among candidates, and whether it advanced national unity.

Evidence: The interpretations draw on the UNTCOK reports, American military government and diplomatic records, southern newspapers, election returns, memoirs of participants, and later studies of the Jeju and Yeosu–Suncheon events. The reliability of turnout and registration figures, the extent of coercion, and the intentions of the actors are all subject to scrutiny.

Points of disagreement: Disagreement concerns interpretation and weight more than established chronology. Scholars differ on how much coercion shaped the results, on whether realistic alternatives existed after the Soviet refusal to admit UNTCOK, and on how much responsibility should be assigned to Korean political leaders rather than the occupation powers.

Why it matters: How historians judge the election affects how they judge the legitimacy of the Republic of Korea's founding and how they understand the opposition to it, including the Jeju uprising and the leftist boycott. A purely celebratory account omits the coercive context. A purely critical account omits the real participation of millions of voters.

Student task: Construct an argument for each interpretation, using the evidence described in this section, and then identify what additional evidence, such as voter testimony, local election records, or the UNTCOK delegates' own notes, would most help decide among them.

Section 66.4

The Constituent Assembly, Constitution, and Founding of the Republic of Korea

The Constituent National Assembly opened in Seoul on May 31, 1948, with Syngman Rhee elected chairman. Its immediate task was to draft a constitution and to select the officers of a new government. A drafting committee, assisted by legal specialists, worked from several earlier proposals, among them a draft prepared by the legal scholar Yu Jin-o and others. The initial draft envisioned a parliamentary form of government with a prime minister and cabinet responsible to the assembly, together with a ceremonial president. Rhee strongly opposed this arrangement and pressed for a strong presidency. The result was a compromise: a presidential system in which the president was elected indirectly by the National Assembly, accompanied by a prime minister and a State Council, and a unicameral legislature.

The constitution was promulgated on July 17, 1948. In its first articles, it declared Korea a democratic republic and located sovereignty in the people. It guaranteed equality before the law and a set of civil rights including freedom of speech, assembly, and association, with the possibility of restrictions by law. It also contained notable provisions on economic and social organization. Natural resources and key industries were to be placed under public ownership or control, farmland was to be distributed to farmers according to law, and workers were guaranteed a right to share in the profits of enterprises. These provisions reflected the influence of ideas associated with the Provisional Government and the broad political consensus in 1948 that postcolonial Korea required some measure of social reform. The constitution also contained a provision permitting the assembly to enact a special law to punish pro-Japanese collaborators, a clause whose later history is examined in Section 66.17.

The constitution asserted that the territory of the Republic of Korea comprised the Korean peninsula and its adjacent islands. This clause is essential to understanding the nature of the founding. The assembly had been elected in the south alone, yet the document claimed sovereignty over the entire peninsula and the government elected under it claimed to be the only legitimate government of Korea. The reserved seats for the north reinforced this claim. The claim to national authority was a matter of principle and of political necessity: no southern leader could have accepted a constitution that conceded permanent division.

Historical Vocabulary
Constituent Assembly

Definition: An assembly elected for the primary purpose of drafting a constitution and, in some cases, of selecting the first officers of a new government.

Historical context: The Constituent National Assembly of 1948 was elected for a two-year term, drafted the constitution in about six weeks, and then continued as the first National Assembly of the Republic. Its dual role meant that the same body wrote the rules and became the first body to operate under them.

Why it matters: The legitimacy of the Republic of Korea's constitution depended on the legitimacy of the election that produced the assembly, which is why the boycott and the failure of two Jeju constituencies carried significance beyond the seats themselves.

Potential misunderstanding: A constituent assembly is not the same as an ordinary legislature elected under an existing constitution. It acts under a mandate to create the constitutional order itself.

On July 20 the assembly elected Syngman Rhee as the first president by an overwhelming majority, with Kim Ku receiving a small number of votes despite his boycott of the election. In a separate vote, it elected Yi Si-yeong, a veteran nationalist and educator, as vice president. Rhee nominated a prime minister, Yi Yun-yeong, whom the assembly declined to approve, and the assembly then accepted Yi Beom-seok, a former Korean Liberation Army figure who had led a youth organization. The cabinet included men from different political backgrounds. Among them was Jo Bong-am, a former Communist who became minister of agriculture, an appointment that reflected the early government's need to address land reform and the assembly's attention to it. The cabinet therefore reflected not a single party but a mixture of Rhee's allies, Korea Democratic Party figures, and independents.

The Republic of Korea was formally established on August 15, 1948. The ceremony in Seoul inaugurated the government and marked the formal end of the American military government as the governing authority. The change was real but incomplete. On August 24, Rhee and Hodge signed an interim agreement on military and security matters providing for the continuing American role in the command of Korean security forces during the transition, and a further agreement on September 11 handled the transfer of property and finances. American troops remained in the country. For this reason, historians of the Jeju and Yeosu–Suncheon operations must distinguish operations conducted under the military government before August 15 from those conducted by a newly established Korean government and military that still operated alongside American advisers and occupation forces.

The founding ideals and political practice diverged almost immediately. The constitution promised civil liberties, yet the new government faced armed rebellion, an active leftist underground, a police force that had grown accustomed to treating political opposition as a security problem, and demands from within the assembly and society for investigation of colonial collaboration. Rhee's own authoritarian tendencies, evident in his approach to the cabinet and the assembly, added a further pressure. The republic that was established on August 15 was therefore both a genuine constitutional order, with elections, an assembly that exercised independent judgment, and a written bill of rights, and a government that immediately employed coercive measures against groups it classified as enemies. Understanding the founding requires holding both facts in view.

Section 66.5

Northern Korea's Constitutional and Political Preparations, 1948

The northern state of 1948 grew from institutions built since 1945. After the Soviet army entered the north, local people's committees formed in many districts, and the Soviet authorities worked with Korean political figures to organize regional administration. In February 1946 an Interim People's Committee was established with Kim Il Sung as chairman, and in February 1947 it was reorganized as the North Korean People's Committee, answerable to an elected People's Assembly. Chapter 65 examined the land reform of March 1946, the nationalization of major industries, the creation of the Workers' Party of North Korea in August 1946 through the merger of Communist and New People's parties, and the removal from politics of non-Communist leaders such as the nationalist Cho Man-sik. By 1948 these steps had created a government with real administrative reach and a political order in which Communist-led institutions held decisive power.

Several organizations operated within this order. The Workers' Party, whose chairman in 1948 was Kim Tu-bong and whose vice-chairman Kim Il Sung was the dominant governmental figure, dominated policy. Two smaller parties, the Democratic Party and the Chondoist Chongu Party, continued to exist formally and held seats in representative bodies, but they operated within a coalition, the Democratic National United Front, whose program was set by the Workers' Party. The formal existence of these parties gave the northern system a coalition appearance that the 1948 constitution preserved. Understanding the northern state therefore requires distinguishing the formal multiparty structure from the practical dominance of the Workers' Party.

Soviet supervision was continuous. Soviet military and civil authorities, including officers who advised the northern leadership, oversaw major decisions through the period of occupation. After the opening of Russian archives following 1991, historians gained access to documents showing that Soviet officials reviewed and commented on northern constitutional drafts and political plans. This evidence supports the conclusion that northern state formation was not an independent Korean act but also not a simple imposition: Korean leaders, including Kim Il Sung, Kim Tu-bong, and Pak Hon-yong, took active part, adapted Soviet models, and pursued objectives of their own, such as establishing their claim to national leadership.

The army was reorganized in the same period. The Korean People's Army was formally established on February 8, 1948, building on earlier security forces and drawing on Koreans with experience in the Chinese Communist forces, the Soviet army, and anti-Japanese guerrilla units. The establishment of a formal army, months before the state itself, underlined that the north was preparing institutions of statehood while the southern election was still in preparation.

A draft constitution was prepared and published in early 1948 for public discussion. The draft was debated at meetings in factories, villages, and offices organized by the party and the front organizations, and revisions were later incorporated. Such discussions allowed broad participation of a kind but under controlled conditions: the framework was set in advance, criticism of the basic principles was not possible, and the outcome was never in doubt. Even so, the process mattered. It established the constitutional language and institutional design that would be adopted in September, and it allowed the northern leadership to present its state as the product of popular deliberation.

The transition from a provisional administration toward a state claiming authority over the entire peninsula was therefore gradual. The People's Committee system already governed territory and population; the 1948 steps added a formal constitution, a legislature elected for a stated term, a cabinet responsible to it, and an explicit claim to nationwide jurisdiction. The following section traces how those elements came together in August and September.

The political hierarchy needs to be described in contemporary rather than retrospective titles. Kim Tu-bong chaired the Workers' Party of North Korea in 1948, while Kim Il Sung was its vice-chairman and the much more visible head of the northern executive. These titles were not a mere ceremonial distinction: they expressed the still recognizable presence of different Communist political networks, including veterans of the Korean independence movement in Chinese Communist areas and Soviet-connected guerrilla leaders. At the same time, formal party ranking does not alone establish influence. Soviet officers, Korean security leaders, the government apparatus, and control over personnel appointments helped determine which decisions could be implemented. A textbook must keep the party, the government, and the Soviet command separate even while explaining their close cooperation.

The constitutional committee was established in November 1947 under Kim Tu-bong's chairmanship after the prospects for U.S.–Soviet agreement had sharply diminished. Drafting a constitution therefore preceded the southern May 1948 election; it was part of a northern state-making project whose timing accelerated as separate institutions emerged in the south. Public presentation of the draft as a matter for meetings in workplaces and villages supplied a ritual of popular consultation, but the available accounts do not document unrestricted competition among rival constitutional programs. The draft built on changes already made since 1946 in landholding, industrial ownership, the courts, education, and local administration. Constitutionalization made an existing program a formal statement of the future state's identity.

Section 66.6

The Supreme People's Assembly and the Founding of the DPRK

In the summer of 1948 the northern leadership announced elections for a Supreme People's Assembly, to be held on August 25. In the north, voting occurred in the ordinary way, with voters presented with a single slate of candidates nominated by the Democratic National United Front. Official reports of turnout approached unanimity. Such results indicate the mobilizing capacity of the party-led system and the absence of competitive alternatives rather than a measure of free choice, and historians treat the figures accordingly.

The northern authorities also claimed representation from southern Korea. Because the south was under a different government and the northern leadership denied the legitimacy of the May 10 election, a separate process was organized there. Through an underground campaign, supporters collected signatures and selected delegates, who then gathered at a conference in Haeju, in the north, in late August to choose the southern members of the assembly. The result was an assembly of 572 deputies, of whom a large majority of 360 were declared to represent the south and the remainder the north. This electoral process cannot be verified on the same basis as the UN-observed southern election. It was clandestine, conducted partly under the threat of police suppression, and no independent body observed it. It should be identified as a claim to nationwide representation, which northern authorities used to assert the assembly's national character, and not as an independent confirmation of southern opinion.

The assembly convened in early September. On September 8 it adopted the constitution. On September 9 the Democratic People's Republic of Korea was proclaimed. The assembly elected Kim Il Sung premier, and the cabinet included figures from different political backgrounds. Pak Hon-yong, the southern Communist leader who had moved north, became a vice premier and foreign minister. Kim Tu-bong became head of the assembly's standing body. The cabinet's inclusion of figures from the smaller parties and of southern-born leaders reinforced the claim that the new state was a national coalition. The Soviet Union recognized the DPRK in October, and its Soviet-bloc partners followed. The Soviet ambassador in Pyongyang, Terentii Shtykov, had previously been the head of the Soviet civil administration, a continuity of personnel that underlines the closeness of the relationship.

Historical Vocabulary
People's Democracy

Definition: A term used by Communist-led governments of the late 1940s for a political order described as transitional between capitalism and socialism, in which a coalition of "democratic" classes and parties, led in practice by the Communist party, held power.

Historical context: The 1948 DPRK Constitution described the state in these terms. It reflected the model adopted in several Soviet-aligned states in Eastern Europe, where multiparty coalitions existed formally while Communist parties controlled key institutions.

Why it matters: The term explains why the northern state could include multiple parties and a bill of rights on paper and yet be directed by one party in practice.

Potential misunderstanding: "Democracy" in this phrase did not mean competitive multiparty politics of the Western type. Nor should the 1948 system be confused with the later North Korean political order, which dropped much of this language and was reshaped around Juche and hereditary leadership.

The relationship between formal representation and political control is the central analytical issue in the northern founding. The constitution established a legislature, a cabinet, courts, and local people's committees, and described the assembly as the highest organ of state power. In practice, the Workers' Party leadership determined policy, and the assembly met infrequently and ratified decisions made elsewhere. This pattern, common to Soviet-model states, means that institutional description alone cannot explain how the state functioned. It also means that the state was not a fiction: it governed territory, collected taxes, ran schools, and administered the economy.

Section 66.7

Two Governments Claiming One Korean Nation

Comparing the two constitutions reveals both parallels and differences. Both documents declared popular sovereignty, both established unicameral legislatures, both guaranteed equality and civil liberties, and both claimed the entire peninsula. Both also reflected a postcolonial consensus that economic and social reform was required, expressed in provisions about land, resources, and labor. Both governments described themselves as heirs of Korean resistance to Japanese rule. The parallels reflect the common intellectual inheritance of Korean nationalists across the political spectrum and the pressures of the moment.

The differences were equally clear. The ROK was constituted as a presidential republic with an assembly elected under a plurality system and a president selected by that assembly. Its constitution rested on private property and a competitive party system, though limited by social-reform provisions and, in practice, by the pressure of security concerns. The DPRK was constituted as a people's democracy with a legislature, cabinet, and local committees in which a Communist-led front held decisive power. Its land reform had already redistributed land from landlords without compensation in 1946, while the ROK's land reform, authorized by the constitution, was to follow through legislation passed in 1949 and implemented later. The ROK emphasized anticommunism and alliance with the United States, while the DPRK emphasized its alignment with the Soviet Union and its claim to anti-imperialist revolutionary legitimacy.

Each government claimed to represent the whole Korean nation, and each denied the other's claim. The ROK held that it was the only lawful government, resting on an election observed by the United Nations. The DPRK held that it was the only government that represented all Korea through its assembly of north and south deputies. These claims were not symmetrical in international standing, as discussed in Section 66.18, but they were symmetrical in their ambition. The consequence was that each state regarded the other's territory as part of its own, a conviction that made coexistence politically almost impossible and that supplied a standing justification for confrontation.

Distinguishing constitutional guarantees from political conditions is essential. In the south, the guarantees of speech and association operated in a setting of police power, an active insurgency, and a government that soon enacted the National Security Law. In the north, formal guarantees operated within a system where political organization outside the front was not possible and where those classified as landlords, collaborators, or enemies of the people faced exclusion, displacement, or worse. Neither constitutional text describes lived political reality. Historians use the documents as evidence of aspirations, legal structure, and official self-presentation, and they use other evidence, including police records, testimony, and administrative documents, to assess practice.

The two states also differed in how they handled the colonial inheritance. The northern leadership removed many former officials and Japanese-era elites in the course of its reforms and presented itself as having made a clean break with collaboration, though it also relied on technicians and officials with colonial-era training. In the south, the American military government and then the Rhee government retained many officials and police with colonial service, which became a point of sharp domestic controversy, as the experience of the Special Investigation Committee, discussed in Section 66.17, would show. This difference in the treatment of collaboration shaped each state's claim to nationalist legitimacy.

Section 66.8

The Social and Economic Consequences of Formal Division

The formal establishment of two governments hardened a division that had existed in practice since 1945 but had remained porous. In the years after liberation, people had crossed the 38th parallel in both directions, many of them northerners moving south. Landlords, Christians, merchants, and others who feared or had lost status in the northern reforms went south, as did many whose motives were economic or personal. Estimates of the total number of northerners who moved south in the years before 1950 vary widely, but they were in the hundreds of thousands, and these refugees became a significant political and social force in the south, forming the base of rightist organizations such as the Northwest Youth League. Smaller numbers of southerners, mainly leftists, moved north.

With two governments, the border became a state frontier. Travel required permission that was increasingly difficult to obtain. Postal and telegraph connections, already disrupted, ceased to function normally. Families found themselves separated, often without any means of communication, and the experience of divided families, which would be greatly magnified by the war, began in this period. Questions of nationality and citizenship were also raised. Each government considered all Koreans its citizens, so a person's practical status depended on where they lived and which authorities they dealt with, while Koreans in Japan and Manchuria faced choices about which government, if either, to support. The southern government soon passed a nationality law in late 1948.

The economic consequences were substantial. Under Japanese rule the peninsula's economy had developed regionally complementary roles, with heavy industry, chemicals, and hydroelectric power concentrated in the north and much of the rice-growing agriculture and light industry in the south. After 1945, the occupation boundary disrupted the flow of goods and power. The northern authorities cut electricity to the south in mid-May 1948, at a time when the south depended heavily on northern generation, creating shortages that affected industry and households. The south turned to emergency measures and to American assistance, while the north reoriented its trade toward the Soviet Union. Each side developed separate currencies, planning, and transport policies, so that the two economies drifted further apart.

Land and property regimes diverged. In the north, land had been redistributed in 1946, and former Japanese-owned enterprises had been nationalized, creating a state sector that would expand in later years. In the south, former Japanese-owned property had passed to the American military government, which sold farmland to tenants in 1948 and transferred other enterprises to Korean owners, and the new republic inherited this property through the agreement signed in September. These policies created different property interests that tied groups in each zone to the survival of the existing order, thereby making reunification on neutral terms harder.

Education, press, and culture were reorganized separately. In both zones, authorities extended schooling and literacy and promoted Hangeul, but the curricula, textbooks, and organizations differed, reflecting the political outlook of each state. Newspapers and publishing were subject to restrictions in both zones, though the specific controls differed: in the south, a competitive press existed under the pressure of police censorship and the threat of closure; in the north, the press was organized under party direction. For ordinary people, these differences shaped what they could read, say, and learn about the other side, and they created the beginning of the divergent political cultures that later chapters examine.

For Koreans in Japan, the establishment of two states reinforced existing divisions between organizations aligned with the left and the right. Those divisions would later form the basis of separate communities associated with each state. Everywhere, the formal creation of two governments transformed a question of political preference into a question of loyalty to a state with the power to reward and punish. That change in the character of political choice was among the most important social consequences of 1948.

Treat Jeju as a major historical episode in its own right, not merely an unsuccessful election boycott or an example of Communist rebellion, recognizing both the armed attacks initiated in April 1948 and the far larger civilian catastrophe associated with counterinsurgency.
Section 66.9

Jeju Before April 3: Colonial Legacies, Police Violence, and Political Radicalization

Jeju is a volcanic island dominated by Mount Halla, with a coastal ring of villages, an upland zone of mid-mountain settlements, and a history of relative isolation and distinct local culture. In the late 1940s its population was roughly 280,000 to 300,000 people, who lived by farming, fishing, and the diving of the haenyeo, women who harvested seafood. The island had been closely tied to Japan's economy during the colonial period, and many Jeju people worked or lived in Japanese cities, especially Osaka. After liberation, tens of thousands returned from Japan to an island whose resources could not easily absorb them. In 1946 the island was made a separate province, no longer part of South Jeolla.

The immediate postwar years brought severe hardship. Returning migrants, crop failures, a cholera epidemic in 1946, shortages of consumer goods, and grain requisition policies strained households. At the same time, the island had developed an unusually active local political life. People's committees formed in 1945 and for a time administered local affairs with relatively little disorder. When the American military government arrived and moved to establish its own administration, Jeju residents' relations with the new authorities were shaped by their experience of the police, many of whom had served under Japanese rule, and by the reappearance of colonial-era officials in positions of power. Left-leaning organizations, including the South Korean Workers' Party, built a substantial presence, though how many islanders were active members is uncertain.

The critical event of the pre-April period occurred on March 1, 1947. On the anniversary of the 1919 independence movement, large crowds gathered in Jeju city for commemoration ceremonies. After the gathering, police fired on a crowd near the police station, killing several civilians, among whom were bystanders. Police justified the shooting as self-defense after a child was struck by a mounted policeman's horse and the crowd reacted; later investigations judged the use of force disproportionate. The shooting angered many islanders, and in the following days a general strike spread across the island, extending even to government offices and, according to some accounts, to parts of the police itself. The USAMGIK and the police leadership treated the strike as a communist-directed challenge and responded by sending reinforcements from the mainland and carrying out arrests.

The reinforcements included members of the Northwest Youth League, a rightist youth organization composed largely of refugees from the north, many of whom brought intense anticommunist convictions and personal grievances against the northern reforms. Accounts by islanders describe abuses by league members, including beatings, extortion, and intimidation, and the league became a symbol of outside repression. Between March 1947 and March 1948, thousands of people were arrested on Jeju, with a commonly cited figure of about 2,500, many for participation in the strike or for alleged leftist associations. In early 1948, the deaths of detainees under police interrogation intensified public outrage. The island acquired a reputation in official circles as the "Red Island," a label that shaped how American and Korean authorities interpreted subsequent events.

The resistance to the separate election added another layer. Many islanders and organizations opposed a southern-only election on nationalist grounds, as did people elsewhere in Korea, and the South Korean Workers' Party made the opposition a central aim of its Jeju campaign. In early 1948 the party's local organization debated whether to respond to repression by armed action. Historians differ over how far the decision to launch an uprising came from local leaders reacting to police violence and the threat of mass arrest, and how far it reflected directives from higher party levels on the mainland and the broader national campaign against the election. Evidence suggests both pressures operated. The result was a decision to attack.

The background establishes a crucial point about the later conflict. The April 1948 uprising did not arise from nothing, and it was not the first act of violence on the island. Police shootings, mass arrests, torture deaths, the activity of outside rightist groups, and a political climate of mutual suspicion preceded it. At the same time, an armed uprising is a deliberate act with its own responsibility. Both parts of this picture, the preceding repression and the choice to use armed force, need to be kept in view.

The March 1947 shootings altered politics beyond the immediate scene of the demonstration. Bereaved families and students challenged police accounts, and the general strike that followed drew participation from administrative offices and workplaces as well as militant organizations. Police recruitment from outside the island, including personnel connected to harsh colonial methods, reinforced a sense that decisions were being imposed by an unfamiliar authority. The Northwest Youth League became particularly feared in local testimony because its members combined militant anticommunism with policing and intimidation. Yet Jeju society was not united behind one political program: merchants, farmers, fishers, religious groups, migrants returned from Japan, and local officials weighed different risks when deciding whether to join protests, provide food, or keep silent.

Jeju's geography compounded these pressures. Coastal settlements depended on access to markets, ferries, schools and administrative offices, while upland households farmed smaller plots and relied on paths over the slopes of Mount Halla. The same routes that could carry political organizers also became routes of flight when the conflict widened. A person moving uphill might be seeking protection, visiting relatives, carrying supplies or joining an armed group; later counterinsurgency practices often treated such different actions as equivalent. This is why geography should be used to reconstruct civilian movement, not to infer political allegiance from a person's location alone.

Section 66.10

The April 3, 1948 Armed Uprising and the Struggle over Elections

In the early hours of April 3, 1948, armed groups attacked a number of police stations on Jeju, roughly half of the island's police posts, together with the homes and offices of rightist organizations and individuals. The attackers numbered in the low hundreds, a commonly cited figure being about 350 armed fighters, and were organized by the South Korean Workers' Party's Jeju committee, led by Kim Dal-sam. Leaflets distributed at the time called for opposition to the separate election, an end to police repression and the release of those arrested, national unification, and the withdrawal of American forces. The attackers killed police and members of rightist groups, and some of the dead were killed along with family members, an act that the later truth investigation also documented as part of the history of the period.

The role and limits of popular support are difficult to measure. The insurgents drew on genuine grievances against the police and the Northwest Youth League, and many islanders sympathized with, helped, or hid fighters. Others did not support the armed action, feared both sides, or were caught in the middle. After the first attacks, villages and families experienced pressures from both insurgents and security forces, and decisions to flee to the mountains, remain in the village, or cooperate with one side or another were shaped by local circumstances and personal ties as much as by ideology. A responsible account should neither identify the whole population with the insurgency nor treat the insurgents as imposed on a unanimous populace.

The initial response came from the police and from the Korean Constabulary's 9th Regiment, stationed on the island under Colonel Kim Ik-ryeol, with American military government officials directing overall policy. The American command regarded the uprising as a communist challenge to the election and initially supported a forceful response. Kim Ik-ryeol, however, was reluctant to launch a large-scale operation against the island's population and sought a negotiated end to the fighting. On April 28 he met Kim Dal-sam and the two reached an understanding, commonly summarized as providing for an immediate halt to fighting, gradual disarmament of the insurgents, and fair handling of the incidents. The agreement reflected a real possibility of reducing violence before the election, though its terms and the commitment of both sides are known mostly through later accounts.

It did not hold. On May 1, in the village of Ora-ri near Jeju city, unidentified armed men burned houses and attacked villagers in an event that came to be known as the Ora-ri arson. Most scholarship attributes the attack to rightist youth and police elements, though responsibility remains disputed, and in any case the event was widely understood as sabotage of the agreement. The negotiated settlement collapsed. At a security meeting in early May involving the American military governor and Korean officials, the decision was taken to pursue a harder line. Kim Ik-ryeol was replaced as commander, and the conflict entered a more violent phase. The reasons for the collapse included the opposition of the American command and police leadership to compromise, the actions of rightist groups, doubts within the insurgent leadership, and the pressure of the approaching election.

On May 10 the election took place on the island under conditions of disruption. Insurgents attacked polling stations and officials in some places, and many residents left their villages or stayed away. In the two Jeju constituencies, turnout fell below the level required for a valid election, and the constituencies returned no members. The failure was a political embarrassment for the military government and the Rhee camp, and it was often cited later as proof of Jeju's disloyalty. It is better understood as evidence of how contested the election was on the island, where a combination of armed resistance, boycott, and fear prevented it.

After the election, the conflict continued with lower intensity through the summer. The insurgents, driven to the mountain areas, maintained a presence and some leaders attended the Haeju conference in August that selected southern deputies for the northern assembly. In June, a regimental commander on Jeju was assassinated by subordinates, a sign of the political divisions inside the constabulary. The summer was also marked by arrests and violence at the local level. The escalation of the following months, examined in the next section, followed from decisions made in the autumn by the new Republic of Korea.

The unsuccessful April 28 negotiation between Kim Ik-ryeol and Kim Dal-sam needs to be approached as an episode in which both parties had reasons to test restraint but neither controlled all armed actors. There were discussions of halting attacks and limiting military action, yet police, irregular rightist forces and guerrilla units were not a single chain of command. The May 1 incident at Ora-ri and the rival accounts of who set the fires became evidence used to discredit the possibility of accommodation. Contemporary military and police summaries, later testimony and photographic records must be compared before deciding which claims concern direct observation and which repeat accusations. The breakdown cannot be explained simply as an inevitable victory of hardliners without examining these institutional limits.

The two unsuccessful Jeju electoral contests also require precise language. The failure was not proof that the entire island supported an armed rebellion; neither was it evidence that voters who stayed home were necessarily intimidated by guerrillas. Villagers could fear attacks by armed opponents of the election, punishment by police, or consequences from both sides. Turnout and legal validity must be assessed at the constituency level and against the election rules. Repeated attempts to assign every inhabitant to one side obscure how the political contest placed civilians under multiple coercive demands.

Section 66.11

Counterinsurgency, Scorched-Earth Operations, and Mass Civilian Deaths

Historical Vocabulary
Counterinsurgency

Definition: The set of military, police, political, and administrative measures used by a state to defeat an armed insurgency, ranging from targeted operations against fighters to measures directed at the surrounding population.

Historical context: On Jeju, counterinsurgency by late 1948 included the designation of upland areas as hostile zones, the burning of villages, the forced relocation of residents to coastal settlements, detention, and executions without trial.

Why it matters: The category helps explain why civilians were the principal victims: operations aimed at separating insurgents from the population treated the population itself as a target.

Potential misunderstanding: Counterinsurgency does not by definition entail atrocity, and the term does not itself settle questions of responsibility or legality. The documented practices on Jeju included collective punishment and extrajudicial killing, which are analyzed as such in this chapter.

The proclamation of the Republic of Korea on August 15 changed the formal authority under which security operations on Jeju took place. Before that date, the military government had authority, with the constabulary and police acting under its direction. After it, the new government assumed responsibility, although American advisers and the Interim Security Agreement continued to shape command arrangements. Historians must therefore examine operations before and after August 15 separately and not attribute all events to a single undifferentiated authority. The most destructive operations occurred after the Republic's founding, under Korean command with American advisers present, a fact that bears on questions of responsibility.

By October 1948 the Rhee government was determined to end the island's insurgency. A command structure for Jeju was created in October, and on October 17 the commander of the 9th Regiment, Major Song Yo-chan, issued a notice declaring that areas more than five kilometers inland from the coast would be treated as hostile zones and that anyone found in them after a stated date would be regarded as an enemy and subject to being shot. The mid-mountain villages, where much of the island's agricultural population lived, thus fell within the declared zone. On November 17 President Rhee declared martial law on Jeju. The operations that followed, over the winter of 1948–1949, were the most destructive of the entire period.

The security forces, which included constabulary and later army units, police, and rightist youth auxiliaries, burned villages in the mid-mountain areas, killed residents they identified as enemies or suspected supporters, and forced survivors to relocate to coastal areas. Entire villages were destroyed, and the truth investigation later documented that a majority of the mid-mountain villages were burned. Residents who remained in the uplands were shot, and many who fled to the coast were detained and interrogated. A well-documented example is the killing of several hundred residents of Bukchon in January 1949, after insurgents ambushed soldiers in the vicinity. Such massacres often followed insurgent attacks, and reprisals against civilians were generally disproportionate and indiscriminate. Executions of detainees without trial occurred in many places.

The insurgents were also responsible for violence. They attacked police, rightist leaders, and their families, burned the homes of those they regarded as enemies, and killed individuals accused of collaboration or informing. The truth investigation counted a smaller share of identified victims as killed by the armed insurgents than by security forces, but the killings by insurgents were real and were part of the experience of many survivors. The conflict thus involved violence from both sides, though in very different proportions and with different institutional responsibility. Government forces carried out systematic operations with state authority; the insurgents conducted attacks as an armed political organization.

Responsibility for the worst operations is established at some levels and contested at others. The orders and notices of regimental and island commanders, the declaration of martial law, and the pattern of destruction make clear that the campaigns were conducted as policy, not as isolated misconduct by individual soldiers. Questions that remain contested include the extent of direction from Seoul, the degree of American involvement and knowledge, which specific units committed which massacres, and the intent behind particular orders. The truth investigation drew on official records, testimony, and field research, and attributed principal responsibility for the large-scale killing of civilians to the security forces, while also recognizing the insurgents' attacks. In 1949 a regional combat command was established, and by the spring a program of amnesty was offered to those who surrendered. The insurgent leadership was gradually destroyed, and the remaining Jeju guerrilla commander Yi Deok-gu was killed in June 1949, but violence and detention continued after that.

Major Song Yo-chan's October 17 notice supplied a particularly consequential rule for defining danger. It announced that, after October 20, unauthorized movement more than five kilometers inland from the coast or into mountain areas would lead to a person being treated as a rebel and subject to shooting. A resident's presence in an agricultural settlement or passage toward a field could therefore become an asserted ground for lethal punishment without individualized proof of insurgent activity. The later declaration of martial law on November 17 provided an additional emergency framework, but it did not erase the requirement to distinguish civilians from combatants or create evidence of guilt by residence. Contemporary newspaper reproduction of the notice makes it possible to analyze the official wording rather than rely solely on memory.

Forced evacuation had contradictory effects. Families ordered toward the coast lost harvested grain, livestock, implements and shelters just as winter approached. Coastal registration and screening sometimes exposed evacuees to detention because a relative was absent or because a household had passed through a suspected insurgent area. In the uplands, burned homes and destroyed storehouses reduced the possibility of civilian survival and could push frightened people toward guerrilla-controlled spaces. Security officials presented the policy as a way to deprive insurgents of food and concealment, but the indiscriminate destruction of civilian means of subsistence intensified the crisis and helped extend the conflict.

Bukchon provides a geographically distinct and well-documented example. It was a village on Jeju's northern coast in Jocheon-eup, not one of the destroyed highland settlements. On January 17, 1949, after an attack in which soldiers were killed nearby, troops assembled villagers and killed several hundred civilians in a mass reprisal; official historical treatments commonly give an approximation of 400 victims. The setting matters: people could be massacred even in a coastal settlement that should not be conflated with the restricted mountain belt. Later survivor accounts, village memorials and truth-investigation findings make Bukchon an appropriate case study for separating the stated anti-guerrilla purpose of an operation from the treatment of noncombatants.

Section 66.12

Jeju's Civilians, Survivors, and the Problem of Historical Evidence

Most of those who suffered on Jeju were ordinary people: farmers, fishers, divers, laborers, teachers, women, children, and the elderly. The truth investigation's analysis of identified victims found that a substantial share were women and that children and elderly people made up a significant proportion of the dead. Many were killed because they lived in or fled from the mid-mountain villages, because relatives were suspected insurgents, or because they were denounced by neighbors. Some were killed by insurgents because they were suspected of cooperating with the police. People who had no involvement in any political organization found themselves classified as enemies by one side or the other.

Everyday Life and Social History

Displacement and Family Survival during Repression

For households in the mid-mountain villages, the winter of 1948–1949 meant the loss of homes, food stores, and animals. Families who obeyed orders to move to the coast often arrived with little and depended on relatives or on fortified coastal settlements built, in many accounts, by the residents themselves. Others hid in caves and forest areas on the slopes of Mount Halla, enduring cold and hunger. Women often bore the burden of keeping children and the elderly alive when men had been killed, detained, or had fled.

Accusation was itself a danger. Neighbors could be denounced as sympathizers; a family with a member in the mountains could be treated as suspect, and relatives were sometimes punished in place of those who had escaped. Later, a practice sometimes called guilt by association (yeonjwaje) meant that families with relatives killed or accused of being insurgents faced discrimination in employment, education, and public service for decades. Many survivors kept silent about their experience, and many left the island, in part for Japan. Because most surviving official records come from the security forces, historians depend on oral testimony and family memory to recover how survivors experienced these years.

Estimating the number of victims has been a central difficulty. The official truth investigation, whose report was completed in 2003, estimated approximately 25,000 to 30,000 deaths, a figure that corresponds to roughly one-tenth of the island's population at the time. It also documented several thousand individually identified victims, with a large majority attributed to security forces and a smaller proportion to the armed insurgents. These figures concern the entire period, not any single operation or season, and should not be assigned wholesale to the winter of 1948–1949. The range itself reflects the difficulty of counting: many deaths were unrecorded, bodies were not recovered, families did not report deaths for fear of reprisal, and victim registrations depended on later reports.

The events did not end in 1949. Operations against remaining insurgents continued, and many people detained during the conflict were sent to mainland prisons, where large numbers were later killed in the first months of the Korean War as part of preventive detention and executions of suspected leftists, a subject treated in Chapter 71. On Jeju, access to the Mount Halla area remained restricted until September 21, 1954, the date the official historical period ends. The official period runs from March 1, 1947, to that date, and this chapter focuses its narrative on 1947 through 1949 while recognizing that the suffering and the political consequences extended much further.

The later history of investigation shows how political conditions shape historical knowledge. A survivor's evidence may consist of several different kinds of record. Some families remember the date when relatives were taken, a neighbor's account of an execution, or the loss of a home; civil registers and school records may identify people missing from later lists. Other evidence comes from excavated remains, army and police reports, administrative correspondence, trial records and prison rosters. These sources do not answer the same question. The literary and commemorative recovery of Jeju also forms part of its history. For many years, accusation of association with a rebel could affect relatives' educational and professional opportunities, and public mourning was dangerous. Writers and researchers from Jeju challenged that silence before the democratic-era truth process took hold. Section 66.13

The Fourteenth Regiment and the Origins of the Yeosu Rebellion

The Korean security forces of 1948 were assembled quickly and from diverse materials. The American military government created a Korean Constabulary in early 1946 as a lightly armed internal security force, and it expanded through regional regiments formed over the following two years. Officers came from varied backgrounds: former members of the Japanese Imperial Army, veterans of the Manchukuo army, men who had served with Chinese nationalist or Communist forces or with the Korean Liberation Army, and graduates of a constabulary officer school established in 1946. Enlisted men were recruited from the general population. The force was thus a collection of people with different political experiences, many of whom had little training in a common doctrine.

Leftist networks existed within this force. The South Korean Workers' Party, which had been banned or driven underground in much of the south, recruited soldiers and noncommissioned officers, and organized party cells in several units. Many enlisted men had come from families affected by the land and rice problems of the immediate postwar period and held grievances against the police, who treated the constabulary as a rival and were themselves resented. Friction between police and constabulary was common, and in some localities it produced open clashes. The authorities were aware of leftist infiltration, and screening efforts had begun in 1948, but they were incomplete.

The 14th Regiment was formed at Sinwol-ri in Yeosu on May 4, 1948, in a port city on the southern coast of South Jeolla Province. Its recruits included men with heterogeneous military and political backgrounds, and its early internal organization was still fragile when the October deployment order arrived. It included a party cell whose most active members included noncommissioned officers and some junior officers. In October 1948, after the failure of earlier efforts to suppress the Jeju insurgency, the government ordered units of the regiment to prepare for deployment to Jeju by sea. Many soldiers regarded this order with alarm. Some objected to fighting fellow Koreans, others had sympathy with the Jeju insurgents or opposition to the new government, and members of the party cell saw an opportunity to act.

On the night of October 19, 1948, as troops were assembling for embarkation, leaders of the cell, among them a senior sergeant, Ji Chang-su, and several junior officers, took control of the regiment's armory and arms and called on soldiers to refuse the order. Officers who resisted were killed or detained. A significant portion of the regiment, estimates of the number varying with the source, joined the rebellion, while others did not and some escaped. The rebellion thus originated within a particular unit, led by a group with an organized political network, and did not represent the whole constabulary or army. Understanding this limited base matters for assessing both the scale of the rebellion and the government's later purge.

The rebel statements as reported in the following days included opposition to dispatching troops to Jeju, opposition to the separate government and the division of the country, and demands connected with the withdrawal of American forces. The rebellion therefore linked itself directly to the Jeju conflict, but it arose from the specific circumstances of a military unit, not from the Jeju population. Its origins lay partly in the Jeju deployment order and partly in a preexisting network of leftist soldiers whose plans for action, historians believe, might have been developed on a different timetable.

The early 14th Regiment had particular vulnerabilities. Formed at Yeosu's Sinwol-ri on May 4, 1948, it brought together recruits before a stable officer corps, training program and agreed political identity had been consolidated. Its members included men shaped by different wartime military experiences and local disputes. Leftist recruitment networks operated among some soldiers and noncommissioned officers, while other personnel were politically uncommitted or hostile to Communist leadership. That mixture is important: the rebellion was undertaken by part of the regiment, not by a united army, and coercion within the barracks also affected who joined the rising once weapons had been distributed.

The conflict between troops and police had a history beyond party doctrine. Police officers exercised powers of arrest and interrogation that soldiers sometimes resented, and local confrontation sharpened suspicion between the services. The order to embark for Jeju followed news of an increasingly violent suppression campaign, while purges of suspected leftists made members of underground cells fear arrest. These elements combined to give the October 19 organizers both a political appeal and an immediate tactical incentive to seize the unit before it could be deployed or screened. The motives of leaders, rank-and-file soldiers and civilian supporters therefore need to be separated rather than deduced from a single slogan.

Section 66.14

The Seizure of Yeosu and Suncheon and the Government Counteroffensive

In the early hours of October 20 the rebels took Yeosu. They seized military installations, the police station, and other government buildings, and they killed police officers and others identified as rightists or officials. The city's own population was affected in varying ways. Some local leftists, students, and workers joined the rebellion; some civilians welcomed it or acted under its direction; many stayed indoors or fled. Rebels established a people's committee to administer the city, set up a people's court to try those accused of collaboration with the police and rightist organizations, and issued proclamations and orders. Accounts of these trials describe executions of police, officials, and rightist figures, along with other violence.

On the same day, detachments of rebel soldiers, many using trucks and the railway, moved on Suncheon, a rail junction to the north. They took the town after fighting with local police and parts of a nearby unit. In Suncheon too the rebels seized police posts and installations, and some local sympathizers, among them students, joined them. The rebellion spread to surrounding districts, including Gwangyang, Gurye, and Beolgyo, as small rebel units advanced and local leftists acted. The rapid advance along the railway and roads showed the vulnerability of the area's governing structure and the importance of control over transport routes.

The government responded by mobilizing units from other parts of the country. A suppression command was established under Brigadier General Song Ho-seong, and the American military advisory group, which had been established in the summer to assist the new army, monitored operations. President Rhee declared martial law in the affected area within days. Government forces moved against Suncheon first, retaking the town on about October 23 after sharp fighting, and then against Yeosu, which was recaptured by about October 27 after combined assaults, including action from the sea. Most of the rebels withdrew toward the mountains, especially the Jiri mountain range, where they formed the core of guerrilla bands that continued to operate in following months.

It is important to separate three phases. The first was the short-lived seizure of Yeosu and Suncheon, lasting only days. The second was the government counteroffensive and recapture of the towns, which was accompanied by violence against civilians suspected of cooperating with the rebels. The third was the longer guerrilla conflict in the mountains and surrounding districts, which continued into the following years and which later South Korean legislation treats as part of an extended Yeosu–Suncheon incident period reaching into 1955. This chapter's narrative concentrates on the period from 1948 to 1949, and later events in the mountains are treated in Chapter 67 as part of the insurgency problem.

The chronology of the rebellion is thus well established, although details of particular days and the numbers involved vary among sources. The rebellion was not spontaneous and not a mass uprising of the whole population or the entire military. It was a mutiny organized by a particular group within one regiment, which gained support among some local residents and encountered the indifference or opposition of others. The speed with which it was suppressed reflects the limited base, but the manner of suppression produced a second wave of violence.

The spatial sequence explains the speed of the government's crisis. Yeosu offered a garrison and port; Suncheon, connected by rail, was the key junction toward inland South Jeolla. When insurgent soldiers moved north, they entered communities where party activists, local officials and rightist associations had already clashed over land, political authority and security. Some local groups actively assisted the rebels, some took opportunities to settle grievances, and others attempted to protect neighbors or avoid involvement. The seizure of administrative buildings was consequently not the same as a region-wide endorsement of the revolt. The movement's ability to spread outstripped its capacity to establish a durable government.

The recovery of Suncheon on October 23 preceded the recapture of Yeosu on October 27. The counteroffensive was coordinated under Brigadier General Song Ho-seong and involved multiple regular units, with American military advisers in proximity to the newly formed ROK command. Distinguishing the operational chain of command is essential when discussing responsibility: advisers' presence does not automatically make them the officers issuing Korean orders, just as formal Korean command cannot be assumed to have been free of external influence. In Yeosu, the main rebel force had largely withdrawn before the final assault; armed opposition in the town, searches after its recapture, and subsequent executions were therefore not one continuous battlefield action.

The retreat toward Baegunsan and the Jirisan area creates an important chapter boundary. Military insurgents who left the towns could merge with older underground networks, and the government continued operations against armed fugitives long after the urban rebellion ended. But Chapter 66 should reconstruct those immediate origins and the effects on civilians, not preempt Chapter 67's account of armed conflict between states and wider insurgent warfare on the road to June 1950.

Section 66.15

Civilian Deaths, Collective Punishment, and the Suppression of the Uprising

When government forces retook Suncheon and Yeosu, they set about identifying and punishing those who had taken part in or supported the rebellion. The process was chaotic and, in many places, violent. Residents were assembled and screened; people were sorted by denunciation, by appearance, by clothing or other supposed signs, and by rumors. Those identified as rebels or sympathizers could be detained, subjected to improvised hearings, or killed on the spot. Courts-martial were held, and in some cases executions followed brief proceedings. In practice, legal protections broke down in the early days after recapture, and the distinction between a combatant, a civilian who had been compelled to serve, and a resident who had done nothing could not be reliably made.

Both sides killed civilians. The rebels executed police officers, officials, and members of rightist groups, and in some cases family members, both in the days of control and during their retreat. The scale of these killings was a significant factor in the hostility of many residents and in the government's later claims about rebel atrocities. Government forces, for their part, killed residents accused of collaborating with the rebels, in executions that included people whose connection to the rebellion was slight or invented. Reprisals extended to families, and property was destroyed, with fires damaging substantial parts of Yeosu during the fighting and the recapture.

The evidence demands care in assessing equivalence. Both rebel and government violence is documented, but they were not the same in institutional character or in effect. The rebels held territory for only days and acted without legal authority; the government acted under claimed legal authority, with large forces and the power to detain and try. Identifying both does not make them equivalent in scale or responsibility. The historian's task is to reconstruct each from identifiable evidence, such as official reports, press accounts, survivor testimony, and later investigations, and to avoid assuming equality or prioritizing either side's narrative.

Estimating casualties is difficult. Older accounts and official reports of the time gave figures in the low thousands for total deaths, mixing rebels, soldiers, police, and civilians. Later investigations, especially the work of a truth and reconciliation commission in the 2000s, identified several hundred civilian victims whose deaths could be verified, while recognizing that many more could not be identified and that the verified figure was a minimum. Competing claims about the scale of rebel killings and of government reprisals were part of political conflict for decades, with anticommunist accounts emphasizing the former and later democratic-era investigations emphasizing the latter. Confirmed deaths therefore need to be distinguished from later claims, and the uncertainty acknowledged.

The social effects were lasting. Families of victims on both sides were marked, and those suspected of connection to the rebels faced stigma and discrimination similar to that encountered on Jeju. The towns were left with destroyed buildings, disrupted livelihoods, and divisions among neighbors. For many years, public discussion of the events was constrained by the official anticommunist narrative, and survivors of government reprisals could not speak openly. Only after democratization did extended investigation and recognition become possible.

Multiple Perspectives

Yeosu–Suncheon, October 1948: Six Vantage Points

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns historians have identified across many sources. They are not quotations from any named individual, and the groups did not have equal power, information, or freedom of action.

Rebel soldiers: For members of the party cell and those who joined, the Jeju order meant being sent to fight fellow Koreans on behalf of a government they considered illegitimate and tied to foreign occupiers. Others joined out of fear, group pressure, or hostility toward the police. Their decision exposed them to execution if the rebellion failed.
Police: Police officers were among the first targets of the rebels and of local leftists. They experienced the rebellion as an attack by armed soldiers and as retaliation for earlier police conduct. After recapture, police played a central role in identifying suspects, and some took part in reprisals.
Civilian residents: Residents of Yeosu and Suncheon faced demands from the rebels, then violent screening by government forces. Many tried to stay out of sight; some were compelled to join rebel activities; others were denounced by neighbors. For many, the decisive danger came after the government returned.
Bereaved families: Families of police and rightist victims saw the rebellion as a massacre of their relatives. Families of those killed in reprisals saw state violence against innocents. Each group's grief was real, and each was publicly recognized or silenced at different times.
Government officials: Officials in Seoul regarded the mutiny as a mortal threat to a government only weeks old, especially because it came from the army and was linked to Jeju and to the northern state. This view justified the speed and severity of the response and the security measures that followed.
Loyal soldiers: Troops that remained loyal and fought to retake the towns had to decide whom to trust. Some units were suspected of sympathy, and soldiers carried out the screening and reprisals that later investigations examined.

Why the perspectives matter: The differences show why the same events have been remembered as a communist rebellion, a tragedy of civilian suffering, or both. They also show that ordinary residents had the least control over what happened.

A particularly revealing feature of the suppression was the effort to identify supposed collaborators in crowds. Residents were assembled at schools, police stations and other public places. Officers and local informants sorted people by whether they had served in rebel organizations, attended assemblies, supplied food or simply lived in a suspect neighborhood. The evidentiary threshold was often extraordinarily low; in some accounts a gesture, hairstyle, acquaintance or accusation sufficed to trigger detention. Later documentation includes emergency proceedings and military trials lacking the protections ordinarily expected in a criminal case. The problem for historical analysis is not whether the government had a legitimate interest in arresting armed participants. It is how that interest was transformed into collective accusation and punishment of people who had not been shown to have committed a crime.

The regional geography of victimization extended beyond the two cities. Nearby districts in eastern South Jeolla experienced rebel passage, government searches and subsequent reprisals, while families traveling toward relatives or markets could become suspect merely by crossing the path of a military operation. Studies differ in their estimates because they count different periods and categories: combatants, police, civilians killed by rebels, civilians killed by security forces, the missing, and people who later died in prisons. A single undifferentiated total cannot reveal these distinctions. The legally recognized Yeosu–Suncheon incident period extends to April 1, 1955, and should not be used as if all casualties occurred in the October 1948 recapture.

A 2019 South Korean Supreme Court decision ordering retrials in cases arising from Yeosu–Suncheon illustrates why legal records must be read critically. The fact that a conviction was recorded does not establish that evidence was heard in a fair proceeding, or that a death sentence was lawfully imposed. Modern investigations of summary executions and irregular court-martial procedures must be evaluated alongside survivors' testimony and the original military files. The question is the quality of proof and the conduct of authorities, not simply whether later generations prefer the government's or the insurgents' political program.

Section 66.16

Military Purges, Emergency Security, and the Transformation of the ROK Armed Forces

The government's response extended well beyond the recapture of the cities. Beginning in the weeks after the rebellion, authorities launched a screening of the army to identify and remove personnel suspected of leftist affiliation. Military counterintelligence organs, working with American advisers and police, investigated officers and soldiers, using denunciations, interrogations, and records of party activity. Thousands of personnel were discharged, imprisoned, or in some cases executed, a process known as the military purge. Estimates of the total number removed vary, but they were in the thousands and reached into many units, including the officer corps.

One of those arrested in the purge was an army major named Park Chung-hee, who was found to have had connections to the leftist network. He was sentenced but received leniency, reportedly in part because he provided information about other members, and he remained associated with army intelligence. His later career as president is treated in Chapter 75, and his case illustrates both the reach of the purge and the ambiguity of political alignments in the early army.

Institutional changes accompanied the purge. The government strengthened military intelligence, established loyalty procedures, and reorganized parts of the command structure. Defense legislation in late 1948 gave the armed forces a formal statutory basis, and the constabulary organization became the army of the republic. Police powers also expanded. In December 1948 Rhee's government merged several rightist youth groups into a single Korean National Youth Corps, which acted as an auxiliary for anticommunist mobilization. The rebellion thus accelerated the construction of a security apparatus in which the army, police, and youth organizations were all directed toward the internal enemy.

The rebellion also affected the government's political position. It provided a powerful argument that the new state faced a serious internal threat, and it strengthened the Rhee government's authority to take emergency measures. Critics in the National Assembly and elsewhere pointed to abuses in the suppression, and some deputies called for restraint, but the prevailing atmosphere favored security. The rebellion gave the government a justification for the National Security Law enacted in December and for expanded police surveillance of leftists, and it was invoked repeatedly in later years. At the same time, the army's purge deprived it of some experienced officers and left a legacy of mistrust within the force.

The relationship between the rebellion and the growth of the national-security state should be described with care. The rebellion did not create anticommunism in the south, which had existed since 1945, nor did it create the police and military institutions that carried out repression. But it increased the capacity and legitimacy of those institutions at a decisive moment. It showed leaders that the new army could not be assumed loyal, that leftist networks were active inside the state, and that failure to control them could threaten the government's survival. Those lessons shaped policy through the following years and into the war that began in 1950.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Police and Military Institutions, 1945–1949

What changed

  • Formal authority passed from the American military government to the Republic of Korea on August 15, 1948, though American advisers and troops remained.
  • The constabulary became the army of a sovereign republic, with a ministry, statutory basis, and, after Yeosu–Suncheon, a counterintelligence and loyalty apparatus.
  • The government gained a legal instrument, the National Security Law, to prosecute organizations it classified as threats.
  • Youth groups were consolidated into a national corps with official sanction.

What remained continuous

  • The police retained many personnel with colonial-era service and continued to treat political opposition as a security matter.
  • Anticommunism remained the organizing principle of security policy, as it had been under the military government.
  • Rightist youth organizations remained available as auxiliaries to the police and army.
  • Civilians in conflict zones continued to bear the main burden of violence.

Timing and pattern: Change was sudden in formal terms, as in August 1948, and gradual in practice. Those who experienced change most directly were officers and soldiers subject to purge and residents of conflict areas; those who experienced greater continuity were police and rightist organizations, whose methods and personnel carried over. Why it matters: The pattern explains why the new republic's coercive capacity grew so quickly: it did not have to be created from scratch.

The purge also changed how the armed forces recruited and promoted personnel. Commanders sought loyalty checks, political informants and intelligence reporting as safeguards against another internal revolt. Such procedures could uncover actual clandestine cells, but they also made accusations a powerful instrument in professional disputes and encouraged officers to define service to the state through anticommunist commitment. Units assembled from former colonial soldiers, Chinese-based independence fighters, returned conscripts and younger local recruits were pressured to develop an organizational history centered on obedience to the new republic. The consequences were felt in careers, training and the willingness of officers to question emergency commands.

Army–police rivalry did not disappear merely because their leaders shared a security agenda. Soldiers blamed police repression for provoking resistance in some places, while police viewed politically heterogeneous military recruits as unreliable. The government's response was to expand both intelligence coordination and coercive legal authority. This is an institutional story as well as a military one: the Republic's coercive apparatus acquired procedures, records and personnel during crises that later shaped its treatment of political opponents. The relationship to the new National Security Law belongs in the next section, but the causal sequence must not imply that all its provisions were invented in response to this single rebellion.

Section 66.17

The National Security Law, Anticommunism, and the Consolidation of ROK Authority

The National Security Law was enacted and took effect on December 1, 1948, weeks after the Yeosu–Suncheon rebellion. It was a short statute consisting of six substantive articles, together with supplementary provisions, and its central purpose was to criminalize organizations and activity aimed at overthrowing the constitutional order or the state. Its provisions imposed severe penalties on those who organized or led such groups, lesser penalties on members, and penalties for providing support, for propaganda, and for certain forms of communication with such organizations. The law's categories were defined broadly, and its text left considerable discretion to police, prosecutors, and courts. Its original text is available in South Korea's official legal database, and students should read it directly instead of relying on later summaries.

The government advanced a clear rationale. The new republic, it argued, faced armed rebellion on Jeju, a mutiny in its own army, an underground leftist organization with ties to a rival state, and a threat to its survival. The existing legal framework, it said, was inadequate: some earlier measures dated from the colonial or military-government period and no longer fit a sovereign republic. The law was presented as a necessary instrument of self-defense. Supporters in the assembly pointed to the rebellion as evidence that ordinary law could not meet the danger. Opponents, a minority, warned that the vague definitions would permit abuse and violate the constitution's guarantees of association and expression.

In practice the law became the principal instrument of political prosecution. In the months after enactment, tens of thousands of people were arrested on suspicion of leftist activity, including members of the South Korean Workers' Party, labor and peasant organization members, teachers, students, and some who had only slight connection to leftist politics. Officials also used the law against suspected sympathizers within the assembly and the bureaucracy. An organization founded in April 1949, the National Guidance League, was designed to induce former leftists to renounce their affiliations, and it enrolled large numbers of people, a measure that would later have lethal consequences during the war. The law was amended and expanded in December 1949.

Anticommunism was therefore not only an ideology but a legal and institutional system. It defined who was an enemy of the state, authorized the police and army to act against them, and shaped public life by making expression of leftist or neutralist views dangerous. At the same time, anticommunism drew on real sources of support. Many southerners, including refugees from the north, landowners, Christians, and others, had reasons of their own to fear a Communist-led order, and they regarded the security measures as defense. The difficulty for historians is to explain the system without either dismissing these fears or accepting the state's definition of the enemy.

Primary Source Window IV

The National Security Law, December 1, 1948

Creators: the National Assembly and the Rhee government · Date: enacted and effective December 1, 1948 · Source type: Statute; the original text is held in the official South Korean legal database

Context: The law was passed in the weeks after the Yeosu–Suncheon rebellion, amid arrests of suspected leftists, ongoing counterinsurgency on Jeju, and debate over the proper limits of the new constitution's guarantees.

Paraphrase: The following is an editorial paraphrase, not a verbatim quotation. Students should read the original text.

The statute makes it a crime to organize or join an association or group whose purpose is to disturb the state or its constitutional order, with the heaviest penalties for leaders and lesser ones for members. It also punishes those who knowingly assist such groups or who spread propaganda on their behalf. The terms describing the prohibited aims and the degree of connection that suffices for guilt are broad.

Analysis: The law shows how the new state defined threats to itself and how it translated the experience of rebellion into permanent legal tools. Its brevity and breadth are historically important because they left wide discretion to those who enforced it.

Limitations: The statute does not show how many were prosecuted, how often convictions followed, or how police and courts interpreted its terms. Those facts require records of arrests, trials, and testimony. The text also cannot reveal the intentions of legislators or the arguments made in debate.

Perspective: The law expresses the government's and assembly majority's concept of security. It does not represent those targeted by it, the minority who objected in the assembly, or ordinary citizens who experienced its enforcement.

The security policy interacted with an unresolved question inherited from colonial rule: the punishment of collaboration. The constitution had authorized a special law, and in September 1948 the assembly passed a law on punishing anti-national acts. A Special Investigation Committee for Anti-National Activities was established in October, and it began arrests in early 1949, including prominent businessmen and some well-known writers and police officials. It met strong resistance. Rhee and much of the government argued that the new state needed the administrative and police expertise of former colonial officials in the struggle against communism and that prosecuting them would divide the country. In June 1949 police units raided the committee's office, and the law was amended to shorten its life. The committee effectively lost investigative capacity after the police raid and subsequent political and statutory obstruction in June–August 1949; it was formally dissolved following the October 4, 1949 amendment. It handled several hundred cases but produced very few lasting punishments.

The fate of the committee shows the connection between security policy and the treatment of the colonial past. The same police institutions that carried out the suppression of leftists were the targets of the investigation, and the state's reliance on them for security gave them powerful protection. For critics, this was the clearest sign that the new republic had not broken with its colonial inheritance. For supporters, it was a necessary choice under threat. The question would remain a source of controversy for generations.

The original law's six substantive articles provided a different kind of power from the battlefield emergency orders used on Jeju or at Yeosu. Their reach was national and their prohibitions could apply to organizational activity, assistance and expression as well as to violent action. The distinction between legal text and enforcement is especially important: prosecutors could use broad language to pursue an organization, whereas policing practices and courtroom safeguards determined whether individuals could contest the allegations against them. Read alongside the constitutional guarantees of association and speech, the original act illustrates how the early Republic sought to reconcile its asserted democratic identity with an exceptionally expansive concept of internal security.

The confrontation over colonial collaboration supplies an equally concrete test. The National Assembly's special investigative body commenced work in early 1949 and investigated hundreds of people suspected of having aided Japanese rule. On June 6 armed police raided its headquarters, detained staff and disabled the committee's security unit. The raid came amid prosecutions and allegations directed against members of the assembly, further reducing the body's autonomy. Its work largely collapsed through the summer, while legislation narrowed its effective period. The formal termination followed the October 4 amendment, rather than the summer crisis itself. Preserving these separate dates enables students to distinguish political obstruction, administrative paralysis and legal dissolution.

Section 66.18

Foreign Recognition, the United Nations, and the Withdrawal of Occupation Forces

International recognition came in stages and unevenly. The Republic of Korea submitted its case to the United Nations General Assembly, then meeting in Paris, and on December 12, 1948, the Assembly adopted Resolution 195 (III). It declared that a lawful government had been established having effective control and jurisdiction over that part of Korea where the Temporary Commission had been able to observe and consult, and in which a large majority of the Korean people lived. It stated that this government was based on elections that were a valid expression of the free will of the electorate in that part of Korea and that had been observed by the commission, and that it was the only such government in Korea. The resolution passed by a large majority, with the Soviet bloc opposing, and it created a new United Nations Commission on Korea to continue the work of UNTCOK and to promote unification.

The resolution's language must be read precisely. It recognized the ROK as a lawful government with effective authority in the area where the commission could observe. It did not state that a peninsula-wide election had occurred or that UN observers had verified political conditions in the north. The phrase "the only such government" is limited by the qualifying clauses and was understood by many delegations as describing the government in the observed area. The ROK government and its supporters treated the resolution as recognition of its claim to be the sole legitimate government of Korea. Critics, including the Soviet Union, pointed out that the resolution said nothing about the whole peninsula. The ambiguity reflected a compromise, and it has been invoked by both sides ever since.

Other recognitions followed. The United States recognized the ROK on January 1, 1949, and its special representative, John J. Muccio, became ambassador. A number of other states, particularly in the Western bloc, extended recognition during 1949. The Soviet Union recognized the DPRK in October 1948, and the states of the Soviet bloc followed shortly thereafter. The northern government concluded economic and cultural agreements with Moscow in early 1949. The two states thus gained recognition from different groups of states, and neither was recognized by the other's allies. The imbalance should not be exaggerated: the DPRK was recognized as a government by its allies but was not recognized by the United Nations in the way that the ROK was in the resolution.

The withdrawal of occupation forces followed on separate timelines. The Soviet Union announced the completion of its troop withdrawal from the north in late December 1948, though Soviet advisers and the close political relationship remained. The United States withdrew most of its remaining troops by the end of June 1949, leaving a military advisory group of several hundred men. The withdrawal had been demanded by the north and by moderate Korean leaders and was endorsed by the General Assembly. In the south it caused anxiety. Rhee and his government feared that without American troops the north might attack, and they pressed for military aid and a security commitment. The United States provided some assistance but declined to supply the heavy weapons Rhee requested, a subject that belongs to Chapter 67.

Foreign influence did not end with withdrawal. In the south, American advisers, economic aid, and diplomatic presence remained substantial. In the north, Soviet advisers, trade, and political guidance continued, and the Soviet Union remained the principal external supporter. Each state thus began its separate existence dependent on a patron, which was a source of strength and also of constraint. Understanding the formal withdrawal as the end of foreign involvement would be a serious misreading.

Resolution 195 (III), adopted on December 12, 1948, must be read in connection with what the United Nations Commission had and had not observed. It acknowledged the existence of a lawful government with effective control in the area where UNTCOK had been able to observe and consult; it did not document an election conducted throughout the entire Korean peninsula. The vote at the General Assembly also exposed differences within the international organization about how to characterize the result. This matters for the textbook's comparison: international recognition can confer diplomatic standing, but it does not itself establish that competing national claims have been settled or that everyone subject to a government accepts its legitimacy.

The departure of the occupation armies also did not create symmetrical independence from foreign influence. The Soviet Army's withdrawal in late 1948 left Soviet advisers and substantial political and military ties to the northern government. The U.S. withdrawal of most combat troops by June 1949 left a military advisory mission, diplomatic representation and significant dependence on American assistance in the south. The practical capacity of the rival governments to finance administration, train their troops and maintain international relations still depended in differing ways on foreign patrons. Yet Korean leaders also pressed their patrons and made choices that could frustrate outside expectations.

Section 66.19

Two States, One Divided Society: Institutions, Civilians, and the Legacy of 1948

By the end of 1948 two states existed on the peninsula, each with a constitution, a legislature, an executive, an army, and a patron. Their origins differed. The ROK emerged from an election held in the south under UN observation, within a political field that included party competition, a boycott by major leaders, and substantial coercion; it was soon marked by the exercise of emergency powers. The DPRK emerged from a process organized under Communist-led supervision, with a single-slate election in the north and an underground process in the south, and was built on institutions created under Soviet occupation and on the social revolution of 1946. Each had a different relationship to property, class, and the colonial past, and each claimed the entire nation.

For the people of Jeju and Yeosu–Suncheon the transformations of 1948 were concrete and personal. They meant imprisonment, displacement, destroyed villages, and the loss of relatives, along with competing demands for loyalty from armed groups and the state. The same year that gave Koreans constitutions and elections gave some of them a bitter lesson in the power of state violence. These experiences became part of the foundation of the new republic's relations with its own citizens, and remained so even when they were not discussed.

Memory and silence shaped the afterlife of these events. In the south, the official account for decades treated Jeju and Yeosu–Suncheon as communist rebellions and discouraged other accounts. Survivors, families of the dead, and writers who tried to describe what had happened faced censorship and, at times, punishment. The annual commemoration of Jeju by islanders began in earnest only after democratization, and the broader public heard of the events through literature and scholarship that emerged in the 1980s and 1990s. In the north, official histories portrayed the events as heroic people's uprisings against American imperialism and the southern regime, a reading that omitted complexities such as the killings by insurgents and the limited base of the Yeosu rebellion.

The later investigations, treated at several points in this chapter, changed public understanding. The Jeju special act of 2000 and the investigation report of 2003, followed by a presidential apology, recognized the events as state violence against civilians as well as an armed uprising. Later measures included annual state commemoration, retrials for some of those convicted by military tribunals, and legislation providing compensation. For Yeosu–Suncheon, a truth commission in the 2000s identified civilian victims, and special legislation later extended recognition. These processes remain subjects of political and scholarly debate, in particular over how to describe the armed uprising, how to assess responsibility, and how to balance recognition of civilian victims with the narrative of the state's founding.

The unresolved national question persisted. The new states claimed legitimacy over the same population and territory, and the collapse of negotiations, the suppression of dissent, and the withdrawal of occupation forces left no mechanism for peaceful reunification. In June 1949, Kim Ku was assassinated in Seoul by an army officer, removing the most prominent southern nationalist who had insisted on unity; in the same month, the northern and southern Workers' Parties merged in the north into a single Korean Workers' Party. The island of Jeju finally held its two missing assembly elections in May 1949. These developments marked the transition from the founding of the states to the contest between them, which Chapter 67 examines through border clashes, insurgency as a military problem, and the movement toward war.

Cause and Effect

Separate Elections and the Formation of Two States

Long-term causes

Colonial rule and its sudden end; the occupation division at the 38th parallel; the differing social orders built in each zone; deep divisions among Korean nationalists over land, class, collaboration, and trusteeship.

Short-term causes

Collapse of the Joint Commission in 1947; American transfer of the question to the UN; Soviet refusal to admit UNTCOK; the Interim Committee decision of February 26, 1948; Rhee's determination to establish a southern government.

Immediate context

An election campaign accompanied by strikes, police and rightist pressure, leftist attacks, a boycott by major leaders, the failed Pyongyang talks, and the Jeju uprising.

Transformation

Two constitutions and two governments in August and September 1948, each claiming all Korea, supported by different patrons and resting on different social orders.

Immediate effects

Rebellion and repression on Jeju and at Yeosu–Suncheon; military purges; the National Security Law; recognition by different groups of states; withdrawal of occupation armies.

Long-term consequences

A durable rivalry between two states with competing claims; entrenched anticommunist security institutions in the south and a party-state in the north; decades of silence and later investigation of 1948 violence.

Historical complexity: No single actor produced the outcome. Great-power decisions created the conditions, Korean leaders made choices within them, and local conflicts shaped the character of the new states. The outcome was not inevitable: alternatives existed at several points, though each faced obstacles that made it unlikely.

The local histories of Jeju and Yeosu–Suncheon complicate any narrative that treats the year 1948 simply as a constitutional milestone. People could support independence, oppose a separate state, reject an insurgency, fear the police, and still be accused of political disloyalty. On Jeju, coastal and mountain communities suffered different kinds of attack but shared the destruction of family networks and livelihoods; in eastern South Jeolla, urban recapture and rural searches produced distinct victim histories. These experiences were shaped by the founding of the ROK and by resistance to it, but they cannot be reduced to either government's official account of national legitimacy.

The chapter also leaves several questions deliberately open. Records about specific orders, who had authority over a particular unit, and the numbers of people killed in individual operations are incomplete. The relationship between insurgent attacks and government reprisals must be established locally, not inferred from the existence of violence on both sides. Truth investigations can clarify patterns, recognize victims and identify institutional responsibility, but they should not be presented as an effortless substitute for evaluating documents and testimony. The appropriate conclusion is neither that the violence is unknowable nor that every incident fits one general explanation: accountable history works from verified findings while making room for unresolved detail.

Section 66.20

Chapter Review and Historical Synthesis

This final section gathers the chapter's summary, vocabulary, review apparatus, source register, and reflection. It contains no new narrative material.

Chapter Summary

This chapter examined how an occupation-era administrative division became two formally established states in 1948, and how that process was accompanied by political resistance, armed rebellion, state repression, and civilian suffering. Its central argument is that the founding of the Republic of Korea and the Democratic People's Republic of Korea transformed a division of convenience into a contest between two governments claiming authority over the Korean nation, and that the violence of Jeju and Yeosu–Suncheon revealed the coercive methods and political conflicts accompanying that transformation.

The diplomatic road began with the collapse of the Joint Commission and the transfer of the Korean question to the United Nations. Resolution 112 (II) created UNTCOK, the Soviet Union refused to admit it to the north, and the Interim Committee decided on February 26, 1948, to proceed in areas accessible to the commission. The decision made a southern election possible, and interpretations of its authority and consequences remained contested.

Korean leaders responded in different ways. Rhee and the Korea Democratic Party supported the election. Kim Ku and Kim Kyu-sik opposed it and traveled to Pyongyang in April 1948, where meetings produced statements calling for troop withdrawal and a unified provisional government. The talks reflected genuine nationalist feeling but also the interests of the participants, and they produced no workable alternative.

The May 10 election created a Constituent National Assembly through a vote observed by UNTCOK, with extended suffrage and high participation, but under conditions of boycott, intimidation, and violence. Only 198 of 200 constituencies returned members, because the two on Jeju could not complete valid elections. Historians weigh the election as a step toward independence, as a consolidation of division, or as both.

The assembly drafted a constitution promulgated on July 17, elected Syngman Rhee president on July 20, and saw the Republic established on August 15. The constitution combined republican institutions, social-reform provisions, and a claim to all of Korea. The establishment of the government ended the military government formally while leaving American advisers and troops in place, which complicates questions of command responsibility.

In the north, institutions built since 1945 under Soviet supervision were converted into a state. After single-slate northern elections and an underground process in the south, the Supreme People's Assembly adopted a constitution on September 8, and the DPRK was proclaimed on September 9. The southern claims were not independently verifiable. The first constitution described a people's democracy, named Seoul the capital, and contained formal rights, but political control rested with the Communist-led front.

On Jeju, repression following the March 1, 1947 shootings, mass arrests, the activities of the Northwest Youth League, and opposition to the election led the South Korean Workers' Party to launch an armed uprising on April 3, 1948. A brief negotiated understanding collapsed, the election failed on the island, and after the founding of the Republic the government adopted scorched-earth counterinsurgency. The truth investigation estimated 25,000 to 30,000 deaths over the entire period, with most identified victims attributed to security forces, while also documenting killings by insurgents.

At Yeosu in October 1948, members of the 14th Regiment, led by a leftist cell, refused to deploy to Jeju, seized Yeosu and Suncheon, and were suppressed within days, with violence against civilians by both rebels and government forces. The rebellion led to military purges, expanded intelligence and youth organizations, and the National Security Law of December 1, 1948.

International recognition followed unevenly. Resolution 195 (III) recognized the ROK as a lawful government with effective control in the area the commission could observe, without establishing a peninsula-wide election. The Soviet Union recognized the DPRK, and both occupation armies withdrew, the Soviets by the end of 1948 and the Americans by June 1949, leaving advisers and patrons in place.

The year 1948 created two states without resolving the question of Korea's national future. Each government claimed all Korea, each rested on a different social order, and each inherited unresolved conflicts, including those of the colonial past and of 1948 violence, which were later silenced and then investigated. Chapter 67 follows how those unresolved claims were pursued by armed confrontation.

The May 1, 1948 burning of a village near Jeju city that undermined the April 28 negotiated understanding.
Counterinsurgency
Measures used by a state to defeat an insurgency, including, on Jeju, measures directed at the civilian population.
Scorched earth
The destruction of villages, crops, and shelter to deny support to insurgents; practiced on Jeju in the winter of 1948–1949.
Collective punishment
Punishment of a group, family, or community for acts attributed to some of its members.
Extrajudicial execution
Killing by state agents without a legal trial or lawful process.
Yeonjwaje
Guilt by association; the practice of penalizing families of accused or killed persons.
14th Regiment
The constabulary unit at Yeosu whose members rebelled on October 19, 1948.
Yeosu–Suncheon incident
The October 1948 military rebellion and its suppression, with violence continuing in surrounding areas.
Military purge
The screening and removal of suspected leftists from the armed forces after the rebellion.
National Security Law
The December 1, 1948 statute criminalizing organizations and activities deemed aimed at overthrowing the state.
Anticommunism
The ideology and system of laws and institutions of the ROK that defined communists and their sympathizers as enemies.
Special Investigation Committee for Anti-National Activities
The 1948–1949 body investigating collaboration with Japan, obstructed and dissolved in 1949.
Resolution 195 (III)
The December 12, 1948 UN resolution recognizing the ROK as lawful government with effective control in the observed area.
Effective control and jurisdiction
The legal phrase in Resolution 195 (III) limiting recognition to the area where the commission could observe.
Truth commission
A body created to investigate past state violence; the Jeju committee reported in 2003.
Transitional justice
The set of processes, such as investigation, apology, and compensation, by which societies address past violence.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter shows that the creation of two Korean states was not a single act but a sequence of decisions, elections, rebellions, and repressions, in which diplomacy, constitution-writing, and violence were intertwined. The formal founding of the Republic of Korea and the Democratic People's Republic of Korea can be dated to a few weeks in the summer of 1948. Their meaning cannot be understood without the months of conflict before and after.

It also shows why attention to ordinary people changes the story. The people of Jeju and of Yeosu and Suncheon did not experience 1948 as a constitutional milestone. For them, the state's founding arrived as armed men, burned villages, denunciations, and losses that families carried for decades. A history that treated their experience as marginal would misdescribe what the new states were and how they exercised power.

The chapter's themes recur through the rest of the textbook. The anticommunist security institutions that took shape in 1948 shaped the Rhee government and its successors, discussed in later chapters on the First Republic and Park Chung-hee. The northern party-state's institutions and its claim to national leadership would be reshaped under Juche. The contested memory of Jeju and Yeosu–Suncheon belongs to the history of democratization and of the long effort to reckon with state violence.

Finally, the chapter prepares the ground for Chapter 67. Two governments, each claiming the entire peninsula, now stood face to face, each with an army, a patron, and unresolved internal conflicts. That outcome was not inevitable, and the next chapter examines how border clashes, insurgency, and military buildup carried the two states toward war in June 1950.
