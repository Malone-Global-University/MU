The Han Commanderies, Samhan, and Emerging Polities

Korean History: Ancient Kingdoms to a Divided Peninsula

Chapter Opening

In 108 BCE, after a siege that Chinese court historians remembered as difficult and costly, the walls of Wanggeomseong finally fell. Han imperial forces established new administrative centers on territories formerly governed by Wiman Joseon and on adjoining regions over which the Han court claimed authority. It would be tempting to treat this moment as a hinge on which Korean history swung cleanly from "Gojoseon" to "the Three Kingdoms," as though one door closed and another opened onto a stage already set for Goguryeo, Baekje, Silla, and Gaya. The actual historical record resists that convenience.

What followed the fall of Wiman Joseon was not a vacuum waiting to be filled by predetermined successor kingdoms, and it was not a simple imperial takeover in which Han administration replaced a native Korean state across the whole of the peninsula. It was something messier and, in its own way, more historically interesting: a long period, stretching across the final century BCE and the early centuries CE, during which multiple kinds of political community coexisted and competed across the peninsula and southern Manchuria. Han commanderies, garrisoned and administered from walled cities, controlled substantial territory in the northwest, but their reach was never uniform, never permanent in its original form, and never extended over the whole region. To the north and east, communities that Chinese sources called Buyeo, Okjeo, and Dongye maintained distinct political and social arrangements, some subordinate to more powerful neighbors, some independent, all shaped by contact with Han administrators, with each other, and with early Goguryeo, which was itself only beginning to emerge as a serious regional power. To the south, communities that later Chinese sources grouped under the labels Mahan, Jinhan, and Byeonhan developed through agricultural expansion, iron production, and increasingly dense local political organization, largely outside the direct reach of Han administration. Still earlier and more fragmentary references mention a polity or region called Jin, whose precise relationship to the later networks called Mahan, Jinhan, and Byeonhan remains uncertain, a problem examined carefully in Section 7.9.

This chapter's task is to make that crowded landscape legible before the textbook narrows its focus. Chapters 8 through 11 will follow Goguryeo, Baekje, Silla, and Gaya one at a time, tracing how each grew from a modest polity into a major historical actor. But those four narratives can mislead students if they are read in isolation, because they can create the false impression that Korean history moved directly and inevitably from four ancient commanderies to the Three Kingdoms and Gaya, with nothing of interest happening in between. In reality, the intervening centuries were populated by dozens of political communities, most of which have left only fragmentary traces in the historical and archaeological record, and many of which never became part of any later kingdom's origin story at all. Some communities were absorbed. Some were destroyed. Some persisted in weakened or transformed forms for centuries. Still others simply vanish from the sources, their fates unrecorded and, in most cases, unrecoverable.

Understanding this plural world matters for reasons beyond mere completeness. It is only by seeing how crowded, contested, and uncertain the political landscape actually was after 108 BCE that students can appreciate what an achievement, and what a contingent one, it was for Goguryeo, Baekje, Silla, and eventually the Gaya confederacy to become the dominant political formations of the peninsula's early historic age. None of these later-dominant polities enjoyed an obvious head start. Buyeo, not Goguryeo, was for a long time the more prestigious and more secure power in the northern interior. The southern networks later described as Mahan, Jinhan, and Byeonhan, whatever their uncertain relationship to the still earlier and more fragmentary Jin, developed complex political and economic life well before any single southern polity had achieved lasting dominance. The commandery city of Lelang endured far longer, and drew far more direct benefit from continental trade and administration, than most of the small polities that eventually coalesced into Baekje or Silla.

The evidence for this period comes disproportionately from outside observers. Chinese dynastic historians, writing from the perspective of an expanding continental empire, recorded ethnographic and political descriptions of peoples living beyond direct Han control, sometimes based on genuine administrative reports and diplomatic contact, sometimes recycling older and increasingly outdated material, and always filtered through categories, interests, and biases that reflected Han imperial concerns rather than the self-understanding of the societies being described. Archaeology supplies an important corrective and an independent body of evidence, but it, too, has limits: burial goods, settlement patterns, iron tools, and imported ceramics can reveal a great deal about production, exchange, and social differentiation, but they rarely confirm the political labels that later Korean and Chinese writers applied to particular regions and peoples.

This chapter, then, is not a chapter about four Han commanderies, however prominent Lelang looms in the documentary and archaeological record. It is a chapter about a plural, unsettled, and interconnected Northeast Asia in which imperial administration, indigenous state formation, migration, trade, and conflict operated simultaneously and unevenly across a large and varied geographic space. Reading it carefully will prepare students to understand why the four narrower chapters that follow can responsibly narrow their focus to Goguryeo, Baekje, Silla, and Gaya without pretending that those four polities were the only political communities that mattered, or that their eventual prominence was ever guaranteed.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain the circumstances and course of the Han war against Wiman Joseon and the fall of Wanggeomseong in 108 BCE, while distinguishing documented events from the Han-centered perspective of the surviving sources.
  2. Describe the establishment, uneven duration, and geographic limits of the four Han commanderies, and explain why Lelang's long survival should not be generalized to the other three.
  3. Analyze Lelang as an imperial administrative and commercial center embedded within a larger Korean-Manchurian world, without treating it as evidence that the whole peninsula became part of a Chinese cultural or political sphere.
  4. Assess patterns of interaction, exchange, and negotiation between commandery administrators and local populations, avoiding both an assumption of one-way cultural transformation and an assumption that the commanderies were historically marginal.
  5. Identify Buyeo, Okjeo, and Dongye as distinct political societies with their own institutions, economies, and external relationships, rather than treating them merely as peoples later absorbed by Goguryeo.
  6. Trace the early emergence of Goguryeo within this changing northern landscape without narrating its later imperial expansion, which belongs to Chapter 8.
  7. Explain the difficult textual relationship between earlier references to Jin and later Chinese descriptions of Mahan, Jinhan, and Byeonhan, and articulate why the Samhan should not be understood as three centralized kingdoms.
  8. Distinguish the political networks that became Mahan, Jinhan, and Byeonhan from the later states of Baekje, Silla, and the Gaya confederacy that eventually arose within or alongside them.
  9. Evaluate the multiple mechanisms, including conquest, migration, trade, iron production, and local political competition, that reshaped the peninsula's political landscape after 108 BCE.
  10. Critique teleological narratives that treat Goguryeo, Baekje, Silla, and Gaya as predetermined successors to a plural early historic landscape.
  11. Interpret Chinese ethnographic writing about Northeast Asian peoples as a historical source with identifiable strengths, limitations, and biases.
  12. Contextualize the developments of this chapter within the wider Northeast Asian world of Han imperial expansion, Manchurian polities, and maritime exchange networks reaching toward the Japanese archipelago.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did the destruction of Wiman Joseon produce fragmentation and plurality rather than an immediate, unified successor state?
  2. How extensive and how permanent was Han administrative control over the peninsula, and how should historians describe the limits of that control?
  3. What can archaeological evidence from Lelang reveal about commandery society that Chinese textual sources cannot, and what remains beyond its reach?
  4. In what ways did local populations participate in, negotiate with, resist, or benefit from commandery administration, rather than simply receiving or rejecting it?
  5. Why do Buyeo, Okjeo, and Dongye deserve treatment as political societies in their own right rather than as footnotes to Goguryeo's later history?
  6. What kinds of evidence allow historians to reconstruct the political organization of communities, such as the Samhan, that left few or no written records of their own?
  7. Why is it historically inaccurate to equate Mahan with Baekje, Jinhan with Silla, or Byeonhan with Gaya from the very beginning of this period?
  8. What combination of causes, beyond the simple fact of Han conquest, drove political change across the peninsula and Manchuria in this period?
  9. Why should students resist reading this period as an inevitable path toward the Three Kingdoms and Gaya?

Historical Context

Chapter 6 traced Gojoseon from its association with the Dangun foundation tradition through its documented development as a political power in the northwestern peninsula and southern Manchuria, culminating in the reign of Wiman and his successors. By the second century BCE, Wiman Joseon controlled a substantial and increasingly centralized state, one wealthy and strategically positioned enough to attract the concern of the recently consolidated Han empire under Emperor Wu. Han sources describe Wiman Joseon's ruler, Ugeo, as obstructing neighboring peoples' direct access to Han diplomatic and trade relationships, positioning Joseon as a commercial and political intermediary whose continued independence Han authorities came to regard as an obstacle to their own regional ambitions.

Diplomatic friction escalated into open conflict, and in 109 BCE Han forces launched a coordinated land and naval campaign against Wiman Joseon. The resulting war, examined in detail in the first main section of this chapter, ended in 108 BCE with the fall of the Joseon capital, Wanggeomseong, and the establishment of Han administrative commanderies on former Joseon territory. This event marks one of the most securely documented turning points in Korea's early history, since it is recorded not only in later Korean historiographical compilations but in the roughly contemporary Chinese court history, the Shiji, and its immediate successors.

Yet the conquest of Wiman Joseon did not simply transfer the whole of the earlier state's territory, population, and political authority into Han hands, nor did it extend Han control across the entire peninsula and Manchuria. Wiman Joseon itself had never governed the whole of that space; its authority was concentrated in the northwest, and it maintained variable, often indirect relationships with numerous neighboring communities rather than direct rule over them. When Han forces destroyed Wiman Joseon's central government, they inherited a territory whose actual extent of prior political integration was considerably smaller than the phrase "the fall of Gojoseon" might suggest, and they immediately confronted the far larger, far more loosely organized world of peoples and polities that had never belonged to Wiman Joseon's political structure in the first place.

This chapter accordingly examines two large and interconnected historical processes. The first is the Han imperial presence itself: how the commanderies were established, how unevenly they survived, and what kind of colonial or quasi-colonial society developed within and around them, particularly at Lelang. The second is the far broader world of Buyeo, Okjeo, Dongye, and the emerging northern power of Goguryeo, together with the southern networks of Jin and the Samhan, none of which owed its existence to Han conquest and all of which developed through their own internal and regional dynamics, sometimes in contact with commandery administration and sometimes largely independent of it. Readers should keep both processes in view simultaneously, since neither the commanderies nor the surrounding polities can be properly understood in isolation from the other.

Section 7.1

The Crisis of Wiman Joseon and the Han War

By the middle decades of the second century BCE, Wiman Joseon had grown into a wealthy and militarily capable state under King Ugeo, grandson of the state's founder Wiman. Chinese sources describe Ugeo's Joseon as controlling important overland and maritime routes connecting the Han empire to neighboring peoples further east and northeast, including groups in the peninsula's interior. This intermediary position brought Joseon considerable wealth through trade and tribute, but it also generated friction, since Han authorities specifically accused Ugeo's government of obstructing neighboring polities from establishing direct diplomatic and commercial relationships with the Han court, effectively monopolizing access to Han recognition and trade for Joseon's own benefit. The surviving accusation concerns these neighboring peninsula and Manchurian polities; it should not be extended, without further evidence, to imply that Joseon also controlled or blocked access for communities in the Japanese archipelago.

Han Emperor Wu, whose reign was marked by aggressive military and diplomatic expansion on multiple fronts, including campaigns against the Xiongnu confederacy to the north and west, viewed Wiman Joseon's position as both a commercial obstacle and a strategic concern along the empire's northeastern frontier. Diplomatic exchanges between Han envoys and Ugeo's court failed to resolve the dispute, and in 109 BCE Han forces launched a coordinated campaign, combining a land army advancing from Liaodong with a naval force operating along the coast. The campaign did not proceed smoothly for the Han side. Surviving Chinese accounts, primarily preserved in the Shiji compiled by Sima Qian and in later dynastic histories drawing on similar material, describe extended fighting, coordination difficulties between the Han land and naval commands, and a prolonged siege of Wanggeomseong that lasted roughly a year.

The siege ultimately ended not through a single decisive battle but through a combination of military pressure and internal political collapse within Joseon's leadership. Chinese sources report divisions among Ugeo's officials over whether to continue resistance or negotiate surrender, culminating in Ugeo's assassination by members of his own court and the eventual capitulation of Wanggeomseong in 108 BCE. Students should treat this narrative with appropriate source-criticism: the surviving accounts were composed by Han court historians working from a position that had every interest in presenting the campaign, despite its evident difficulties, as a justified and ultimately successful assertion of imperial authority over a state that had defied legitimate Han demands. No comparable Joseon-authored account of these events survives, and the internal political dynamics described within Ugeo's court, including the specific motives attributed to individual officials, cannot be independently verified.

What can be stated with reasonable confidence is that the war was costly for the Han side as well, and that several Han commanders involved in the campaign were later demoted, executed, or otherwise punished for perceived failures of coordination or strategy, a detail Chinese sources record without apparent embarrassment, since Han historiography did not require presenting the campaign as effortless in order to present it as ultimately justified. This detail is itself useful evidence: it suggests that Wiman Joseon possessed real military capacity and that its defeat was neither quick nor easy, complicating any assumption that Han imperial power simply overwhelmed a weak or peripheral state.

The fall of Wanggeomseong in 108 BCE ended Wiman Joseon's independent political existence and, with it, the line of documented rulers descending from Wiman. It did not, however, end organized political life in the region. Members of the former Joseon elite, officials, and populations experienced the transition in varied ways: some communities came under direct Han administration, some fled or migrated toward neighboring polities, and some almost certainly continued local forms of governance under new imperial oversight, a pattern examined further in Section 7.4. The remainder of this chapter follows the consequences of this conquest outward, first into the commandery system Han authorities established on former Joseon territory, and then into the much larger world of communities that had never been part of Wiman Joseon at all.

Section 7.2

The Four Han Commanderies

Following Wanggeomseong's fall, Han authorities organized former Wiman Joseon territory, along with adjoining regions the Han court claimed authority over, into four commanderies. In the standard scholarly reconstruction, Lelang (Korean: Nangnang), centered in the vicinity of the former Joseon capital in the northwestern peninsula, along with Lintun (Korean: Imdun), positioned along the peninsula's northeastern coast, and Zhenfan (Korean: Jinbeon), established to the south of Lelang in a location that remains debated among historians and archaeologists, were established in 108 BCE; Xuantu (Korean: Hyeondo), established further north and east toward the Manchurian interior, followed in 107 BCE. Students should treat this sequencing as the best available reconstruction from later Chinese administrative sources rather than as a precisely documented, contemporaneous record.

Students encountering this arrangement for the first time often assume that it represented a stable, long-lasting system of four roughly equal administrative units governing the peninsula for an extended period, comparable to provinces within the settled interior of the Han empire. The historical record does not support this assumption. Lintun and Zhenfan proved to be the shortest-lived of the four, with the standard reconstruction placing their abolition, reorganization, or absorption into neighboring administrative units around 82 BCE, roughly a quarter-century after their establishment. The precise circumstances of their dissolution are not fully documented, but the surviving evidence suggests a combination of administrative costs exceeding practical benefit, resistance or non-cooperation from surrounding populations, and a broader Han reassessment of how much direct control over these more distant and less economically central territories was actually worth maintaining.

Xuantu commandery experienced a different but equally significant transformation. Established initially in a location further east, closer to the middle Amnok (Yalu) region and other northern peoples, Xuantu came under sustained pressure from surrounding communities, and around 75 BCE Han authorities relocated its administrative center westward, reducing its effective territorial reach. The transmitted sources attribute this pressure to local Yemaek communities in the region; connecting this development directly to an already-consolidated early Goguryeo state would overstate what the evidence secures, though the relocation clearly occurred within the broader political environment from which early Goguryeo, examined further in Section 7.8, was emerging. Unlike Lintun and Zhenfan, Xuantu did not disappear: it continued to exist as a Han administrative unit in its relocated, reduced form. This relocation is nonetheless historically significant, because it demonstrates that Han imperial authority in this region was neither uncontested nor stable even within the first few decades after the conquest of Wiman Joseon.

Among the four original commanderies, only Lelang endured as a major, durable administrative center in the northwestern peninsula, persisting from its establishment in 108 BCE through several centuries of continuous or near-continuous Han and, later, successor-dynasty administration. Xuantu, as noted above, survived in its relocated and reduced form farther to the west, while Lintun and Zhenfan disappeared from the administrative map after 82 BCE. Lelang's exceptional durability makes it historically distinct among the four original commanderies, and it is the reason this chapter devotes its most sustained attention to Lelang specifically rather than treating "the Han commanderies" as a single undifferentiated bloc. Students should resist textbooks, including popular or older accounts, that describe "the four Han commanderies" as though they formed a single, stable, multi-century administrative structure governing the entire peninsula. The historical reality was considerably more limited in both geographic extent and chronological uniformity: one durable imperial center, embedded within a landscape of independent and semi-independent peoples whose relationships with that center varied enormously by location, period, and circumstance.

Section 7.3

Lelang and the Northwestern Peninsula

Lelang commandery, centered near modern Pyongyang, developed over the centuries following 108 BCE into one of the most substantial urban and administrative centers anywhere in the region, and it provides historians with an unusually rich body of archaeological evidence, including monumental tomb architecture, administrative seals, inscribed objects, imported luxury goods, and the remains of what appears to have been a genuinely cosmopolitan walled settlement.

Administratively, Lelang functioned according to Han bureaucratic conventions, with appointed governors, subordinate officials, and a system of administrative subdivisions, or counties, organizing the commandery's territory for purposes of taxation, census-taking, and legal administration. Archaeological finds of official seals and sealing clay, used to authenticate documents and secure shipped goods, attest to an active bureaucratic culture that produced, transmitted, and stored written records, a level of documentary administration considerably more extensive than anything previously practiced within the region on this scale. Tomb excavations at Lelang, particularly those associated with wealthy administrative or merchant families, have yielded lacquerware, bronze mirrors, silk textiles, and other luxury items originating from the Chinese interior, alongside locally produced goods, indicating that Lelang's elite population maintained active connections with markets and workshops far to the west while also participating in regional production and exchange.

Lelang's economy extended well beyond simple imperial administration. Coinage, ceramics, and craft goods moving through Lelang connected the commandery to Han markets, to other regional powers, and, through maritime routes along the western and southern coasts, to communities further south on the peninsula and, ultimately, to the Japanese archipelago. Archaeological and textual evidence, including references examined later in this chapter's Primary Source Window, indicates that Lelang functioned as an important node through which prestige goods, technological knowledge, and diplomatic recognition circulated among the peninsula's southern polities, several of which sought contact with Lelang precisely because association with the commandery conferred political and economic advantages, including access to Han-manufactured goods and titles that could enhance a local ruler's standing among rival polities.

It is essential that students understand what Lelang was and was not. Lelang was a genuine center of Han imperial administration, embedded with real institutional depth into the surrounding region, and its population included Chinese-origin administrators and settlers alongside populations descended from local communities of the former Gojoseon region, whose ancestors had lived under earlier political arrangements entirely. Lelang was not, however, evidence that "Korea became Chinese" during this period, nor should its long survival and evident prosperity be read as proof that Han cultural and political forms simply displaced indigenous ones across the wider region. Lelang's influence, considerable as it was, radiated outward from a specific urban and administrative center whose direct governmental authority did not extend across the peninsula as a whole, and whose cultural and economic influence, while real and significant, operated through selective adoption, exchange, and adaptation by surrounding communities rather than through simple imposition or replacement.

Section 7.4

Administration, Local Society, and Cultural Interaction

Older historiography on this period sometimes described the Han commanderies through one of two extreme frameworks: either as agents of civilizational transformation that brought writing, bureaucracy, and advanced technology to a previously undeveloped Korea, or, in a later and largely corrective reaction, as historically marginal foreign outposts whose presence had little lasting significance for the region's own development. This textbook rejects both extremes.

What Han administration actually controlled, particularly outside Lelang's immediate urban core, was considerably more limited than the word "commandery" might suggest to students familiar only with the settled interior provinces of the Han empire. Direct administrative authority, meaning the ability to appoint officials, collect regular taxation, and enforce Han law, was concentrated in and around the commandery's administrative centers and the agricultural districts most closely tied to them. Beyond these zones, Han authority operated more indirectly, through relationships with local elites who retained considerable autonomy in exchange for varying degrees of cooperation, tribute, or acknowledgment of Han authority.

Local populations within and around commandery territory were not passive recipients of an external administrative and cultural system. Archaeological and textual evidence indicates that connections with Lelang could offer local elites political and economic advantages: access to prestige goods, recognition that could be leveraged in relationships with rival local powers, and participation in commercial networks reaching into the Chinese interior and, via maritime routes, toward the Japanese archipelago. Some local families appear to have entered directly into commandery administrative structures, serving as officials or intermediaries between Han authorities and surrounding populations, a pattern that parallels frontier administration elsewhere in the Han imperial system, where local elites were frequently incorporated into imperial governance rather than displaced by it.

Cultural interaction moved in multiple directions rather than in a single, one-way current from a supposedly more advanced Han center outward to supposedly less developed surrounding populations. Writing, administrative techniques, coinage, certain craft technologies, and some religious and ritual practices associated with the commandery world spread outward through trade, diplomacy, and elite emulation, and were selectively adopted, modified, and integrated by surrounding communities according to their own needs and interests rather than passively absorbed wholesale. At the same time, local agricultural products, craft goods, and, quite plausibly, elements of local ritual and social practice moved into commandery society, and the commandery population itself, over the centuries of its existence, increasingly represented a genuinely mixed community rather than a simple transplant of Han interior society onto foreign soil.

This textbook's position, consistent with the interpretive commitments established in Chapter 1, is that the commandery world should be understood as a zone of sustained and multidirectional interaction within an unequal imperial relationship, embedded within a much larger Korean-Manchurian political and cultural landscape that continued to develop according to its own internal dynamics well beyond the commanderies' direct reach.

Historical Vocabulary
Commandery and Frontier

Commandery (Chinese jun) was a standard unit of Han imperial administration, used throughout the empire to organize territory for taxation, conscription, and legal administration under an appointed governor. Applying this term to Lelang, Xuantu, Lintun, and Zhenfan accurately reflects how Han administrators themselves classified these territories, but students should not assume that a commandery established on a distant frontier functioned identically to a commandery in the settled agricultural heartland of the Han empire. Frontier commanderies typically exercised more limited, more indirect, and more geographically uneven authority than interior commanderies, relied more heavily on cooperation with local elites, and were more vulnerable to contraction, relocation, or abolition when the costs of maintaining them outweighed the benefits to the imperial court.

Frontier, as used in this textbook, does not describe a fixed line on a map but a zone of variable, often overlapping and contested authority, in which imperial administration, indigenous political communities, migration, and trade interacted without any single power exercising complete or permanent control. The peninsula and southern Manchuria in the century following 108 BCE functioned as exactly this kind of frontier zone, and the term helps students avoid imagining a simple, uniform division between "Han territory" and "non-Han territory."

Describing Lelang loosely as "a Chinese colony over Korea" risks two errors: it can suggest that Han jurisdiction extended over the whole peninsula, which it did not, and it can import modern assumptions about colonialism that do not map neatly onto ancient frontier administration. At the same time, avoiding the term "colonial" entirely can obscure the genuine reality of foreign imperial rule, extraction, and unequal power that Lelang represented for the populations under its direct administration. This textbook uses "commandery," "imperial administrative center," and, where appropriate, "colonial administration" together, with each term's specific meaning made explicit, rather than relying on any single label to carry the full weight of description.

Section 7.5

Beyond the Commanderies: A Politically Diverse North

Having examined the commandery system in some depth, this chapter now turns to the much larger portion of the northern peninsula and Manchuria that never came under direct, sustained Han administration. Students should picture the geographic reality clearly: Han commandery territory, even at its original 108 BCE extent, and especially after the contraction that followed the loss of Lintun and Zhenfan and the relocation of Xuantu, occupied only a portion of the northwestern peninsula and adjoining areas. Vast stretches of Manchuria, the northern and eastern peninsula, and the emerging northern frontier remained under the authority of independent or semi-independent political communities that Chinese sources described, with varying degrees of accuracy and consistency, under the names Buyeo, Okjeo, Dongye, and, increasingly through this period, Goguryeo.

These communities were not simply "not yet conquered" appendages waiting for eventual absorption into a Han or, later, Korean political order. They possessed their own political institutions, economic bases, social hierarchies, and external relationships, conducted largely on their own terms even when those relationships included tribute payments, diplomatic exchange, or periodic conflict with Han commanderies or with each other. Some of these communities, particularly Buyeo, achieved a level of political organization and regional prestige that, for much of this period, exceeded that of the still-developing Goguryeo. Others, including Okjeo and Dongye, remained more decentralized and, over time, became increasingly subject to pressure and eventual domination by stronger neighbors, a process examined more fully in Chapter 8.

This section's purpose is to reorient students' mental map of the region before turning to each of these polities individually. Rather than a peninsula divided neatly into "Han territory" in the northwest and "future Korean territory" everywhere else, students should picture a genuinely plural political landscape: one durable Han-founded commandery center at Lelang, several unstable or vanished commanderies, an emerging Goguryeo still far from its later imperial scale, an established and prestigious Buyeo, and the more localized societies of Okjeo and Dongye, all interacting, trading, competing, and occasionally fighting across a shared but far from unified regional space.

Section 7.6

Buyeo and the Manchurian Interior

Buyeo, centered in the agricultural plains of the Manchurian interior to the north of the emerging Goguryeo state, was among the most significant political powers in the region during the centuries following the fall of Wiman Joseon, and it deserves treatment in its own right rather than as a mere prelude to Goguryeo's or Baekje's later histories. Chinese ethnographic and dynastic sources, particularly material later incorporated into the Sanguozhi's account of the peoples of the east, describe Buyeo as an agriculturally prosperous society organized around a royal government, a hierarchy of officials bearing titles associated with animals, such as horse, ox, pig, and dog, and a body of customary law that included provisions for punishing certain offenses, including theft and adultery, with notable severity.

Buyeo's economy rested on settled agriculture in fertile river-plain environments, supplemented by animal husbandry, particularly horse breeding, which contributed to Buyeo's military capacity and to its trade relationships with neighboring peoples and with Han commanderies. Buyeo maintained diplomatic and, at various points, tributary relationships with Han China and with successor Chinese dynasties, exchanging horses, furs, and other goods for recognition, prestige items, and access to markets. These relationships were not uniformly peaceful; Buyeo's history also includes periods of conflict with Xianbei and other neighboring steppe and Manchurian powers, as well as complex relationships with Goguryeo, alternating between cooperation and rivalry as Goguryeo's own power grew across the following centuries.

Buyeo's historical significance extends well beyond its own political and economic achievements. Later Korean foundation traditions closely associate Buyeo with the origins of both Goguryeo and Baekje. The Jumong tradition, examined briefly in the following section and treated more fully in Chapter 8, presents Goguryeo's founder as having originated from Buyeo before establishing an independent state to the south. Baekje's own founding tradition, examined in Chapter 9, similarly traces its royal lineage back through Goguryeo to Buyeo. Historians should treat these traditions with the same source-critical caution applied to the Dangun narrative in Chapter 6: they represent later political and dynastic claims to prestigious origins rather than verified historical genealogy, but their persistence across multiple, otherwise rival dynastic traditions indicates that Buyeo held a real and widely recognized position of prestige within the political memory of the region's later kingdoms.

This textbook treats Buyeo as a significant independent polity whose own internal history, while not developed at length in this survey chapter, provided an essential political, economic, and symbolic backdrop against which Goguryeo's emergence, examined next, must be understood.

Section 7.7

Okjeo and Dongye

Along the peninsula's northeastern coast and interior river valleys, Chinese sources describe two further political societies, Okjeo and Dongye, whose geography, economy, and political organization differed significantly from both the commandery world to the west and Buyeo's more centralized agricultural state to the north. Okjeo occupied a narrow coastal and riverine zone, its communities supported by fishing, agriculture in limited arable land, and, according to Chinese sources, tribute obligations paid to more powerful neighbors, including at various points Goguryeo and, earlier, commandery authorities. Dongye, situated further south along the coast and adjoining interior valleys, is described in similar ethnographic terms: a settled agricultural and fishing society, organized in relatively small and decentralized political units rather than a single unified kingdom, and likewise subject to periodic tribute relationships and pressure from stronger neighboring powers.

Chinese ethnographic accounts of Okjeo and Dongye, again drawing primarily on material preserved in the Sanguozhi's account of eastern peoples, describe social customs including marriage practices, funerary customs, and local products such as textiles, marine resources, and a distinctive local breed of small horses associated with Dongye. These descriptions provide valuable, if imperfect, evidence for social life in a region that produced little or no writing of its own during this period. Students must approach such ethnographic material carefully: Chinese authors compiling these accounts were often working from secondhand reports, administrative records, or earlier written sources of uncertain reliability, and their descriptions tend to emphasize customs that seemed distinctive, unusual, or noteworthy from a Chinese cultural perspective, a selective lens that can distort as much as it reveals about the full range of social and political life in these communities.

Politically, both Okjeo and Dongye are generally described as lacking a strong centralized monarchy comparable to Buyeo's, instead being organized around local chiefs or headmen exercising authority over smaller communities, without the kind of unified royal government and formal officialdom that Chinese sources attribute to Buyeo. This decentralization made both societies more vulnerable to domination by stronger neighboring powers, a vulnerability that became increasingly significant as Goguryeo's power expanded in subsequent centuries, a development examined fully in Chapter 8. Within the chronological scope of the present chapter, however, Okjeo and Dongye should be understood as functioning communities with their own social organization, economic base, and relationships to neighboring powers, not merely as territories awaiting eventual absorption.

Section 7.8

Early Goguryeo in Context

Among the polities examined in this chapter, early Goguryeo occupies a distinctive position: a relatively modest power during the period this chapter surveys, and one that later became a major power in the northern peninsula and southern Manchuria within the following centuries, as Chapter 8 will demonstrate in detail. This chapter's task is narrower and more limited: to situate Goguryeo's emergence within the plural landscape already described, without narrating the expansion, warfare, institutional development, and international relationships that belong properly to the next chapter.

Later Korean and Chinese sources associate Goguryeo's founding with a figure named Jumong, traditionally said to have originated from Buyeo before establishing a new political center, conventionally dated to 37 BCE, in the middle Amnok (Yalu) River valley, a mountainous and defensible but agriculturally limited region. As with the Dangun and early Gojoseon traditions examined in Chapter 6, students should distinguish the Jumong foundation narrative, recorded in written form considerably later than its traditional setting, from the more fragmentary but roughly contemporary evidence for an emerging political and military power in this region during the first century BCE, evidence that includes Chinese administrative references to conflict and negotiation between Xuantu commandery and the peoples of the middle Amnok valley.

The surviving Chinese administrative record indicates conflict and negotiation between Xuantu and communities of the middle Amnok (Yalu) region during the first century BCE. Xuantu's relocation in 75 BCE is attributed in the transmitted record to pressure from local Yemaek communities. These developments occurred within the same broader political environment from which Goguryeo was emerging, but they should not be treated as secure evidence that an already consolidated Goguryeo state itself forced Xuantu's relocation. This early Goguryeo was not yet the expansive, institutionally sophisticated state that would challenge Chinese dynasties and dominate much of the northern peninsula in later centuries; it was one competitive polity among several in a mountainous frontier zone, notable chiefly for the political and military organization it was beginning to develop within a generation of Xuantu's founding.

This chapter deliberately stops here. Goguryeo's subsequent territorial expansion, its evolving political institutions, its long and complex relationships with successive Chinese dynasties and with steppe peoples, and its emergence as a major Northeast Asian power are the substance of Chapter 8, which picks up the narrative from this point.

Section 7.9

The South after Gojoseon: Jin and the Samhan Problem

The southern peninsula's political history in this period cannot be understood as simply beginning after 108 BCE, since Wiman Joseon's fall had comparatively limited direct impact on regions far to the south that had never been part of its territory. Chinese sources contain scattered, difficult-to-interpret earlier references to a southern region or polity called Jin, mentioned in connection with diplomatic contact or attempted contact with Han China prior to the fuller ethnographic descriptions of Mahan, Jinhan, and Byeonhan that appear in later texts, particularly the Sanguozhi's account of the peoples of the east, compiled in the third century CE but describing conditions extending back over the preceding centuries.

The relationship between this earlier Jin and the later, more fully described Samhan, meaning the "three Han" societies of Mahan, Jinhan, and Byeonhan, remains a genuine and unresolved problem in the historical scholarship. Some historians have proposed that Jin represented an early, perhaps more unified or more prominent political entity that later fragmented or was succeeded by the more numerous and more loosely organized polities described under the Samhan terminology. Others caution that the surviving references to Jin are too fragmentary and too distant in time from the fuller Samhan descriptions to support confident claims about direct political continuity, succession, or fragmentation, and suggest instead that "Jin" may have referred, at different times or in different sources, to a specific ruling center, a broader geographic region, or a shifting political network whose precise relationship to later Mahan, Jinhan, and Byeonhan communities cannot be reconstructed with confidence from the available evidence.

Whatever the precise relationship between Jin and the Samhan, students must approach the Samhan terminology itself with considerable care. Mahan, Jinhan, and Byeonhan were not three centralized kingdoms with fixed capitals, standing armies, and clearly bounded territories comparable to later Baekje, Silla, or unified Goguryeo. The Sanguozhi and related sources describe each of the three as a loose grouping of numerous small polities, sometimes numbering several dozen according to the counts these sources provide, each under its own local ruler or chief, with widely varying size, wealth, and military strength, and with membership, boundaries, and relative influence that almost certainly shifted over the centuries this chapter surveys. Treating "Mahan," "Jinhan," and "Byeonhan" as though they named three unified, static kingdoms risks importing a degree of political centralization that the evidence does not support, and risks obscuring the very real internal diversity and competition that characterized each of these three networks.

The sections that follow examine each of the three Samhan networks in turn, always with the understanding that the states which eventually emerged from or alongside them, Baekje from within the Mahan sphere, Silla from within the Jinhan sphere, and the Gaya confederacy from within the Byeonhan sphere, represent later, more consolidated political developments that Chapters 9 through 11 examine in full. This chapter's task is to describe the plural, competitive, and still-forming political environment from which those later kingdoms arose, not to anticipate their mature histories.

Historical Vocabulary
Jin and the Samhan

Jin refers to an early and only fragmentarily documented southern polity or region mentioned in Chinese sources prior to the fuller ethnographic accounts of Mahan, Jinhan, and Byeonhan. Its precise nature, extent, and relationship to the later Samhan communities remain genuinely uncertain and are the subject of ongoing scholarly discussion rather than settled historical fact.

Samhan ("three Han") is the collective term used in later historiography for Mahan, Jinhan, and Byeonhan, three loosely organized networks of small polities occupying the southwestern, southeastern, and south-central regions of the peninsula respectively. The element "Han" in this context is a proper name applied to these southern peoples and confederated political networks in Chinese and Korean sources; it is unrelated to the Han dynasty of China despite the identical English spelling, a point of potential confusion this textbook flags explicitly, just as it flagged the similarly unrelated "Han" element in the modern Republic of Korea's official name in Chapter 1.

Why it matters: Students who conflate Mahan, Jinhan, and Byeonhan with unified, centralized kingdoms, or who assume that these networks map cleanly and immediately onto Baekje, Silla, and Gaya from the very beginning of this period, will misunderstand the gradual, contested, and uneven process of state formation examined in Chapters 9 through 11. The Samhan networks provide the political and economic environment within which later consolidation occurred; they are not simply earlier names for the later kingdoms themselves.

Section 7.10

Mahan

Mahan, the largest and, according to Chinese sources, the most numerous of the three Samhan networks, occupied the southwestern and west-central regions of the peninsula, encompassing the fertile agricultural lands of the Han River basin's southern reaches and extending across much of the later Honam and west-central regions. The Sanguozhi attributes to Mahan a very large number of small polities, described as numbering more than fifty, though such figures should be treated as approximate rather than precise, reflecting the difficulty Chinese observers faced in accurately surveying a genuinely fragmented and locally variable political landscape rather than a securely counted administrative reality.

Mahan's communities were predominantly agricultural, supported by rice and other grain cultivation in the region's productive lowlands, supplemented by textile production, including notable local weaving traditions, and participation in exchange networks extending both northward toward Lelang and southward toward the other Samhan regions and, ultimately, the Japanese archipelago. Political organization within Mahan appears to have varied considerably by locality, with some polities apparently exercising influence over smaller neighboring communities and others remaining largely autonomous, a pattern of unequal size and shifting local hegemony rather than fixed hierarchical order.

Chinese sources indicate that political and demographic pressures reshaped Mahan's landscape during this period, including migration from the north, plausibly connected to displacement following the fall of Wiman Joseon and subsequent instability in the northwest, and growing differentiation among Mahan's constituent polities as some centers accumulated greater wealth, population, and military capacity than their neighbors. One polity within this network, associated with the core territory later identified with the Baekje royal house, gradually achieved increasing prominence during the centuries surveyed in this chapter, a development this textbook only foreshadows here. Baekje's actual process of state formation, its expansion at the expense of neighboring Mahan polities, and its emergence as a major kingdom are examined in full in Chapter 9, which should be understood as picking up directly from the more fragmented, competitive Mahan world described in this section.

Section 7.11

Jinhan

Jinhan occupied the southeastern portion of the peninsula, broadly centered on the region surrounding the later city of Gyeongju and extending along portions of the upper and middle Nakdong River basin. Chinese sources describe Jinhan as comprising a smaller number of constituent polities than Mahan, commonly given as around a dozen, though again this figure should be treated as an approximate reflection of external observation rather than a precise or stable count, since the number and relative strength of Jinhan's constituent communities almost certainly shifted over time.

Jinhan's economy combined settled agriculture in the river valleys with early iron production, a resource and technology that connected Jinhan communities to wider regional exchange networks, including trade with Lelang, with neighboring Byeonhan communities, and, through maritime and coastal routes, with communities across the Korea Strait. Chinese ethnographic descriptions of Jinhan customs, again drawn primarily from material underlying the Sanguozhi's account, include references to distinctive practices such as certain forms of head or body modification and particular funerary customs, details that illustrate the kind of ethnographic curiosity that shaped Chinese observers' interest in describing peoples beyond direct imperial administration, while also reminding students that such details capture only isolated, externally noticed fragments of a much fuller and more complex social world.

Among the polities within the Jinhan network, one centered in the Gyeongju basin and known as Saro gradually achieved increasing local prominence during the period this chapter surveys, accumulating population, agricultural resources, and political influence relative to its neighbors. As with Baekje's antecedents within Mahan, this textbook introduces Saro here only to identify it as the polity from which Silla's later political consolidation emerged; it deliberately withholds the fuller narrative of Silla's bone-rank society, royal government, Buddhist adoption, and territorial expansion, all of which belong to Chapter 10. Students should understand Saro in this chapter's context as one competitive polity among several within a still-forming Jinhan network, not yet the centralized kingdom it would become.

Section 7.12

Byeonhan and the Nakdong Iron Networks

Byeonhan, situated in the south-central peninsula and closely interwoven geographically with Jinhan along portions of the lower Nakdong River basin, holds particular significance for this chapter because of its role in early iron production and its position within maritime and riverine exchange networks that would later underpin the Gaya confederacy, examined fully in Chapter 11. Chinese sources describe Byeonhan, like Jinhan, as comprising roughly a dozen constituent polities, and note explicitly that the region produced iron in significant quantities, iron that was reportedly sought by surrounding peoples, including other Samhan communities, and, according to some interpretations of the relevant passages, by populations across the Korea Strait as well.

Archaeological evidence from sites along the lower Nakdong basin corroborates this textual emphasis on iron, revealing evidence of iron smelting, tool and weapon production, and burial goods indicating that access to and control over iron production conferred significant wealth and status on local elites. This iron-centered economy connected Byeonhan's communities into exchange networks extending toward Lelang to the north, toward neighboring Mahan and Jinhan polities, and, across the Korea Strait, toward political communities in the Japanese archipelago. The Sanguozhi tradition explicitly reports that Han, Ye (a neighboring peninsular people), and Wa all obtained iron from this southern region, indicating that Byeonhan participated in exchange networks that contributed to technological developments across a wide area; the fuller implications of this connection for Wa's own political development are examined further, with appropriate caution, from the Korean perspective in Chapter 11's treatment of Gaya's maritime relationships with Wa.

Students should understand Byeonhan in this chapter as an economically significant and increasingly interconnected network of iron-producing communities, not yet the political confederacy that would later be known as Gaya. The transition from Byeonhan's loose network of small polities to the more identifiable Gaya confederacy, with its distinctive combination of iron production, maritime trade, and diplomatic relationships with Baekje, Silla, and Wa, occurred gradually over subsequent centuries and forms the substance of Chapter 11. This chapter's contribution is to establish the economic and geographic foundation, centered on Nakdong basin iron, upon which that later confederacy was built.

Section 7.13

Migration, Exchange, and Political Change after 108 BCE

Having surveyed the commandery world, the northern polities, and the southern Samhan networks individually, this section draws these threads together by examining the mechanisms that reshaped the peninsula's political landscape across the period this chapter covers. Han conquest in 108 BCE was a significant catalyst, but it was only one among several interacting causes, and treating it as the sole engine of subsequent political change would seriously oversimplify a genuinely multi-causal process.

Population movement following Wiman Joseon's collapse contributed to political change across a wide area. Communities and elite families displaced by the war and by the subsequent establishment of Han administration relocated to various destinations, some southward into Mahan, Jinhan, and Byeonhan territory, some into neighboring polities such as early Goguryeo or Buyeo, carrying with them agricultural techniques, craft knowledge, and, in some cases, claims to prestigious northern origins that later dynastic founding traditions, including those of Baekje examined in Chapter 9, would draw upon. Commandery markets, particularly at Lelang, created new economic opportunities and new patterns of demand that rippled outward into surrounding regions, encouraging local elites to organize production, extract surplus from dependent populations, and compete for access to the wealth and prestige that commandery trade and diplomatic recognition could confer.

Iron production, expanding significantly across this period in the Nakdong basin and elsewhere, provided both a new source of economic and military advantage and a new axis of regional exchange, connecting communities that might otherwise have had little sustained contact and generating wealth differentials that accelerated local political consolidation. Agricultural expansion, driven by population growth, technological diffusion, and, in some regions, migration-driven demographic pressure, increased the productive capacity and, correspondingly, the political ambitions of an increasing number of local centers. Elite competition for control over agricultural surplus, iron resources, trade access, and prestige goods intensified political rivalry within each of the regional networks this chapter has surveyed, producing gradual differentiation between more and less successful local polities well before any of the later kingdoms achieved clear dominance within their respective regions. Finally, military pressure, whether from Han commanderies, from stronger neighboring polities such as Buyeo, or from rival communities within the same regional network, forced weaker polities into subordinate relationships, absorption, or, in some cases, outright destruction, processes that are visible in the archaeological record through shifting settlement patterns and burial practices even where textual sources are silent.

No single one of these mechanisms, conquest, migration, commerce, iron production, agricultural change, elite competition, or military pressure, operated in isolation from the others, and their relative importance varied by region and period. Students should resist any account of this period that reduces its complexity to a single cause, whether "Han influence," "iron technology," or any other monocausal explanation, and should instead understand the peninsula's political transformation after 108 BCE as the product of multiple, interacting historical processes operating unevenly across a large and varied geographic space.

Section 7.14

From Many Polities toward the Three Kingdoms and Gaya

By the close of the period this chapter surveys, extending roughly from 108 BCE through the early centuries CE, the political landscape of the peninsula and southern Manchuria remained genuinely plural, but certain patterns of differentiation had become increasingly visible. Lelang remained a durable and influential imperial center, though its long-term future, examined in Chapter 8, would eventually be determined by the growing power of its neighbors rather than by continued Han dynastic strength. Goguryeo had grown from a competitive frontier polity into an increasingly assertive regional power capable of challenging commandery authority directly. Buyeo remained prestigious and independently significant, though its long-term position relative to Goguryeo would shift substantially in the centuries that followed. Within Mahan, Jinhan, and Byeonhan, certain constituent polities, including the centers later associated with Baekje and with Saro, had begun to accumulate disproportionate wealth, population, and influence relative to their neighbors, while the broader Byeonhan network's iron-centered economy was creating the conditions from which the Gaya confederacy would eventually take shape.

None of this, however, should be read as evidence that Goguryeo, Baekje, Silla, and Gaya were predetermined winners in an inevitable historical competition. The historical record preserves the names, and sometimes fragmentary details, of numerous other polities within Mahan, Jinhan, Byeonhan, and the northern frontier zone that did not survive as independent political centers, that were absorbed into stronger neighbors, or that simply disappear from the sources without recorded explanation. Zhenfan and Lintun commanderies vanished from the administrative map within decades of their founding. Countless small polities within the Samhan networks left only a name, or not even that, in the surviving textual and archaeological record. Okjeo and Dongye, as Chapter 8 will show, would eventually lose their political independence to an expanding Goguryeo. Even Buyeo's long-term trajectory, though beyond this chapter's chronological scope, would eventually see it eclipsed by the very neighbor, Goguryeo, whose founding tradition claimed connection to it.

The four chapters that follow, treating Goguryeo, Baekje, Silla, and Gaya in turn, necessarily narrow this chapter's wide-angle view to follow four specific historical trajectories in depth. Readers should carry this chapter's central lesson forward into those narrower narratives: each of these four polities emerged from, and initially represented only one competitive option within, a much larger and more crowded political world, a world whose other participants, though largely forgotten by later national historiography, were just as real, and, at various points, just as historically significant, as the later-dominant polities whose stories the remainder of Part II will tell.

Primary Source Window

The Sanguozhi on the Peoples of the East, 3rd Century CE

Creator: Chen Shou (233–297 CE), compiled under the Western Jin dynasty in the late third century CE; the received text also carries later annotation traditions · Date: compiled in the late third century CE, drawing on earlier reports and administrative records describing conditions extending back over the preceding two centuries · Type: Chinese dynastic history, specifically its account of neighboring peoples, conventionally referred to as the Dongyi zhuan ("Account of the Eastern Peoples") section

Context: The Sanguozhi's account of the peoples living to the east of Han and successor Chinese administration, including Buyeo, Okjeo, Dongye, and the Samhan communities, represents one of the most important surviving bodies of textual evidence for this chapter's subject. Although compiled under the Western Jin dynasty in the late third century CE, considerably later than the events at the center of this chapter, it incorporates earlier material, including administrative reports connected to Han and successor commandery officials, and it provided later Chinese and Korean historians with much of their basic information about these societies.

Paraphrase: The following is a paraphrase, not a direct quotation, of representative content from this section of the text.

The account describes the peoples of Mahan as living in numerous separate small communities without a single overarching ruler, engaged in agriculture and textile production, and notes their settlement pattern, dwellings, and certain distinctive social customs. It similarly describes Jinhan and Byeonhan as neighboring networks of smaller communities sharing some customs but differing in others, remarks on Byeonhan's production of iron sought by neighboring peoples, and separately describes Buyeo's more centralized government, animal-titled officials, and legal customs, alongside briefer descriptions of Okjeo's and Dongye's coastal and riverine communities and their tribute relationships with more powerful neighbors.

Analysis: This passage is valuable because it is one of the few sustained bodies of evidence describing the political and social organization of peoples who left little or no writing of their own during this period. It allows historians to reconstruct, however imperfectly, the approximate number, scale, and character of political communities across a wide region, and it preserves specific details, such as Byeonhan's iron production, that archaeological evidence independently corroborates.

Limitations: The account was compiled by outside observers working from administrative reports, secondhand information, and possibly outdated or incomplete earlier sources, filtered through Han and successor-dynasty cultural categories and interests. Numerical counts of constituent polities within Mahan, Jinhan, and Byeonhan should be treated as approximate impressions rather than precise censuses. The text's selection of "noteworthy" customs likely reflects what seemed unusual or significant to Chinese observers rather than a comprehensive account of these societies' full social and political life, and the voices and perspectives of the described peoples themselves do not survive independently.

Historical Debate

What Were the Han Commanderies: Imperial Enclaves, Colonial Administration, or Nodes in a Mixed Frontier?

The question: How should historians characterize the Han commanderies, and especially Lelang, in relation to the surrounding Korean-Manchurian world: as relatively contained imperial administrative enclaves with limited territorial reach, as instances of colonial rule exercising significant extractive and transformative power over subject populations, or as nodes within a broader, more evenly interactive frontier zone in which no single framework of "ruler" and "ruled" adequately captures the relationship?

Interpretation A — Bounded imperial enclaves: Some historians emphasize that Han commandery authority, even at Lelang, was geographically concentrated around administrative centers and closely connected agricultural districts, with authority over the wider region remaining indirect, negotiated, or altogether absent. On this view, describing the commanderies as governing "Korea" risks significant overstatement, since most of the peninsula and Manchuria never came under direct commandery administration at all.
Interpretation B — Colonial administration with real extractive power: Other historians emphasize that within their zones of direct control, the commanderies, and especially the long-lived Lelang, exercised genuine colonial power: appointing officials, collecting taxation, enforcing Han law, and extracting economic value from subject populations over an extended period, a relationship that, whatever term is used, involved real inequality, foreign rule, and, at least for some populations, subordination to an imperial administration whose ultimate authority lay far away in the Han interior.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the same archaeological record from Lelang, including administrative seals, tomb goods, and settlement remains, and on the same body of Chinese textual evidence describing commandery administration and its geographic limits, but they weigh the significance of Lelang's genuine administrative depth differently against the acknowledged limits of its territorial reach.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement here concerns scale and characterization rather than the basic facts, since both interpretations accept that Lelang exercised real administrative authority within a limited zone and that this authority did not extend across the whole region.

Why it matters: How students characterize the commanderies shapes how they understand the balance between foreign imperial presence and continuing indigenous political development in this period, and shapes the vocabulary, colonial, administrative, or interactive, that later chapters, and later historical debates about Korean history more broadly, will draw upon.

Student task: Using the evidence about Lelang's administrative depth on one hand and its geographic limits on the other, construct an argument for how you would characterize the commanderies, and explain what combination of terms you believe most accurately captures their historical role.

Multiple Perspectives

Living in a Plural Frontier

Note: the categories below summarize what surviving archaeological and textual evidence indicates about each group's circumstances and observable behavior. Since none of these groups left first-person accounts of their own, individual attitudes and motivations generally cannot be recovered, and none are invented here.

Lelang administrative and merchant elite, evidence from tombs and material culture: Archaeology recovers elaborate tombs, official seals, sealing clay, and imported luxury goods, including lacquerware, bronze mirrors, and silk textiles, from Lelang's wealthier administrative and merchant families, indicating sustained access to Han and successor-dynasty markets and continued participation in bureaucratic administration across an extended period.
Local populations within and around commandery territory, evidence from administration and exchange: Evidence indicates that some local families entered commandery administrative structures and participated in commandery-linked trade networks, while the broader population was also subject to taxation and the reduced political autonomy that direct or indirect Han administration entailed; what individual families felt about this arrangement is not recoverable from the surviving record.
Buyeo and early Goguryeo, evidence from political and military organization: Chinese textual sources and archaeological evidence indicate that both Buyeo and early Goguryeo maintained organized government and external tribute and trade relationships. In the middle Amnok region, the same evidence points to political and military consolidation occurring amid conflict and negotiation with Xuantu commandery; the specific role of an already consolidated Goguryeo in Xuantu's 75 BCE relocation remains uncertain, while both Buyeo and the communities of this region competed with neighboring peoples for territory and regional influence.
Samhan local polities, evidence from agriculture, iron, and exchange: Archaeological and textual evidence indicates that local rulers within Mahan, Jinhan, and Byeonhan accumulated agricultural surplus and, in Byeonhan's case, iron resources, and that some maintained trade or tribute connections with Lelang and with each other; the specific strategic reasoning or motivations of individual rulers are not preserved in the sources.
Modern archaeological and historical interpretation: Historians and archaeologists combine external Chinese textual accounts with excavated settlement remains, burial goods, and material culture to reconstruct the approximate political and economic organization of this period, while remaining aware that this combined evidence still leaves substantial gaps, particularly regarding individual experience and motivation, that cannot be responsibly filled by invention.
Continuity and Change Over Time

From Gojoseon to a Plural World

What Changed

  • A single, increasingly centralized Wiman Joseon state in the northwest gave way to a directly administered Han commandery zone of considerably more limited and unstable extent
  • Political authority in the northwest shifted from a documented Korean royal government to Han-appointed officials operating according to imperial bureaucratic conventions
  • The number and relative prominence of competing polities across the peninsula and Manchuria increased and shifted, with some communities gaining influence, such as emerging Goguryeo, Buyeo, and leading centers within Mahan and Jinhan, while others, such as Lintun and Zhenfan, disappeared entirely
  • Iron production and long-distance exchange networks expanded significantly, particularly in the Nakdong basin, reshaping regional economic relationships

What Stayed the Same

  • Political plurality, rather than unity, continued to characterize the region, extending the pattern already visible before Wiman Joseon's rise and continuing the theme introduced in Chapter 1
  • Interaction with the Chinese mainland, whether through conflict, tribute, trade, or administrative incorporation, remained a constant feature of the region's political life
  • Local elites across the region continued to compete for agricultural surplus, prestige goods, and political advantage relative to their neighbors
  • The peninsula's population continued to include diverse, regionally distinct communities without a single overarching political or cultural identity uniting them

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both the disruption caused by Han conquest and the underlying continuity of regional plurality prevents students from treating 108 BCE as a clean historical break after which everything changed, or as an irrelevant episode after which everything continued exactly as before.

Cause and Effect

Why Fragmentation, Not Succession?

Long-term causes

Wiman Joseon itself had never governed the whole of the peninsula and Manchuria, having maintained variable and often indirect relationships with numerous neighboring communities; its destruction therefore could not, by itself, produce a single unified successor covering that much larger space.

Short-term causes

The Han war and conquest of 108 BCE removed the northwest's central government abruptly. Among the four original commanderies, only Lelang remained a durable major administrative center in the northwestern peninsula over the long term; Xuantu survived in relocated form farther west, while Lintun and Zhenfan disappeared early. This left most of the wider region to develop through its own internal and regional dynamics.

Effect

A genuinely plural political landscape persisted and, in some respects, intensified across the following centuries, populated by a durable Han-founded commandery center, an increasingly assertive Goguryeo, an independently prestigious Buyeo, decentralized Okjeo and Dongye communities, and the loosely organized Mahan, Jinhan, and Byeonhan networks.

This textbook's response

Devoting this chapter to the plural landscape as a whole before narrowing focus, in Chapters 8 through 11, to the four polities that eventually achieved lasting prominence, while explicitly cautioning students against reading that later prominence backward as inevitable.

Historical complexity: The rapid disappearance of Lintun and Zhenfan, alongside the eventual disappearance of dozens of small Samhan polities whose names survive only as fragments in Chinese texts, is itself important evidence: fragmentation and disappearance, not just consolidation and survival, are essential parts of this period's history.

Everyday Life and Social History

Ordinary Life across a Plural Landscape

The political history recounted in this chapter, of commanderies, kingdoms, and confederacies rising and falling, can obscure the more ordinary rhythms of daily life experienced by most people living across the peninsula and southern Manchuria in this period. Within Lelang commandery, most inhabitants were not officials or wealthy merchants but farmers, laborers, artisans, and their families, whose lives were shaped by taxation, labor obligations, and market access more directly than by the grand narrative of imperial administration, and whose material remains, more modest than the elaborate tombs of Lelang's elite, are correspondingly less visible in the archaeological record.

Across Buyeo, Okjeo, Dongye, and the Samhan networks, most people lived by farming, fishing, textile production, and, in the Nakdong basin especially, iron working, organized within households and local communities whose obligations to chiefs, headmen, or kings took the form of labor service, agricultural tribute, or participation in military levies during periods of conflict. Chinese ethnographic sources occasionally preserve details of marriage customs, funerary practices, and social hierarchy, including references to distinctions between free commoners and enslaved or bonded persons in some of these societies, but such details remain fragmentary, filtered through outside observation, and heavily weighted toward practices that struck Chinese observers as distinctive rather than toward a full accounting of ordinary daily experience. Reconstructing this "hidden" social history more fully requires combining these textual fragments with archaeological evidence, including settlement patterns, burial goods, and the material remains of everyday production, an approach later chapters on Goguryeo, Baekje, Silla, and Gaya will develop in considerably greater depth as the surviving evidence, both textual and archaeological, becomes correspondingly richer.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced the transition from the documented history of Gojoseon, examined in Chapter 6, into the plural, contested political world that followed the fall of Wiman Joseon in 108 BCE. It began with the crisis that produced that fall: growing friction between King Ugeo's increasingly wealthy and strategically positioned Joseon state and an expansionist Han empire under Emperor Wu, escalating into a costly war that ended with the siege and capture of Wanggeomseong, an event documented with unusual confidence thanks to roughly contemporary Chinese historiography, even as the internal political details of Joseon's final months remain filtered through a Han-authored perspective.

The chapter then examined the Han commandery system established on former Joseon territory, emphasizing that this system was neither stable nor uniform. Lintun and Zhenfan commanderies disappeared within a few decades of their founding, and Xuantu was relocated and reduced under pressure from local Yemaek communities within the broader regional environment from which Goguryeo was emerging. Only Lelang endured as a major and long-lived administrative, commercial, and cultural center, and the chapter examined Lelang in some depth as a genuine node of Han imperial power, embedded within, rather than simply imposed upon, a much larger and more complex Korean-Manchurian world.

Careful attention was given to the character of interaction between commandery administration and surrounding populations, avoiding both an assumption of straightforward Han cultural transformation of the region and an assumption that the commanderies were historically insignificant. Connections with Lelang could offer local elites real benefits, and evidence indicates that some local families participated in its administration and helped shape the flow of goods, technology, and prestige between the commandery and the wider region, even as commandery authority itself remained geographically concentrated and dependent on continued negotiation with local power holders.

The chapter then turned to the much larger portion of the region that never came under direct Han administration. Buyeo emerged as an independently significant agricultural and political power in the Manchurian interior, its prestige later claimed by both Goguryeo's and Baekje's founding traditions. Okjeo and Dongye were examined as distinct, if more decentralized, political societies along the northeastern coast and interior valleys. Early Goguryeo was introduced within this landscape as a competitive but not yet dominant frontier power, its later expansion explicitly deferred to Chapter 8.

Turning south, the chapter addressed the difficult and only partially resolved relationship between early references to a polity or region called Jin and the later, fuller Chinese descriptions of Mahan, Jinhan, and Byeonhan, cautioning students against assuming either a simple continuity or a clean break between these categories. It then examined each of the three Samhan networks in turn: Mahan's numerous agricultural communities in the southwest, among which the polity later associated with Baekje's founding gradually gained prominence; Jinhan's communities in the southeast, including the increasingly influential polity of Saro, later associated with Silla's origins; and Byeonhan's iron-producing communities along the lower Nakdong basin, whose economic networks would eventually underpin the Gaya confederacy.

Throughout, the chapter emphasized that Mahan, Jinhan, and Byeonhan were not centralized kingdoms but loosely organized networks of numerous small polities, and that the later kingdoms, Baekje, Silla, and Gaya, emerged gradually from within or alongside these networks rather than existing from the beginning under different names. The chapter's discussion of causation identified multiple interacting mechanisms, conquest, migration, commerce, iron production, agricultural expansion, and elite competition, that together reshaped the region's political landscape, explicitly warning against monocausal explanations centered exclusively on Han conquest or any other single factor.

The chapter's concluding section drew these threads together by emphasizing that the eventual prominence of Goguryeo, Baekje, Silla, and Gaya was not predetermined. Numerous other polities, some named in the historical record and many more entirely lost to it, competed within the same plural landscape and did not survive as independent political centers. This observation is intended to inform students' reading of Chapters 8 through 11, which necessarily narrow their focus to these four eventual successors while carrying forward the understanding, established in this chapter, that each began as only one competitive option within a much larger and more crowded regional world.

Finally, this chapter modeled several recurring historical skills central to the wider textbook: careful source-criticism of Han court historiography describing Wiman Joseon's fall, and of the later Chinese dynastic ethnography, especially the Western Jin-era Sanguozhi, describing Buyeo, Okjeo, Dongye, and the Samhan; disciplined caution around chronology, causation, and terminology, particularly regarding the Samhan; and an insistence on describing political plurality honestly rather than flattening it into a simpler, more linear narrative of national succession.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has asked students to resist a natural but misleading simplification: that the fall of Wiman Joseon cleared a stage upon which the Three Kingdoms and Gaya were always destined to appear. The actual historical record shows something more demanding and, ultimately, more instructive. A single, durable Han-founded commandery center at Lelang persisted for centuries alongside commanderies that vanished within a generation. A prestigious Buyeo long overshadowed the still-modest Goguryeo that would later eclipse it. Dozens of small polities within Mahan, Jinhan, and Byeonhan competed, merged, and disappeared, and only a handful of their number left descendants prominent enough to be remembered as the ancestors of later kingdoms.

This plurality is not a historical inconvenience to be simplified away for narrative convenience. It is the actual texture of this period's history, and understanding it prepares students to read the next four chapters, on Goguryeo, Baekje, Silla, and Gaya, with appropriate humility about historical contingency. Each of these four polities achieved genuine and lasting historical significance, but each did so by outcompeting, absorbing, or outlasting numerous rivals whose own histories, however fragmentary, were equally real.

This chapter has also modeled a set of historical skills essential to the rest of this textbook: careful, source-critical reading of externally authored accounts, whether Han court historiography describing Wiman Joseon's fall or Chinese ethnographic writing describing the Samhan; disciplined resistance to teleological narratives that treat later outcomes as inevitable; and a willingness to hold multiple, simultaneously unfolding regional stories in view rather than collapsing them into a single linear narrative.

Students who carry these habits forward, remembering that the commandery world, the northern polities, and the southern Samhan networks all developed according to their own overlapping and interacting dynamics, will be well prepared to understand why Chapters 8 through 11 can responsibly narrow their focus to Goguryeo, Baekje, Silla, and Gaya, while never forgetting that these four polities emerged from, and were shaped by, the much larger and more crowded world this chapter has described.
