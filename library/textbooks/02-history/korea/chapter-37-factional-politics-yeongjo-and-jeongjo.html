Factional Politics, Yeongjo, and Jeongjo

Korean History: Ancient Kingdoms to a Divided Peninsula

Chapter Opening

In 1762 King Yeongjo ordered his only surviving son and regent, Crown Prince Sado, confined in a rice chest in the palace grounds, where the prince died eight days later. Thirty-two years afterward Sado's son, King Jeongjo, began building a walled city at Suwon, south of Seoul, to shelter his father's relocated tomb and to project a new vision of royal authority. Between those two events lay a century of purges, reversals, rebellions, and attempts at reconciliation. How did a monarchy that had been challenged by competing scholar-official factions come to claim the center of political life again, and what did the claim cost?

Part VI of this textbook traced the military crises that ended with Joseon's submission to the Qing. Part VII turns to the long recovery that followed and to the political, intellectual, economic, religious, and social changes of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. This chapter establishes the political framework within which those changes occurred. It asks how the kings and the scholar-official factions of later Joseon competed for authority, how successive monarchs tried to manage that competition, and to what extent the reigns of Yeongjo (r. 1724–1776) and Jeongjo (r. 1776–1800) altered the balance among royal government, elite factions, and the wider kingdom.

The chapter's subject is easily distorted. Later Joseon political conflict has often been described, by modern commentators and by some contemporaries, as petty, irrational, and a cause of national decline. This chapter does not accept that judgment. The factions grew out of a scholarly tradition that treated disagreements about ritual, historical judgment, and moral conduct as matters of public importance. They also competed for offices, land, marriage alliances, and royal favor. Both things were true at once, and historians must hold them together. Likewise, Yeongjo and Jeongjo have often been presented as enlightened rulers of a golden age. The evidence supports substantial achievements, but it also shows persistent conflict, repression, unresolved succession problems, and limits on reform. The task is to explain, not to praise or condemn.

The chapter follows a chronological sequence with analytical interludes. It begins with the legacy of the seventeenth-century crises, then examines the formation of factions and the institutions through which they competed. It addresses the mourning-ritual disputes of the seventeenth century, the dramatic reversals of ruling factions under King Sukjong, and the succession conflicts of the Gyeongjong–Yeongjo transition. It then examines Yeongjo's policy of impartiality, known as tangpyeong, and his tax, legal, and administrative reforms. The tragedy of Crown Prince Sado receives a section of its own because its causes, its consequences, and its sources all require careful treatment. The chapter then follows Jeongjo's accession, his strategies for strengthening royal authority, the Kyujanggak royal library, and the Hwaseong project, and closes by weighing achievements and limits.

The sources for this period are abundant and partisan. The annals of the Joseon kings survive for every reign, but for several reigns of this chapter there exist both an original and a revised or supplementary version, because a faction that regained power often revised the record compiled by its rivals. The Royal Secretariat's daily records, official legal and administrative compilations, the writings of Jeongjo, and the detailed commissioned record of the Hwaseong project supply further evidence. The memoirs of Lady Hyegyeong, written decades after the events she describes, are indispensable for understanding the crisis of 1762 but cannot be read as a neutral account. Throughout, the chapter distinguishes what contemporary records establish from what later memory, polemic, and family interest supplied.

The chapter also looks beyond the capital where evidence permits. Political decisions in Seoul shaped the lives of provincial households through taxation, military obligations, labor service, and the consequences of rebellion. The Musin Rebellion of 1728, the Equalized Tax Law of the mid-eighteenth century, and the construction of Hwaseong all drew in people far from the court, including soldiers, taxpayers, laborers, artisans, and residents of Suwon. Their experiences are less fully recorded than those of officials, and the chapter marks where the surviving evidence thins.

Finally, the chapter prepares the ground for what follows. Chapter 38 examines the Practical Learning movement and new approaches to knowledge, some of whose scholars served the kings discussed here. Chapters 39 through 42 treat commercialization, religion, popular culture, and social change. Chapter 43 examines the nineteenth-century political order, in which royal in-law families dominated the court. The achievements and unresolved problems of Yeongjo's and Jeongjo's reigns set the conditions for that later order, and the chapter ends by identifying that connection without narrating it.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how the Sarim scholarly tradition and the institutions of the Neo-Confucian state shaped the formation of political factions.
  2. Distinguish ideological disagreement, kinship and patronage networks, and institutional competition as interacting sources of factional conflict.
  3. Trace the major divisions among the Easterners, Westerners, Northerners, Southerners, Noron, and Soron.
  4. Analyze the seventeenth-century mourning-ritual controversies and explain why ritual interpretation carried consequences for legitimacy and power.
  5. Explain the hwanguk reversals under Sukjong and their relationship to royal strategy, succession, and elite networks.
  6. Assess the disputes surrounding Gyeongjong and Yeongjo's accession and the challenges Yeongjo faced in restoring stability.
  7. Evaluate Yeongjo's tangpyeong policies, including their intellectual basis, implementation, and limits, without equating them with the end of factions.
  8. Describe Yeongjo's tax, legal, administrative, and military reforms, weighing achievements against implementation problems.
  9. Interpret the death of Crown Prince Sado and the source problems involved, including the composition of Lady Hyegyeong's memoirs.
  10. Analyze Jeongjo's strategies for consolidating royal authority, including the Kyujanggak, scholarly recruitment, and military reform.
  11. Explain the political, military, economic, and commemorative purposes of Hwaseong, and distinguish documentary evidence from popular claims about individual inventors.
  12. Evaluate the reigns of Yeongjo and Jeongjo as periods of achievement and constraint rather than as an uncomplicated golden age.

Historical Context

The seventeenth-century crises left Joseon with a changed political environment. The Imjin War and the Manchu invasions, examined in Chapters 31 through 36, damaged the kingdom's economy and tested its institutions, but they also altered the balance of political legitimacy. The 1623 coup that enthroned Injo had been justified in part by loyalty to Ming China, and after the Qing conquest that loyalty became both a moral stance and a political resource. Officials who invoked the memory of Ming could claim to defend civilization against barbarism even while the kingdom paid tribute to Qing. This tension between practical subordination and ideological loyalty shaped the rhetoric of later court politics, and kings who wished to assert authority learned to appropriate it, as the chapter shows in its discussion of Sukjong's commemoration of the Ming emperors.

The institutional framework of the state, described in Chapter 26, remained in place. The monarch governed through the State Council, the Six Ministries, and the censorial and remonstrance institutions, and recruited officials through the civil service examinations. Unusually for a premodern monarchy, Joseon gave a prominent political role to bodies whose function was to criticize: the Office of the Inspector-General, the Office of Censors, and the Office of Special Counselors, collectively known as the Three Offices (samsa), whose officials were expected to remonstrate with the king and to impeach officials. Appointments to many of these posts, and to the personnel offices that controlled recommendations, were shaped by custom and by networks of recommendation. These institutions gave scholar-officials a legitimate channel to challenge the king and one another, and they made the control of key posts a central object of factional competition.

Postwar recovery also changed society beneath the court. Agricultural production and population recovered over the later seventeenth century, markets expanded, and the tax system was reformed in stages, notably through the Daedong law, which converted tribute obligations into a land-based levy. These developments, treated in Chapter 39, increased both the resources at stake in politics and the number of people with an interest in the outcome. A growing number of families with education and status sought office in a bureaucracy whose posts did not expand correspondingly, which intensified competition. Provincial scholars and lineages were drawn into factional networks through academies and shrines, and the capital-based elite increasingly dominated access to the highest posts.

A final piece of context concerns the monarchy itself. Joseon kings held substantial formal authority, but their power in practice depended on their ability to manage officials, secure legitimacy, and maintain an orderly succession. Several seventeenth- and eighteenth-century kings succeeded under difficult circumstances, including questions about the status of their mothers, the presence of rival claimants, and the support of factions. Queens, queen mothers, royal consorts, and the families of royal in-laws played important roles in the conflicts that followed. Understanding later Joseon politics therefore requires attention to the royal household as well as to the bureaucracy.

Section 37.1

Joseon after the Seventeenth-Century Crises: Legitimacy, Ming Loyalism, and Court Politics

When Injo died in 1649 and Hyojong succeeded him, Joseon's political class inherited a set of problems that war had produced but not resolved. The kingdom paid tribute to the Qing, as Chapter 36 explained, yet its elites had been educated to regard the Ming as the source of civilization. Hyojong, who had spent years as a hostage in Shenyang, spoke of a northern expedition against the Qing and strengthened the capital's military establishment, though the expedition was never launched. His successor Hyeonjong and then Sukjong inherited the rhetoric without the plan. In this setting, loyalty to Ming became less a guide to foreign policy than a standard of moral seriousness against which kings and officials measured one another, and it supplied vocabulary for domestic politics. Sukjong, in 1704, sponsored an altar in the palace grounds to honor the Ming emperors, a gesture that drew on the sentiment and also let the king present himself as the guardian of the civilization that the Qing had displaced.

The war years also shaped the monarchy's legitimacy. Injo had come to the throne through a coup, and the Westerner-led coalition that supported him dominated the court for decades afterward, with Southerners admitted as junior partners at times. The memory of the coup, of the surrender of 1637, and of the hostage princes' return shaped debates about who had the right to speak for the dynasty. When Sohyeon, Injo's eldest son, died soon after returning from captivity in 1645 and his younger brother Bongnim became crown prince and then King Hyojong, questions about the order of succession entered later ritual disputes, as the next sections show. Succession was never merely a family matter in Joseon; because the king's lineage defined which relatives owed which forms of mourning and deference, questions about the succession became questions about ritual propriety and, through ritual, about legitimacy.

The political conflicts of the period should not be read as an inevitable consequence of war. The factional structure that emerged in the late sixteenth century predated the Imjin War, and its continuation in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries depended on institutional features of the Joseon state and on specific decisions by kings. What the wars did was alter the stakes. They enlarged the military and fiscal establishment, created new commands and revenues that officials could compete to control, and gave Ming loyalism a moral force that factions could use. The sections below explain how those conditions interacted with the older structures of recommendation, remonstrance, and kinship to produce the politics of later Joseon.

Section 37.2

Sarim, Factions, and the Machinery of Competition

The origins of the later factions lie in the Sarim, a scholarly tradition of Neo-Confucian literati, many of them from provincial landed families, who entered the central bureaucracy in the late fifteenth and sixteenth centuries. They emphasized moral cultivation, local self-government through village compacts, and the criticism of officials and kings by appeal to Confucian principles, and they took up posts in the Three Offices and in the personnel bureaus where such criticism was institutionalized. The Sarim suffered a series of purges in the late fifteenth and early sixteenth centuries at the hands of rival officials and royal relatives, and they eventually secured control of central offices in the second half of the sixteenth century. Having won, they promptly divided.

The first major division came in 1575, when a dispute over appointment to a senior post in the personnel office, involving two scholar-officials with different circles of supporters, split the Sarim into the Easterners and the Westerners, names derived from the locations of the leaders' residences in Seoul. Here the sources reveal a mixture of motives that recurs throughout the chapter. The disagreement was partly about personal conduct and the qualifications of a candidate, partly about the interpretation of Neo-Confucian teaching, partly about the loyalties of teachers and students, and partly about who would control the recommendations by which officials were appointed. In 1591, following a political crisis connected with the question of the royal heir, the Easterners divided again into Northerners and Southerners. The Northerners split further under Gwanghaegun into factions whose later fate Chapter 36 touched on, and the Westerners, restored by the coup of 1623, remained the dominant group for much of the seventeenth century.

Historians distinguish several overlapping bases of factional identity. One was intellectual lineage: a faction usually revered a particular teacher or tradition of interpretation, and its members tended to be linked by master–disciple ties and by shared positions on ritual and moral questions. A second was kinship and marriage: families allied through marriage formed networks that crossed generations, and a faction's strength often depended on the numbers and offices of its allied families. A third was patronage and region, since provincial scholars were drawn into factions through academies and shrines, so that the factions had local bases as well as capital cores. A fourth was institutional position: control over the recommendation of junior officials to the censorial and secretarial offices gave a faction the power to place its supporters in posts from which they could impeach opponents. No single factor explains factional membership, and individuals sometimes changed sides.

The institutional machinery mattered as much as the ideology. The Three Offices were expected to remonstrate with the king and to impeach officials, and a monarch who ignored remonstrance risked being seen as failing in his duty. Officials in the personnel bureaus held customary rights of recommendation that let them shape appointments. The Border Defense Council, which had grown in importance during the wars, gave senior officials a venue for deciding military and fiscal questions, and the royal secretariat controlled the flow of documents to and from the king. The system was designed to restrain arbitrary royal rule by providing channels of criticism. Under conditions of factional competition, it also provided weapons: impeachment could remove an opponent, and a coordinated campaign of remonstrance could paralyze a government.

Students should avoid two common errors. The first is to treat factionalism as pure ideology, a contest over principles with no material stakes. The second is to treat it as pure interest, a scramble for office disguised as principle. The evidence shows that participants experienced their disagreements as matters of principle, that those principles had consequences for who held office, land, and honor, and that the two aspects reinforced one another. A dispute over how long a queen mother should mourn was a dispute about the nature of kingship, and it was also a contest over which group would control appointments. It was both because the Neo-Confucian state made them inseparable.

Historical Vocabulary
Bungdang, Faction, and Sarim

Definition. Bungdang (朋黨) is the term used for factions or parties of officials in Joseon. Sarim (士林, "the forest of scholars") refers to the Neo-Confucian literati whose rise in the sixteenth century produced the groups that later became the factions.

Historical context. In classical Confucian thought, a "party" of officials banding together for private interest was a political evil that rulers should suppress. Sarim scholars countered with an argument that a group of gentlemen united by principle, a "party of the virtuous," could be legitimate. The distinction between legitimate and illegitimate association underlies much of the later debate about factions and about tangpyeong.

Why it matters. The term shows that Joseon thinkers did not regard factions as ordinary political parties. Participants usually denied that their own group was a faction in the pejorative sense and attributed factional motives to their opponents.

Potential misunderstanding. Joseon factions were not modern political parties. They had no platforms, elections, or organizations independent of the court, and their membership was shaped by kinship and teacher–student ties as much as by shared policy positions. Translating bungdang as "party" suggests a pluralism that Joseon political thought did not endorse.

Section 37.3

The Ritual Controversies and Hyeonjong's Reign

The two great ritual controversies of the seventeenth century, known as the yesong, show how a question about funeral dress could become a question about the monarchy. When King Hyojong died in 1659, the court had to decide how long Queen Dowager Jaui, Injo's second consort and Hyojong's stepmother, should wear mourning for him. Neo-Confucian ritual manuals prescribed different periods depending on whether the deceased was the mourner's eldest son or a younger son. Hyojong was Injo's second son, and Sohyeon, the eldest, had died before him. The Westerner officials, led by the scholar Song Si-yeol, argued that ritual rules should apply to the king as they applied to any family, so that a stepmother mourned a second son for one year. The Southerner officials, among them Heo Mok and Yun Hyu, argued that a king who succeeded to the throne took on the status of eldest son in the line of succession, so that the longer period of three years was appropriate. The court adopted the one-year period, and the Southerners were rebuffed. The debate concerned not only a number of months but whether the king stood under the ordinary ritual rules of an elite family or above them.

The question returned in 1674, when Hyojong's queen, Queen Inseon, died, and the court had to decide how long the same queen dowager should mourn her daughter-in-law. The Westerners now argued for nine months, the period for a second son's wife, and the Southerners for one year, the period for an eldest son's wife. King Hyeonjong, who had come to the throne in 1659 and who had accepted the earlier one-year decision, now sided with the Southerners, in effect repudiating the logic of the first controversy. The Westerners fell from power, and the Southerners took the dominant positions in government. Hyeonjong died later that year, and his son Sukjong succeeded.

Why did a funeral rule matter so much? The answer lies in the Neo-Confucian conception of political order. Rituals defined relationships: a ruler who followed the correct rites embodied the moral order, and a ruler who deviated from them endangered it. The Westerners' position implied that the monarchy was subject to the same moral rules as every other family, so that the king's authority depended on his conformity to ritual. The Southerners' position implied that the king's office had a unique status that set him apart. These were competing visions of royal authority, and they had practical consequences, since the faction whose reading prevailed gained influence over appointments and policy. Kings were not passive in the dispute. Hyeonjong's decision in 1674 can be read as a king asserting his own and his father's special status, and as a response to the Westerners' growing dominance. The sources do not permit certainty about his motives.

The record of Hyeonjong's reign illustrates how factional conflict could contaminate the historical record itself. The annals of his reign were first compiled under a Southerner-influenced government, and after the Westerners returned to power a revised version was prepared, which reflects their view of events. Both versions survive. Students who read one without the other receive a partisan account, and historians use the comparison to see where the two versions differ. The same pattern recurs for the reign of Gyeongjong, as discussed below. The existence of competing official records is itself evidence of how seriously the factions took the question of how history would judge them.

Outside the capital, Hyeonjong's reign was marked by hardship that politics only partly addressed. Famine and epidemic struck in the 1670s, and the court's relief measures were limited. The ritual disputes, which absorbed the attention of the highest officials, belonged to a political class whose concerns were not those of the farmers who paid the taxes. This gap between the preoccupations of the court and the conditions of the countryside recurs in the chapters that follow and reminds students that the political history of the elite is only one part of Joseon history.

Historical Vocabulary
Yesong and Ritual Authority

Definition. Yesong (禮訟) means "ritual dispute" and refers especially to the two mourning controversies of 1659 and 1674, often called the first and second yesong.

Historical context. Neo-Confucian ritual codes specified mourning periods according to relationship, and Joseon scholars treated adherence to these codes as a foundation of social and political order. The controversies arose because the royal family's situation did not fit the standard categories, and different readings implied different understandings of kingship.

Why it matters. The disputes show that ritual interpretation was a form of political argument and that the monarch's own status was open to debate within Neo-Confucian terms.

Potential misunderstanding. The controversies were not trivial quarrels over clothing. Participants believed that the answer would define the relationship between the king and the moral order, and their decisions led to changes of government. Nor were they purely cynical: the arguments drew on serious readings of ritual texts.

Section 37.4

Sukjong and the Politics of Reversal

Sukjong ruled from 1674 to 1720, and his reign is remembered for a sequence of abrupt changes in the governing faction, known as hwanguk, a term that means a change of the political situation. Historians debate whether to read these reversals as the product of a calculating king who used factions against one another, as the expression of factional conflicts that the king merely ratified, or as a combination of both. The evidence supports elements of each. Sukjong was young when he took the throne, and the first years were shaped by the Southerners who had risen under his father. Over the next two decades, however, the king repeatedly dismissed ruling officials and replaced them with their rivals, often after investigations of alleged treason or after crises involving the royal household.

The first reversal came in 1680. In that year the Southerners fell from power in a sequence of events that began with a political incident involving a Southerner official and ended with accusations of treason against Southerner leaders, who were executed or exiled. The Westerners, who had been out of power since 1674, returned to the central offices. Sukjong's motives are debated. He was reportedly concerned about the growing power of the Southerners and about the behavior of certain officials, and the incident provided the occasion. In the aftermath the Westerners' victory proved fragile, because their own internal disagreements, partly over how severely to treat the defeated Southerners and partly over the relationship between the faction and the royal family, split them into the Noron, or Old Doctrine group, which followed Song Si-yeol, and the Soron, or Young Doctrine group, associated with Song's former student Yun Jeung. The split became definitive over the early 1680s, and it mattered for the next century.

The second reversal came in 1689 and illustrates how closely politics and the royal household were entangled. Sukjong's queen, Queen Inhyeon, had not produced a son, while his consort Jang Huibin, a woman of lesser status and a favorite of the king, bore a son in 1688. When Sukjong proposed to name the infant as heir, Song Si-yeol and other Westerners objected that it was premature. The king, angered, dismissed the Westerners, exiled Song and later had him executed, deposed Queen Inhyeon, and elevated Jang Huibin to queen. The Southerners, who had supported the move, returned to power. The case demonstrates a pattern: royal succession decisions, factional alliances, and the personal relationships of the king all interacted. A faction's position on the royal household could determine its fate, and a king's personal attachments could be formulated as questions of state.

The third reversal, in 1694, again swung the pendulum. Sukjong came to regret his treatment of Queen Inhyeon, and in a sequence of events beginning with disputes about the Southerners' conduct, he removed the Southerners from office, restored Queen Inhyeon, and demoted Jang Huibin. The Southerners never returned to dominance. In the following years power was shared by the Noron and Soron, with the Noron gradually gaining the upper hand. In 1701, after Queen Inhyeon's death, Jang Huibin was ordered to die after accusations that she had used shamanic ritual to harm the queen. Her son, the heir apparent, survived and later became King Gyeongjong.

Interpretations of the reversals should consider the king's perspective. A monarch who allowed any faction to hold unchecked power risked becoming its captive, and Sukjong's repeated reversals can be understood as a strategy for preventing that outcome. The cost was severe. Officials who fell from power faced exile or death, their families lost standing, and the bitterness of defeated groups fed demands for revenge when they returned. The reversals thus created a cycle in which each faction came to see its opponents as an existential threat. Politics increasingly came to be understood in terms of treason and loyalty rather than ordinary disagreement. The costs fell not only on officials but on communities connected to them, since a change of government could disrupt the networks of local patronage on which provincial scholars and their dependents relied.

Historical Vocabulary
Hwanguk and Okssa

Definition. Hwanguk (換局) means a "change of the situation" and refers to a sudden replacement of the governing faction, particularly under Sukjong. Okssa (獄事) means a "prison case" and refers to a political trial or purge in which officials were prosecuted for alleged treason or conspiracy.

Historical context. Reversals were often triggered by an okssa: an accusation of treason against officials of the governing faction led to arrests and executions, after which the opposing faction took power. The names of the reversals and trials, usually formed from the sexagenary year, became shorthand for episodes of political violence.

Why it matters. These terms reveal how political change in later Joseon was mediated by the language of treason and how the judicial system became an instrument of factional competition.

Potential misunderstanding. A hwanguk was not a coup in the usual sense. The king himself, acting through established procedures, dismissed one set of officials and appointed another. The dynasty and the throne remained stable even as governments changed.

Section 37.5

Gyeongjong, the Noron–Soron Struggle, and Yeongjo's Succession

Gyeongjong came to the throne in 1720 as the son of Jang Huibin, with a reputation for poor health and a history of tension between his supporters and those of his half-brother Prince Yeoning, who was born in 1694 to a consort of low status, Choe Suk-bin. Sukjong had no other living sons, and the question of succession made the heir's relationship with the factions critical. The Noron, who had supported Yeoning as a possible alternative, controlled the State Council at Gyeongjong's accession, while the Soron looked to the new king as their natural patron. The story of Sukjong's private meeting with a Noron official in 1717, held without the usual historiographers present, in which the old king was said to have indicated a preference for Yeoning, became a point of dispute, since the absence of a witness left the content open to competing accounts.

The conflict that followed was intense. In 1721 the Noron pressed the childless and ailing Gyeongjong to designate Yeoning as heir, which he did, and then to allow the prince to act as a regent. The Soron responded by impeaching four leading Noron ministers for acting improperly toward the king, and the king dismissed them. In 1722, in the so-called Imin case, an official named Mok Ho-ryong accused the Noron of plotting to kill the king, and the investigation led to the execution or death in exile of numerous Noron officials, including the four ministers, Kim Chang-jip, Yi I-myeong, Yi Geon-myeong, and Jo Tae-chae. The Soron thus gained control of the government and held it until Gyeongjong's death in 1724. The purges of 1721 and 1722 deepened the Noron's bitterness and established the grievance that would shape Yeongjo's reign: the Noron regarded the four ministers as martyrs, while Soron held that their actions had threatened the king.

Gyeongjong died in the autumn of 1724 after a short illness, and Yeoning succeeded as King Yeongjo. Rumors that he had been involved in his half-brother's death, including a story that he had provided food that harmed the king, circulated among Soron and other opponents, and the king and the officials of his government rejected them. Historians generally treat the rumors as politically motivated, though the sources do not provide a complete account of Gyeongjong's final illness. What is clear is that Yeongjo took the throne under a cloud. His mother's low status, the rumors about his role in Gyeongjong's death, and the Noron's identification with his cause meant that his legitimacy was questioned from the beginning. Some Soron and Southerner radicals maintained that he was not the rightful heir.

The new king's first actions showed the dilemma he faced. The Noron expected vindication, and he restored them to office and rehabilitated the executed ministers. But a purge of the Soron in turn would have repeated the cycle that had just wrecked the court. Yeongjo's early years were therefore marked by oscillation: he dismissed Soron officials, then recalled some, and issued statements deploring factional strife. The effort to find a stable course led to the policy of impartiality discussed in the next section, and the Musin Rebellion of 1728 showed what was at stake.

The sources for the reigns of Gyeongjong and Yeongjo require care. The annals of Gyeongjong's reign were first compiled under a Soron-influenced administration and presented the Soron view of the events of 1721–1722. After Jeongjo took the throne in 1776, a revised version was compiled that incorporated the Noron perspective. Historians therefore use both versions and compare them with the Royal Secretariat's daily records, which are less subject to later editing but still reflect the selection of what was recorded. The mere existence of two versions of the same king's reign is evidence that the question of who was right in 1721 had not been settled even half a century later.

Section 37.6

Tangpyeong under Yeongjo: Theory, Practice, and Limits

Yeongjo's response to the cycle of reversals was a policy known as tangpyeong, a term drawn from the classical phrase about a king's impartial and even-handed rule. The policy's central idea was that the king should appoint officials without regard to faction, balance the groups in office, and condemn factional partisanship as harmful to the dynasty. Yeongjo set out the argument in royal statements, and in 1742 he had a stele erected at the Royal Confucian Academy where the future officials studied, inscribed with a statement of the principle that a gentleman is impartial while a petty person is partisan. The location signaled that the doctrine was meant to shape the education of the next generation of officials, and the king's decisions on appointments and punishments were intended to model it.

The policy had several practical elements. Yeongjo sought to appoint balanced numbers of officials from the Noron, Soron, and, in time, Southerners to the State Council and other major posts, and he favored officials who were willing to work across factional lines, known as the tangpyeong party. He tried to curb the customary powers by which junior officials in the personnel offices controlled recommendations, since those powers helped factions place their members, and he restricted the autonomy of the censorial offices when they were used for factional attack. He also ordered the closing of many private academies and shrines that served as local bases of factional networks, though enforcement varied. And he used royal pronouncements to define the loyalty of officials in terms that could be shared across factions.

The Musin Rebellion of 1728 tested the policy. In the third lunar month of that year, a group of rebels, many of whom were radical Soron and Southerner sympathizers, rose against Yeongjo, claiming that he was not the legitimate heir of Sukjong and invoking the cause of the late Gyeongjong. The rebels, some of whom held official posts and had access to military resources, raised forces in Chungcheong, Gyeongsang, and Jeolla provinces, and captured the town of Cheongju. The rebellion was put down within weeks by government forces, but it was the largest armed uprising of the eighteenth century and was widely understood as a threat to the dynasty. Its geographic spread showed that factional grievance had provincial roots, and its suppression involved soldiers and officials from many regions. The rebellion convinced Yeongjo and many officials that the cycle of factional revenge had to be broken, and it strengthened the case for the policy of balance. It also left a long shadow: Soron associated with the rebellion carried a taint for decades.

The limits of tangpyeong were also real. The Noron remained the most powerful faction through much of the reign, and the policy of balance did not prevent their dominance of key offices. Factions continued to compete, using new issues, and serious conflict recurred. In 1755 a placard posted in Naju in Jeolla Province attacked the king and the government, and the investigation that followed produced new purges of Soron officials, showing that grievances remained. The policy's critics, including some officials of the Noron and Soron, regarded it as a device that entrenched royal control and protected officials of marginal ability who owed their posts to the policy. And the doctrine's emphasis on the king's role as arbiter meant that it relied on the monarch's personal authority, which was vulnerable when disputes touched the royal family itself, as the Sado case would show.

It would therefore be wrong to describe tangpyeong as the end of factions or as a form of modern pluralism. Yeongjo did not propose that factions should compete as legitimate political parties; he held that partisanship was an evil and that the king's duty was to suppress it by balancing and by moral exhortation. The policy operated within the hereditary monarchy and the Neo-Confucian governing order, and its aim was to preserve the monarch's authority over competing elites, not to limit it. Whether it achieved that aim is the subject of the debate that follows.

Historical Vocabulary
Tangpyeong

Definition. Tangpyeong (蕩平) is a term from the classical Book of Documents, referring to a king's broad, even, and impartial rule. In later Joseon it names the policy of balancing factions in appointments associated with Yeongjo and Jeongjo.

Historical context. The idea of a king who stands above partisan division had precedents in earlier reigns, including Sukjong's, but Yeongjo made it the centerpiece of his government and articulated it through decrees, a public stele, and appointment practice. It was later popularly linked to a mung-bean jelly dish said to have been served at a banquet of officials from different factions, but this association belongs to later tradition and cannot be confirmed from contemporary sources.

Why it matters. The policy shaped the political landscape of the eighteenth century and illustrates how Joseon kings tried to manage elite competition through moral and administrative means.

Potential misunderstanding. Translating tangpyeong as "harmony" or "balance of parties" risks implying a pluralist system. Yeongjo and his officials did not recognize factions as legitimate; the policy aimed to suppress partisanship by royal arbitration.

Historical Debate

Did Tangpyeong Weaken Factional Power or Strengthen the Monarchy?

The question: Did Yeongjo's and Jeongjo's policy of impartiality meaningfully reduce the power of factions, or did it chiefly strengthen the monarch's ability to manage and control competing elites?

Interpretation A — A genuine reduction in factional conflict: Some historians argue that the policy succeeded in lowering the intensity of factional violence. After the purges of 1721–1722 and the rebellion of 1728, the later decades of Yeongjo's reign saw fewer executions of officials over factional disputes, more cross-faction appointments, and a reduction of the censorial offices' use as weapons. On this view, the policy gave the court a period of relative stability in which reform could proceed.
Interpretation B — An instrument of royal authority: Others emphasize that the policy's chief effect was to enhance the king's role as arbiter. By distributing offices and controlling recommendations, the monarch gained leverage over officials who depended on his favor, and by condemning partisanship as disloyalty he could discipline critics. On this reading, the policy shifted the balance of power from factions and censorial institutions toward the throne, while leaving the underlying factional networks intact.
Interpretation C — A limited and unstable settlement: A third view stresses limits. The Noron retained a dominant position, the Soron and Southerners remained under suspicion, new purges followed the Naju incident and the Sado crisis, and the policy depended on the personal authority of the king, which weakened when disputes concerned the royal family itself. On this view, tangpyeong modified the style of factional competition more than its substance, and it contributed to a political environment in which royal in-law families became more important, a trend examined in Chapter 43.

Evidence: Historians draw on appointment records showing the factional composition of officeholders, royal decrees and the stele inscription, records of purges and trials, and the annals and secretariat diaries. The evidence is fragmentary for questions such as how far provincial factional networks changed, and appointments data require interpretation because individuals' factional identities were not always fixed.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns the interpretation of the evidence rather than its existence: whether reduced violence reflects policy or other causes, how to weigh royal power against factional power, and how to define success.

Why it matters: If the policy reduced factional power, the eighteenth century appears as an era of improved governance. If it chiefly enlarged royal authority, the era's stability depended on monarchs who could exercise it, with consequences for what happened when kings were weak.

Student task: Identify one body of evidence that would help distinguish Interpretation A from Interpretation B, and explain why the evidence could be read in more than one way.

Section 37.7

Yeongjo's Government: Taxes, Law, Military Policy, and Compilations

Yeongjo's long reign produced a body of administrative and legal work that historians consider among its most significant legacies. The best known is the Equalized Tax Law (Gyunyeokbeop), enacted in 1750 and implemented in the following years. To understand it, students must grasp the problem it addressed. Since the sixteenth century, adult male commoners had owed military service, which in practice was commuted into an annual payment of cloth (gunpo). Over time the system acquired distortions that fell hardest on poor households. Quotas of cloth were assigned to counties and then to villages, and when a taxpayer died, fled, or was a child, the quota did not fall; local officials instead demanded the cloth from relatives and neighbors. Records and complaints describe the levying of cloth on the dead and on infants, practices known as baekgol jingpo, the levying of cloth on deceased men, and hwanggu cheomjeong, the improper registration of infants and underage boys as military taxpayers. Meanwhile many yangban families and others with privileged status avoided the levy altogether. The system thus taxed the poorer and more vulnerable more heavily than the better-off.

The reform reduced the annual cloth obligation of commoners by half, from two bolts to one, and compensated the lost revenue through other sources. These included a surcharge on land, taxes on fisheries, salt production, and shipping, and other levies. The measure was prepared through extensive debate. Yeongjo consulted officials and, according to records, listened to petitioners, and the court considered more radical alternatives, such as a household-based tax that would have applied to yangban families, before settling on a compromise that left the privileged largely exempt. The reform eased the burden on the commoner population, and the court regarded it as a major accomplishment. But its limits are also clear. The land surcharge shifted some burden to landholders, yet landholders could pass costs to tenants, and local abuses did not disappear. The military cloth system continued in modified form, and the problem of unfair assessment recurred in later decades, with consequences discussed in Chapter 44.

Yeongjo's legal and administrative reforms ranged widely. The court compiled a revised supplement to the national code, the Sok Daejeon, promulgated in 1746, which incorporated decades of new rules and precedents. It also compiled a large reference work on institutions and ceremonies, the Dongguk Munheon Bigo, finished in 1770. The king limited the use of some forms of harsh punishment, issued regulations to reduce abuses by local officials, and in 1771 revived the practice of allowing petitioners to beat a drum outside the palace to bring grievances to the king's attention. The petition drum had precedents earlier in the dynasty, and its revival gave commoners, in principle, a channel to the throne; how many petitions reached the king and with what effect is difficult to determine from surviving records. In 1760 the court organized the dredging of the Cheonggye stream in Seoul, an infrastructure project in which wage labor was used for part of the work, with consequences for the capital's economy discussed in Chapter 39. The king also issued prohibitions on the manufacture of alcohol in years of poor harvest, which show the court's interest in controlling grain use but were difficult to enforce.

Military and fiscal policy continued the post-invasion reorganization described in Chapter 36. The capital's armed forces, organized under several commands, were reviewed and adjusted, and the king paid attention to the training of troops and the maintenance of fortresses. Yeongjo's military reforms were incremental rather than transformational, and financial constraints limited what could be done. The king also took a visible interest in scholarship, sponsoring compilations and personally attending lectures, a pattern that Jeongjo would extend. In all these areas the king's personal engagement was a hallmark: Yeongjo was famously frugal and attentive to detail, and he used decrees and public gestures to project the image of a ruler concerned for his people.

Assessment of these reforms must weigh achievement against constraint. The Equalized Tax Law reduced a heavy burden and demonstrated the court's willingness to take on a politically difficult problem. The legal compilations improved the clarity of governing rules. At the same time, the reforms operated within a system that protected privileged groups, depended on local officials for implementation, and lacked the administrative capacity to verify assessments reliably. The king's reliance on his own judgment meant that policies could change with his attention. These patterns recur in the reign of Jeongjo, and they help explain why the achievements of the eighteenth century did not prevent the fiscal and administrative problems of the nineteenth.

Section 37.8

Crown Prince Sado: Crisis, Death, and Memory

Sado was born in 1735, the second son of Yeongjo, and became crown prince in 1736 after the death of his elder brother. From 1749 Yeongjo, then in his mid-fifties, entrusted him with regency duties, delegating the handling of many state affairs. In 1744 Sado married the daughter of Hong Bong-han, a Noron official, and she later became known as Lady Hyegyeong. The marriage tied the heir to a prominent family, and it shaped the alliances that would be tested in 1762. Surviving evidence suggests that the relationship between father and son deteriorated over the years. Yeongjo was demanding and sometimes harshly critical of the prince, and Sado's conduct as regent displeased him, according to the memoirs and other sources. Sado's behavior also became increasingly erratic in his later years, with episodes of violence recorded in family accounts.

The events of 1762 unfolded quickly. In the fifth month, an official named Na Gyeong-eon submitted a memorial to the king accusing Sado of misconduct, including violent acts and other offenses. Yeongjo, according to the account that the court recorded and that Lady Hyegyeong later wrote, ordered Sado confined in a rice chest in the palace grounds, a method chosen in part because custom held that a member of the royal family should not be killed by methods that shed blood. Sado died eight days later, in the intercalary fifth lunar month of 1762, in July by the Gregorian calendar. Yeongjo subsequently conferred the posthumous title Sado, which can be translated as "thinking of him with grief," indicating the king's sorrow even while he had ordered the death. Sado's son Yi San, then ten years old, was soon after designated heir, and in 1764 Yeongjo formally made him the heir of an earlier deceased crown prince, Hyojang, a legal step that distanced the future king from his father's condemnation and protected the succession from the taint of the case.

Historians have offered several explanations for the death. One view emphasizes Sado's mental illness and the danger his behavior posed to the court, a view that Lady Hyegyeong's memoirs largely support. A second emphasizes political conflict, in which the prince's relationship with various factions and with officials close to the king made him a target. Sado's death also created political disagreements that shaped later factional alignments. During Jeongjo's reign these conflicts contributed to the development of the Sipa and Byeokpa divisions, which crystallized in the political contests of the early to mid-1780s, although the groups cannot be understood solely as sympathizers with and opponents of Sado. They also differed over the king's governing policies, political legitimacy, ministerial alliances, and the interpretation of earlier royal decisions. A third view stresses the king's own situation: Yeongjo's legitimacy had been questioned from his accession, and a prince whose conduct or associations could be turned against the throne might have seemed a threat to the dynasty's stability. The sources do not allow certainty, and these explanations are not mutually exclusive.

The source problem is acute. The Annals, the secretariat diaries, and other court records describe the events with the restraint of official documents, and the annals' account was shaped by the knowledge that the prince's son would become king. The fullest narrative comes from Lady Hyegyeong's memoirs, which were written decades later, between 1795 and 1805, for specific audiences and purposes, as the Primary Source Window explains. They defend her family, address her son and grandson, and shape the memory of the events in ways that reflect her interests. Historians therefore treat the memoirs as indispensable but not transparent, and they compare them with court records while recognizing that those records too reflect the constraints under which they were written. The later struggle over Sado's reputation, in which Jeongjo and his successors conferred new honors on the dead prince, shows that his memory remained politically charged for generations. Sensationalism, which has often accompanied popular retellings, should be avoided; the evidence supports a tragedy of illness, politics, and family conflict whose details remain partly unknowable.

Cause and Effect

The Death of Crown Prince Sado, 1762

Long-term causes

An unstable succession history, the legitimacy questions surrounding Yeongjo's own accession, the Noron–Soron legacy of 1721–1722, the heavy reliance on the heir as regent, and a deteriorating father–son relationship.

Short-term causes

Reports of Sado's violent and erratic conduct, tensions between the prince and officials around the king, the political positions of the families of the prince's wife and of the king's consorts, and concern for the dynasty's security.

Immediate trigger and event

The memorial of Na Gyeong-eon in the fifth month of 1762 and the king's order confining the prince in a rice chest, from which he did not emerge alive.

Immediate effects

The posthumous title Sado, the elevation of his son as heir, political disagreements over the case that fed later factional alignments, and the challenge of managing a succession in which the next king was the son of a condemned prince.

Long-term consequences: The incident shaped Jeongjo's reign, including his commemoration of his father and his wariness toward officials associated with the case, and it remained a source of contention in the following decades. Historical complexity: The death was at once a family tragedy, a result of the prince's condition, and a political event in which factions and relatives had stakes. Competing narratives were produced by participants with strong interests, and no single cause explains it.

Section 37.9

Jeongjo's Accession and the Consolidation of Royal Authority

Yeongjo died in 1776 and was succeeded by his grandson Yi San, who reigned as Jeongjo. The accession itself was freighted with the legacy of 1762. Yeongjo had formally made his grandson the heir of the late Crown Prince Hyojang, not of Sado, so that Jeongjo, in strict legal terms, was not the son of the condemned prince. Yet in his first public statement as king, Jeongjo declared himself the son of Sado, a statement that signaled his intention to honor his father and that alarmed those who had supported the decision of 1762. He had been under threat during his years as heir, according to accounts that attribute plots against him to officials who feared his accession, and within the first years of his reign, there were attempts on his life that the court attributed to conspirators.

The first years were dominated by conflict with officials associated with the events of 1762 and with relatives of earlier queens. Officials identified as hostile to Sado, among them members of the Hong family associated with the faction opposed to him, were purged, and Jeongjo relied heavily on an early confidant, Hong Guk-yeong, who held wide authority in the first years of the reign. Hong's power attracted opposition and, in 1779, he was dismissed. The episode showed both the king's dependence on trusted individuals and his wariness of allowing any one of them to become too powerful, a theme with echoes of Sukjong's practice though with a very different method and temperament.

Jeongjo continued and extended the policy of tangpyeong, but he sought to change its character. Rather than merely balancing factions, he aimed to recruit able officials of any background and to shape their outlook through his own scholarly leadership. He promoted Southerners who had been marginalized, including officials such as Chae Je-gong, who rose to become chief state councilor, and he took limited steps to open posts to men of secondary-son status (seoeol), appointing several talented scholars of such background to positions in the royal library. These steps, which Chapter 42 treats in the context of changing status, widened the pool from which the king drew advisers, while leaving the social hierarchy in place. Jeongjo also confronted the problems posed by the spread of Catholic ideas among some Southerner scholars, an episode in 1791 that tested his tolerance. The king preferred measured responses, and the episode is treated in Chapter 40. He likewise tried to regulate literary style through a campaign against what he regarded as unorthodox prose, a campaign that touched the cultural world examined in Chapter 41.

Royal authority in the eighteenth century had both traditional and innovative components. The king relied on established tools: appointments, decrees, and the manipulation of the censorial offices. He also built new institutions, notably the Kyujanggak, which gave him a body of scholar-officials loyal to him personally, and an elite military unit, discussed below. This combination allowed him to strengthen the monarch's hand without breaking the formal structure of the bureaucracy. The consolidation was never complete, and the Noron remained influential, but by the later part of the reign Jeongjo exercised a degree of initiative that few of his predecessors had matched.

Section 37.10

The Kyujanggak, Royal Scholarship, and the Cultivation of Officials

In 1776, in the year of his accession, Jeongjo established the Kyujanggak in the grounds of the Changdeok Palace. It combined several functions. It was a library holding royal writings, documents, and books; a repository for the writings of earlier kings; a research and compilation office; and a body whose members served as the king's advisers. In 1782 an annex was established on Ganghwa Island to store copies of important records, a response to the vulnerability of the capital in the war years. The Kyujanggak's staff included officials who held ordinary government posts and also served the king in the library, and in 1779 the king appointed four scholars of secondary-son status as library officers, a decision that drew attention because of their social background.

The Kyujanggak served political as well as scholarly purposes. The king used it to gather a cadre of talented officials who owed their positions to him personally rather than to factional patrons. From 1781 he instituted a program for selecting younger officials for further study under his supervision, a system in which the king himself examined and instructed them. The program aimed to produce officials who combined learning with loyalty to the throne, and it gave Jeongjo a channel for shaping the intellectual formation of a generation. The king's own role was prominent: he lectured, questioned scholars, and wrote extensively, and his collected writings and the diary of daily self-examination kept by the heirs and kings, known as the Ilseongnok, are major sources for his reign.

Royal scholarship also produced a substantial output of compiled works. The Kyujanggak sponsored the editing and printing of books, including works on statecraft, history, and classical learning, and supported projects such as the illustrated manual of martial techniques completed in 1790, which brought together scholars and military officers. These projects advanced learning, and they also expressed the king's claim to be the leader of the learned community, a claim with echoes of the Neo-Confucian ideal of the philosopher-king. Chapter 38 treats the intellectual currents, including Practical Learning, that shaped these scholars' work; for present purposes it is enough to note that several Practical Learning scholars served in or around the Kyujanggak and that their relationship to the king was close but not uncomplicated.

Jeongjo also took steps in military and administrative policy that illuminate his approach. In the mid-1780s he established an elite royal guard that was expanded in the early 1790s into a more substantial force, known as the Jangyongyeong, with a component based in Suwon. The unit gave the king a body of troops whose loyalty and training he supervised, supplementing the capital commands that had developed after the invasions. In 1791 the court acted to reduce the privileges of licensed monopoly merchants in Seoul, allowing more freedom of trade, a measure that responded to commercial pressures and that Chapter 39 discusses. These initiatives show a king using a range of tools to build capacity directly under his authority. They do not amount to a transformation of the state, and their implementation depended on the continued support of officials.

Section 37.11

The Hwaseong Project

The project that best exemplifies Jeongjo's methods and ambitions began with a funeral. In 1789 the king relocated the tomb of Sado from Yangju, north of Seoul, to a site at Hwasan, near the town of Suwon, south of the capital. The new location, selected after consultation about its geomantic qualities, was treated as a site of royal commemoration, and the tomb was given a new name. The relocation required moving the existing town of Suwon to a new site nearby, and Jeongjo began to plan a walled city in which the relocated population could live and which would protect the tomb. Construction began in 1794 and, according to the records, continued until the autumn of 1796.

The project had several purposes that historians distinguish. The first was commemorative and political. Honoring Sado, whom the king could not publicly vindicate while respecting his grandfather's decision, was a goal that the monument achieved through filial piety, a virtue that no one could easily criticize. The second was military. Suwon sat on the road south of Seoul, and the new fortress, with the Jangyongyeong stationed nearby, gave the king a defensible base near the capital. The third was economic and urban. The king provided for the town's growth by encouraging commerce, giving loans to residents, and establishing agricultural projects, and the new city was a planned settlement unusual in the dynasty's history. A fourth purpose, advanced by some historians and attested in later accounts, was that the king may have contemplated Hwaseong as a retirement seat after he handed power to his son, an interpretation that rests on later testimony and remains debated.

The construction was directed by senior officials. Chae Je-gong, a leading minister, supervised the project, and other officials held supervisory posts. The scholar Jeong Yak-yong, then a young official with the king's confidence, contributed proposals on fortress design and on lifting machinery, drawing on Korean and Chinese military treatises and on technical works that reached Korea through the Qing, including books containing Western mechanical knowledge in Chinese translation. The fortress walls, about 5.7 kilometers in circuit according to modern measurement, combined stone and brick, and the design included features for defense against firearms as well as traditional weapons. Cranes and specially designed carts were used to move heavy materials.

Distinguishing documented engineering from later popular claims is essential. Popular accounts, repeated in tourist literature and school textbooks, often credit Jeong Yak-yong alone with designing the fortress and inventing its cranes. The documentary record shows a collective effort: officials, military engineers, master craftsmen, and the king himself, who took an active role in decisions, all contributed. Jeong's contributions are attested by his own writings and by the record, but the cranes and carts drew on earlier designs and imported technical literature, and historians treat the claim that one person invented the machinery as an exaggeration. The project is better understood as an organized state enterprise in which specialized knowledge was brought together under royal direction.

The project's labor organization was notable. The state recorded the names, skills, and wages of artisans and workers, and sources emphasize that many laborers were paid wages rather than called up through the traditional forced-labor obligation. Historians treat this as a significant departure, though the extent to which wage labor fully replaced obligation, and how wages were funded, are discussed by scholars, since sources describe funding from several sources, including royal household funds and other revenues. The workers included soldiers, skilled masons, carpenters, and laborers from many provinces, and the project's scale made it among the largest public works of the dynasty since the building of the capital. Their experiences, including conditions of work and the effects of the project on residents displaced to make room for it, are less fully documented than the decisions of officials.

The project left a remarkable documentary legacy. The Hwaseong Seongyeok Uigwe, compiled under Jeongjo and completed in manuscript form in 1796, was printed in 1801 after the king's death. It is a detailed record of the construction in several volumes, with plans, illustrations, lists of materials and workers, accounts, and descriptions of equipment. The uigwe genre, in which the court recorded major ceremonies and projects, was well established, and this volume is notable for its technical detail. It later made possible the reconstruction of structures damaged in the twentieth century. A separate record documented the king's procession to Hwaseong in 1795, which included celebrations marking Lady Hyegyeong's sixtieth birthday, a ceremony that combined filial display with a demonstration of royal presence and military strength. The sources thus allow historians to study the project with unusual precision, while reminding them that the record was produced by the court to commemorate the king's achievement.

Section 37.12

Achievements, Limitations, and Historical Legacy

An assessment of Yeongjo and Jeongjo must begin by acknowledging real accomplishments. Yeongjo's reign saw a reduction of the most violent forms of factional conflict, a significant tax reform, improved legal compilation, and a monarch who engaged personally with the grievances of his subjects. Jeongjo's reign saw the creation of an institution that fostered scholarship and recruited able officials, an ambitious public work, and a body of printed scholarship. The two reigns together covered most of the eighteenth century, a period in which the kingdom's population and economy recovered and its cultural life flourished, developments examined in the chapters that follow. The kings contributed to a political environment in which such developments could occur.

The limitations are equally clear. Factional conflict was reduced, not ended: purges followed the Naju incident and the Sado case, the Noron remained dominant, and the Sipa–Byeokpa divisions took shape during the next reign. Reform depended on the personal initiative of monarchs, and it did not build lasting institutional structures that could survive a weak king. The tax reform left privileged groups largely exempt and did not fix the problem of unequal assessment. Jeongjo's vision of a king-led community of scholars rested on his personal prestige, and when he died suddenly in 1800, the structure he had created did not secure his projects. The Hwaseong project, impressive as it was, was not accompanied by a transformation of the kingdom's fiscal or military base. Presenting these reigns as a golden age flattens a more complicated record.

Jeongjo died in the sixth lunar month of 1800, in the summer, and his son Sunjo, then about ten years old, succeeded him. Queen Dowager Jeongsun, Yeongjo's second consort, a member of the Gyeongju Kim family, acted as regent, and the following year the court moved against Catholic communities in a persecution examined in Chapter 40. Rumors about the cause of Jeongjo's death circulated, but the evidence does not allow historians to determine whether it was other than natural. The succession of a child king, the regency of an older woman whose family had been among Sado's opponents, and the role of royal in-law families established conditions that Chapter 43 explores as the beginning of the political order of the nineteenth century. Chapter 37 does not narrate that order but notes how the unresolved succession problems, the dependence on the monarch's personal authority, and the importance of royal relatives prepared it.

The legacy of these reigns also includes memory. Jeongjo and Yeongjo became figures of later cultural and historical imagination, and modern popular culture has often portrayed them in dramatic terms, especially the story of Sado. Historians face the task of distinguishing these representations from the evidence. Hwaseong, restored in the late twentieth century on the basis of the uigwe, now serves as a heritage site, and the record of its construction gives modern readers a window into the state's capacity and ambitions in the late eighteenth century.

Everyday Life and Social History

Taxpayers, Soldiers, and Workers beyond the Court

For most households, the politics of the court mattered through its effects on taxes, service, and local order. A commoner family faced the cloth levy that the Equalized Tax Law halved: the burden could fall on a household for a family member who had died or fled, and the petitions and complaints that reached the court describe villages in which poorer households carried obligations that wealthier ones avoided. The reform reduced the amount owed, yet other charges, including grain loans and local fees, remained, and the experience of taxation varied with the honesty of local officials and the power of local elites.

Rebellion and its suppression affected provincial communities directly. The Musin Rebellion drew soldiers and local supporters from several provinces, and its suppression brought troops, investigations, and punishments to the towns and villages involved. Studies of the rebels using the trial records show participants from a number of districts, including areas near Suwon, indicating that the uprising involved people at various social levels, not only elite plotters. The families of those convicted faced loss of property and status.

The Hwaseong project offered a different kind of experience. Skilled artisans and laborers worked for wages that were recorded, and the project's scale drew workers from many provinces to Suwon. Residents of the old town were relocated to make room for the new city, and the fortress's economic provisions affected the town's growth. The sources are mainly official, and they record names and sums rather than the experiences of the workers, whose accounts of conditions, hardships, or satisfaction are largely absent. Historians therefore reconstruct their situation from budget records and from the structure of the project, and they should be cautious about celebratory claims.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Royal Authority and Elite Factions, 1659–1800

What Changed

  • The monarch's strategy shifted from replacing factions (Sukjong) to balancing them (Yeongjo) to building institutions and cadres loyal to the throne (Jeongjo)
  • The intensity of factional violence declined after 1728, though it did not vanish
  • The tax system and legal codes were revised, and large state projects used recorded wage labor
  • The royal household and its relatives became increasingly central to political outcomes

What Stayed the Same

  • The hereditary monarchy, the Neo-Confucian governing institutions, and the examination system endured
  • Factional networks based on teachers, kinship, and region persisted
  • Privileged groups remained largely exempt from the heaviest taxes
  • Succession and legitimacy remained the most dangerous political questions

Pattern and timing: Change was gradual and uneven, accelerating after the crises of 1721–1728. Officials and royal relatives experienced political change most directly, while rural taxpayers experienced it mainly through tax and service burdens. The pattern matters because it shows that the eighteenth-century monarchy gained effectiveness through the personal engagement of capable kings, a source of strength that could not be guaranteed to continue.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how Joseon's kings and scholar-official factions competed for political authority from the mid-seventeenth century to 1800, and how the reigns of Yeongjo and Jeongjo altered the balance among royal government, elite factions, and the wider kingdom. Its central argument is that factional conflict was neither mere irrationality nor mere interest: it grew from a scholarly tradition that made moral and ritual judgment matter for public life, and it was sustained by institutions that gave officials the means to criticize, impeach, and recommend one another.

The Sarim tradition produced officials who regarded moral criticism of rulers and colleagues as a duty. The divisions of 1575 and 1591 created the Easterners, Westerners, Northerners, and Southerners, and later splits produced the Noron and Soron. Intellectual lineage, kinship, patronage, region, and institutional position all shaped factional identity, and no single factor explains membership. The institutions of remonstrance and recommendation made control of posts central to competition.

The mourning controversies of 1659 and 1674 showed how ritual questions carried consequences for the monarchy's status and for appointments. Hyeonjong's decision to side with the Southerners in 1674 illustrates that kings were participants in these disputes. The existence of competing versions of reign annals shows how seriously factions treated the historical record.

Sukjong's reversals of 1680, 1689, and 1694 used royal power to replace governing factions, and they were intertwined with disputes over queens and heirs. They secured the king's independence from any single faction at the price of cycles of exile, execution, and revenge, and they contributed to a political culture in which opponents were treated as traitors.

Gyeongjong's brief reign and the purges of 1721–1722 revealed how an unresolved succession could produce mass prosecutions. Yeongjo took the throne under a cloud of suspicion about his mother's status and his role in his brother's death, and the Musin Rebellion of 1728 demonstrated the depth of the challenge. His policy of tangpyeong sought to end the cycle by balancing factions and condemning partisanship. Historians debate whether it reduced factional power or chiefly strengthened royal authority, and the evidence supports a mixed conclusion: violence declined, but factions and the dominance of the Noron persisted.

Yeongjo's reforms, notably the Equalized Tax Law, legal compilations, and the revival of the petition drum, addressed real problems and improved some conditions, yet they left privileged groups largely exempt and depended on local officials for implementation. The reign shows both the capacity of an engaged monarch and the limits of reform within existing structures.

The death of Crown Prince Sado in 1762 was a family tragedy and a political event. Its causes included the prince's condition, tensions with the king and officials, and the legitimacy problems of the dynasty, and it shaped later factional alignments, including the Sipa–Byeokpa divisions that crystallized under Jeongjo. Lady Hyegyeong's memoirs are the fullest narrative but were written decades later for specific audiences and purposes, so they must be read critically alongside court records.

Jeongjo consolidated authority by building institutions loyal to himself: the Kyujanggak, a program for educating selected officials, and an elite guard. He recruited from a wider pool, including marginalized Southerners and men of secondary-son status, while leaving the hierarchy intact. The Hwaseong project united filial commemoration, military planning, and urban development, and its detailed uigwe allows unusual insight into state organization and wage labor, though popular claims about a single inventor overstate the evidence.

The reigns of Yeongjo and Jeongjo were periods of achievement and constraint, not an uncomplicated golden age. Their successes depended on the personal initiative of the kings, and Jeongjo's sudden death in 1800 left a child king, a regency, and a prominent role for royal in-law families. These conditions set the stage for the political order examined in Chapter 43.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter establishes the political framework for the rest of Part VII. Late Joseon's remarkable intellectual, economic, and cultural developments occurred within a political order shaped by factions, ritual disputes, succession crises, and the efforts of kings to manage them. Understanding that order helps explain why some reforms succeeded, why others stalled, and why the achievements of the eighteenth century coexisted with persistent tensions.

The chapter also shows how deeply politics and the royal household were intertwined. Disputes about mourning, queens, heirs, and tombs were not distractions from governance but central to it, because the Neo-Confucian state defined legitimacy through family and ritual relationships. This insight matters for later chapters, especially those on family and status and on the nineteenth-century rise of royal in-law families.

Yeongjo and Jeongjo emerge as capable and engaged monarchs whose achievements were real and whose limits were also real. Their reliance on personal authority produced effective initiatives and left institutions vulnerable. The records they commissioned, from legal compilations to the uigwe of Hwaseong, also give historians unusually rich evidence, evidence that must be read with attention to its purposes.

Finally, the chapter illustrates a general lesson about historical interpretation. The stories of factional strife, of Sado, and of an enlightened king have been retold in many forms, and each retelling reflects the concerns of its time. Distinguishing documentary evidence from later memory, and explaining rather than judging, is part of understanding how the political world that Chapter 38 and the following chapters describe came to be.
