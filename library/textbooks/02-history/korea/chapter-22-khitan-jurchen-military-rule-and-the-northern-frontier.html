Khitan, Jurchen, Military Rule, and the Northern Frontier

Korean History: Ancient Kingdoms to a Divided Peninsula

Chapter Opening

In the winter of 993, a Khitan army stood on Goryeo soil and a Goryeo envoy named Seo Hui walked into the enemy camp not to surrender territory but to argue for more of it. A century and a half later, in the autumn of 1170, a company of Goryeo's own soldiers turned on the civil officials who had commanded and humiliated them for generations, and within a single night the kingdom's political order was overturned. Between these two moments lies the story this chapter tells: how a state built and defended its power along a dangerous northern frontier, and how the soldiers who bore that burden eventually seized authority from the civil officials who had long constrained them.

Chapter 20 described Goryeo's government as a structure of overlapping institutions: a State Council staffed largely by civil aristocrats, a censorial apparatus that scrutinized administration, an examination system that rewarded literary learning, and a military establishment organized into central and provincial forces. That chapter also introduced, without dwelling on it, a basic inequality built into Goryeo's ranking system: the distinction between civil officials, or munban, and military officials, or muban. This inequality might have remained a matter of court protocol and salary schedules had Goryeo not spent much of its first two and a half centuries fighting, negotiating with, and adapting to two of the most formidable powers in Northeast Asian history: the Khitan empire of Liao and the Jurchen peoples who would eventually found the Jin dynasty.

Goryeo's northern frontier was never a peaceful backwater. It was a zone of fortresses, garrisons, and contested settlements running along the Amnok River and into territory that had once belonged to Goguryeo and Balhae. Successive Goryeo kings, beginning with Wang Geon himself, treated recovery of that northern inheritance as a central purpose of the dynasty. That ambition brought Goryeo into direct and repeated contact with Liao, whose Khitan rulers governed a vast steppe and agrarian empire stretching across Manchuria and much of northern China, and later with Jurchen communities whose shifting political organization eventually produced the Jin dynasty that would, in turn, displace Liao altogether.

These confrontations were not simple stories of invasion and resistance. Goryeo diplomats negotiated as often as Goryeo soldiers fought, and some of the kingdom's most consequential victories, including Seo Hui's negotiations of 993, were achieved without a decisive battle at all. Other confrontations, particularly the Khitan invasions of 1010 and 1018 to 1019, involved the destruction of Goryeo's capital, the flight of a king, and campaigns that reshaped the kingdom's military institutions for generations. Still later, the rise of Jurchen power under Wanyan leadership transformed Goryeo's northeastern frontier and forced the kingdom to abandon hard-won fortresses when holding them proved unsustainable.

Two and a half centuries of frontier defense, intermittent warfare, military mobilization, and diplomacy left Goryeo with an increasingly capable and increasingly resentful military establishment. Military officers who had defended the kingdom's borders, built its fortifications, and, in periods of active war, died in its campaigns found themselves permanently subordinated within a government dominated by civil aristocrats who monopolized the highest offices, controlled royal favor, and, on occasion, treated military officers with open contempt. That resentment did not depend on constant fighting to sustain itself; it accumulated steadily within the structure of the court itself, through restricted promotion, exclusion from senior command, and repeated favoritism, and in 1170 it erupted.

The coup of 1170 is often remembered as a single dramatic event, but this chapter treats it as the product of long-term structural conditions meeting a particular moment of court excess and political opportunity. What followed the coup was neither immediate stability nor the destruction of the Goryeo dynasty. Instead, Goryeo entered a distinctive and durable form of government in which military strongmen held real power while the Wang royal house continued, often as a subordinated institution, to occupy the throne. This arrangement reached its most stable and long-lasting form under the Choe family beginning in 1196, but it also generated new forms of unrest, including uprisings among peasants, slaves, and other groups whose grievances predated the coup but found new opportunities for expression once the old civil order had been shaken.

This chapter follows that arc from the northern frontier to the royal palace and back out into the countryside. It asks how warfare against Khitan and Jurchen powers built a Goryeo military establishment capable of overturning the government that had long constrained it, why that establishment remained politically subordinate for so long before rebelling, how the coup of 1170 unfolded and why it did not immediately produce stable rule, and what the subsequent decades of military government and social rebellion reveal about the deeper tensions within Goryeo society. The Mongol invasions that would eventually confront this military-dominated Goryeo are the subject of the next chapter; this chapter's task is to explain the kingdom the Mongols would find when they arrived.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain the strategic and historical importance of Goryeo's northern frontier along the Amnok River and its connections to former Goguryeo and Balhae territory.
  2. Describe the Khitan Liao empire as a major Northeast Asian power rather than as a simple external threat to Goryeo.
  3. Analyze Seo Hui's negotiations of 993 as an example of diplomacy conducted from a position of prepared strength.
  4. Trace the escalation of Khitan-Goryeo conflict across the invasions of 993, 1010, and 1018 to 1019, distinguishing their causes and outcomes.
  5. Explain how repeated frontier warfare shaped Goryeo's fortification networks, military institutions, and northern settlement policy.
  6. Describe the changing political organization of Jurchen communities and their relationships with Goryeo and Liao before the rise of Jin.
  7. Assess Yun Gwan's campaigns, the Byeolmuban, and the Nine Fortresses as an episode revealing both Goryeo ambition and the practical limits of frontier expansion.
  8. Explain how the Jurchen founding of the Jin dynasty altered the regional balance of power and required Goryeo diplomatic adjustment.
  9. Analyze the structural inequality between civil officials (munban) and military officials (muban) as a long-term cause of the coup of 1170.
  10. Describe the events of the 1170 coup and evaluate competing explanations for why it occurred when it did.
  11. Trace the unstable succession of military strongmen between 1170 and 1196 and explain how the Choe family established more durable control.
  12. Explain how the Choe military regime exercised power while preserving the Wang monarchy as a formal institution.
  13. Analyze social rebellions of the late twelfth century, including peasant, slave, and local uprisings, as evidence of tensions extending beyond elite politics.
  14. Evaluate the relationship between frontier warfare, civil-military inequality, and internal political transformation as interconnected historical processes.

Historical Context

Chapter 19 explained how Wang Geon founded Goryeo in 918 and reunified the peninsula's rival successor states by 936, and emphasized his dynasty's claim to inherit the legacy of Goguryeo, including ambitions toward former Goguryeo territory in the north. Chapter 20 examined the resulting state's institutions in detail: a monarchy supported by a State Council and Six Ministries-style administration, a censorial apparatus, civil service examinations that rewarded literary and classical learning, landholding arrangements tied to office and military service, and a formal ranking system that placed civil officials, or munban, above military officials, or muban, in prestige, promotion opportunities, and access to the highest positions of state. Chapter 21 then examined the cultural flowering that this aristocratic and Buddhist civil order produced, including Goryeo Buddhism, celadon ceramics, and the beginnings of the woodblock printing tradition that would eventually produce the Tripitaka Koreana.

This chapter turns to a dimension of early and middle Goryeo history that ran alongside, and eventually collided with, that civil and cultural order: the kingdom's sustained military engagement with two major Northeast Asian powers, the Khitan Liao empire and the Jurchen peoples who later founded the Jin dynasty, and the internal political consequences of that engagement. Goryeo's northern border followed roughly the line of the Amnok River, a frontier inherited in contested form from the collapse of Balhae in 926, an event examined in Chapter 18. Balhae's fall left a vacuum of authority across large stretches of former Goguryeo and Balhae territory in Manchuria and the northern peninsula, and Khitan power expanded rapidly into that space during the tenth century as the Liao dynasty consolidated a large multi-ethnic empire spanning both steppe and agricultural zones.

Goryeo therefore came into being, and grew through its first century, in direct proximity to one of the most powerful states in contemporary East Asia. Liao's relationship with the Song dynasty of China, established in 960 and itself locked in a long rivalry with Liao over territory and tribute, shaped the wider diplomatic context in which Goryeo operated. Goryeo's own posture toward Liao, alternating between resistance, negotiation, and eventually tributary accommodation, cannot be understood in isolation from this larger three-way relationship among Goryeo, Liao, and Song China.

By the early twelfth century, the regional balance shifted again as Jurchen communities in Manchuria, previously fragmented and, in some cases, formally subordinate to Goryeo or Liao, unified under Wanyan leadership and founded the Jin dynasty in 1115. Jin rapidly destroyed Liao and established a new supremacy over Manchuria and much of northern China, forcing Goryeo to renegotiate its diplomatic posture once again. These external pressures, examined in the chapter's first half, provide the essential background for understanding the internal political transformation examined in its second half: the 1170 coup, the unstable military strongmen who followed it, the consolidation of power under the Choe family beginning in 1196, and the social rebellions that accompanied this half-century of upheaval.

Section 22.1

Goryeo's Northern Frontier

Wang Geon's founding of Goryeo, examined in Chapter 19, was never simply a matter of reunifying the peninsula's rival successor states. From its earliest years, the new dynasty presented itself as the political heir of Goguryeo, whose northern territories in Manchuria had once extended far beyond the peninsula proper. This inherited ambition gave Goryeo's northern frontier an importance that went well beyond ordinary border defense. Recovering, or at least securing, territory associated with Goguryeo's former reach became an explicit dynastic purpose, reflected in the choice of the dynastic name itself and in early Goryeo's efforts to extend its authority northward along the Amnok River.

The geography of this frontier shaped everything that followed. The Amnok River, along with a network of tributary valleys and mountain passes, formed a natural but permeable boundary between the peninsula and the Manchurian interior. Gaegyeong, Goryeo's capital, lay well to the south of this frontier, meaning that any serious threat along the Amnok required either a rapid mobilization of forces across considerable distance or a standing military and fortification network capable of absorbing an initial attack. Both approaches, in fact, developed over the following century, as this chapter's later sections describe.

The frontier's political status was also unstable in ways that had nothing to do with Goryeo's own ambitions. Following Balhae's collapse in 926, discussed in Chapter 18, the territories north of the peninsula that Balhae had once governed passed into a period of contested and shifting control. Various peoples, including Jurchen communities whose organization at this period remained fragmented and localized, occupied parts of this space, while the expanding Khitan Liao empire steadily absorbed much of Manchuria into its own administrative and military structure. Goryeo therefore inherited not a stable neighbor to its north but a zone of overlapping claims, migrating populations, and, increasingly, a single dominant imperial power whose interests did not align with Goryeo's own northern ambitions.

This combination of dynastic ambition and unstable frontier geography explains why Goryeo's foreign relations during its first two centuries were dominated so heavily by northern concerns, in sharp contrast to the more settled maritime diplomacy that Silla had once conducted with Tang China and Japan, examined in Chapter 17. Goryeo's most consequential foreign relationships were not primarily maritime but overland and, in a very literal sense, adjacent: a shared and contested border with an empire, Liao, whose military and administrative reach vastly exceeded Goryeo's own, followed later by an equally consequential relationship with a rising Jurchen power that would itself become an empire, Jin. Understanding Goryeo's political and military development in the eleventh and twelfth centuries therefore requires beginning not with the royal court in Gaegyeong but with this northern frontier and the powers that faced Goryeo across it.

Section 22.2

Khitan Liao and the Changing Northeast Asian Order

The Khitan people, a confederation with roots in the eastern steppe and forest zones of Manchuria and Mongolia, built one of the most successful and durable empires in medieval Northeast Asian history. Under the Liao dynasty, founded in the early tenth century, Khitan rulers governed an unusually diverse realm that combined steppe pastoral populations, sedentary agricultural regions, and, following campaigns into northern China, substantial numbers of Chinese subjects. Liao's rulers developed a dual administrative system that governed steppe and agricultural populations through separate institutions, an innovation that allowed the dynasty to sustain both nomadic military strength and settled agricultural revenue simultaneously.

Liao's expansion into Manchuria during the tenth century brought it into direct contact with the territories once governed by Balhae, and Liao forces were instrumental in Balhae's final collapse in 926. This expansion placed Liao in a position of direct proximity to Goryeo's northern frontier and gave the Khitan empire a set of strategic interests, control over Manchurian trade routes, security along its eastern frontier, and diplomatic standing relative to Song China, that did not necessarily align with Goryeo's own ambitions toward former Goguryeo territory.

It is important for students to resist describing Liao simply as a foreign threat that periodically invaded Goryeo. Liao was a sophisticated imperial state with its own internal politics, succession disputes, and long-term strategic calculations, and its relationship with Goryeo unfolded within a wider triangular diplomatic system that also included Song China. Song, founded in 960 after the political fragmentation that had followed the Tang dynasty's collapse, regarded Liao as a dangerous rival controlling territory Song considered rightfully Chinese, and the two empires fought repeated wars before settling into an uneasy and, at times, tribute-based peace. Goryeo's diplomatic choices, whether to align more closely with Song, accommodate Liao, or attempt to balance between the two, therefore carried consequences that extended well beyond the immediate northern frontier.

By the closing decades of the tenth century, Liao had grown confident enough in its regional position to turn its attention toward Goryeo's independent conduct of foreign relations, particularly Goryeo's continuing diplomatic and commercial contact with Song. This concern, combined with unresolved questions about the status of territory near the Amnok frontier, set the stage for the first major Khitan invasion of Goryeo in 993, examined in the next section.

Section 22.3

The First Khitan Invasion and Seo Hui, 993

In 993, a large Khitan army under the command of Xiao Sunning crossed into Goryeo territory, advancing with sufficient strength to alarm the Goryeo court and to raise, within the government, serious debate over whether to sue for peace by ceding northern territory outright. This crisis produced one of the most celebrated episodes in Goryeo diplomatic history: the negotiations conducted by the Goryeo official Seo Hui.

Rather than treating the Khitan invasion purely as a military problem to be resolved on the battlefield, Seo Hui approached Xiao Sunning directly to negotiate a settlement. According to the account preserved in later Goryeo historical compilations, Seo Hui argued that Goryeo, as the legitimate successor to Goguryeo, held a rightful claim not only to the territory it currently occupied but to additional lands associated with Goguryeo's former extent. He further argued that the true source of tension between Liao and Goryeo lay not in any Goryeo hostility toward the Khitan but in territory then held by Jurchen groups that separated the two states and disrupted communication and trade, and he proposed that Goryeo's assumption of control over this intervening territory would serve Liao's own interest in stable and manageable relations along its eastern frontier.

This argument succeeded. Rather than ceding territory, Goryeo secured Khitan recognition of its claim to land east of the Amnok River, an area subsequently organized into what later sources describe as the Six Garrison Settlements, a network of fortified points intended to anchor Goryeo's northern defense and to formalize the frontier along more favorable lines. In exchange, Goryeo acknowledged a nominal tributary relationship with Liao and, at least for a time, curtailed some of its independent diplomatic contact with Song China.

Students should understand Seo Hui's achievement as resting on more than rhetorical skill alone. Goryeo's negotiating position depended on the credible presence of a mobilized Goryeo military capable of resisting continued Khitan advance, on Liao's own calculation that a negotiated settlement served its interests better than an extended and costly campaign, and on the specific geopolitical logic that made Goryeo control over the intervening Jurchen-held territory genuinely useful to Liao's frontier management. Diplomacy and military readiness worked together in this episode rather than functioning as alternatives, and the lesson would recur throughout Goryeo's later frontier history: negotiated settlements tended to succeed when backed by demonstrated military capacity, and to fail when they were not.

Section 22.4

The Khitan Wars of 1010 and 1018–1019

The settlement achieved in 993 did not resolve Khitan-Goryeo tensions permanently. In 1009, a Goryeo military officer named Gang Jo carried out a coup at the Goryeo court, deposing and killing King Mokjong and installing Hyeonjong, a member of the royal family, on the throne in his place. This violent change of monarch gave Liao's Emperor Shengzong an explicit pretext for renewed military action: the Liao court presented its subsequent invasion as a punitive campaign directed against Gang Jo for the killing of a legitimate Goryeo king, even though broader strategic concerns, including continuing Goryeo diplomatic contact with Song China and Goryeo's growing involvement among Jurchen communities to its northeast, almost certainly also shaped Liao's decision to invade.

The resulting second Khitan invasion, launched in 1010, proved far more destructive than the first. Khitan forces advanced deep into Goryeo territory, captured and burned significant portions of the capital at Gaegyeong, and forced King Hyeonjong to flee southward, an event that exposed the vulnerability of Goryeo's central government to a sufficiently determined Khitan campaign despite the settlement of 993. Goryeo resistance during this invasion and the following year was not confined to the flight of the king, however. The general Yang Gyu conducted a sustained campaign of harassment and counterattack against the retreating Khitan army through the winter of 1010 and into 1011, recovering captives and inflicting significant losses even though he was ultimately killed in the fighting. His resistance, alongside the eventual Khitan withdrawal, helped Goryeo begin to recover from the destruction of its capital.

The 1010 invasion is therefore best understood not as an isolated military event but as directly connected to Goryeo's own internal politics. Gang Jo's 1009 coup gave Liao both a stated justification and a genuine opportunity to intervene militarily in a kingdom whose royal succession had just been violently disrupted, rather than relying solely on diplomatic pressure as it had in 993. The destruction of the capital and the king's flight represented a severe blow to Goryeo prestige and forced a reassessment of the kingdom's frontier defenses once the immediate crisis had passed.

That reassessment culminated in a third major Khitan campaign in 1018 and 1019, this time met by a far more effective Goryeo military response under the general Gang Gam-chan. Khitan forces advancing toward Gaegyeong were harassed, delayed, and ultimately drawn into a decisive engagement at Gwiju in 1019, where Gang Gam-chan's forces inflicted a severe and, by most later accounts, near-total defeat on the retreating Khitan army. The Battle of Gwiju effectively ended the era of major Khitan invasions of Goryeo. Following this defeat, Liao abandoned further large-scale military efforts against Goryeo, and the two states settled into a more stable, if still formally tributary, relationship that would endure for roughly a century.

Students should treat these three invasions, 993, 1010, and 1018 to 1019, as distinct episodes with different causes, different courses, and different outcomes rather than compressing them into a single generic "Khitan war." The first ended in a diplomatic settlement favorable to Goryeo; the second exposed real vulnerability and connected external invasion to internal political instability; the third demonstrated Goryeo's growing capacity to defeat Khitan forces decisively once its military institutions had adapted to the threat. This progression, from negotiated settlement, through crisis, to decisive military victory, illustrates a broader pattern in Goryeo's frontier history: sustained conflict tended to produce institutional learning and adaptation rather than simple repetition.

Section 22.5

War, Fortifications, and Frontier Government

The cumulative experience of three Khitan invasions left permanent marks on Goryeo's institutions, well beyond the immediate military outcomes described above. Following the Battle of Gwiju, Goryeo undertook a sustained program of fortress construction and frontier settlement intended to prevent any future invasion from penetrating as deeply, or as destructively, as the 1010 campaign had done. The centerpiece of this program was the Cheolli Jangseong, or "Thousand-Li Wall," an extended defensive wall and fortress network constructed across the northern frontier over the following decades, garrisoned by troops drawn from the kingdom's provincial military organization described in Chapter 20 and supported by systems of supply, communication, and rotation designed to sustain a permanent frontier defense rather than relying solely on emergency mobilization. Alongside this northern wall system, Goryeo also strengthened the fortifications surrounding Gaegyeong itself, a direct response to the capital's capture and burning in 1010 and a recognition that frontier defense alone could not guarantee the safety of the kingdom's political center.

These fortifications were not simply defensive walls in the abstract sense; they were administrative and settlement projects as well. Establishing a permanent garrison required establishing a permanent population to support it, including farmers who supplied food, artisans who supplied equipment, and local administrators who coordinated the whole system. Northern frontier districts therefore developed a distinctive character within Goryeo, shaped more directly by military requirements than were the agricultural heartlands closer to Gaegyeong. Military officers and troops assigned to frontier duty accumulated direct, practical experience defending the kingdom under difficult and dangerous conditions, experience that stood in sharp contrast to the comparatively secure and prestigious careers available to civil officials serving in the capital.

This is the point at which the abstract institutional distinction between munban and muban, introduced in Chapter 20, becomes concrete rather than merely formal. The military officers responsible for Goryeo's frontier defense were not simply a ceremonial class within an aristocratic hierarchy; they were the men, and the troops under their command, who had defended the kingdom's independence during three major invasions and who continued to bear responsibility for its ongoing security along a genuinely dangerous frontier. Yet, as later sections of this chapter explain, this practical indispensability did not translate into political or social equality with the civil aristocracy that dominated the court in Gaegyeong. The tension between military necessity and military subordination, present already in Goryeo's founding institutions, deepened considerably as a direct consequence of the kingdom's eleventh-century wars.

Section 22.6

The Jurchens and Goryeo's Northeastern Frontier

While Goryeo's relationship with Liao dominated the eleventh century, a different and, in the long run, more consequential set of relationships was developing along the kingdom's northeastern frontier with various Jurchen communities. Students should resist assuming that "the Jurchens" formed a single unified people or state during this period. Jurchen communities in the eleventh century were organized into numerous distinct, often competing groups, some settled in agricultural communities, others more mobile, and their political relationships with neighboring powers, including Goryeo and Liao, varied considerably from group to group and shifted over time.

Some Jurchen communities maintained tributary or client relationships with Goryeo, acknowledging Goryeo authority in exchange for trade access and, at times, military protection against rival Jurchen groups or against Liao pressure. Others aligned more closely with Liao, which itself sought to organize Manchurian populations, including various Jurchen communities, within its own administrative structures. Still others remained largely independent, contesting territory and resources with neighboring groups without stable allegiance to any larger power. This fragmented landscape meant that Goryeo's northeastern frontier policy during the eleventh century involved constant, small-scale diplomatic and, at times, military engagement with multiple distinct Jurchen communities rather than a single consistent relationship with one Jurchen state.

This situation began to change significantly toward the end of the eleventh century, as a particular Jurchen lineage, the Wanyan clan, began to extend its authority over neighboring Jurchen groups through a combination of military pressure, alliance-building, and administrative consolidation. As Wanyan influence grew, encounters between Jurchen forces and Goryeo's northeastern frontier garrisons became more frequent and, in some cases, more serious than the localized skirmishes that had characterized earlier decades. These growing tensions, combined with a renewed sense within the Goryeo court that a more assertive northeastern frontier policy might extend the kingdom's territory and prestige, set the stage for the major military campaigns undertaken under Yun Gwan's leadership in the early twelfth century, examined in the next section.

Section 22.7

Yun Gwan, the Byeolmuban, and the Nine Fortresses

Concerned by growing Jurchen strength along the northeastern frontier and by a series of clashes in which existing Goryeo garrison forces performed poorly, the Goryeo court authorized the official Yun Gwan to organize a substantially reformed and enlarged military force capable of decisive campaigning against Jurchen communities in the region. The resulting force, known as the Byeolmuban, or "special military corps," was distinctive both for its scale and for its internal organization, which reportedly incorporated infantry, cavalry, and specialized units, including monk-soldiers, in a deliberate effort to overcome the tactical weaknesses that earlier Goryeo forces had displayed against mobile Jurchen cavalry.

Between 1107 and 1109, Yun Gwan led the Byeolmuban on a series of campaigns into Jurchen-held territory in the northeast, achieving significant initial success. Goryeo forces defeated Jurchen resistance across a substantial area and constructed a network of fortifications, traditionally enumerated as nine major fortresses, intended to secure permanent Goryeo control over the newly conquered territory. The construction of the Nine Fortresses represented, at least momentarily, one of the most significant territorial expansions in Goryeo's history and a striking demonstration of the kingdom's military capacity when organized and resourced for a specific, sustained campaign.

This success, however, proved difficult to sustain. The conquered territory lay at a considerable distance from Goryeo's core population centers, and maintaining garrisons, supply lines, and administrative control over the new fortresses placed a heavy and continuing burden on Goryeo resources. Jurchen communities in the surrounding region, meanwhile, continued to resist Goryeo control and petitioned repeatedly for the return of the territory, offering, according to later accounts, pledges of tributary loyalty in exchange for Goryeo's withdrawal. Faced with the practical costs of holding territory that offered limited ongoing strategic or economic benefit relative to the resources required to defend it, the Goryeo court ultimately decided to return the Nine Fortresses to Jurchen control within a few years of their construction.

Students should understand this outcome not as a simple failure but as a genuine strategic recalibration. Yun Gwan's campaigns demonstrated that Goryeo possessed the military capacity to defeat Jurchen forces and seize territory when it committed sufficient resources to the effort. The subsequent withdrawal demonstrated an equally important recognition: that holding distant frontier territory against sustained local resistance could cost more than it was worth, particularly when the territory's population remained hostile to Goryeo administration. This episode also illustrates a recurring theme in frontier history more generally, one that recurs across many of the empires examined throughout this textbook: the distinction between the capacity to conquer territory and the capacity to govern it sustainably.

Section 22.8

The Rise of Jin and Goryeo Diplomacy

The Jurchen communities that Goryeo had fought and then accommodated in the northeast did not remain fragmented for long. Under the leadership of the Wanyan lineage, whose growing regional authority had already been visible during Yun Gwan's campaigns, Jurchen groups achieved a far more extensive political unification in the early twelfth century. In 1115, Wanyan Aguda proclaimed the founding of the Jin dynasty, marking the emergence of a unified Jurchen state with imperial ambitions extending well beyond its Manchurian homeland.

Jin's rise proceeded with remarkable speed. Within a decade, Jin forces, in alliance for a time with Song China, moved decisively against the Khitan Liao dynasty, Goryeo's long-standing northern rival, culminating in Liao's destruction in 1125. Faced with this sudden collapse of the power that had dominated Goryeo's northern frontier for two centuries, and with Jin now demanding recognition of its own superior status, the Goryeo court accepted a formal tributary relationship with Jin in 1126, acknowledging Jin as the new dominant power in the region. Only in 1127, the year following Goryeo's accommodation, did Jin turn against its former Song ally and destroy the Northern Song regime, forcing the Song court to relocate south and continue as the Southern Song; this later development lies primarily within the history of China rather than Korea, but it confirmed the scale of the new imperial order to which Goryeo had already begun adjusting.

For Goryeo, Jin's sudden ascent required rapid diplomatic adjustment. The kingdom that had once accepted tributary status toward Liao, and that had fought Jurchen communities directly during Yun Gwan's campaigns barely a decade earlier, now faced a unified and vastly more powerful Jurchen empire on its northern border. Rather than attempting renewed military resistance against a power that had just destroyed Liao outright, the Goryeo court chose accommodation, establishing tributary relations with Jin in 1126 in a manner broadly analogous to its earlier relationship with Liao.

This diplomatic recalibration illustrates an important pattern in Goryeo's foreign relations more broadly: the kingdom's strategy toward its powerful continental neighbors rested less on any fixed ideological commitment to resistance or submission than on a pragmatic assessment of relative power and the costs and benefits of different diplomatic postures at a given moment. Goryeo resisted Liao militarily when resistance appeared achievable, negotiated with Liao when negotiation served its interests, expanded against Jurchen territory when opportunity and capacity aligned, and accommodated Jin once Jin's overwhelming power made resistance impractical. This adaptability, rather than any single fixed foreign policy, characterizes Goryeo's international conduct across the eleventh and twelfth centuries, and it stands as an important qualification to any narrative that would present Goryeo simply as a besieged victim of larger continental powers.

Section 22.9

Civil Officials and the Military Establishment

Having traced two centuries of frontier defense, intermittent warfare, and diplomacy, this chapter now turns inward to examine how that experience interacted with Goryeo's domestic political order. Chapter 20 described Goryeo's government as dominated by civil aristocratic families who staffed the State Council, the Censorate, and the upper reaches of the examination-based bureaucracy. That same chapter noted, without extended discussion, that Goryeo's formal ranking system placed military officials, or muban, in a structurally subordinate position relative to their civil counterparts, or munban, regardless of the two groups' respective contributions to the kingdom's actual survival.

This subordination took several concrete forms. Military officials were generally restricted from advancing beyond a certain rank ceiling, regardless of merit or achievement, while equivalent civil positions carried no such restriction. The most senior military commands were frequently held, at least in ceremonial or ultimate authority, by civil officials rather than by career military officers, a practice that placed men with little direct military experience in positions of authority over experienced frontier commanders. Patronage networks, marriage alliances, and access to royal favor, all of which shaped career advancement throughout Goryeo's aristocratic system, disproportionately benefited established civil aristocratic lineages, leaving military families with comparatively limited access to the kingdom's highest circles of power and prestige.

These inequalities might have remained a matter of chronic but manageable resentment had they not been compounded by direct, personal humiliation at court. Later Goryeo historical accounts describe repeated instances in which senior military officers were subjected to public disrespect by civil officials and by King Uijong's own courtiers, including incidents in which military officers were mocked, physically struck, or otherwise treated with open contempt during court gatherings and entertainments. Whatever the precise accuracy of individual anecdotes preserved in later sources, the broader pattern they describe, a military establishment that had spent generations defending the kingdom against Khitan and Jurchen forces while remaining subject to persistent institutional subordination and, at times, personal humiliation, is well supported by the structural evidence examined throughout this section.

Students should understand this section as building the structural foundation for the coup examined next, rather than as background to be set aside once the narrative reaches 1170. The coup did not result from a single insult, however dramatic later accounts may make particular incidents appear. It resulted from a long accumulation of institutional inequality, specifically the discrimination built into the muban rank ceiling, the placement of senior field commands in civil rather than military hands, restricted promotion, and the everyday grievances of lower-ranking soldiers, compounded by the immediate court politics of Uijong's reign, among a military establishment that had grown, over two centuries of frontier defense and intermittent warfare, increasingly confident of its own indispensability to the kingdom's survival.

Section 22.10

The Coup of 1170

By the reign of King Uijong, who ascended the throne in 1146, the structural tensions described in the preceding section had reached a critical point. Uijong's court became known for elaborate entertainments, extended excursions, and lavish patronage of Buddhist temples and literary gatherings, activities that consumed royal attention and resources while doing little to address the grievances accumulating within the military establishment. Civil officials close to the king continued to enjoy privileged access and influence, while military officers accompanying the king on his frequent excursions were, according to later accounts, repeatedly subjected to demeaning treatment even while serving in roles that placed them physically closest to royal danger.

In the autumn of 1170, during one such royal excursion, a group of senior military officers, most prominently Jeong Jung-bu, Yi Ui-bang, and Yi Go, organized a coordinated uprising against the king's civil retinue. According to later historical accounts, the immediate trigger involved a fresh incident of humiliation directed at a senior military officer during the excursion, though historians generally treat this incident as the spark that ignited already accumulated grievances rather than as a sufficient explanation for the coup's occurrence on its own. The conspirators moved swiftly and violently, killing large numbers of civil officials who had accompanied the king, seizing control of the capital, and ultimately removing King Uijong from the throne, exiling him and installing his brother, who took the throne as King Myeongjong, as a new monarch who would reign from 1170 to 1197, nearly three decades, with little practical authority of his own.

The scale and speed of the coup reflected careful organization among its military leadership as well as the depth of accumulated resentment among rank-and-file soldiers and officers who joined or supported the uprising once it began. Civil officials who had dominated Goryeo's government for over two centuries were killed, imprisoned, or driven from office in large numbers, and the formal institutions of civil aristocratic government, while not immediately abolished, lost their practical monopoly on political power virtually overnight.

Historians studying the coup of 1170 generally agree that it cannot be reduced to a single cause. The long-term structural inequality between munban and muban, examined in the previous section, created the underlying conditions that made a violent uprising conceivable and, eventually, achievable. The specific excesses of King Uijong's court and the immediate incidents of humiliation experienced by military officers during the fatal 1170 excursion provided the proximate trigger that transformed long-standing grievance into coordinated action. Both dimensions, structural and immediate, are necessary to explain not only why the coup occurred but why it occurred when it did and why it succeeded so completely once launched.

Section 22.11

From Coup to Military Government

The success of the 1170 coup did not immediately produce a stable new political order. Having overturned the civil aristocratic government, the military officers who had led the uprising now faced the considerably more difficult problem of governing the kingdom themselves, and of managing the ambitions of one another. The quarter-century following 1170 was marked by a rapid and often unstable succession of dominant military strongmen, each of whom held effective power for a period before death, overthrow, or displacement opened the way to another ruler within the same military establishment that had carried out the original coup. The clearest way to trace this succession is as a sequence of five figures: Yi Ui-bang, Jeong Jung-bu, Gyeong Dae-seung, Yi Ui-min, and finally Choe Chung-heon.

Yi Ui-bang, one of the coup's three principal leaders alongside Jeong Jung-bu and Yi Go, emerged as the dominant figure in the years immediately following 1170, using his position to eliminate rival officers, including Yi Go, and to consolidate personal control over the new regime, before he too was killed by opponents within the military establishment. Jeong Jung-bu, who had been the coup's most senior figure from the outset, then rose to dominant power, but his position likewise proved unstable, and he was eventually overthrown and killed by younger officers who accused him of failing to control abuses within the regime he headed. Gyeong Dae-seung subsequently rose to dominant power, ruling for a period marked by continuing instability and by his own efforts to establish more durable institutional mechanisms of control, including the creation of the Dobang private guard examined in the next section, before his death opened the way for further competition among military leaders. Yi Ui-min then achieved dominance for a time, continuing the pattern of concentrated but insecure military strongman rule that had characterized the period since 1170.

This unstable sequence illustrates an important point that students should not overlook: overturning an existing government is a fundamentally different task from constructing a durable replacement. The military officers who seized power in 1170 shared a common grievance against the civil aristocratic order, but they did not necessarily share a common vision for how Goryeo should be governed once that order had been overturned, nor did they possess institutional mechanisms comparable to the examination system, established bureaucratic offices, and aristocratic lineage networks that had underpinned civil aristocratic government for two centuries. In the absence of such mechanisms, personal loyalty, military force, and, frequently, assassination became the primary means by which power was contested and transferred among the kingdom's new military rulers, a pattern visible across the successive removals, deaths, and transfers of power among Yi Ui-bang, Jeong Jung-bu, and Gyeong Dae-seung in turn.

This period of instability finally gave way to a more durable arrangement in 1196, when Choe Chung-heon seized power from Yi Ui-min and began constructing the institutional foundations of what would become the longest-lasting and most systematically organized phase of Goryeo military government, examined in the following section.

Section 22.12

The Choe Military Regime and the Survival of the Monarchy

Choe Chung-heon's rise to power in 1196 initiated a regime that would dominate Goryeo politics for over six decades, under his successors, and extend Choe family control across four generations until 1258, with a broader system of military-dominated government continuing until 1270. What distinguished the Choe regime from the unstable strongman rule of the preceding quarter-century was Choe Chung-heon's construction of durable personal and institutional mechanisms for exercising power that his predecessors had lacked or had only begun to develop.

Goryeo's military rulers already possessed two existing institutions on which to build. The Jungbang, a council of senior military officers that had existed since the early Goryeo period, became under military rule an important instrument through which the ruling strongman coordinated policy and rewarded loyal subordinates. The Dobang, a private military retinue functioning as both personal bodyguard and instrument for suppressing rivals, had first been created in 1179 by Gyeong Dae-seung during his own period of dominance, but it lapsed after his death and did not survive the subsequent instability of Yi Ui-min's rule. Choe Chung-heon revived and substantially reorganized the Dobang around the year 1200, rebuilding it as a large, well-resourced force answerable directly to the Choe family rather than to the kingdom's formal military establishment.

Choe Chung-heon's most significant institutional innovation, however, was the creation of the Gyojeong Dogam in 1209, a new administrative body that functioned as the Choe regime's central organ of political control. Through the Gyojeong Dogam, the Choe rulers supervised appointments, tax administration, and a wide range of state affairs, effectively substituting a personal, Choe-controlled apparatus for the older civil aristocratic institutions, including the State Council examined in Chapter 20, without formally abolishing those institutions. The combination of the reorganized Dobang, providing coercive force, and the Gyojeong Dogam, providing administrative control, gave the Choe regime a durability that the strongman rule of Yi Ui-bang, Jeong Jung-bu, Gyeong Dae-seung, and Yi Ui-min had conspicuously lacked.

Perhaps the most historically significant feature of the Choe regime, and of Goryeo military government more broadly, was its decision to retain the Wang royal house on the throne rather than replacing it with a new dynasty. This choice presents an important institutional paradox that repays careful attention. Kings continued to occupy the throne, to receive formal deference, and to perform the ceremonial and ritual functions long associated with Goryeo kingship. Yet real political authority, including the appointment of officials, control over military forces, and ultimate decision-making on matters of state, rested with Choe Chung-heon and his successors, who governed through their own household administration and military establishment operating alongside, and effectively above, the formal structures of monarchy.

Several considerations likely explain this arrangement. Removing the Wang dynasty outright would have required the Choe family to establish an entirely new claim to legitimate rule, a far more difficult and destabilizing undertaking than governing through an existing, broadly accepted royal institution while controlling its practical function. The Wang monarchy also retained symbolic and ceremonial value, both domestically and in Goryeo's continuing tributary relations with Jin, that a usurping military family could exploit more easily by preserving than by discarding. Whatever the precise balance of these motivations, the practical result was a distinctive form of government in which formal sovereignty and practical power operated on separate, if intersecting, tracks, a pattern that would persist, with variations, through the remainder of the Choe regime and, in modified form, into the Mongol period examined in the next chapter.

Students should recognize this arrangement as the direct institutional payoff of the tensions traced throughout this chapter. Chapter 20 asked students to distinguish formal from practical authority within Goryeo's aristocratic government; the Choe regime demonstrates that same distinction in its most extreme and consequential form, with an entire military family exercising sustained practical sovereignty while a continuously reigning royal house supplied the kingdom's formal legitimacy.

Section 22.13

Social Rebellion and the Limits of Military Rule

The political upheaval traced in the preceding sections did not remain confined to the elite struggle between civil aristocrats and military officers. Beginning in the 1170s and continuing through the following decades, Goryeo experienced a wave of uprisings among peasants, local communities, and enslaved people that historians generally treat as evidence of deeper social and economic tensions extending well beyond, and predating, the immediate political crisis at court.

One of the earliest and most significant of these uprisings began in 1176 at Myeonghakso, a so under the district of Gongju, led by figures identified in later sources as Mangi and Mangso. So communities were a distinctive category within Goryeo's local administrative system, special districts whose inhabitants were required to supply particular goods, such as metals, ceramics, or other specialized products, to the state, and whose residents bore heavier obligations and lower social status than the populations of ordinary rural districts. This origin gives the uprising a specific institutional character beyond generic rural discontent: it began as a protest against status discrimination and burdens built directly into Goryeo's administrative structure, not simply against taxation and labor obligations in the abstract. The rebellion spread rapidly across much of the Chungcheong region before it was finally suppressed in 1177, drawing on long-standing grievances that had existed under the civil aristocratic government examined in Chapter 20 and that the political instability following the 1170 coup did not create but arguably provided new opportunities to express, as central authority weakened and local administrative control grew less certain.

A larger uprising erupted in 1193 in the southern provinces, associated with the leaders Kim Sami and Hyosim. This rebellion reportedly drew substantial popular support and required significant military effort to suppress, illustrating that rural discontent during this period was not confined to isolated local incidents but could, under the right conditions, mobilize populations across a considerable region. The scale of this uprising underscores a point historians increasingly emphasize: the instability of the late twelfth century reflected not merely a crisis among Goryeo's political elite but genuine strain within the kingdom's social and economic order, strain that had likely been building for some time before finding expression through rebellion.

Perhaps the most striking episode of this period occurred in 1198, when an enslaved man named Manjeok organized a conspiracy among Gaegyeong's slave population aimed, according to later accounts, at nothing less than overturning the institution of slavery itself and eliminating the status distinctions that subordinated enslaved people within Goryeo society. Though the conspiracy was discovered and suppressed before it could be carried out, its existence reveals that aspirations for fundamental social change extended into the very heart of the kingdom's unfree population, in the capital itself, during a period when the ruling military elite was itself demonstrating that established hierarchies could be challenged and overturned through organized action.

Students should consider carefully whether these rebellions are best understood as direct responses to military rule or as expressions of social and economic tensions that had accumulated over generations under the civil aristocratic order examined in earlier chapters, tensions that political instability at the center may have made easier, rather than more necessary, to express. Both interpretations carry some validity, and this chapter's Historical Debate feature, along with its Multiple Perspectives feature, invite students to weigh the evidence for each. What is clear is that the political transformation of 1170 did not occur within a socially static kingdom. It occurred within a Goryeo already carrying substantial unaddressed tension between elite privilege and popular grievance, tension that the coup and its aftermath did not create but that subsequent instability helped bring into open conflict.

Historical Vocabulary
Munban and Muban

Munban ("civil ranks") and muban ("military ranks") together formed Goryeo's dual aristocratic ranking system, introduced in Chapter 20. Officials in both tracks held formal rank within the same overall hierarchy of eighteen grades, but in practice the two tracks were far from equal. Civil officials dominated the State Council, the Censorate, and the most prestigious ceremonial and advisory positions, while military officials, regardless of accomplishment, faced informal and sometimes formal restrictions on how high they could advance, and the most senior nominal command over military affairs was frequently held by a civil official rather than a career soldier.

This distinction should not be confused with a simple division between "warriors" and "scholars." Many military officials came from established aristocratic families and possessed considerable education, and civil officials were not without their own responsibilities in matters of state security and frontier policy. The distinction was fundamentally one of institutional access and prestige, not of learning or social origin alone, and it was precisely this structural, institutionalized inequality, rather than any deeper cultural divide, that generated the resentment culminating in the coup of 1170.

Historical Vocabulary
Jungbang, Dobang, and Gyojeong Dogam

The Jungbang was a council of senior military commanders that had existed within Goryeo's formal military establishment since the dynasty's early history, originally serving as a coordinating body for military policy under the broader civil-dominated government. Following the coup of 1170, the Jungbang became, for a time, the central institution through which competing military strongmen exercised collective and, at times, contested authority.

The Dobang was a private military retinue distinct from the kingdom's formal military establishment. It was first created in 1179 by the strongman Gyeong Dae-seung as a personal guard, but it lapsed after his death and did not survive the following period of instability under Yi Ui-min. Choe Chung-heon revived and substantially reorganized the Dobang around 1200, rebuilding it as a large force answerable directly to the Choe family. In its Choe-era form, the Dobang functioned simultaneously as a personal bodyguard, an internal security force capable of suppressing rivals, and a mechanism for rewarding loyal followers with status and material benefit.

The Gyojeong Dogam, established by Choe Chung-heon in 1209, was a new administrative organ that became the Choe regime's principal instrument of political control, supervising appointments, taxation, and a wide range of state affairs. Together, the reorganized Dobang and the Gyojeong Dogam gave the Choe regime the combination of coercive force and administrative reach that had allowed it to achieve a stability earlier military strongmen, who lacked comparable institutions, had been unable to sustain.

Primary Source Window

Seo Hui's Negotiations with Xiao Sunning, 993

Creator: preserved in later Goryeo historical compilations describing the negotiations between Seo Hui and the Khitan commander Xiao Sunning · Date: events of 993, recorded in sources compiled later · Type: diplomatic negotiation as reconstructed in later Goryeo historiography

Context: Facing a Khitan invasion force under Xiao Sunning, and amid internal Goryeo court debate over whether to cede northern territory to secure peace, the official Seo Hui undertook direct negotiations with the Khitan commander rather than accepting the territorial concession some at court favored.

Paraphrase: The following is a paraphrase, not a direct quotation, of the negotiation's substance as later sources describe it.

Seo Hui is described as arguing that Goryeo, as heir to Goguryeo, held rightful claim to territory beyond its current holdings, and that the source of friction between Liao and Goryeo lay in Jurchen-held land separating the two states rather than in any Goryeo hostility toward Liao; he proposed that Goryeo's control of this intervening territory would benefit Liao by stabilizing the frontier and enabling more reliable diplomatic contact.

Analysis: This episode illustrates that diplomacy in this period rested on more than persuasive argument alone. Seo Hui's case succeeded because it offered Liao a plausible strategic benefit, stability along its eastern frontier, while being backed by a Goryeo military force capable of continued resistance if negotiations failed.

Limitations: The specific wording attributed to Seo Hui comes from later compilations rather than a contemporary transcript, and historians must treat the precise phrasing with appropriate caution while treating the broader outcome, Goryeo's acquisition of the Six Garrison Settlements, as well established.

Perspective: The account reflects a Goryeo court perspective celebrating a diplomatic achievement; no comparable Khitan account of the same negotiation survives in this textbook's available sources, limiting a fully two-sided reconstruction.

Historical Debate

Was the 1170 Coup a Class Conflict or an Elite Power Struggle?

The question: Should the coup of 1170 be understood primarily as a conflict between two categories of aristocratic elite, munban and muban, competing over status and access to power within a shared aristocratic system, or should it be understood as a broader social conflict reflecting deeper structural inequalities within Goryeo society as a whole?

Interpretation A — An elite power struggle: Some historians emphasize that both munban and muban officials belonged to Goryeo's aristocratic establishment, held formal rank, and possessed education and family status. On this reading, the coup represents a redistribution of power within the existing aristocratic order rather than a challenge to that order's fundamental social hierarchy, since the military officers who seized power did not attempt to dismantle Goryeo's status system, its landholding arrangements, or its treatment of commoners and enslaved people.
Interpretation B — A conflict with class dimensions: Other historians point out that ordinary soldiers, not only senior military officers, participated in and supported the coup, suggesting that resentment extended beyond a narrow elite rivalry into broader frustration with civil aristocratic dominance felt by common soldiers who bore the physical burden of frontier warfare. On this reading, the coup's aftermath, including the instability of the 1170s and 1180s and the subsequent wave of social rebellions examined in Section 22.13, reflects a genuine loosening of social control that extended well beyond the immediate rivalry between two aristocratic factions.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the same body of later Goryeo historical compilations describing the coup and its aftermath, but differ in how much weight to place on the participation of rank-and-file soldiers, on the coup leadership's subsequent behavior, and on the causal relationship between the coup and the social rebellions of the following decades.

Why it matters: How historians answer this question shapes whether the coup of 1170 is understood mainly as a turning point in elite Goryeo politics or as a broader marker of social transformation connecting the political crisis at court to the peasant, local, and slave uprisings examined later in this chapter.

Multiple Perspectives

Living Through Frontier War and the Coup of 1170

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns historians have identified across many sources. They are not quotations from any single named historical actor.

From the perspective of a civil aristocratic official at Gaegyeong before 1170: Government exists to be conducted by those trained in classical learning and refined conduct; military officers serve an essential but subordinate function, and their exclusion from the highest offices reflects a proper and long-established ordering of talents rather than an injustice requiring correction.
From the perspective of a Goryeo military officer who served on the northern frontier: Having spent years defending the kingdom's border against Khitan and Jurchen forces, watching civil officials monopolize promotion, prestige, and royal favor while facing public humiliation at court feels like a fundamental betrayal of the sacrifice frontier service requires.
From the perspective of a common soldier serving under a frontier garrison commander: Military campaigns and fortress construction demand years of hard labor and danger far from home, and the resentment senior officers express toward civil aristocratic privilege reflects grievances that ordinary soldiers share just as strongly, if with even less voice to express them.
From the perspective of a peasant farmer in a southern province during the 1170s and 1180s: Political upheaval among elites in Gaegyeong matters most when it weakens the local authorities who collect taxes and enforce labor obligations, and the instability following 1170 offers both new dangers and, for those willing to organize, new opportunities to resist long-standing burdens.
From the perspective of an enslaved person in Gaegyeong around 1198: Watching a military establishment overturn a government once considered permanent demonstrates that hierarchies presented as fixed and natural can, in fact, be challenged through organized action, a lesson that inspires hope for changing one's own status even against considerable danger.
Continuity and Change Over Time

Goryeo Government Before and After 1170

What Changed

  • Effective political authority shifted from a civil aristocracy staffing the State Council and Censorate to military strongmen exercising power through personal military retinues and the Jungbang
  • New institutions specific to military rule, including the Choe family's Dobang, emerged alongside, and increasingly above, Goryeo's older formal government structures
  • Large numbers of civil officials, particularly those closest to King Uijong, were killed or removed from office in the aftermath of 1170
  • Rural and unfree populations found new, if still dangerous, opportunities to organize open rebellion as central authority weakened during the unstable decades after the coup

What Stayed the Same

  • The Wang royal house continued to occupy the throne and to perform its ceremonial and symbolic functions throughout the period of military rule
  • Goryeo's formal governmental institutions, including the State Council and provincial administration, continued to exist, even as real power shifted around and above them
  • Goryeo's basic social hierarchy, including the status of enslaved people and commoners, remained formally intact despite the aspirations expressed in uprisings such as Manjeok's conspiracy
  • Goryeo continued to maintain tributary diplomatic relations with its dominant continental neighbor, shifting from Liao to Jin without fundamentally altering the underlying diplomatic posture of pragmatic accommodation

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both the depth of the 1170 transformation and the institutions that survived it prevents students from treating the coup as either a minor court incident or a complete revolution; it was, instead, a fundamental redistribution of practical power within a formal structure that persisted in modified form.

Cause and Effect

The Coup of 1170

Long-term causes

Two centuries of institutionalized inequality between munban and muban, including the muban rank ceiling, civil control of senior field commands, restricted promotion, and lower-soldier grievances, deepened by the practical burdens and dangers of frontier defense against Khitan and Jurchen forces without corresponding political or social advancement for military officers.

Short-term causes

King Uijong's court excesses, favoritism toward civil officials during royal excursions, and repeated public humiliations of senior military officers in the years and months before 1170.

Event

The coordinated 1170 uprising led by Jeong Jung-bu, Yi Ui-bang, and Yi Go, resulting in the killing of numerous civil officials, the removal of King Uijong, and the seizure of practical power by the military establishment.

Consequences

Two and a half decades of unstable military strongman rule, the eventual establishment of the durable Choe regime in 1196, and a wave of social rebellions among peasants and enslaved people that continued through the following decades.

Historical complexity: Neither the long-term structural inequality nor the immediate court excesses alone fully explains the coup; historians generally treat the two as jointly necessary, since similar structural inequality had persisted for generations without producing an uprising until an immediate crisis provided the opportunity and trigger for coordinated action.

Everyday Life and Social History

Soldiers, Farmers, and the Costs of Frontier Defense

The wars and political upheavals examined in this chapter were experienced most directly not by kings and generals but by the ordinary soldiers who garrisoned northern fortresses, the farmers whose labor obligations helped supply frontier defenses, and the rural and unfree populations whose lives were shaped by both distant elite politics and immediate local administration. Constructing and maintaining the fortress networks described in Section 22.5 required sustained labor from local populations conscripted for construction, transport, and supply duties, obligations that fell heavily on communities near the frontier regardless of whether active fighting was underway in a given year.

Common soldiers who served in campaigns against Khitan and Jurchen forces, and later in the Byeolmuban under Yun Gwan, faced years of dangerous service far from their home communities, with far less prospect of advancement, reward, or recognition than the senior officers whose grievances this chapter has examined in detail. When those senior officers rebelled in 1170, their frustration with civil aristocratic privilege resonated with common soldiers whose own burdens, while different in degree, arose from the same underlying system of civil-dominated, military-dependent government. The subsequent rebellions among peasants and enslaved people examined in Section 22.13 further remind students that Goryeo's late twelfth-century crisis was experienced across a wide social spectrum, from the royal court to fortress garrisons to rural villages to the unfree households of the capital itself, and that each of these groups drew different lessons and pursued different aims from the same period of upheaval.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced Goryeo's history across two and a half centuries of frontier defense, intermittent warfare, military mobilization, diplomacy, and internal political transformation, arguing that these two dimensions of the kingdom's experience were deeply interconnected rather than separate storylines. Goryeo's northern frontier along the Amnok River, inherited in contested form from the collapse of Balhae, placed the kingdom in direct and sustained contact with two of the most formidable powers in medieval Northeast Asia: the Khitan Liao empire and, later, the Jurchen-founded Jin dynasty.

Goryeo's relationship with Liao unfolded through a sequence of distinct episodes rather than a single undifferentiated conflict. Seo Hui's negotiations of 993 secured Goryeo's claim to the Six Garrison Settlements through diplomacy backed by credible military strength. The second Khitan invasion of 1010 exposed real vulnerability, resulting in the destruction of Gaegyeong and the flight of King Hyeonjong. The third invasion, defeated decisively by Gang Gam-chan at the Battle of Gwiju in 1019, ended the era of major Khitan incursions and prompted a sustained program of fortress construction and frontier settlement that transformed Goryeo's northern institutions permanently.

Goryeo's northeastern frontier presented a different, more fragmented challenge, as numerous distinct Jurchen communities maintained shifting and varied relationships with both Goryeo and Liao. Yun Gwan's campaigns of 1107 to 1109, conducted through the specially organized Byeolmuban, achieved significant territorial expansion through the construction of the Nine Fortresses, but the practical costs of holding distant frontier territory against continued local resistance led Goryeo to return the fortresses within a few years, illustrating the important distinction between conquering territory and governing it sustainably.

The subsequent unification of Jurchen communities under Wanyan leadership and the founding of the Jin dynasty in 1115 transformed the regional balance of power. Jin's destruction of Liao in 1125 removed Goryeo's long-standing rival and required a rapid diplomatic accommodation, as Goryeo shifted its tributary relationship from Liao to the newly dominant Jin, demonstrating the kingdom's characteristic pragmatism in adjusting to changing continental power balances rather than adhering to any single fixed foreign policy.

This sustained frontier engagement left a lasting mark on Goryeo's domestic political order. The formal distinction between civil officials, or munban, and military officials, or muban, examined initially in Chapter 20, deepened into serious structural grievance as generations of military officers bore the practical burden of frontier defense while remaining excluded from the highest offices, promotion opportunities, and royal favor monopolized by the civil aristocracy. This structural inequality, compounded by the specific excesses and humiliations associated with King Uijong's court, produced the coordinated military coup of 1170, led by Jeong Jung-bu, Yi Ui-bang, and Yi Go, which overturned two centuries of civil aristocratic dominance in a single violent episode.

The coup did not immediately produce stable government. The quarter-century between 1170 and 1196 witnessed a violent and unstable succession of military strongmen, running from Yi Ui-bang through Jeong Jung-bu, Gyeong Dae-seung, and Yi Ui-min, each holding power for a period before death, overthrow, or displacement opened the way to another ruler. Stability finally emerged in 1196 under Choe Chung-heon, whose regime developed durable institutional mechanisms, including the revived and reorganized Dobang retinue, continued use of the Jungbang council, and the newly created Gyojeong Dogam, to sustain military dominance across four generations of Choe family rule.

The Choe regime's most historically significant feature was its preservation of the Wang monarchy as a formal institution even while exercising practical sovereignty independently of it, an arrangement that allowed the ruling military family to claim legitimacy through an established royal house rather than constructing an entirely new basis for authority. This distinction between formal and practical power, introduced conceptually in earlier chapters, reached its most extreme and consequential expression in the Choe military government.

Finally, this chapter has emphasized that the political crisis of 1170 and its aftermath unfolded alongside, and interacted with, broader social tensions extending well beyond elite politics. Uprisings led by Mangi and Mangso in Gongju during the 1170s, by Kim Sami and Hyosim in the southern provinces in 1193, and by Manjeok among Gaegyeong's enslaved population in 1198 reveal that Goryeo's late twelfth-century instability reflected deep and long-accumulating social and economic grievances among peasants, local communities, and enslaved people, grievances that predated the coup but that found new, if still dangerous, opportunities for expression as central authority weakened.

By the close of this chapter, Goryeo has become a kingdom governed in practice by a military family operating through its own private institutions while a continuously reigning royal house supplies formal legitimacy, a kingdom whose frontiers have been secured through decades of costly warfare and diplomacy, and a kingdom whose social order has been visibly shaken by rebellion from below even as its political order has been transformed from above. This is the Goryeo that the Mongol invasions, examined in the next chapter, would soon confront.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has shown that Goryeo's history cannot be divided neatly into a story of foreign wars and a separate story of internal politics. The two were deeply intertwined. Two and a half centuries of frontier defense, intermittent warfare, and diplomacy involving the Khitan Liao empire and, later, Jurchen and Jin power built a Goryeo military establishment that was militarily indispensable to the kingdom's survival while remaining politically and socially subordinate, through the muban rank ceiling and civil control of senior command, within a civil aristocratic order that Chapter 20 first introduced. That contradiction, sustained across generations, eventually broke open in the coup of 1170, transforming Goryeo's government far more fundamentally than any single battle or treaty examined earlier in this chapter.

Yet this chapter has also resisted a simple narrative of military triumph followed by stable military rule. The unstable years between 1170 and 1196 demonstrate that seizing power and governing effectively are different achievements, and the eventual stability of the Choe regime rested on specific institutional innovations, including the Dobang, rather than on the coup's violence alone. The regime's decision to preserve the Wang monarchy while exercising practical sovereignty independently of it reveals a sophisticated, if unusual, solution to the problem of political legitimacy, one with echoes in other periods and other societies where formal and practical authority have diverged.

Finally, this chapter's attention to social rebellion, from Mangi and Mangso to Kim Sami and Hyosim to Manjeok, insists that the history of elite political transformation cannot stand alone. Goryeo's peasants, local communities, and enslaved population carried grievances of their own, grievances that predated 1170 but that found new expression once the old order's apparent permanence had been shattered by the military establishment's own example. Students should carry this lesson forward into the following chapters: political crisis at the center of a kingdom rarely remains contained there, and the social consequences of upheaval often extend far beyond the immediate participants in an elite power struggle.

The Goryeo that emerges at the end of this chapter, ruled in practice by the Choe military family, formally headed by a subordinated Wang king, its northern frontier secured through generations of costly adaptation, and its social order visibly strained by rebellion, is the kingdom that the Mongol Empire would soon confront. Understanding this internal condition, as much as understanding Mongol military power itself, is essential to understanding why the crisis examined in the next chapter unfolded as it did.
