The Fall of Silla and the Later Three Kingdoms

Korean History: Ancient Kingdoms to a Divided Peninsula

Chapter Opening

In the year 889, according to the Samguk Sagi, provinces and districts across the kingdom had fallen widely behind on tribute and taxes owed to the Silla court. When the king dispatched officials to press for payment, the response was not renewed compliance but spreading disorder, and among the rebel leaders who emerged from that disorder, two, named Wonjong and Aeno, established an armed base at Sabeol-ju. Within a generation, the kingdom that had once claimed authority over much of the Korean peninsula found that authority contested by rebels, provincial strongmen, and, eventually, rival kings who ruled from their own courts under their own dynastic names.

By the middle of the ninth century, Unified Silla had governed, in some meaningful sense, most of the Korean peninsula south of Balhae's frontier for roughly two centuries. Chapter 14 examined the institutions through which Silla kings and their aristocracy had organized that government: a central bureaucracy staffed overwhelmingly by the bone-rank aristocracy, a system of provincial administration reaching outward from Gyeongju, land and tax arrangements that supported the court and its officials, and a military structure capable, for a considerable period, of defending the kingdom's borders and suppressing internal disorder. Chapter 15 showed how that same court and aristocracy patronized an extraordinary Buddhist cultural world, and Chapter 17 followed Silla's connections outward across the Yellow Sea and the Korea Strait, culminating in the career of Jang Bogo, a man who, after receiving royal authorization in 828 to establish a maritime garrison at Cheonghaejin, built commercial and military resources there substantial enough to make him a figure the court came to regard with unease. According to the traditional accounts, Jang Bogo was assassinated, in a court-connected plot, in either 841 or 846 depending on the source, and Cheonghaejin itself was not formally abolished until 851, when its remaining population was relocated inland to Byeokgol-gun.

This chapter explains what happened after that. It is not a chapter about a single dramatic collapse, a foreign conquest, or one catastrophic battle that ended Silla rule overnight. It is a chapter about a slower and more complicated unraveling: a royal court whose control over succession, over the provinces, and over its own revenue weakened across several decades; an aristocracy whose internal privileges increasingly worked against the kingdom's capacity to adapt; a countryside where tax burdens and local grievance produced rebellion; and a series of provincial military leaders, some of aristocratic background and some not, who converted local power into territorial states of their own. Two of those leaders, Gyeon Hwon in the southwest and Gung Ye in the north-central peninsula, built polities substantial enough that later historians would describe the resulting political landscape, alongside a diminished Silla, as a second "Three Kingdoms" period, distinct from the Three Kingdoms of Goguryeo, Baekje, and Silla examined in Part II.

The chapter also has to account for a development north of the peninsula proper. Balhae, the state examined in Chapter 16 as Silla's northern counterpart for over two centuries, did not survive the disorder of this period either. Its conquest by the Khitan Liao state in 926 sent refugees, including members of its own royal house, southward into the newly forming Goryeo state, a development with lasting consequences for how the emerging Korean state understood its own territorial and historical claims.

Out of this fragmentation emerged Wang Geon, a man whose family's wealth rested on regional landholding and maritime trade around the port town of Songak, in a pattern that should recall Jang Bogo's earlier career. Wang Geon rose through military service under Gung Ye, gained a substantial following among fellow commanders, and in 918 was elevated to the throne after Gung Ye's own commanders turned against him. Wang Geon named his new state Goryeo, a revival of the old name of Goguryeo, and from this moment the peninsula's later reunification, examined fully in Chapter 19, becomes possible.

This chapter deliberately stops short of that reunification. Its task is to explain how the political order created by Unified Silla broke apart, and how the pieces of that broken order supplied the raw material, socially, militarily, and ideologically, for the states that would compete to replace it. Understanding this process requires resisting a simple narrative in which "Silla declined" and "Goryeo rose" as though these were two unrelated events separated by an empty interval. The men who built Later Baekje, Later Goguryeo, and eventually Goryeo did not step onto an empty stage from outside Silla society. They were products of it: provincial elites, military officers, and, in Gyeon Hwon's case, a soldier who had served in the Silla army itself before turning his forces toward rebellion.

By the chapter's end, the Korean peninsula in the year 918 will look very different from the peninsula Chapter 14 described two centuries earlier. Silla still existed, reduced to a fraction of its former territory and prestige. Later Baekje controlled substantial resources in the southwest. A new state calling itself Goryeo had just been proclaimed in the north-central peninsula, its future still genuinely uncertain. Understanding how the peninsula reached this point of open, multi-sided competition is essential background for Chapter 19, which follows Wang Geon's transformation from a newly proclaimed king into the ruler of a unified peninsula.

Learning Objectives

  2. Analyze how the bone-rank aristocratic system, once a source of Silla's institutional stability, increasingly constrained the kingdom's capacity for political adaptation.
  3. Describe the emergence of provincial strongmen, or hojok, and explain how regional military and landed power came to rival the authority of the Gyeongju court.
  4. Trace the causes and course of major late-ninth-century rebellions, including the uprising of 889, and evaluate their significance for Silla's central authority.
  5. Compare the rise of Gyeon Hwon and Later Baekje with the rise of Gung Ye and Later Goguryeo, including their use of earlier kingdoms' historical memory.
  6. Distinguish the Later Three Kingdoms period from the earlier Three Kingdoms period, explaining both similarities and important differences.
  7. Explain the circumstances of Balhae's conquest by the Khitan Liao state in 926 and assess the significance of the resulting refugee movements for the emerging Goryeo state.
  8. Trace Wang Geon's regional, commercial, and military background and connect it to broader patterns of provincial power examined in Chapter 17.
  9. Explain the circumstances under which Gung Ye was overthrown in 918 and Wang Geon proclaimed the founding ruler of Goryeo.

  12. Identify continuities between late Silla provincial society and the political order that emerged from its collapse.

Historical Context

Chapter 14 described the governing institutions of Unified Silla: a monarchy drawn from the sacred bone and later true bone lineages, a central bureaucracy staffed by the bone-rank aristocracy, and a network of provincial administration through which the court attempted to extract revenue, raise soldiers, and enforce law across a kingdom that, after 668, encompassed most of the peninsula south of the line eventually contested with Balhae. That system depended on the court's continued ability to command resources from the provinces and on a rough consensus among the aristocracy about how power and privilege should be distributed.

Chapter 15 examined the Buddhist cultural achievements this system supported, from Bulguksa and Seokguram to the scholarly and artistic patronage of the eighth and early ninth centuries, achievements that depended on a functioning court treasury and settled aristocratic patronage. Chapter 17 then turned outward, following Silla's maritime and diplomatic connections to Tang China and Japan, and gave particular attention to Jang Bogo, whose royally sanctioned base at Cheonghaejin, established in 828, grew during the 820s and 830s into a center of substantial military and commercial power, demonstrating that a man of relatively modest official rank could accumulate resources rivaling those of the established aristocracy so long as he controlled assets the court itself needed, in his case control over piracy suppression and maritime trade along the southwestern coast. According to the traditional accounts, Jang Bogo was assassinated amid court intrigue over his growing influence, in either 841 or 846 depending on the source, and Cheonghaejin itself continued to exist for some years afterward before its formal abolition in 851. His death removed one powerful regional figure. It did not remove the underlying conditions that had allowed such figures to emerge.

This chapter picks up the story from roughly the mid-ninth century, several decades after Jang Bogo's death, and follows Silla's political order through its fragmentation into competing regional powers and, ultimately, into the multi-state landscape known to later historians as the Later Three Kingdoms period. It also incorporates a second thread, established as essential in the Authoritative Chapter Outline for this Part: the fall of Balhae, examined in Chapter 16 as a major northern and Manchurian state that had coexisted with Silla for over two centuries. Balhae's conquest by the Khitan-led Liao state in 926 occurred within a few years of the political transformations unfolding on the peninsula, and the resulting flow of refugees southward, including members of Balhae's own royal family, became entangled with the newly founded Goryeo state's own claims to represent a broader northern inheritance.

Readers should keep in view that the developments traced in this chapter unfolded over roughly half a century, from the visible weakening of royal authority in the 870s and 880s through Wang Geon's accession in 918, and that Goryeo's eventual reunification of the peninsula, examined in Chapter 19, still lay nearly two decades beyond that founding moment. The Silla court did not disappear at any single point in this chapter; a much-reduced Silla state continued to exist, its final king submitting to Goryeo only in 935, immediately before Chapter 19's narrative of unification concludes.

Section 18.1

Late Silla after the Mid-Ninth Century

The Silla examined in Chapter 14 had never been a perfectly centralized state, but for much of the seventh and eighth centuries its royal court exercised a level of authority over provincial administration, taxation, and military recruitment sufficient to sustain the cultural and religious flowering described in Chapter 15 and the maritime engagement described in Chapter 17. By the middle decades of the ninth century, however, that authority had begun to weaken in ways that later chronicles, however imperfectly, record with some consistency.

The clearest symptom was instability at the very top of the political order. Succession to the Silla throne, which had generally passed within a relatively narrow set of royal lineages, became increasingly contested during the ninth century, with reigns growing shorter and transitions more frequently accompanied by factional conflict, and in some cases outright violence, among rival branches of the royal and near-royal aristocracy. A court preoccupied with managing, or surviving, succession disputes had correspondingly less capacity to attend to provincial administration, tax collection, or the maintenance of military forces outside the capital region.

This weakening did not mean that the Gyeongju court ceased to function, or that Silla institutions vanished overnight. Court offices, ritual functions, and much of the outward apparatus of Silla government persisted for decades after the problems described in this section became visible. What changed was the court's practical reach: its ability to compel obedience, extract resources, and enforce decisions in provinces at increasing distance from the capital diminished steadily, even as the formal structure of Silla kingship endured. This distinction between institutional survival and practical authority is essential for understanding the slow, uneven character of Silla's fragmentation, rather than imagining a single moment of collapse.

Section 18.2

The Politics of the Bone-Rank Aristocracy

Chapter 14 explained how the bone-rank system distributed political office, social privilege, and access to the throne according to inherited status, with the sacred bone and true bone lineages monopolizing the highest offices and the kingship itself. This system had provided a stable framework for allocating power during Silla's period of consolidation and expansion. By the ninth century, that same framework increasingly worked against the kingdom's ability to respond to new pressures.

Because political office and privilege were distributed according to inherited status rather than according to demonstrated capacity or shifting political need, the bone-rank aristocracy had few institutional mechanisms for incorporating new sources of power, whether military, commercial, or regional, into the existing order. Individuals or families who accumulated wealth or military strength outside the traditional aristocratic hierarchy, as Jang Bogo had done in the 820s and 830s, found few avenues for converting that strength into recognized political standing within the court system. According to the traditional accounts, Jang Bogo was ultimately killed amid court intrigue over his growing influence rather than being absorbed into a durable position of aristocratic authority, an outcome that, if the traditional attribution is accepted, illustrates a broader pattern: the bone-rank order tended to treat outside sources of power as threats to be eliminated rather than as resources to be incorporated.

At the same time, competition within the aristocracy itself intensified. As succession disputes multiplied, rival aristocratic factions increasingly treated control of the throne, and the offices and revenues that came with it, as prizes to be seized through court intrigue rather than as a shared inheritance to be administered collectively. This intensifying internal competition further reduced the court's capacity for coordinated governance precisely when external pressures, discussed in the following sections, were mounting.

Historians caution against reducing Silla's fragmentation to the bone-rank system alone. The system had functioned for centuries without producing kingdom-wide collapse, and other factors, including fiscal pressure, regional economic change, and the accumulated consequences of earlier military and diplomatic decisions, contributed as well. What can be said with more confidence is that the bone-rank system, whatever its earlier advantages, offered the late ninth-century court few tools for adaptation at the moment adaptation was most needed.

Section 18.3

Provincial Society and the Rise of Local Power

As the Gyeongju court's practical authority receded from the provinces, power did not simply disappear from those regions; it relocated to new holders. Historians commonly use the term hojok, meaning roughly "powerful families" or "strongmen," to describe the diverse array of provincial figures who accumulated regional authority during this period. The term covers a genuinely varied group: local landed families who had long possessed wealth and social standing but had previously deferred to central authority; military officers, sometimes former Silla officials, who commanded soldiers originally raised for provincial defense or tax enforcement; leaders of local self-defense forces organized in response to banditry and disorder; and, as discussed in the following section, leaders who emerged directly from rebellion.

What united these figures was not a shared social origin but a shared capacity: each controlled, through landholding, military force, commercial wealth, or some combination of these, resources sufficient to establish effective authority within a local or regional area independent of consistent direction from Gyeongju. Some hojok initially continued to acknowledge Silla's nominal sovereignty even while acting with substantial practical independence. Others moved more quickly toward open autonomy or alliance with rival power centers. Still others, as the following sections describe, eventually attached themselves to the larger political projects of Gyeon Hwon, Gung Ye, or Wang Geon, exchanging local autonomy for a role within a larger emerging state.

This shift from centralized to regional power should be understood as the central structural transformation of the period covered by this chapter. It did not happen instantaneously, and it did not happen uniformly across the peninsula; some regions retained stronger ties to Gyeongju longer than others, and the pace of change varied with local geography, economic resources, and the presence or absence of strong local leadership. But by the last decade of the ninth century, the assumption that political authority radiated outward from a single royal capital, an assumption that had underpinned Silla governance for over two centuries, no longer described the peninsula's political reality in most regions.

Historical Vocabulary
Hojok and the New Provincial Elite

Definition: Hojok is a modern historiographical term, not a title used systematically by the historical actors themselves, applied to the diverse landed, military, and commercial elites who accumulated regional power in the Korean peninsula during the late ninth and early tenth centuries as central Silla authority weakened.

Historical context: Hojok emerged from several overlapping backgrounds. Some were established local landholding families whose wealth predated the crisis but whose political independence grew as central oversight weakened. Others were military commanders who had once served Silla but redirected their forces toward local or regional ends. Still others rose through participation in rebellion or through organizing local defense against banditry and disorder.

Why it matters: The hojok phenomenon explains how Silla's fragmentation translated into concrete political structures rather than mere disorder. Regional power bases built by hojok became the building blocks from which Gyeon Hwon, Gung Ye, and eventually Wang Geon constructed larger states, whether by winning hojok support through alliance and marriage or by defeating and absorbing rival hojok territories.

Potential misunderstanding: Students should avoid imagining hojok as a single unified class with common political goals. They were a heterogeneous group whose members frequently competed with one another, and their eventual incorporation into Goryeo, examined fully in Chapter 19, occurred gradually and through varied arrangements rather than through uniform conquest.

Section 18.4

Taxation, Rural Distress, and Peasant Rebellion

The weakening of central authority described in the preceding sections had a direct fiscal dimension. Silla's government, like most premodern states, depended on taxation and corvée labor drawn from the agricultural population to support the court, the bureaucracy, and the military. As court factionalism intensified and provincial oversight loosened, the administration of taxation appears, on the evidence of later chronicles, to have become simultaneously less effective at generating revenue for the central government and, in various localities, more burdensome or more erratically enforced upon the rural population, whether through the continued demands of local officials attempting to meet quotas, the exactions of emerging local strongmen, or both.

This combination, a weakening center still attempting to extract resources alongside a countryside experiencing intensified local pressure, created conditions favorable to rural unrest. The best documented episode is the crisis of 889, recorded in the Samguk Sagi. According to that chronicle, provinces and districts throughout the kingdom had fallen widely behind on tribute and tax payments; when the king sent officials to enforce collection, the result was not renewed compliance but the spread of banditry and armed rebellion across many regions. Among the rebel leaders who emerged from this general disorder, two recorded by name, Wonjong and Aeno, established themselves at Sabeol-ju. The chronicle's account therefore describes a kingdom-wide breakdown in fiscal compliance from which the Sabeol-ju rebellion emerged as one prominent instance, rather than a single localized incident that then spread outward.

Historians treat these rebellions primarily as evidence of the Silla state's declining capacity rather than as a simple story of a downtrodden peasantry rising up against oppression in the abstract. The rebellions demonstrate that the court could no longer reliably enforce its fiscal demands, that local grievances no longer found effective redress through ordinary administrative channels, and that armed resistance had become a viable option for populations who judged the risks of rebellion preferable to continued extraction under deteriorating conditions. At the same time, the sources available to historians were written by and for the literate aristocratic elite, and they tend to describe rebellion primarily as disorder to be suppressed rather than to explore, in the rebels' own terms, the specific grievances that motivated participants. This is an important evidentiary limitation, discussed further in this chapter's Primary Source Window.

The immediate military suppression of the 889 uprising, where it succeeded at all, did not restore the underlying conditions that had produced it. Rebellion, once demonstrated as possible, provided both a model and, in some cases, a direct route by which ambitious local leaders could build armed followings, a development examined in the next section.

Section 18.5

From Rebellion to Regional Warlordism

The rebellions of the 880s and 890s did not simply burn out. In numerous cases, rebel leaders who had assembled armed followings during periods of unrest retained those followings afterward, converting temporary insurgent bands into more durable regional military forces. Other hojok, who had not necessarily participated directly in rebellion, recognized in the general breakdown of central authority an opportunity to expand their own local power through similar means: recruiting soldiers, fortifying settlements, and asserting control over surrounding territory and its resources.

This period therefore saw a proliferation of what historians describe as regional military entrepreneurship. Successful local leaders built personal armed followings loyal primarily to themselves rather than to the Silla state, financed those followings through control of local taxation, trade, or plunder, and used them to expand territorial control at the expense of both weaker neighbors and, where possible, the diminishing reach of Gyeongju itself. Alliances among these regional leaders were frequently unstable, shifting according to immediate advantage, and outright warfare among rival provincial forces became increasingly common as the ninth century gave way to the tenth.

Out of this volatile landscape of competing regional military leaders, two figures eventually distinguished themselves by their capacity to consolidate power across substantially larger territories than their rivals, and by their decision to formalize that power through the explicit revival of earlier kingdoms' names and historical legacies. The following two sections examine each of these figures, Gyeon Hwon and Gung Ye, in turn.

Section 18.6

Gyeon Hwon and Later Baekje

Gyeon Hwon, according to the surviving accounts, began his career as a soldier in Silla's own military, serving in garrison duty along the southwestern coast, a region historically associated with the territory of the earlier kingdom of Baekje, examined in Part II. Amid the disorder of the 890s, Gyeon Hwon turned his military position toward independent ends, gathering a following of soldiers and gradually asserting control over territory in the southwestern peninsula, an area with substantial agricultural wealth and access to maritime trade routes across the Yellow Sea, connections already familiar to students from Chapter 17's discussion of Baekje's earlier maritime relationships.

By the year 900, Gyeon Hwon judged his position secure enough to declare the founding of a new state, which he named Later Baekje (Hu Baekje), explicitly invoking the memory of the kingdom destroyed by the Silla-Tang alliance more than two centuries earlier, examined in Chapter 13. This choice of name was a deliberate political act. By claiming continuity with Baekje, Gyeon Hwon appealed to regional historical memory and, potentially, to lingering resentment in the southwestern peninsula toward the Silla state that had conquered the earlier kingdom, while also asserting a legitimacy for his new state that a purely personal military claim to power could not provide on its own.

Later Baekje developed into a substantial and durable power under Gyeon Hwon's leadership, controlling significant territory, agricultural resources, and military manpower in the southwestern peninsula for several decades. Gyeon Hwon pursued an active military and diplomatic policy, at times attacking Silla directly, including a notorious campaign against the Silla capital itself in the 920s that resulted in the death of a Silla king, and at other times contesting territory and influence with the rising power of Gung Ye's state and, later, Wang Geon's Goryeo. Later Baekje also maintained diplomatic contact with continental powers, seeking recognition and support in ways that paralleled the diplomatic practices of the original Three Kingdoms examined in Chapter 12.

Students should resist treating Later Baekje as merely an extended rebellion against Silla. By any reasonable measure, it functioned as an independent state: it controlled a defined territory, commanded organized military forces, conducted its own foreign relations, and endured for decades rather than collapsing quickly. Its eventual fate, discussed further in Chapter 19, involved internal succession conflict within Gyeon Hwon's own family as much as external defeat, a reminder that the same dynamics of factional competition that had undermined Silla could just as easily afflict its successor states.

Section 18.7

Gung Ye and Later Goguryeo

Gung Ye's background is considerably more difficult for historians to reconstruct with confidence than Gyeon Hwon's, since later chronicles describing his early life are fragmentary and, in places, colored by hostile retrospective judgment written after his overthrow. According to these accounts, Gung Ye had connections, contested and perhaps partly legendary, to the Silla royal house, and spent part of his early life as a Buddhist monk before turning to a military and political career amid the disorder of the 890s. Whatever the precise truth of his origins, Gung Ye demonstrated substantial military and organizational ability, building an armed following in the north-central peninsula and steadily expanding his territorial control through the 890s.

In 901, Gung Ye declared the founding of a new state, which he named Goryeo, explicitly invoking the memory of Goguryeo, the northern kingdom examined extensively in Part II and Part III of this volume, and presenting himself as an avenger of that kingdom's destruction at Silla and Tang hands in the seventh century, examined in Chapter 13. Historians commonly refer to this state as Later Goguryeo (Hu Goguryeo) to distinguish it from the Goguryeo of the earlier Three Kingdoms period and from the later Goryeo dynasty Wang Geon would found under the same name in 918, but "Goryeo" was the name Gung Ye himself actually used at its founding. This choice, like Gyeon Hwon's invocation of Baekje, functioned as a claim to historical legitimacy grounded in regional memory and resentment toward Silla, though Gung Ye's own connection to the historical Goguryeo royal house, unlike his territorial base in formerly Goguryeo-associated regions, rested on considerably less secure ground.

Gung Ye's state underwent a series of name changes and ideological transformations over the following years. He renamed his state Majin in 904, and later Taebong in 911, while relocating his capital to Cheorwon, a strategically positioned site in the north-central peninsula. Later chronicles describe Gung Ye increasingly adopting elaborate Buddhist religious claims about his own identity, presenting himself in terms that verged on divine kingship, alongside increasingly harsh and unpredictable treatment of his own officials and commanders, including, according to these accounts, executions carried out on the basis of suspicion generated by Gung Ye's claimed ability to read others' hidden thoughts.

Historians reading these later accounts must exercise caution, since chronicles compiled under Goryeo, the state founded by Gung Ye's successor, had an obvious interest in portraying the ruler Wang Geon replaced as erratic and illegitimate, providing retrospective justification for his overthrow. Whatever the precise accuracy of the specific episodes recorded, the broader pattern, growing autocracy and declining trust between Gung Ye and his leading commanders, appears consistent enough across the available evidence to be treated as a genuine feature of his later reign, and it directly prepares the events examined in Section 18.11.

Section 18.8

The Later Three Kingdoms

By the first decade of the tenth century, the Korean peninsula's political map had reorganized into three principal centers of power: a much-diminished Silla, still ruling from Gyeongju but controlling only a fraction of its former territory and prestige; Gyeon Hwon's Later Baekje in the southwest; and Gung Ye's northern state, known successively as Later Goguryeo, Majin, and Taebong, centered eventually at Cheorwon. Later historians, writing well after these events, applied the label Later Three Kingdoms to this configuration, drawing a deliberate parallel to the earlier Three Kingdoms period of Goguryeo, Baekje, and Silla examined in Part II.

This label is useful for organizing the period, but students should treat it with the same caution this textbook has applied to other retrospective historical categories, discussed in Chapter 1's treatment of periodization and anachronism. The Later Three Kingdoms were not a simple restoration of the earlier Three Kingdoms. None of the three states of this later period governed anything resembling the same institutions, territory, or population as its namesake predecessor centuries earlier. Later Baekje and Later Goguryeo were new political creations, built by military leaders who had risen through the disorder of late Silla society, and their invocation of earlier kingdoms' names functioned primarily as a claim to historical legitimacy and a tool for mobilizing regional loyalty rather than as evidence of genuine institutional continuity with the seventh-century kingdoms whose names they borrowed.

At the same time, the parallel was not entirely without substance. Both Gyeon Hwon and Gung Ye built their territorial bases substantially within the geographic regions historically associated with Baekje and Goguryeo respectively, and both appear to have calculated that regional historical memory, whatever its precise content by this period, remained a politically useful resource. The Later Three Kingdoms period therefore illustrates a recurring theme in this textbook's treatment of Korean history: the political uses of historical memory, and the ways successive states have selectively invoked earlier kingdoms to serve contemporary political purposes, a theme that will recur when this textbook examines modern Korean nationalism in later volumes.

Historical Vocabulary
"Later Three Kingdoms"

Definition: A retrospective historiographical label applied to the period from roughly 900 to 936, when a much-reduced Silla, Gyeon Hwon's Later Baekje, and Gung Ye's Goryeo/Later Goguryeo (later renamed Majin and Taebong) competed as the three principal centers of power on the peninsula.

Historical context: Neither Gyeon Hwon nor Gung Ye used the label "Later Three Kingdoms" themselves; the term was applied afterward, by analogy with the earlier Three Kingdoms period of Goguryeo, Baekje, and Silla examined in Part II, to describe the three-way competition their states created alongside a surviving Silla.

Why it matters: The label helps organize a genuinely complex period of multi-state competition and highlights the deliberate way Gyeon Hwon and Gung Ye each invoked an earlier kingdom's name and memory to claim political legitimacy.

Potential misunderstanding: Students should not read "Later Three Kingdoms" as evidence that the earlier kingdoms were somehow restored or reconstituted. Later Baekje and Gung Ye's state were new political creations built from late Silla's own social and military materials; they borrowed names and historical memory rather than reconstructing the institutions, territory, or population of their seventh-century namesakes.

Section 18.9

The Fall of Balhae and Northern Refugee Movements

While the peninsula's political order was reorganizing into the Later Three Kingdoms configuration described above, an entirely separate crisis, unrelated in its causes to Silla's internal fragmentation, was unfolding to the north. Chapter 16 examined Balhae as a major state founded substantially by former Goguryeo elites and Mohe peoples, governing extensive territory in the northern peninsula and Manchuria alongside Silla from the late seventh century onward, and maintaining active diplomatic and cultural relationships with Tang China throughout much of its existence.

In 926, Balhae was conquered by the Khitan, a steppe people whose ruling house had recently established the Liao dynasty and was engaged in a broader project of territorial expansion across the northeastern frontier of the former Tang world. The specific military and political circumstances of Balhae's fall are less fully documented in surviving sources than comparable events on the peninsula itself, but the outcome is clear: Balhae's independent statehood, which had endured for well over two centuries, came to an end, and its territory passed under Khitan Liao control.

The consequences of this conquest extended southward. Substantial numbers of Balhae's population, including, according to later Korean sources, members of the Balhae royal house itself, migrated into the peninsula over the years following the conquest, arriving in territory increasingly under the control of the newly founded Goryeo state. This migration mattered for reasons beyond simple humanitarian displacement. Goryeo's rulers, beginning with Wang Geon and continuing under his successors, had reason to welcome and, in some measure, incorporate these refugees, since doing so supported Goryeo's own claims to represent a broader northern Korean historical inheritance extending beyond Silla's legacy alone, an inheritance that included both Goguryeo, invoked in Goryeo's very name, and now Balhae as well.

Students should be careful not to conflate the detailed institutional history of Balhae, covered fully in Chapter 16, with the more limited treatment given here. This section's purpose is narrower: to explain how Balhae's collapse intersected chronologically and politically with the reordering of the peninsula examined throughout this chapter, and to establish the refugee movements that would shape Goryeo's early self-understanding, a thread Chapter 19 will pick up in its account of Wang Geon's approach to unification and territorial claims.

Section 18.10

Wang Geon's Rise

Wang Geon's family origins lay in the region around Songak, a settlement on the north-central peninsula's western coast that would later become the city known as Kaesong. According to the surviving accounts, Wang Geon's family had accumulated substantial wealth over preceding generations through landholding and, notably, through commercial activity connected to maritime trade routes along the western coast and across the Yellow Sea, a pattern of regional wealth building on connections that should recall, without being identical to, the maritime commercial base Jang Bogo had constructed at Cheonghaejin two generations earlier, examined in Chapter 17.

This combination of landed wealth, commercial resources, and a strategically positioned home region gave the Wang family a strong local base of support well before Wang Geon rose to prominence individually. When Gung Ye's expanding northern state absorbed the Songak region, Wang Geon's family aligned itself with Gung Ye's cause, and Wang Geon himself entered Gung Ye's military service, where he distinguished himself through a series of successful campaigns, including naval operations along the southwestern coast that leveraged his family's maritime experience and further extended Gung Ye's territorial reach at the expense of Gyeon Hwon's Later Baekje.

Over roughly two decades of service, Wang Geon rose steadily through Gung Ye's military hierarchy, eventually attaining senior command and, according to later accounts, considerable personal popularity among fellow officers and soldiers, a popularity that stood in increasing contrast to the deteriorating relationship between Gung Ye and his commanders described in Section 18.7. Wang Geon's position therefore combined several distinct sources of strength: an independent regional and commercial power base predating his military career, a substantial military reputation earned through genuine battlefield success, and a network of personal loyalty among fellow commanders who had grown wary of Gung Ye's increasingly erratic rule.

This combination of resources, regional, commercial, military, and personal, positioned Wang Geon uniquely among Gung Ye's subordinates when the crisis examined in the next section finally arrived. Unlike a purely military figure with no independent base, or a purely commercial figure with no military standing, Wang Geon possessed the full range of resources necessary to be seen by his fellow commanders as a viable alternative to Gung Ye's continued rule.

Section 18.11

The Overthrow of Gung Ye and the Founding of Goryeo, 918

By 918, according to the surviving accounts, Gung Ye's relationship with his leading commanders had deteriorated to the point of open danger for those around him, with purges and executions, whether fully justified by genuine conspiracies or driven substantially by Gung Ye's own suspicion, having eroded confidence in his continued leadership. A group of senior commanders, judging further service under Gung Ye untenable, moved against him, deposing him from power. Later accounts describe Gung Ye fleeing the capital and being killed shortly afterward, though the precise circumstances of his death are not established with full confidence.

The commanders who carried out this action then elevated Wang Geon to the throne in his place. Whether Wang Geon actively organized this coup, was persuaded to accept a role his fellow commanders had already decided upon, or occupied some position between these two possibilities is not fully resolved by the surviving evidence, though later Goryeo-era chronicles, unsurprisingly, tend to emphasize Wang Geon's reluctance and the commanders' insistence, a narrative that served to present the new dynasty's founding as a matter of collective necessity and moral legitimacy rather than personal ambition. Historians treat this framing with appropriate caution while acknowledging that the underlying event, Wang Geon's elevation by a coalition of fellow commanders, is well supported by the broader pattern of evidence.

Once installed as ruler, Wang Geon restored the name Goryeo, a name historically associated with Goguryeo and, as Section 18.7 explained, already the name Gung Ye's own state had used at its founding in 901 before its later renamings to Majin and Taebong. This choice preserved continuity with the historical and regional legitimacy that Gung Ye's state had originally built while distancing the new ruler from the discredited Majin and Taebong names most closely associated with Gung Ye's later, increasingly autocratic and idiosyncratic rule. Wang Geon retained Cheorwon as his capital only through the following year, relocating it in 919 to his own family's home region at Songak, a decision that both rewarded his own regional power base and positioned the new state's center of gravity around resources, commercial and agricultural, that Wang Geon and his family understood well.

The events of 918 mark this chapter's culminating political transition, but students should be careful not to treat them as equivalent to the founding of a unified Korean state. In 918, Goryeo was one of at least three significant powers on the peninsula, its territory still limited substantially to the north-central region inherited from Gung Ye, its relationship with the surviving hojok of other regions still to be negotiated, and its eventual conquest or absorption of Later Baekje and Silla still nearly two decades in the future. Chapter 19 examines that nearly two-decade process of consolidation and unification in full.

Section 18.12

A Peninsula Reordered

By the year 918, the Korean peninsula looked substantially different from the peninsula Chapter 14 described two centuries earlier. Silla still existed, its royal court still functioning at Gyeongju, but its practical authority extended over only a fraction of its former territory, and its long-term survival as an independent state was no longer assured. Later Baekje remained a powerful and expansionist state in the southwest under Gyeon Hwon's continued leadership. In the north-central peninsula, a newly proclaimed state called Goryeo, ruled by a commander who had until recently served another master, was only beginning to establish itself. To the north, Balhae's collapse in 926 would soon add a further population of displaced people and unresolved territorial claims to an already unsettled political landscape.

What had disappeared, more than any single kingdom or dynasty, was the assumption, largely valid for the preceding two centuries, that political authority on the peninsula radiated outward from a single royal capital at Gyeongju. In its place stood a political field that was regional in its power base, militarized in its methods, and genuinely open to new coalitions and alliances. No single outcome, whether Silla's survival, Later Baekje's expansion, or Goryeo's eventual triumph, could have been confidently predicted by an observer standing in the year 918 itself.

This chapter has argued that Silla's authority did not collapse in a single event but eroded gradually, through court factionalism, the structural limitations of the bone-rank system, fiscal pressure on the countryside, rural rebellion, and the conversion of local power into organized regional military strength. It has traced how that erosion produced Later Baekje and Later Goguryeo as genuine competing states, how Balhae's roughly contemporaneous collapse added a further complicating element to the peninsula's northern frontier, and how Wang Geon's particular combination of regional, commercial, military, and personal resources positioned him to inherit Gung Ye's state at the moment Gung Ye's own rule became untenable. Chapter 19 now turns to the nearly two decades of political and military effort through which Wang Geon converted this uncertain position into the unified Goryeo state that would govern the Korean peninsula for over four centuries.

Primary Source Window

The Samguk Sagi on the Rebellions of 889

Creator: compiled under the direction of the twelfth-century Goryeo official Kim Busik and associated compilers · Date: compiled in the twelfth century, describing events of the late ninth century · Type: Official dynastic history, the Samguk Sagi ("History of the Three Kingdoms"), Korea's oldest surviving comprehensive historical chronicle

Context: The Samguk Sagi's account of Silla's final decades, including the 889 uprising, was compiled more than two centuries after the events it describes, under the patronage of the Goryeo court, examined further in Chapter 21. Its compilers drew on earlier Silla-era records that no longer survive independently, but the finished chronicle reflects the perspective, priorities, and literary conventions of a twelfth-century Confucian bureaucratic elite rather than a contemporary ninth-century observer.

Paraphrase: The following is a paraphrase, not a direct quotation, of the chronicle's account.

The account describes provinces and districts throughout the kingdom falling behind on tribute and tax payments, the court dispatching officials to press for collection, and the response taking the form of widespread banditry and armed rebellion in many regions rather than renewed compliance. Among the rebel leaders the chronicle names, Wonjong and Aeno are recorded as having established their rebellion at Sabeol-ju.

Analysis: This passage is valuable because it comes from the most authoritative surviving Korean chronicle covering this period, and its description of a kingdom-wide breakdown in tax compliance, rather than a single localized dispute, supports the broader historical picture of declining central authority developed throughout this chapter. The chronicle's own framing, presenting the unrest primarily as a breakdown of order requiring suppression, itself reveals the perspective of the class that produced and later compiled such records.

Limitations: The chronicle offers little information about the rebels' own perspectives, specific grievances beyond taxation in general terms, or the internal organization of their movement. It was compiled over two centuries after the events described, drawing on intermediate sources that cannot now be independently verified, and it reflects the interests of a later dynastic court examining the failures of a predecessor state whose collapse had, by the time of compilation, already given rise to the very dynasty commissioning the history.

Perspective: The chronicle represents an elite bureaucratic and, ultimately, a Goryeo dynastic perspective. The perspectives of the rebels themselves, of ordinary agricultural households not directly involved in rebellion, and of provincial officials caught between court demands and local unrest are not directly represented.

Historical Debate

Why Did Silla's Authority Collapse?

The question: Historians offer several overlapping explanations for the erosion of Silla royal authority in the late ninth and early tenth centuries. Should the primary explanation be sought in court politics and aristocratic factionalism, in socioeconomic pressures on the rural population, or in the independent rise of regional military power? Historians differ in how they weigh these factors relative to one another.

Interpretation A — Court politics and aristocratic factionalism as the primary cause: Some historians emphasize that succession disputes and factional competition within the bone-rank aristocracy, beginning well before the rebellions of the 880s, progressively undermined the court's capacity for coordinated governance, making effective responses to provincial and fiscal problems increasingly difficult regardless of conditions in the countryside.
Interpretation B — Socioeconomic pressure and fiscal crisis as the primary cause: Other historians emphasize the fiscal dimension, arguing that intensifying tax burdens, whatever their precise cause, alongside a court less able to administer taxation effectively or respond to local grievance, drove rural populations toward rebellion independent of the specific pattern of court intrigue at any given moment.
Interpretation C — Regional power formation as the primary cause: A third approach emphasizes that the independent accumulation of military and economic power by hojok, a process with roots extending back at least to Jang Bogo's career, would likely have eventually challenged central authority regardless of the specific sequence of court politics or the specific triggers of rural unrest.

Evidence: All three interpretations draw on the same limited body of surviving chronicle evidence, principally the Samguk Sagi and later Goryeo-era sources, supplemented by more fragmentary epigraphic and archaeological material. None of these interpretations rests on evidence unavailable to the others; they differ chiefly in which strand of the available evidence they treat as most fundamental.

Why it matters: This textbook treats these three factors as interacting and mutually reinforcing rather than as competing explanations that require choosing one to the exclusion of the others. Court factionalism reduced the state's capacity to respond to fiscal problems; fiscal problems generated rural unrest; rural unrest and the general climate of disorder created opportunities for regional military entrepreneurship; and successful regional military entrepreneurship further reduced the practical scope of central authority, reinforcing the original problem.

Student task: Using the evidence and section material presented in this chapter, construct an argument for which factor you consider most fundamental, while explaining how the other two factors contributed to and reinforced it.

Multiple Perspectives

Living Through the Breakdown

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns historians have identified across many sources. They are not quotations from any single named historical actor.

From the perspective of a Gyeongju aristocrat in the late ninth century: The kingdom's troubles appear as a crisis of order and loyalty requiring firmer enforcement of established hierarchy, tax obligation, and royal authority, rather than as evidence that the existing distribution of privilege itself needs to change.
From the perspective of a provincial hojok family: The weakening of central oversight presents both danger, in the form of disorder and rival local claimants, and opportunity, in the form of expanded local authority, military recruitment, and eventual alliance with one of the larger emerging powers on more favorable terms than continued subordination to a distant and increasingly ineffective court.
From the perspective of a peasant household in a rebellious district: Tax demands from a court no longer capable of providing security or effective administration in return appear increasingly difficult to justify, and armed resistance, despite its dangers, comes to seem preferable to continued extraction under deteriorating conditions.
From the perspective of a soldier serving under Gyeon Hwon or Gung Ye: Service under a successful regional leader offers more immediate security, plunder, and advancement than continued loyalty to a distant and weakening Silla court, particularly when that leader can invoke a historically resonant kingdom's name and demonstrate consistent military success.
From the perspective of a Balhae refugee arriving in Goryeo territory after 926: Displacement from a homeland conquered by the Khitan brings genuine hardship and uncertainty, but also the possibility of incorporation into a new state whose ruler has reason to welcome Balhae's population and historical legacy as part of his own claims to a broader northern inheritance.
Continuity and Change Over Time

Old Materials, New Order

What Changed

  • Political authority shifted from a single royal capital radiating outward to multiple competing regional centers
  • Military recruitment shifted from state-organized forces toward personal followings assembled by regional strongmen
  • Access to political legitimacy widened beyond the bone-rank aristocracy to include successful military leaders of varied backgrounds
  • Balhae's independent statehood in the north ended, and its population and historical legacy became entangled with the emerging Goryeo state

What Stayed the Same

  • The underlying agricultural economy and social hierarchy of the peninsula persisted through the political disorder, even as who controlled and taxed that economy changed
  • The historical memory of the earlier Three Kingdoms remained a living political resource, invoked by new leaders to legitimize new states
  • Maritime and commercial networks along the western and southern coasts, established across the preceding centuries and examined in Chapter 17, continued to generate wealth that regional leaders such as Wang Geon's family could convert into political power
  • The practice of drawing political legitimacy from historical dynastic memory, evident in Gojoseon, the Three Kingdoms, and now the Later Three Kingdoms, continued into the new Goryeo state itself

Why the pattern matters: The new political order that emerged by 918 was not built from entirely new materials. It was constructed substantially from the social, military, commercial, and ideological resources that had developed within, and ultimately outlasted, the Silla state itself.

Cause and Effect

From Court Crisis to Rival States

Long-term causes

The bone-rank system's limited capacity to incorporate new sources of power, and the long-developing regional and commercial resources, examined in Chapter 17, available to figures such as Jang Bogo and later Wang Geon's family.

Short-term causes

Intensifying succession disputes and factional conflict at the Gyeongju court from the mid-ninth century onward, combined with deteriorating fiscal administration in the provinces.

Triggering events

The 889 crisis of widespread tax noncompliance and rebellion, including Wonjong and Aeno's uprising at Sabeol-ju, which demonstrated the court's weakened capacity to enforce order and provided a template and, in some cases, a direct source of manpower for subsequent regional military mobilization.

Effects on the peninsula

The proliferation of hojok power, the founding of Later Baekje in 900 and Gung Ye's Goryeo/Later Goguryeo in 901, and the founding of Wang Geon's Goryeo in 918, together reordering the peninsula's political landscape by the second decade of the tenth century.

A parallel northern development: Balhae's conquest by the Khitan Liao state in 926 was not an effect of this same causal chain. It resulted from an entirely separate process, Khitan military expansion along the northeastern frontier of the former Tang world, examined in Chapter 16. Its significance for this chapter lies in its rough contemporaneity with the peninsula's own reordering and in the southward refugee movement it produced, which intersected with, rather than was caused by, Silla's fragmentation.

Historical complexity: No single cause fully explains the peninsula's own transformation. Court factionalism, fiscal pressure, and regional power formation interacted cumulatively over several decades, and the specific outcome, the eventual dominance of Wang Geon's Goryeo, examined in Chapter 19, depended on contingent factors including individual leadership, timing, and alliance choices that cannot be reduced to any single structural cause.

Everyday Life and Social History

Ordinary Life Amid Political Fragmentation

The political drama of court factions, rebel leaders, and rival kings examined in this chapter unfolded against the backdrop of ordinary agricultural and commercial life that continued, however disrupted, throughout this period. For most inhabitants of the peninsula, the practical experience of Silla's fragmentation was likely felt less through news of events at Gyeongju than through more immediate and local changes: which local strongman now collected taxes or demanded labor service, whether nearby roads and markets remained safe for travel and trade, whether military conscription by a regional leader's forces threatened household labor, and whether armed conflict among rival hojok or between the emerging Later Three Kingdoms states reached one's own district.

For communities in border or contested regions, particularly in the southwest between Silla and Later Baekje, or in the north-central peninsula amid Gung Ye's and later Wang Geon's territorial consolidation, shifting political control could mean repeated changes in which authority claimed taxes, labor, and military service, along with the direct disruptions of warfare itself. For Balhae refugees moving south after 926, the experience involved not only the loss of a homeland but also the practical challenges of resettlement within a still-forming Goryeo state whose own institutions for absorbing new populations were themselves only beginning to take shape. Historians reconstructing these everyday experiences must rely heavily on inference from the better-documented political narrative, since the perspectives of ordinary rural households, migrants, and soldiers rarely survive in their own words from this period.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced the gradual fragmentation of the political order Unified Silla had built and maintained for roughly two centuries, and the emergence from that fragmentation of the multi-state landscape known as the Later Three Kingdoms period. It began not with a single collapse but with a slow erosion of royal authority beginning in the mid-ninth century, visible above all in intensifying succession disputes and factional competition among the bone-rank aristocracy at the Gyeongju court.

The chapter explained why the bone-rank system, which had once provided institutional stability, increasingly limited Silla's capacity to adapt to new pressures, since it offered few mechanisms for incorporating emerging sources of power, whether commercial, military, or regional, into the recognized political order. This structural limitation combined with deteriorating fiscal administration to produce genuine hardship and grievance in the countryside, culminating in the major uprising centered in Sabeol-ju in 889 and related unrest across the kingdom.

From this atmosphere of rebellion and weakening central control, the chapter traced the rise of the hojok, a diverse group of provincial landed families, military commanders, and former rebels who converted local resources into regional political and military power. Two such figures, Gyeon Hwon and Gung Ye, distinguished themselves by consolidating power across substantially larger territories and by formalizing their authority through the explicit revival of the historical memory of Baekje and Goguryeo respectively, giving rise to Later Baekje in 900 and, in 901, the state Gung Ye himself named Goryeo and which historians call Later Goguryeo, later renamed Majin and then Taebong.

The chapter examined each of these founding figures in turn: Gyeon Hwon's rise from Silla military service to the founding of a durable and expansionist southwestern state, and Gung Ye's more ambiguous background, his successive renamings of his state, his relocation of its capital to Cheorwon, and his descent into an increasingly autocratic and religiously idiosyncratic style of rule that alienated his own commanders.

It also incorporated, as the Authoritative Chapter Outline requires, the roughly contemporaneous collapse of Balhae, conquered by the Khitan Liao state in 926, and the resulting southward movement of Balhae refugees, including members of its royal house, into the newly emerging Goryeo state, a development with lasting significance for Goryeo's claims to a broader northern historical inheritance.

The chapter then turned to Wang Geon, tracing his family's regional and commercial base near Songak, his military service and rising reputation under Gung Ye, and the combination of resources, regional, commercial, military, and personal, that positioned him uniquely among Gung Ye's commanders. It examined the events of 918, in which a coalition of commanders deposed Gung Ye and elevated Wang Geon, who then restored the name Goryeo, first used by Gung Ye's own state in 901, before relocating the new state's capital the following year, in 919, to his own family's home region at Songak.

The chapter closed by assessing the condition of the peninsula in 918: a diminished but still-functioning Silla, an expansionist Later Baekje, a newly founded and still-uncertain Goryeo, and, in the near background, the recent or imminent collapse of Balhae in the north. It emphasized that no single outcome could have been confidently predicted at this moment, and that the political order taking shape was built substantially from social, military, commercial, and ideological materials that had developed within, and ultimately outlasted, the Silla state itself.

This chapter's central argument, that Silla's authority fragmented gradually through the interaction of court factionalism, fiscal weakness, rural rebellion, and the rise of provincial military power, rather than collapsing in a single event, provides essential context for Chapter 19, which follows Wang Geon's transformation from a newly proclaimed king in 918 into the ruler of a reunified peninsula by 936, when Later Baekje's final defeat completed a process that had begun with Silla's own submission the previous year.

Readers should carry forward from this chapter a clear sense that the Later Three Kingdoms period was not a simple repetition of the earlier Three Kingdoms examined in Part II, but a genuinely new configuration of power built by new men using old names, operating within a political field that had become regional, competitive, and open to new coalitions in ways the earlier Silla order had not been.

Taebong

Cheorwon
The capital Gung Ye established for his state in the north-central peninsula.
Songak
The home region of Wang Geon's family, later known as Kaesong, and the capital Wang Geon chose for Goryeo.
Later Three Kingdoms
The historiographical label for the period of competition among Silla, Later Baekje, and Gung Ye's/Wang Geon's northern state in the early tenth century.
Khitan
The steppe people whose ruling house founded the Liao dynasty and conquered Balhae in 926.
Liao dynasty
The Khitan-led state that conquered Balhae and controlled substantial territory across the northeastern frontier of the former Tang world.
Balhae refugees
Populations, including members of the Balhae royal house, who migrated south into Goryeo territory following Balhae's conquest in 926.
Regional warlordism
The proliferation of independent, militarily backed regional authority that characterized the late ninth and early tenth centuries.
Wang Geon
Commander from a wealthy Songak family who served under Gung Ye, was elevated to the throne in 918, and founded Goryeo.
Goryeo (918 founding)
The state Wang Geon proclaimed after Gung Ye's overthrow, reviving the name of the ancient kingdom of Goguryeo.
Fiscal extraction
The process of collecting taxes and labor service from the population, whose deterioration and continued burden contributed to rural rebellion in this period.
Court faction
A group within the aristocracy competing for control of the throne and associated offices and revenues.
Coup
The sudden, forceful removal of a ruler by members of the political or military elite, as occurred against Gung Ye in 918.
Historical memory
The political use of earlier kingdoms' names and legacies, as by Gyeon Hwon and Gung Ye, to legitimize new states.
Samguk Sagi
Korea's oldest surviving comprehensive dynastic history, compiled in the twelfth century under Goryeo patronage, an important source for this period.
Military entrepreneurship
The process by which individuals assembled personal armed followings and converted them into independent political and economic power.
Dynastic legitimacy
The claim of a ruler or state to rightful authority, often constructed through appeals to historical memory, lineage, or religious sanction.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has told a story of breakdown that is also, inseparably, a story of construction. The same decades that saw Silla's royal authority erode also saw new political structures take shape from the social, military, and commercial materials that late Silla society itself had produced. Gyeon Hwon, Gung Ye, and Wang Geon were none of them outsiders arriving to exploit a vacuum; each was a product of the very conditions, provincial military opportunity, regional commercial wealth, and the political usefulness of historical memory, that Silla's own long history had generated.

This pattern matters for how students should understand historical change more broadly. Political orders rarely collapse into nothing; they fragment, and their fragments become the building blocks of what follows. The bone-rank aristocracy's rigidity, Jang Bogo's earlier demonstration that a regional base could rival court-sanctioned power, the fiscal pressures that provoked the Sabeol-ju uprising, and the historical memory of Baekje and Goguryeo all persisted through Silla's decline and reappeared, transformed, in the states that competed to replace it.

The chapter also establishes an essential piece of context for the remainder of Part IV. Goryeo's eventual reunification of the peninsula, examined in Chapter 19, will not be a story of a single triumphant new order sweeping away an exhausted past. It will be a story of one among several competing successor states, led by a man whose own rise depended entirely on the conditions this chapter has described, gradually absorbing its rivals through a combination of military pressure, diplomacy, marriage alliance, and the strategic incorporation of populations, including Balhae's refugees, displaced by the same era of upheaval.

Students who understand that Silla's fall was gradual, multicausal, and generative of the very forces that replaced it are well prepared to follow Chapter 19's account of how Wang Geon and his successors built something durable from this uncertain and competitive inheritance, converting the fragmented, multi-state peninsula examined in this chapter into the unified Goryeo dynasty that would govern the Korean peninsula for over four centuries.
