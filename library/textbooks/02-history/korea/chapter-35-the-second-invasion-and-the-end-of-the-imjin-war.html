The Second Invasion and the End of the Imjin War

Korean History: Ancient Kingdoms to a Divided Peninsula

Chapter Opening

On the night of the eighteenth day of the eleventh lunar month of 1598, continuing into the morning of the nineteenth, in the narrow strait of Noryang, ships of Joseon, Ming China, and Japan fought in the dark while a Japanese army elsewhere slipped toward the sea. The admiral who had done more than any other commander to deny Japan control of Korean waters was killed during the engagement. The war he had fought for nearly seven years was already ending, and not by his victory alone.

The Imjin War, which began with the Japanese landings at Busan in 1592, did not end in a single decisive battle, a negotiated peace treaty, or a clear victory for any participant. It ended through the interaction of military stalemate, the collapse of a diplomatic fiction, the destruction of much of southern Korea, and the death of the Japanese ruler who had launched the invasion. This chapter follows that final phase from the uneasy truce of 1593–1596 through the renewed invasion of 1597, the disaster at Chilcheollyang, the recovery at Myeongnyang, the failed allied sieges of the southern fortresses, Hideyoshi's death in 1598, Japan's withdrawal, and the last naval battle at Noryang. It then turns to what seven years of warfare left behind: devastated farmland, depopulated districts, captives carried to Japan, institutions altered by the demands of war, and an East Asian order that would not return to its earlier shape.

The controlling question of the chapter is this: why did Japan's second invasion fail to reproduce the victories of 1592, and how did seven years of war finally end without producing a simple military victory for any one participant? The answer developed here is that the second invasion took place in a fundamentally different strategic environment. Joseon had rebuilt parts of its military system, Ming forces were already committed on the peninsula, local resistance networks survived, and Japanese commanders could no longer expect an uncontested march northward. Yet Joseon also suffered a catastrophic naval command failure at Chilcheollyang, painful defeats at Hwangseoksanseong and Namwon, and enormous civilian destruction, and it remained dependent on Ming military power. Myeongnyang restored a vital maritime barrier; the land offensive was checked around Jiksan; Japanese forces withdrew into a belt of fortified coastal bases that the allies repeatedly failed to reduce. Only Hideyoshi's death, on the eighteenth day of the eighth lunar month of 1598, converted a costly stalemate into an order to leave.

Chapter 31 examined the political, diplomatic, and military conditions that made war possible. Chapter 32 traced the collapse of Joseon's first conventional defense in 1592–1593. Chapter 33 examined Admiral Yi Sun-sin and the war at sea, and Chapter 34 the righteous armies, Buddhist fighters, and Ming intervention that transformed the first invasion. Chapter 35 completes that sequence. It must therefore be read as the culmination of a connected narrative rather than as a freestanding battle history, and it draws on those chapters selectively without retelling them.

The evidence for this phase is unusually rich and unusually contested. Court records, military reports, personal diaries, memorials, Ming dispatches, Japanese campaign documents, temple and family records, and the later writings of survivors survive in quantities that allow historians to reconstruct events with considerable confidence. At the same time, nearly every major episode has been reshaped by commemoration. Yi Sun-sin became, in later centuries, one of the most celebrated figures in Korean memory, and accounts of Myeongnyang and Noryang have often been told in language closer to national epic than to documentary analysis. Japanese and Chinese sources, written for different audiences and purposes, give different numbers and different explanations. This chapter attempts to distinguish what contemporaries recorded, what later tradition added, and what remains uncertain.

The chapter also insists that the war cannot be understood from its commanders alone. Farmers whose fields were burned, soldiers conscripted into units they never expected to join, Buddhist monks, sailors, fortress garrisons, Ming infantry far from home, Japanese troops cut off in alien winter weather, artisans carried into captivity, and families who waited years for people who never returned were all part of the war's ending. Their experiences are less well documented than those of generals and kings, but they are essential to explaining what the war was and what it left behind.

Finally, the chapter prepares the ground for Chapter 36, which examines the Manchu invasions and the reordering of East Asia in the early seventeenth century. The Imjin War did not directly produce the fall of the Ming or the rise of the Manchu state, and this chapter does not claim that it did. But it left Joseon physically devastated, militarily altered, and politically burdened by its debt to the Ming, and it shaped the conditions in which Joseon entered the next great crisis.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why the truce of 1593–1596 was not a peace, and why the renewed fighting of 1597 is best understood as a new phase of the same war.
  2. Analyze why the Ming–Japanese negotiations collapsed, distinguishing incompatible expectations of settlement from the simpler claim that Hideyoshi was insulted by a Chinese title.
  3. Describe the military reforms Joseon undertook during the truce and assess their limits.
  4. Evaluate the dismissal of Yi Sun-sin as a problem of intelligence, command, court politics, and source criticism, rather than as a proven Japanese plot or an act of royal jealousy.
  5. Trace the destruction of the Joseon fleet at Chilcheollyang and explain its strategic consequences.
  6. Compare the objectives and geography of the 1597 invasion with those of 1592.
  7. Assess the battles of Hwangseoksanseong, Namwon, and Jiksan as evidence of both the limits of Joseon–Ming coalition defense and the limits of the Japanese advance.
  8. Interpret the Battle of Myeongnyang, including its geography, the problem of competing ship counts, and the danger of attributing its outcome to a single factor.
  9. Explain the role of the wajo fortress belt and the reasons allied sieges at Ulsan, Sacheon, and Suncheon failed.
  10. Analyze the experience of civilians, including mutilation, enslavement, and captivity, using both official records and a captive's own account.
  11. Distinguish why Japan's invasion failed to achieve its aims from why the war ended when it did, and evaluate the role of Hideyoshi's death.
  12. Assess the postwar consequences for Joseon, Japan, and Ming China without attributing later geopolitical change to the war alone.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did Japan's second invasion fail to reproduce the victories of 1592, and what had changed in the strategic environment?
  2. Why did negotiations that all parties wanted to continue ultimately collapse, and what role did intermediaries play in postponing that collapse?
  3. How should historians weigh intelligence failure, court mistrust, factional conflict, and command rivalry in explaining the removal of Yi Sun-sin?
  4. What did the destruction of the fleet at Chilcheollyang and its partial restoration at Myeongnyang reveal about the relationship between naval power and the war on land?
  5. Why could Joseon–Ming forces defeat Japanese armies in some settings, as at Pyongyang, yet fail to take fortresses such as Ulsan, Sacheon, and Suncheon?
  6. How did Japanese commanders use fortified coastal positions to compensate for the failure of the inland advance?
  7. What does the treatment of civilians and captives reveal about the character of the war, and how can historians recover the experiences of people who left few records?
  8. What actually ended the Imjin War: battlefield stalemate, Hideyoshi's death, or the interaction of both?
  9. How have commemorative traditions shaped our understanding of Myeongnyang, Noryang, and Yi Sun-sin's death, and how can students separate tradition from evidence?

Historical Context

Students approaching this chapter need only a compact summary of the preceding war. In the fourth lunar month of 1592, Japanese armies under Toyotomi Hideyoshi's commanders landed at Busan and advanced rapidly northward, taking Seoul within weeks and Pyongyang soon afterward, while King Seonjo fled toward the Chinese frontier (Chapter 32). Joseon naval forces under Yi Sun-sin and his colleagues won a series of engagements, including Hansan Island, that disrupted Japanese maritime supply and prevented unrestricted movement along the western coast (Chapter 33). Local militias, Buddhist fighters, and eventually a Ming expeditionary force transformed the war on land, forcing Japanese withdrawal from Pyongyang and then Seoul in 1593 (Chapter 34). Japanese forces retreated to a chain of fortified positions along the southeastern coast, where they remained.

That withdrawal left the war suspended rather than concluded. Large Japanese garrisons continued to occupy fortified bases, supplied from across the Korea Strait. Ming and Joseon forces controlled most of the rest of the peninsula but lacked the means, or the political agreement, to dislodge their opponents. Meanwhile, diplomatic contacts between Ming and Japanese representatives, conducted largely without Joseon's participation, created the appearance that a settlement was close. The destruction of that appearance in 1596 was the immediate prelude to renewed invasion.

Joseon's internal condition during these years was precarious. The royal court was divided over strategy and over responsibility for the initial defeat. Fiscal resources were strained, and the countryside had suffered requisition, flight, and famine. Yet the war had also forced experimentation. A new military training agency introduced Ming drill methods, provincial defense was reorganized, and a surviving naval command structure continued to operate. These developments determined whether Joseon could meet a second invasion on different terms from the first.

Geography also shaped the final phase. Southern Korea's coast is deeply indented and studded with islands, with strong tidal currents, narrow channels, and sheltered anchorages. Japanese commanders were able to build and defend coastal strongholds with access to the sea, while allied forces approaching by land faced mountainous terrain, winter weather, and difficult supply lines. The west coast of the Jeolla region, rich in grain, was strategically attractive to Japan and had been protected in 1592 largely because Joseon naval power prevented its seaborne invasion. Understanding why the naval situation mattered so much to the land campaign is essential for the chapter that follows.

Section 35.1

A War That Never Really Ended, 1593–1596

Chapter 34 ended with a stalemate. After the allied recovery of Pyongyang and Seoul, Japanese commanders withdrew to the southeast and built or strengthened a line of fortified bases around Busan and along the coast. Ming and Joseon forces reoccupied most of the peninsula, but neither could force the Japanese out of their strongholds, and neither side was prepared to concede the war's outcome. Historians often call the following years a truce, and the word is useful so long as students do not mistake it for peace.

For the people of southern Korea the "truce" meant continued occupation, requisition, and insecurity. Tens of thousands of Japanese troops remained, along with the Korean laborers who built and supplied their fortifications. Raids and skirmishes continued, and Ming and Joseon forces in the surrounding districts also required grain, labor, and shelter. Harvests had been disrupted by the fighting of 1592–1593 and by the flight of cultivators, and famine and epidemic disease accompanied the war into the truce years. Refugees moved between districts, some returning to ruined villages and others remaining displaced. The state's capacity to feed an army and a population simultaneously was strained throughout.

The Ming and Japanese governments each treated the pause as a chance to gain what they had not won in battle. For Ming officials, withdrawal of Japanese forces and a formal Japanese acknowledgment of Ming overlordship would allow the expeditionary army to be reduced at a time when the Ming treasury faced other demands. For Hideyoshi, a settlement with territorial or commercial concessions would justify the enormous cost of the campaign. Neither expectation was publicly aligned with the other, a gap that the negotiations described in the next section would hide for several years before exposing it.

This section's central point is a classification problem. The 1597 invasion is sometimes described as a "second war," and Korean tradition gives it a separate name, the Jeongyu War, after the sexagenary cycle year. But the fighting of 1597–1598 arose from unresolved issues of 1592–1593, involved many of the same commanders and the same fortified positions, and was fought by armies that had never been demobilized. It is best understood as the resumption of large-scale operations within a single war, not as an unrelated conflict. That continuity matters because it explains why both sides in 1597 reasoned from the experience of 1592–1593: Japan from the belief that the south could be secured and held, and the allies from the belief that coordinated resistance could contain a Japanese advance.

Section 35.2

Why the Peace Negotiations Failed

Negotiations during the truce were conducted chiefly between Ming representatives and Japanese commanders, above all the Ming envoy Shen Weijing and the Japanese commander Konishi Yukinaga. Joseon officials were present at the margins and, in the court's view, were being deliberately sidelined in a settlement concerning their own country. The Joseon court regarded the Japanese invasion as unjustifiable aggression and opposed any arrangement that rewarded it, and it resented the Ming for negotiating over its territory. The king and his advisers did not control the process, but they kept watch on it and registered their objections, and they feared with good reason that a settlement agreed between Beijing and Hideyoshi might leave Japanese garrisons in southern Korea.

The problem at the center of the negotiations was that the participants did not agree on what a settlement would be. From the Ming perspective, an acceptable end to the war was Japanese withdrawal from Korea followed by Hideyoshi's acceptance of an investiture, in which the Ming emperor would confer the title of king of Japan within the framework of tributary diplomacy. Such an arrangement would cast the war as the misconduct of a vassal who had been brought back into order. Hideyoshi, by contrast, had launched the war with territorial and dynastic ambitions and expected the negotiations to produce concessions from a defeated opponent. Surviving Japanese accounts of his demands include terms that Ming and Joseon officials could not have accepted, such as arrangements concerning the partition of parts of southern Korea and a marriage connection to the Ming imperial house. The precise list and wording of the demands varies between sources and the demands changed over time, and students should treat any single enumeration cautiously.

Intermediaries kept the process alive by bridging this gap with misrepresentation. Konishi Yukinaga, a commander with a strong interest in ending the war, and Shen Weijing, an envoy who had staked his reputation on negotiating a settlement, are described in many sources as having conveyed to each side a version of the other's position that was more accommodating than the reality. Hideyoshi was told, in effect, that the Ming were prepared to treat Japan as a peer; the Ming court was told that Hideyoshi would accept submission. Historians differ on how deliberate each falsehood was and on how far individual intermediaries understood the gap, but the pattern across the sources is consistent: ambiguity and concealment sustained negotiations that could not have survived a transparent exchange of terms.

The gap could not be concealed once a formal Ming mission reached Japan in 1596. The mission formally invested Hideyoshi as king of Japan. Later Edo-period narratives dramatized the encounter as an enraged rejection of the title or the decree, but the evidence does not require that interpretation, and Hideyoshi appears to have accepted the investiture itself. The negotiations failed because the settlement did not grant the territorial, dynastic, and other concessions he expected, while the Ming demanded Japanese withdrawal from Korea. Joseon envoys who had accompanied the mission were not received. Soon afterward Hideyoshi began mobilizing for renewed war.

Students should resist the common simplification that Hideyoshi went to war again because he was insulted by a Chinese title. The title symbolized the incompatible assumptions behind the negotiations rather than itself causing renewed war; it was the moment at which a deeper incompatibility became undeniable. Ming expected submission within its diplomatic order; Hideyoshi expected concessions from a state he regarded as having failed to prevent his advance; Joseon sought withdrawal and the restoration of its territorial integrity. These aims were mutually incompatible, and only systematic misrepresentation had concealed that fact. Once the pretense collapsed, the choice facing Hideyoshi was to accept an outcome far short of his objectives or to resume the war. The strategic decision to resume was his own, and it reflected both his ambitions and the political and personal costs of acknowledging failure.

Section 35.3

Joseon Rebuilds during the Truce

While diplomats negotiated, the Joseon court attempted to remedy the weaknesses exposed in 1592. The most significant institutional response was the Training Agency, the Hunryeondogam, introduced in 1593 with the support of leading officials including Ryu Seong-ryong. Its purpose was to produce soldiers trained in methods that had proved effective against the Japanese. These methods drew heavily on Ming practice as taught by Ming officers in Korea and on a Chinese military manual associated with the general Qi Jiguang, the Jixiao xinshu, which emphasized integrated drill among firearms, spear, sword, and archery units. Where the Joseon armies of 1592 had relied heavily on archers and on levies assembled hurriedly and trained little, the new units were organized in smaller tactical groups and drilled to fight in coordination.

The reformers' emphasis on firearms was not accidental. Japanese infantry had made devastating use of matchlock muskets in 1592, and Joseon soldiers had often been unable to respond effectively. The Training Agency incorporated musketeers alongside archers and close-combat troops, and the court encouraged the production of firearms and the training of men to use them. Provincial defense was also reorganized under a system sometimes referred to as the sogo organization, which grouped local militias into units attached to regional commands, thereby attempting to connect the countryside's manpower to a trained and organized structure rather than depending exclusively on ad hoc mobilization. Fortresses were repaired and strengthened, with particular attention to mountain strongholds that could serve as refuges and rallying points. The court also improved intelligence-gathering about Japanese movements, though as the next section shows, it remained vulnerable to confusing and conflicting information.

It is important not to exaggerate what these measures achieved. The Training Agency's units were small compared with the army needed to confront a large invasion, and the quality of training varied. Many fortifications were repaired only partially. Soldiers continued to be recruited through a system that placed heavy burdens on commoner households already suffering from the war, and desertion and evasion remained serious problems. The court also continued to be divided, with disagreements over strategy and over how much to rely on the Ming. The reforms did not transform Joseon into a first-rate military power. They did make it a different one from the state that had collapsed in the spring of 1592.

The difference is crucial to understanding the second invasion. In 1592, Japanese armies encountered a Joseon system that had no sustained wartime experience and little coordination between its defensive layers. By 1597, many officers and soldiers had learned from defeat, some units had received training in new methods, mountain fortresses had been prepared, and the court understood that a Japanese advance would require immediate and coordinated resistance. At the same time, Joseon's recovery was fragile, its fiscal base eroded, and its naval command structure, though still the most effective element of its forces, depended on a small number of leaders and on a court that did not always trust them. The truce years therefore produced neither a secure Joseon nor a defenseless one, and the outcome of the second invasion would depend on how those partial reforms interacted with the shock of renewed attack.

Historical Vocabulary
Wajo (Japanese-built fortresses)

Definition: In Korean historical writing, wajo (倭城) refers to the fortified bases constructed by Japanese forces on the southern coast of Korea, mainly during the occupation of 1593–1598. Japanese sources more often use the word jō (castle) with specific place names.

Historical context: Japanese commanders adapted techniques of castle construction used in Japan, including stone-faced walls, multiple enclosures, and positions on coastal heights near anchorages, to Korean terrain. They used Korean laborers and local materials, and they relied on supply by sea from Japan.

Why it matters: The wajo allowed Japan to hold ground without controlling the interior of Korea. They turned a failed inland offensive into a defensible foothold and made removal of Japanese forces enormously costly.

Potential misunderstanding: The existence of the fortresses does not mean Japan was winning. They were evidence of strategic retrenchment, and their effectiveness depended on access to the sea.

Section 35.4

Yi Sun-sin's Dismissal and the Crisis of Naval Command

The most consequential command crisis of the war's final phase occurred in the first months of 1597, just as Japan prepared to renew its campaign. It centered on Yi Sun-sin, commander of the Joseon naval forces of the three southern provinces, whose fleet had been the main obstacle to Japanese maritime operations since 1592. By the winter of 1596–1597, Yi held a position of unmatched authority at sea, and he also held a record of conflict with some colleagues at court and in the field, particularly the naval officer Won Gyun, with whom his relations had long been strained. Understanding why Yi was removed requires attention to several overlapping factors rather than a single cause.

The immediate episode involved intelligence. Sources describe a Japanese-associated informant, usually named Yoshira in the Korean record, who passed information to a Joseon officer suggesting that Katō Kiyomasa would soon cross from Japan to Korea with reinforcements and that Joseon forces could intercept the fleet at sea. The court, eager to strike a blow before the Japanese army reassembled, ordered Yi to intercept. Yi did not act, and he later explained or was understood to have explained his caution: the information came from a source associated with the Japanese, the sea route to the anticipated crossing was dangerous, and a fleet might be drawn into an ambush in unfamiliar waters. Katō's forces landed without being attacked. Many at court concluded that Yi had failed in his duty at a decisive moment.

The matter did not end there. Yi had accumulated critics who accused him of disobeying commands, and he was in open conflict with Won Gyun, who had himself been criticized for aggressive action elsewhere. Factional politics and personal rivalries shaped how the episode was framed. Yi was arrested, brought to the capital, and interrogated. Officials with various motives argued over his fate. He was spared execution and sentenced to serve as an ordinary soldier, the so-called "serving in white clothes" punishment, under the army official Gwon Yul. During his confinement, his mother died, an event he recorded in his diary.

Later Korean tradition came to interpret the affair as a successful Japanese plot: that the informant's information was designed to lure Yi into a trap or to provoke his dismissal, leaving the fleet to a weaker commander. Contemporary court officials themselves speculated that the episode might have been a Japanese deception, and some modern accounts treat it as established. Students should be careful here. The evidence shows that an informant associated with Konishi Yukinaga's camp supplied information, that Japanese commanders had reason to want Joseon's fleet weakened, and that the outcome benefited them. It does not conclusively show that Japan engineered Yi's removal, and the sources disagree about the informant's intentions and the court's reasoning. The episode may as readily reflect the confusion of wartime intelligence in which even honest sources produced ambiguous guidance.

Yi's dismissal is better explained institutionally. The court operated in an environment of constant uncertainty about enemy movements and intense pressure to demonstrate action, and it mistrusted commanders who appeared to disobey orders, particularly those who commanded independent forces far from the capital. A king who had fled his capital in 1592 and depended on his officers' obedience had reason to take insubordination seriously. Command rivalries between Yi and Won Gyun supplied accusers, and factional alignments turned a tactical judgment into a political crisis. Describing the outcome as the product of royal jealousy would simplify a complicated mixture of intelligence failure, institutional anxiety, factional conflict, and personal rivalry, and it would obscure what the episode reveals about how a state at war distrusted the very commanders it needed most.

Section 35.5

Chilcheollyang: Destruction of the Joseon Fleet

Won Gyun, appointed to replace Yi as naval commander, has entered popular memory as a villain, and the temptation to treat the battle of Chilcheollyang as his personal failure is strong. A more careful account begins with the position he inherited. He took command of a fleet and base system built by Yi over years of effort, at a time when army officials and the court expected him to carry the war to the Japanese. Gwon Yul, serving as dowonsu (supreme commander), and other senior army officials pressed him to attack Japanese positions near Busan, and the court shared their expectation that the navy would prevent reinforcement and supply. Won Gyun is reported to have had misgivings about the feasibility of such an operation, but he was in a difficult position: Yi's removal had been justified partly by his failure to act, and a successor who also refused would risk the same fate.

The fleet sailed eastward in the seventh lunar month of 1597. Accounts describe an exhausting passage through contrary winds and currents, with rowers worn down and sailors and soldiers thirsty and fatigued after long exertion. Japanese forces, informed of the approach, were prepared. The fleet withdrew and anchored near Chilcheollyang, a strait off Geoje Island, and there during the night and morning of the fifteenth to sixteenth day the Japanese attacked by sea and land. The Joseon ships, some of them burned and many abandoned, were overwhelmed. Command collapsed. Won Gyun, who had gone ashore with some of his men, is reported by most sources to have been killed there, though accounts of the manner of his death vary. Yi Eok-gi, another senior commander, died in the fighting. A relatively small number of ships, led by the officer Bae Seol, escaped.

The result was catastrophic. The navy that Yi Sun-sin had built, maintained, and led through nearly five years of action was largely destroyed in a single engagement. Ships, equipment, and trained crews were lost, and the principal base at Hansan Island fell. Historians regard the battle as the greatest Joseon naval defeat of the war, and its consequences were strategic as well as material. In Chapter 33, students saw how victory at Hansan in 1592 denied Japan unrestricted movement along the western coast, protected the grain-producing Jeolla region, and prevented the resupply of Japanese armies by the sea route. Chilcheollyang temporarily reopened that strategic possibility. With the Joseon fleet gone, Japanese naval forces could move westward and a Japanese army could advance into Jeolla with the prospect of naval support.

Historians nonetheless caution against a purely personal explanation. Won Gyun's behavior reflected pressure from superiors, a tactical situation in which a fleet exhausted by a long passage faced an enemy prepared to meet it, and a command structure that had been dismantled when Yi was removed. A more cautious commander might have fared better or might have been dismissed. What the episode shows with greater certainty is the fragility of a naval system dependent on specific leaders and local knowledge, and the cost of political decisions made without regard to those facts.

Section 35.6

The Second Invasion, 1597

The renewed Japanese invasion of 1597 was organized differently from the first, and its objectives were narrower. Japanese forces, estimated in contemporary and modern sources to number well over one hundred thousand, though exact figures vary, were divided into two main armies, a right army and a left army, under senior commanders including Mōri Hidemoto, Ukita Hideie, Katō Kiyomasa, Kuroda Nagamasa, and others, with Kobayakawa Hideaki holding nominal overall command. They were assembled at and around the southeastern bases that had been held since 1593, supplemented by forces crossing from Japan.

The strategic aim was not a rapid march to Pyongyang or the Yalu. Hideyoshi's orders, as preserved in Japanese records and understood by Korean historians, directed his commanders to secure the southern provinces, particularly Jeolla, to destroy the resistance bases and agricultural resources that supported Joseon's military effort, and to establish a defensible territorial holding. The Japanese had learned that a long and exposed supply line to the north was vulnerable. They sought instead to consolidate a zone in the south that could be held and, if negotiations resumed, used as a bargaining position. This was a different war in purpose and geography from the invasion of 1592.

It was also, in several respects, a more harshly conducted one. Commanders were reportedly required to submit proof of kills in the form of severed noses, a practice discussed in Section 35.14, and campaigns through Gyeongsang and Jeolla were accompanied by destruction of settlements, seizure of grain, and enslavement of local populations. The Japanese objective was partly to deny the allies resources by devastating the countryside. The consequences for villages and districts in the line of advance were severe. A full discussion of civilian experience follows in Section 35.14, but students should keep in mind that military and civilian history cannot be separated in this phase.

The opposing allied force was not the Joseon of 1592. Ming forces were already on the peninsula, with a supreme commander, Xing Jie, and field commanders including Yang Hao and Ma Gui coordinating a Ming army that had been reinforced after the failure of negotiations. Joseon officers and soldiers, many of them veterans, were organized under unified command arrangements closer to those of an allied army than the disjointed provincial forces of 1592. Rebuilt fortresses and local militia networks created additional layers of resistance. The result was that Japanese forces advancing in 1597 encountered resistance at fortress sites and, ultimately, a coordinated Ming–Joseon field force that checked their northward movement.

Section 35.7

Hwangseoksanseong and Namwon: Disaster in the South

Two fortress battles of the eighth lunar month of 1597 showed that improved preparation did not by itself prevent disaster. Hwangseoksanseong, a mountain fortress in the Yeongnam–Honam border region, had been repaired and garrisoned in the expectation that it would block the Japanese advance into Jeolla. Its defenders, including officials such as Gwak Jun and Jo Jong-do, resisted determinedly, but the fortress fell after fighting in which the defending force was killed or dispersed. Its loss opened one route into the Jeolla region.

Namwon, a walled town in Jeolla, deserves fuller treatment because it illustrates a failure mode of coalition warfare. Its defenders included Joseon officers and soldiers, local residents who had taken refuge within the walls, and a Ming garrison under commanders including Yang Yuan. The Korean Academy of Korean Studies dates the battle to the thirteenth through sixteenth days of the eighth lunar month and records the deaths of numerous Ming and Joseon officers. The Japanese force that besieged it was large, and its commanders used the approach and the town's construction against it, with accounts describing the building of earthworks and the use of ladders and fire to overcome the walls. The town fell after a short siege and was followed by a massacre of those within the walls.

Students should note what made Namwon different from the allied successes of 1593. At Pyongyang, a Ming army with artillery and a large force had attacked a Japanese garrison. At Namwon, a smaller allied force defended a town against a much larger Japanese army, and Ming and Joseon troops, despite their efforts, were overwhelmed. Reinforcement did not reach the town in time, and commanders disagreed about whether it could be held. The episode shows that the Ming–Joseon alliance was not a guarantee of victory: it could provide defenders, but not always enough of them or soon enough, and its coordination remained imperfect.

The civilian dimension is inseparable from the battle. Namwon's population, along with refugees from surrounding districts, was within the walls when the town fell. Sources describe widespread killing of combatants and noncombatants and the taking of noses as proof of kills. Because the records do not permit precise counts, and because early reports of casualties were often inflated, this textbook does not offer an exact total. What can be said with confidence is that the fall of Namwon was a disaster for the Ming and Joseon forces and for the civilians within the town, and that its memory remained powerful in the region for centuries.

Section 35.8

Jiksan and the Limits of the Second Advance

After Namwon and Hwangseoksanseong, Japanese armies moved through Jeolla and then north toward Chungcheong, with the potential to threaten Seoul. In the ninth lunar month, allied forces encountered them near Jiksan, in what is also remembered through the nearby place name Sosa. Ming forces under commanders including Xie Sheng (解生; Korean sources read the name as Hae Saeng) and others, supported by Joseon units, engaged the Japanese vanguard. Some English-language histories render the name "Jie Sheng"; this textbook uses Hanyu Pinyin, in which 解 is read Xie when it is a surname. Neither side suffered the near-annihilation seen at Namwon. The Ming force, which included cavalry, checked the Japanese advance, and the encounter proved sufficient to persuade Japanese commanders to halt their northward movement.

The significance of Jiksan lies in what it showed rather than in the scale of the fighting. It was not a gigantic decisive battle that single-handedly saved Seoul, and students should be cautious of accounts that present it as such. Its importance is that Japanese forces could no longer advance north as they had in 1592. The allied army was organized, positioned, and prepared to resist, and Japanese commanders, whose supply lines extended through a countryside they had devastated, chose to contract rather than risk a battle far from their coastal bases. The advance had reached, and did not pass, the limit of what Japan could achieve in this phase.

This is the land war's turning point, and it corresponds roughly to what Myeongnyang would accomplish at sea within weeks. Taken together, the two episodes meant that the Japanese objective of securing Jeolla and establishing a defensible position in the south had been only partially achieved. Japan held the southeast but not the west, and it could not advance on the capital. The combination of a checked land advance and a restored naval barrier forced a strategic reassessment, which the following sections trace.

Section 35.9

Yi Sun-sin Returns to a Ruined Navy

After Chilcheollyang, the court restored Yi Sun-sin to command of the naval forces. The decision reflected both desperation and recognition that no other officer had his experience of southern waters and his record. Yi, who had been serving as an ordinary soldier under Gwon Yul, traveled through the southwest, inspecting the surviving forces and attempting to gather sailors, ships, guns, and supplies. His diary, the Nanjung ilgi, records the process in terse and personal entries: marching through burned and abandoned districts, receiving reports of Japanese movements, and assembling the survivors.

The navy he found was a fragment. Official Korean history records twelve surviving warships at this point, a number that Yi himself reported to the court. Later narratives of the battle of Myeongnyang commonly speak of thirteen ships, and the difference reflects timing and classification: one more vessel may have been added or recovered before the engagement, and the term "warship" can include differently sized craft. Students should therefore treat the numbers as indications of the order of magnitude, not as an unquestionable accounting.

At court, officials considered whether a naval force of this size was worth maintaining. The suggestion was made, reportedly, that the surviving sailors be incorporated into the army and the naval effort abandoned in favor of defense on land. Yi's response, as preserved in his report to the king, was that his remaining ships still made resistance at sea possible and that if the navy were abandoned the Japanese would be free to sail up the western coast. He argued that a determined defense could still deny them that movement. This reply is among the best known of the war and is the basis of the first Primary Source Window below.

The commonly repeated formula that Yi faced three hundred ships with twelve is, however, a useful illustration of why students should be careful with received numbers. Contemporary and later sources give different totals for the Japanese fleet, and the numbers of warships, transports, and supporting craft are rarely distinguished in popular retellings. The comparison of twelve with several hundred is a rhetorical statement of the disparity, not a documentary measurement of it, and it should not be repeated without noting its uncertainty. The more important historical point is that Yi proposed to fight with a small force by choosing the ground carefully, and that the court allowed him to try.

Historical Vocabulary
Janggye and Nanjung ilgi

Definition: A janggye (狀啓) is a formal written report sent by a provincial or military commander to the king and court. The Nanjung ilgi (亂中日記, "War Diary") is Yi Sun-sin's personal diary, kept during the war and published long afterward.

Historical context: A janggye was an official document, composed for an audience of superiors, and it shaped its account to inform, justify, or request. The diary was a private record, written for the author himself, containing reflections, family news, weather, and doubts.

Why it matters: Students who use Yi's writings must identify which kind of text they are citing. A formal report and a private diary may describe the same event in different terms and for different purposes, and blending them risks attributing the diary's candor to the report or the report's formality to the diary.

Potential misunderstanding: Neither text is a neutral record. Both were written by an interested participant, and the transmission of both texts through later editions requires attention to textual history.

Section 35.10

Myeongnyang: Geography, Command, and Survival

The Battle of Myeongnyang took place on the sixteenth day of the ninth lunar month of 1597, in late October by the Gregorian calendar, in the Myeongnyang Strait, between the southwestern tip of the peninsula and Jindo Island. The strait is narrow, with a channel that constricts the passage of ships, and it is subject to strong and reversing tidal currents, sometimes called by the local name Uldolmok, "the roaring channel." These conditions were well known to local sailors and to Yi.

Yi's decision to fight there was strategic. The Japanese fleet was advancing westward, intending to support the army in Jeolla and to sail up the western coast. In open water a fleet many times the size of his would encircle his small force. In the strait, only a limited number of Japanese ships could engage at a time. Yi positioned his ships to take advantage of that constriction, and he is reported to have addressed his commanders with grim determination, though the famous words attributed to him in later retellings should be treated carefully unless traced to a specific source. According to accounts, many of his officers hesitated when the Japanese fleet appeared, and some ships held back, leaving Yi's flagship to bear the initial pressure.

The battle's details are drawn from Yi's own report and diary, from later Korean narratives, and from Japanese accounts, which differ. What the sources agree on is that Yi's flagship fought at the front, that the Joseon ships used artillery to damage Japanese ships as they approached through the channel, that the current's direction shifted during the engagement, and that Japanese ships were disordered and damaged. A Japanese commander, Kurushima Michifusa, is recorded as having been killed, and his death is generally treated as having shaken Japanese morale. The Japanese fleet withdrew from the strait, and Yi's ships, though damaged, survived.

The strength of the Japanese force is a matter of uncertainty. Korean reports speak of well over a hundred warships with additional craft. Some Japanese and modern estimates give lower figures, and distinctions between combat and support vessels are not consistent. Students should avoid repeating a single count as established fact and should instead treat the battle as a clash between a very small Joseon squadron and a Japanese fleet several times larger, with the exact ratio unknown.

Students should also avoid presenting the current as an automatic weapon. Yi's success depended on a combination of factors: the choice of battlefield, the concentration of artillery fire on a bottleneck, disciplined action by the ships that did fight, local hydrographic knowledge, the morale of sailors who had to hold their ground, and the Japanese inability to bring their numbers to bear simultaneously. The current favored whoever used it better, and the Japanese were attacking into unfamiliar water. Even so, the outcome was not predetermined; an accident, a failure of nerve, or a slightly different arrangement might have produced a different result.

The consequences were significant but limited. Myeongnyang restored a crucial maritime barrier: the Japanese fleet did not advance up the west coast in force, and the planned coordination between land and sea operations in Jeolla collapsed. It did not, however, end the second invasion by itself. Japanese forces remained in the south, and the Joseon fleet was still small and badly damaged. Yi withdrew northward to rebuild, and in the aftermath the Japanese, exacting vengeance, raided coastal settlements, an episode in which Yi's youngest son was killed at Asan. The victory preserved the possibility of continued naval resistance, which in the following year would be integrated into a larger allied effort.

Section 35.11

From Inland Advance to the Wajo Fortress Belt

After Jiksan and Myeongnyang, Japanese operations contracted toward the southern coast. Commanders withdrew from the more exposed positions in Jeolla and Chungcheong and concentrated on building and reinforcing an arc of fortified bases stretching from the Ulsan area in the east westward toward Suncheon. These wajo became the physical anchor of Japan's position for the remainder of the war.

The fortresses were not signs that Japan had been defeated outright. They were formidable coastal bridgeheads, designed to make removal extraordinarily costly. Their walls, adapted from Japanese castle design, were built on commanding terrain with multiple enclosures, and they were supplied from Japan across the Korea Strait. A garrison in a wajo could be reinforced or withdrawn by sea, which meant that an attacking land army faced a position whose lifelines it could not cut unless it also controlled the water. That was the importance of naval power in this phase: a restored Joseon fleet, together with Ming naval forces, offered the only means of threatening the fortresses' connection to Japan.

Geography again mattered. The southern coast's irregular shoreline, sheltered bays, and surrounding hills gave defenders good sites for fortification and good anchorages, while the winter climate, the rough terrain, and the long supply lines from the Ming and Joseon interior made siege operations difficult. The Japanese commanders also controlled the local labor that had built their fortifications and, in some places, the surrounding countryside, which they exploited for grain and manpower.

This strategic shift changed the character of the war. The contest was no longer about whether Japan could conquer Korea, which it manifestly could not, but about whether the allies could expel an entrenched enemy and whether Japan could maintain its garrisons. Both sides faced rising costs, and neither could easily impose a decision. The allied sieges examined in the next sections tested that proposition.

Section 35.12

Ulsan: The Limits of Allied Siege Warfare

The first allied attempt to break the fortress belt came at Ulsan, at the eastern end, at the turn of the year 1597–1598. The fortress there, known in Korean sources by the name of its hill, Dosan, was held by Katō Kiyomasa, whose men had only begun to complete its defenses. A Ming–Joseon army under Ming commanders including Yang Hao and Ma Gui approached and attacked, drawing on troops that had advanced from the north. The allies nearly isolated the garrison. Katō's forces were reduced to severe shortages of food, and, according to accounts, water, and the defenders suffered greatly in the winter cold.

The siege nonetheless failed. Winter conditions were harsh for the besiegers as well, and their own supply lines were stretched. Disagreements among allied commanders about when and how to press the assault weakened coordination, and Japanese relief forces from neighboring fortresses approached by land and sea. The allied army withdrew rather than risk being caught between the fortress and the relieving force. The garrison survived, having suffered heavily but not having been destroyed.

A second allied effort against Ulsan, mounted during the autumn offensive of 1598, likewise failed to eliminate the fortress. Together, the two attempts demonstrate why Ulsan is essential to understanding the war's final phase. The siege exposed the reverse side of coalition warfare: Ming numerical strength and Joseon local support did not automatically translate into success when the target was a prepared fortress. Allied forces had to coordinate across language, institutional, and operational differences, to supply an army in difficult terrain and weather, and to contend with an enemy who could be reinforced by sea. Coalitions are strongest when their components' strengths complement each other, and weakest when the demands of siege warfare expose disagreements and logistical limits.

The siege also illustrates the human cost of this phase on all sides. Japanese defenders suffered hunger and exposure, and many reportedly died. Ming and Joseon soldiers suffered cold and disease. Local populations, whose labor and grain were demanded by both sides, endured further hardship. The war's final stage was a contest of endurance as much as of tactics.

Section 35.13

The 1598 Allied Offensive: Sacheon and Suncheon

In the autumn of 1598, the allies attempted a broader and more coordinated effort. Ming commanders, reinforced and reorganized under Xing Jie's overall direction, divided their forces into several columns directed at different parts of the fortress belt: an eastern force against Ulsan, a central force against Sacheon, a western force against Suncheon, and a naval force that included Chen Lin's Ming fleet and Yi Sun-sin's Joseon ships. Joseon units operated alongside each of these. The design reflected an understanding that the fortresses had to be attacked simultaneously, so that the Japanese could not shift reinforcements from one to another and that naval pressure could cut the fortresses' lines of supply.

The outcomes were mixed. At Sacheon, a Ming force assaulted a fortress held by the Shimazu, and the engagement turned against the attackers. Accounts describe an accidental explosion in the Ming ranks that disrupted the assault and a Japanese counterattack that inflicted heavy losses on the withdrawing Ming and Joseon troops. At Suncheon, at the western end of the belt, the Ming land commander Liu Ting approached the fortress known as Waegyo, held by Konishi Yukinaga, while naval forces moved into the bay. The attack did not succeed, and Liu Ting withdrew rather than press on. The second effort at Ulsan also failed.

Japanese defensive works proved difficult to reduce, and coalition coordination remained imperfect. Allied columns did not always act in unison, communications were limited, and the Ming reliance on massed assault met entrenched resistance. The offensive was nonetheless significant in that it applied pressure at multiple points and forced Japanese commanders to remain in their fortresses. It also set the stage for the Suncheon garrison's role in the war's last phase: Konishi Yukinaga's force, blockaded by sea and facing a Ming land army, became the object of the final naval campaign.

The offensive also underscored a broader point about the limits of external intervention. The Ming army could contain and pressure Japanese forces, and Ming naval and land contributions were substantial. But Ming commanders operated far from their bases, with their own logistical and political constraints, and could not simply dictate the war's outcome. Students should avoid treating Ming forces as either saviors who rescued Joseon or as ineffective outsiders: they were a large and capable but imperfectly coordinated force whose presence changed the war without ending it.

Section 35.14

War against Civilians: Destruction, Mutilation, and Captivity

The war's most enduring human consequences fell on civilians, and a chapter that narrates battles without them misrepresents the war. The second invasion, in particular, was a campaign of devastation. Japanese armies burned settlements and crops, requisitioned grain, killed those who resisted or were suspected of aiding resistance, and seized people. The allied armies, though acting in defense, also made heavy demands on local populations, taking grain, labor, and shelter. Cultivation collapsed in the regions of heaviest fighting, and famine followed. Epidemic disease, spread by movement of troops and refugees and by hunger, killed many who had survived the fighting. Burial of the dead was often impossible, and records describe unburied bodies along roads and around fortress walls.

A distinctive and well-documented feature of the second invasion was the taking of noses and ears. Japanese commanders were reportedly required to provide evidence of kills, and because heads were too bulky to transport in the numbers involved, noses and in some cases ears were cut off, salted, and shipped to Japan in barrels. The practice was applied to dead combatants but also, according to Korean and some Japanese sources, to civilians, including women and children, and to people who were alive when mutilated. Receipts for such shipments survive in Japanese records. In Kyoto, the Mimizuka (the "ear mound," in fact a mound for noses) was built in 1597 to enshrine them and survives today. Historians interpret the practice as both a system for verifying service and rewarding commanders and as a means of terrorizing the population.

Historical Vocabulary
Mimizuka and nose-taking

Definition: Mimizuka (耳塚, "ear mound") is the name of a burial mound in Kyoto containing noses taken from Koreans during the Imjin War. Despite the name, the remains are generally understood to be noses; the term "ear mound" reflects later usage.

Historical context: The practice of taking body parts as proof of kills had precedents in Japanese warfare, but its scale in Korea was exceptional. Surviving receipts record shipments to Japan, and the mound was constructed at the order of Hideyoshi's regime.

Why it matters: The Mimizuka is physical evidence of the war's violence against combatants and civilians, and it remains a site of memory and dispute in Korean–Japanese relations.

Potential misunderstanding: The mound does not provide a reliable count of the dead, and the number of noses it contains is debated. It also should not be understood as representing all victims, many of whom were killed without being counted.

Captivity was a separate but related phenomenon. Large numbers of Koreans, particularly in the second invasion, were seized and transported to Japan. The Academy of Korean Studies explicitly notes that estimates of the total vary widely and that the reasons people were seized differed. Estimates range from tens of thousands upward, with some higher figures, but none can be treated as secure. Some captives were enslaved or sold, within Japan and, according to some European sources, to foreign traders. Some became servants, farm laborers, or household members. Some artisans, including potters and printers, were retained for their skills, and certain regional ceramic traditions in Japan trace their origins to Korean potters brought during the war. A minority of captives eventually returned to Korea, some through diplomatic and ransom efforts in the early seventeenth century, but many remained in Japan for the rest of their lives.

Students should resist framing this history as a story of cultural transfer. Technologies and skills did move, and Korean craftsmen did contribute to Japanese ceramics and other trades, but that movement occurred through coercion. The people involved were removed from their homes, separated from families, and in many cases enslaved or compelled to labor. Describing their displacement primarily in terms of what it gave Japan erases their experience and the harm done to Korean communities that lost them.

Section 35.15

Hideyoshi Dies and Japan Orders Withdrawal

Toyotomi Hideyoshi died on the eighteenth day of the eighth lunar month of 1598 (in the Japanese calendar, the eighteenth day of the eighth month, in September by the Gregorian count). His death changed the political calculus immediately. He had been the sole architect of the invasion and the only figure with the authority to command its continuation, and he left a young heir, Toyotomi Hideyori, and a regency council of senior lords, including Tokugawa Ieyasu, who were already preoccupied with the succession and with their own rivalries. Those in Japan who had borne the war's costs, in manpower, money, and political capital, now had little reason to prolong it.

The council kept the death secret for a time but issued orders for the withdrawal of Japanese forces from Korea. Commanders in the fortresses began to receive instructions to evacuate, and they undertook negotiations, in some cases through intermediaries, to arrange safe departure. The Ming and Joseon commanders learned of the death and the withdrawal order at different times and with differing degrees of certainty, which affected their decisions in the final months.

Students should make an important causal distinction. Hideyoshi's death triggered the withdrawal order; it did not create the military difficulties that made withdrawal sensible. By 1598, the invasion had failed to secure decisive territorial results. Japanese armies were confined largely to fortified southern positions, the naval barrier had been restored, the allies had resisted every major advance, and the war was imposing continuing costs on Japan's fighters and on the regions that supplied them. A different Japanese ruler might have chosen to continue regardless, or might have sought a negotiated settlement from the fortress belt, but those options were available to a ruler with Hideyoshi's authority and ambition, not to a council facing a succession. This distinction avoids two extremes: the claim that Japan was militarily destroyed, and the claim that only Hideyoshi's death mattered.

Historical Debate

What Actually Ended the Imjin War?

The question: The war ended in the winter of 1598 with Japanese withdrawal, but historians disagree about what to place at the center of the explanation. The question distinguishes two problems: why an invasion failed, and why a war ended when it did.

Interpretation A — Military stalemate and allied resistance: Some historians emphasize that the war had already reached an impasse. Joseon's recovery, Ming intervention, Yi Sun-sin's naval successes, and local resistance prevented Japan from achieving its aims, and the costs of continued occupation were rising. On this view, withdrawal was the logical outcome of military conditions.
Interpretation B — Hideyoshi's death as the indispensable trigger: Others stress that stalemates can persist for years, and that Hideyoshi, who had shown himself willing to bear great costs, would not have ordered withdrawal. His death, and the political circumstances it created, were the decisive event.
Interpretation C — Integrating the two: A third approach holds that military conditions prevented Japan from achieving Hideyoshi's original aims, while his death transformed a difficult stalemate into an actual withdrawal order. Neither factor alone is sufficient.

Evidence: The interpretations draw on the same body of evidence: Japanese campaign records and orders, Korean and Ming dispatches, diaries, and accounts of the fortress sieges. They differ in how they weigh counterfactuals, since the evidence cannot show what would have happened had Hideyoshi lived.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement is largely one of causation and emphasis, not of chronology or fact. It also reflects differences in national historiographies, which have at times emphasized the heroism of Korean resistance or the contingency of Japanese decisions.

Why it matters: How historians weigh these factors affects their understanding of the war's significance, of the effectiveness of Joseon and Ming resistance, and of the fragility of Japanese power. This textbook adopts the integrated view while recognizing its limits.

Student task: Construct the strongest argument for each interpretation, then identify what additional evidence would help determine which factor was more important. Consider what a counterfactual in which Hideyoshi lived another year would require historians to assume.

Section 35.16

Noryang: The Last Battle and Yi Sun-sin's Death

Withdrawal was not simple. Japanese forces in the fortress belt needed to reach their ships and cross the strait, and allied commanders debated whether to let them leave. Konishi Yukinaga's garrison at Suncheon, blockaded and facing a Ming land army, was in a particularly exposed position. Sources suggest that Konishi negotiated with Ming commanders for passage and that some Ming officers were receptive, while Yi Sun-sin and others argued that allowing the enemy to depart unharmed was unacceptable after seven years of devastation. The details of these negotiations are murky, and sources attribute different motives to different participants.

A Japanese fleet, including forces under commanders such as Shimazu Yoshihiro, gathered to relieve Suncheon and to cover the evacuation. A combined Joseon–Ming fleet under Yi Sun-sin and the Ming admiral Chen Lin moved to intercept them in the Noryang Strait, between Namhae Island and the mainland. The Academy of Korean Studies identifies the battle as the final naval battle of the war and describes it as a combined Joseon–Ming operation. The fighting took place at night and into the morning on the eighteenth to nineteenth days of the eleventh lunar month, in mid-December 1598. Japanese ships attempted to break through the strait, and the allied fleet attacked them with artillery and boarding actions in confused fighting. Ming commanders suffered losses, and the senior Ming officer Deng Zilong was among those killed.

Yi Sun-sin was struck by a gunshot during the battle and died. Later tradition holds that he ordered his death concealed so that his men would continue fighting without disruption. This tradition is long-established and powerful in Korean commemoration, and it appears in early accounts, but students should treat it as a commemorative tradition rather than as a verified record of his words. This textbook does not reproduce dialogue attributed to him unless a specific source is cited. What is secure is that he died at Noryang and that his death was a major loss to the allied fleet and to Joseon.

Most importantly, Yi's death did not end the war, and Noryang was not the cause of Japanese withdrawal. The withdrawal was already underway when the battle was fought. Noryang was the final major battle during that withdrawal, the last attempt to punish a departing enemy. Its outcome was mixed: Japanese forces suffered significant losses, but many reached their ships, and Konishi's garrison escaped by a different route. The war ended as the last Japanese forces left the peninsula. Yi was honored posthumously, and in later centuries he became among the most celebrated figures in Korean history, a development that students should understand as part of the memory of the war rather than as part of its course.

Section 35.17

What Seven Years of War Left Behind

When the armies stopped fighting, the people, landscape, and institutions of Joseon bore the marks of seven years of war. Much of the southern and central peninsula had been devastated. Farmland had been abandoned or destroyed, villages burned, and herds and tools lost. Populations had been reduced by killing, famine, disease, flight, and captivity, though no reliable demographic total survives. Towns, temples, and official buildings, including palaces in Seoul, had been damaged or destroyed, and records and libraries were lost, complicating the work of rebuilding the state's knowledge of its own population and land.

The fiscal consequences were severe. Early seventeenth-century registers record an extraordinary contraction in taxable cultivated land, to a fraction of prewar levels. Students should treat these administrative figures with care: they measure land registered for taxation, not literal acreage destroyed, and they reflect abandonment, concealment, and the state's loss of control over its records as well as physical devastation. They nonetheless indicate a crisis of revenue and of social order that took decades to repair. Tax registers had to be reconstructed, land resurveyed, and burdens redistributed, and those processes generated political conflict that extended into the following reigns.

Military consequences were mixed. The war had demonstrated the weakness of the old system and the value of trained, firearm-equipped units, and the Training Agency and related reforms continued to shape Joseon's military organization. Veterans of the war, militia networks, and fortress garrisons remained. But the army was also exhausted, its finances depleted, and its dependence on Ming support a lasting political fact. The Joseon court had been saved from destruction by a Ming army, and gratitude toward the Ming, together with ideological commitment to the Ming order, would shape its choices in the decades that followed.

The captive question remained unresolved. People such as Kang Hang returned, and some were ransomed or escaped, but many did not. Families searched for the missing and waited for news. Efforts to recover captives became one reason that diplomatic contact with post-Toyotomi Japan gradually resumed. That process extended into the early seventeenth century, and its terms will be considered in the context of Joseon's wider foreign relations. The Kang Hang case shows what return could mean: a man who had spent nearly three years in Japan came back to a society struggling with the moral, familial, and practical problems of reintegrating people who had spent years in captivity, while the state itself was still rebuilding.

For the belligerents, the consequences differed. Joseon was physically devastated and militarily transformed. Japan entered the political struggle that ultimately produced Tokugawa rule, in which commanders who had fought in Korea took opposing sides, and the invasion's failure contributed to a changed political environment, though students should be cautious about reducing that transformation to the war alone. The Ming had expended major resources in Korea, at a time when it faced other fiscal and military demands. It would be wrong, however, to say that the Imjin War directly caused the fall of the Ming or directly caused the rise of the Manchus. That larger geopolitical transformation belongs to Chapter 36, which examines the rise of the Jurchen-Manchu state and the invasions that followed.

The war's end was thus not a restoration. Noryang was the final battle, but the real ending of the story lies in what remained: fields to be replanted, registers to be rebuilt, prisoners to be recovered, a court to be reconciled with its own failures, and an East Asian order in which Joseon's position had changed. The experience of the war also entered memory, in monuments, genealogies, temple records, and commemorative narratives that shaped how later generations understood the conflict.

Multiple Perspectives

Living the End of the War: Seven Experiences

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns historians have identified across many sources. They are not quotations from any named individual, and they do not claim to record anyone's actual thoughts.

A survivor of Chilcheollyang: A sailor or rower who escaped the destruction of the fleet experienced it as the collapse of a command he had trusted and of a base that had seemed secure. For such a person, the news that Yi Sun-sin had been restored might have been a source of hope or of dread, since a navy of a dozen ships could hardly promise safety.
Yi Sun-sin rebuilding the navy: A commander recalled from disgrace, bereaved of his mother, and facing the loss of his fleet had to persuade a court, gather scattered men, and decide where to fight. His documents show a man who understood geography and the limits of his force, and who knew that the court could withdraw its support again.
A civilian at Namwon or Hwangseoksanseong: Villagers who took refuge within fortress walls were trusting that the walls and the allied garrison would protect them. For those who died or were seized when the fortresses fell, the war's coalition was not a promise of safety but a gamble that failed.
A Ming soldier outside Ulsan: A soldier from northern or southern China, far from home, faced winter cold, short supplies, and an enemy in a prepared fortress. For him the war was an assignment in a foreign land, and the failure to take Ulsan meant retreat and further hardship, not glory.
A Japanese soldier inside a coastal wajo: A garrison member in a fortress on a foreign shore faced hunger, cold, and the knowledge of being surrounded. For him the war had become a matter of survival, and the withdrawal order was a release, though he did not know whether the sea route home would be open.
A captive transported to Japan: A person seized from a village experienced removal from family, language, and landscape, and subjection to new masters. Some were sold; some were put to work; some, like skilled artisans, were valued. Their experience varied enormously, but all of them lost the world they knew, and many never returned.
A family member searching for someone who never returned: A parent, spouse, or child in a ruined village waited for news of a person who had been killed, seized, or lost in the confusion of flight. Without records or reliable information, the uncertainty could last for years, and in many cases never ended.

Historical note: These groups did not have equal power or equal capacity to shape events. Commanders and courts made decisions; soldiers and civilians bore consequences. The purpose of the exercise is to recover the human range of the war's ending, not to equate its experiences.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From the Spring of 1592 to the Winter of 1598

What Changed

  • Japan's objective shifted from conquest of the whole peninsula and beyond to securing a defensible southern zone and then to withdrawal
  • Joseon's military moved from a largely unprepared system to one with Ming-influenced units, reorganized provincial defense, and experienced officers
  • The war changed from a rapid northward advance to a contest of fortresses, coastal positions, and naval barriers
  • The Ming went from distant observer to major combatant, and the war became a multinational coalition conflict
  • The conduct of the war toward civilians became more systematically destructive, with organized mutilation and mass capture

What Stayed the Same

  • The importance of the sea: control or denial of maritime supply shaped outcomes in both invasions
  • Dependence of Joseon on a combination of regular forces, local militias, and Ming support
  • The heavy burden on commoner households for grain, labor, and military service
  • Tension between court expectations and field conditions in the direction of commanders

Pattern and pace: Change was uneven and often abrupt: the destruction of the fleet and its partial recovery occurred within weeks, while institutional reform and demographic recovery took years or decades. Commanders and court officials experienced the shifts of strategy most directly; farmers and displaced families experienced the continuity of hardship. The pattern matters because it shows that the war's end was not a return to the pre-war condition but the outcome of a seven-year transformation.

Cause and Effect

Why the Second Invasion Failed to Repeat 1592

Long-term causes

Japan's logistical dependence on sea supply; Joseon's accumulated war experience; the survival of local resistance networks; the Ming commitment to Korea's defense; the geography of southern Korea, favoring defense at fortresses and straits.

Short-term causes

The Training Agency's reforms and fortress repair; the allied coordination established under Xing Jie and Ming field commanders; Hideyoshi's narrower strategic aim; the restoration of Yi Sun-sin to command.

Immediate trigger and events

The failure of the Ming investiture mission in 1596 led to renewed invasion. Chilcheollyang opened the way; Namwon and Hwangseoksanseong fell; Jiksan and Myeongnyang checked the advance.

Immediate effects

Japanese contraction into the wajo belt; failed allied sieges; devastation of civilians in Gyeongsang, Jeolla, and Chungcheong; large-scale captivity.

Long-term consequences

Fiscal and demographic crisis in Joseon; military reform; a captive and diplomatic legacy; a changed relationship with Ming and Japan; the context for the crises of Chapter 36.

Historical complexity

No single battle, commander, or decision explains the outcome. Contingency mattered, as at Myeongnyang and Chilcheollyang, as did structural factors.

Why one cause is not enough: Attributing the failure to Yi Sun-sin alone ignores the land war and the Ming; attributing it to the Ming ignores Joseon's recovery; attributing it to Japanese overreach ignores that Japanese forces won significant victories in 1597. The failure was the product of the interaction of these conditions.

Everyday Life and Social History

Fields, Households, and the Aftermath of War

For most people in southern Korea, the war's ending was experienced through the condition of the land and the household. Abandoned fields grew over with weeds or were stripped of crops; seed grain, draft animals, and tools had been requisitioned, eaten, or destroyed. Households that had fled returned, when they could, to find houses burned and neighbors dead or missing. Famine and epidemic disease followed the armies, and bodies lay unburied in many places. Burial, mourning, and the performance of ancestral rites, central to Joseon social life, were disrupted, and families struggled to carry them out for the dead whose remains could not be found.

Women and children were among the displaced and the captured, and they experienced the war in distinct ways. Women faced the risk of sexual violence, separation from husbands and fathers, and the burdens of supporting households alone. Children were seized, orphaned, or lost. Enslaved and bonded people (nobi), already at the bottom of the social order, were exposed both to capture and to the opportunities that war's disorder sometimes created, including flight and, in some cases, state-sanctioned release in return for service. The destruction of records, including household and tax registers and, in some places, the documents that attested to status, created both hardship and opportunity for those whose standing depended on paper.

Most of the sources for these experiences were written by officials, scholars, and commanders, and they show ordinary people chiefly as objects of policy or victims of violence. Historians reconstruct everyday life by reading such sources for indirect evidence, and by drawing on genealogies, local gazetteers, temple records, petitions, and the later writings of survivors. The result is a picture that is incomplete, but that makes clear that the war's ending, for most people, meant not a moment of peace but the beginning of a long and difficult recovery.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has followed the final phase of the Imjin War from the unresolved stalemate of 1593 to the departure of the last Japanese forces in 1598, and it has asked why the second invasion failed to reproduce the victories of 1592 and how seven years of war ended without a simple military victory. Its central argument is that the second invasion took place in a changed strategic environment, and that the war ended through the combination of military stalemate and the political decision that followed Hideyoshi's death.

The truce years of 1593–1596 were not peace. Japanese forces remained in southeastern fortresses, the countryside suffered continued hardship, and negotiations between Ming and Japanese intermediaries concealed a fundamental incompatibility: Ming expected submission within its tributary framework, Hideyoshi expected concessions, and Joseon expected withdrawal. When the Ming mission to Japan exposed the gap in 1596, Hideyoshi resumed the war. The breakdown was not simply a matter of insult over a title but of irreconcilable understandings of what settlement meant.

During the truce, Joseon undertook partial military reform through the Training Agency, Ming-influenced drill, expansion of firearms use, and reorganization of provincial defense. These measures did not make Joseon a strong military power, but they made it different from the unprepared state of 1592. The reforms were limited by fiscal strain, uneven training, and continued court division.

The removal of Yi Sun-sin in early 1597 reflected an intelligence failure, institutional anxiety, factional politics, and command rivalry, and not clearly a proven Japanese plot. Its consequence was the disaster at Chilcheollyang, where Won Gyun's fleet was destroyed under pressure from superiors and in difficult circumstances. The battle reopened the western sea to Japan and exposed the fragility of a naval system dependent on specific leaders.

The second invasion had narrower aims than the first: to secure the south, particularly Jeolla, and to destroy resistance bases. It was also harsher toward civilians. The fall of Hwangseoksanseong and Namwon showed that improved preparation and Ming participation did not guarantee success at fortress defense. But the Japanese advance was checked near Jiksan, where it became clear that Japan could no longer march north as in 1592.

At sea, Yi Sun-sin was restored to command of a fragment of a fleet, argued against abandoning naval resistance, and fought at Myeongnyang in the ninth lunar month of 1597. The battle's outcome depended on choice of ground, artillery, morale, and local knowledge, not on the current alone. It restored a crucial maritime barrier, though it did not by itself end the invasion. Differing ship counts illustrate why students should treat received numbers with caution.

Japanese forces then contracted into the wajo fortress belt, and allied attempts to reduce it at Ulsan, Sacheon, and Suncheon failed. The failures revealed the limits of coalition warfare against prepared fortifications supplied by sea. Meanwhile, the war's human cost, including destruction of villages and fields, famine, epidemic, mutilation, enslavement, and captivity, fell heavily on civilians. Kang Hang's account of captivity provides a rare direct source, while its limits remind students that most captives left no record.

Hideyoshi's death in 1598 triggered the order to withdraw. It did not create the military difficulties that made withdrawal sensible, and the Historical Debate on what ended the war shows why students should distinguish between why an invasion failed and why a war ended. Noryang, the last major battle, was fought during the withdrawal; Yi Sun-sin's death there did not end the war, and the tradition of his concealed death is best treated as commemorative tradition.

The war's consequences were enormous. Joseon was devastated physically, demographically, and fiscally, and militarily transformed. Captives and their families faced long separation, and efforts to recover them contributed to the gradual resumption of diplomacy with Japan. Japan entered a political struggle that led to Tokugawa rule, and the Ming had expended major resources. The war did not directly cause the fall of the Ming or the rise of the Manchus, but it shaped the conditions in which Chapter 36's crises unfolded.

The chapter's larger lesson concerns how historians explain endings. The war ended through contingency, structure, and political decision acting together, and students who understand this are better equipped to read both the commemorative narratives that later generations built around it and the documents that survive from those who lived through it.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter completes the Imjin War sequence, and its importance lies partly in what it reveals about how wars end. The Imjin War did not conclude with a decisive battle or a treaty but through the convergence of stalemate, exhaustion, and a ruler's death. That pattern is a reminder that historical outcomes depend on both structures and contingencies, and that explanations which isolate a single hero, battle, or decision are usually incomplete.

The chapter also shows how much the war transformed Joseon. A state that had been unprepared in 1592 emerged with a different military institution, a depleted treasury, a devastated countryside, and a changed relationship with its larger neighbors. The destruction of fields and registers, the loss and captivity of people, and the demands of reconstruction shaped Joseon's politics and society for decades, and they form the background to the later seventeenth-century crises that Chapter 36 examines, beginning with the rise of the Jurchen-Manchu state and the invasions of 1627 and 1636.

At the same time, the chapter shows how memory shapes history. Yi Sun-sin's reputation, the story of Myeongnyang, and the commemorative traditions of Noryang have become part of Korean national consciousness, and they have in turn influenced how historians and students approach the sources. Learning to separate evidence from commemoration, without dismissing either, is among the most important skills this chapter develops. It is equally important to attend to those whom commemoration often omits: the farmers, captives, women, children, and soldiers of several nations whose experiences make up the war's human history.

Finally, the Imjin War connected Korea, Japan, and China in ways that outlasted the fighting. It reshaped Joseon's diplomacy and military organization, contributed to the transformation of Japanese politics, and strained Ming resources. The people carried from Korea to Japan, the potters and scholars and laborers among them, became part of that connection, though under conditions of coercion that must not be forgotten. The war thus belongs both to Korean history and to the wider history of Northeast Asia, and understanding its end is necessary to understanding what came next.
