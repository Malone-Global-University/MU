Koreans in Japan’s Total War, 1937–1945

Korean History: Ancient Kingdoms to a Divided Peninsula

Chapter Opening

Between 1937 and 1945 the same colonial state that had spent decades governing Korea’s farms, schools, and workplaces began directing Koreans toward mines, factories, armies, and military-run facilities across an empire at war. Some of these systems were called recruitment, some volunteering, some placement, some conscription. Understanding what Koreans actually experienced requires taking each name seriously as a legal category and none of them as a complete description of lived reality.

Chapter 61 explained why Korea mattered to Japan’s empire as it expanded across Manchuria and China: its railways, ports, northern industry, and manpower. This chapter turns from imperial strategy to the people on whom that strategy depended. Between the outbreak of full-scale war with China in July 1937 and Japan’s surrender in August 1945, Koreans were drawn into the war economy and the armed forces as farmers delivering grain, students assigned to labor service, workers sent to coal mines and shipyards, soldiers and sailors, military civilian employees, and women whose labor, and in many cases bodies, were claimed by the military and the state.

Historians who study this subject face a vocabulary problem. Japanese and colonial officials described several programs as “recruitment,” “volunteering,” or “placement,” terms that implied choice or administrative routine. Many Koreans experienced the same programs as unavoidable pressure, deception, or outright compulsion. Other Koreans entered wartime institutions through economic need, ambition, or a calculated effort to protect family and status within an unequal colonial order. The categories used in official records are indispensable evidence, but they are not neutral descriptions. This chapter therefore distinguishes legal category, administrative practice, and individual experience throughout.

The chapter also insists on chronological precision. Wartime labor mobilization passed through three principal stages: company recruitment beginning in 1939, government-directed placement beginning in 1942, and the application of labor conscription to Koreans on a broad scale in 1944. Military enlistment followed a separate track: the Army Special-Volunteer System of 1938, expanded recruitment of students and sailors in 1943, and compulsory military service implemented in 1944. These tracks overlapped in time and sometimes in the families they affected, but they were different legal and administrative systems with different statistics. Merging them produces totals and narratives that no source supports.

Evidence for this chapter is uneven and sometimes painful. Official ordinances, ministry and Government-General records, company documents, and military files record what authorities intended and counted. Survivor testimony, family accounts, temple and municipal death registers, grave surveys, and Allied interrogation reports reveal other dimensions. Many records were destroyed or never created, and the voices of the people most affected often entered the historical record decades later. The sections that follow explain what each type of source can and cannot establish.

The chapter examines the home front first, then forced labor, then military service, then women’s labor and Japanese military sexual slavery, and finally the deaths, displacement, and unresolved consequences that followed Japan’s defeat. The treatment is deliberately social-historical. Its central argument is that total war imposed different kinds of obligation on Korean workers, soldiers, women, students, and families, and that describing all of them as simply “voluntary” or simply “forced” erases distinctions that matter both historically and morally.

The chapter connects directly to what precedes and follows it. Chapters 58 and 60 described the colonial economy and the assimilation policies within which mobilization took place; Chapter 61 described the military expansion that produced the demand; Chapter 63 will turn to Koreans who organized independence movements abroad; Chapters 64 through 66 will follow liberation, occupation, and division. Postwar diplomatic disputes, compensation arrangements, and court decisions concerning wartime mobilization belong largely to later chapters, notably Chapter 88, and are introduced here only to explain why the consequences of this period remained unresolved.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how the 1938 National General Mobilization Law and later ordinances converted an imperial military emergency into a system for directing civilian labor, goods, and lives.
  2. Trace the three phases of Korean wartime labor mobilization (company recruitment from 1939, government-directed placement from 1942, and labor conscription in 1944) and distinguish their legal and administrative features.
  3. Distinguish military enlistment systems from labor mobilization systems and explain why their dates and statistics must not be merged.
  4. Analyze why the label “volunteer” does not by itself establish free personal choice in the colonial context.
  5. Describe working and living conditions in mines, factories, shipyards, and construction sites in Japan, Korea, Manchuria, and Sakhalin.
  6. Compare the roles of uniformed soldiers, naval personnel, and military civilian employees, including Korean guards of Allied prisoners, with attention to individual responsibility and coercion.
  7. Distinguish the Women’s Volunteer Labor Corps from the Japanese military system of sexual slavery, while explaining how deception and fear connected them in public experience.
  8. Evaluate the evidence historians use to reconstruct military sexual slavery, including contemporaneous documents, survivor testimony, and later government findings.
  9. Assess the experiences of Koreans during air raids and the atomic bombings, and explain why casualty estimates remain uncertain.
  10. Explain why Japan’s surrender did not automatically produce return, payment of wages, or identification of the missing, using Sakhalin as a case.
  11. Interpret the differences among official statistics, administrative records, and survivor testimony as kinds of historical evidence.

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did a wartime emergency law passed in Japan become a set of obligations for Koreans, and who administered it at each level?
  2. Why do legal categories such as “recruitment,” “volunteer,” and “placement” both illuminate and obscure what Koreans experienced?
  3. How did the demands of the empire’s war economy affect the food, schooling, and household security of ordinary Korean families?
  4. Why did the Japanese military resist universal Korean conscription for years, and what changed by 1943–1944?

  7. Why are statistics about wartime mobilization so difficult to interpret and compare?
  8. How did the end of the empire in August 1945 change, and fail to change, the situation of Koreans who had been mobilized?

Historical Context

By 1937 Korea had been a Japanese colony for more than a quarter century. The Government-General of Korea, examined in Chapter 55, governed through a centralized administration, a large police apparatus, and a system of provincial, county, and village officials in which Koreans served in subordinate positions. Chapter 58 described how colonial agriculture, tenancy, and industrialization had reshaped Korean livelihoods: rice production oriented toward the Japanese market, a growing industrial sector concentrated in the north after the 1930s, and large-scale migration of Koreans to Japan, Manchuria, and elsewhere. By 1939 roughly a million Koreans lived in Japan, many of them migrants who had left rural Korea for wage work; by 1945 the figure had approached two million, a growth that included both earlier voluntary migration and wartime mobilization.

Chapter 60 explained the assimilation policies of the late 1930s: the imperial subject oath, the pressure to use Japanese in public life, and the 1940 name-change policy. Those policies presented Koreans as subjects of the emperor who owed the empire loyalty, and that rhetoric later justified demands for labor and military service. Yet the colonial order remained unequal. Koreans were subject to different laws than Japanese in many respects, were generally excluded from military conscription before 1944, and held far fewer positions of authority. The tension between a language of equality and a reality of hierarchy shaped how mobilization was publicized and how Koreans responded.

Chapter 61 described how the 1931 Manchurian crisis and the 1937 war with China transformed Korea’s strategic position. The war demanded soldiers and labor on a scale that Japan’s domestic population could not supply as men were drawn into the army. Korea offered a large population within the empire’s legal reach. Wartime mobilization therefore did not begin with a Korean policy; it began with an imperial labor shortage, and Korea became a principal source of workers, goods, and, eventually, soldiers. The sections that follow show how that demand moved through laws, offices, companies, villages, and households.

Division I · Section 62.1

Korea’s Entry into Japan’s Total-War Mobilization System, 1937–1939

The fighting that began near Beijing in July 1937 quickly became a full-scale war that Japan’s leaders had neither planned nor knew how to end. As the army expanded in China, the government sought legal means to direct industry, trade, labor, and finance toward war production. The result was the National General Mobilization Law, promulgated in April 1938, which gave the government authority to regulate the use of people and resources through subsequent imperial ordinances without returning each time to the Diet. The law itself was a framework. Its practical effect depended on the ordinances, administrative orders, and quotas issued under it.

That distinction is essential for Korea. The law was extended to the colony, but it did not by itself send a single Korean to a mine or an army. It authorized later measures: control of wages and employment, requisition of materials, direction of industry, and, through separate ordinances, the National Service Draft Ordinance of 1939, authority to requisition subjects for work. The labor programs examined in Sections 62.4 through 62.6 were built from these authorities in stages. A historian who treats the 1938 law as the origin of forced labor in a single step erases the sequence of decisions by which obligation was expanded and by which Korean workers, in particular, were treated differently from Japanese.

In Korea the system depended on a layered administration. The Government-General issued directives; provincial governors and county and township officials translated them into local quotas; police supervised compliance and public order; and employers, military authorities, and quasi-official associations made demands for workers and goods. Mobilization organizations expanded to reach neighborhoods and villages, including the Korean League for National Spiritual Mobilization of 1938 and its successors, which organized meetings, savings drives, and neighborhood groups. These bodies had little formal legal power but gave officials a channel for pressure and a means of recording who complied.

Early mobilization emphasized exhortation, production campaigns, and the redirection of goods. The mass wartime dispatch programs had not yet reached their later scale; this does not imply that colonial Koreans had previously been free of forced policing, harsh labor discipline, or other compulsion. The principal significance of 1937–1939 is institutional: it established the legal vocabulary, offices, and expectation of obedience through which harsher measures later operated. Contingency mattered. Had the war ended or stalled differently, the demands on Korea might have remained limited. Instead, Japan’s decision to widen the war created steadily larger demands.

The legal reach of the new regime was deliberately expansive, but it did not eliminate the distinction between Japan's metropolitan institutions and colonial administration. The National General Mobilization Law was promulgated in Japan in April 1938 and brought into force in Korea in May under the Governor-General's administration. The power to allocate labor and materials was translated into notices, registers, and locally assigned targets. A township clerk might receive an instruction expressed as a quota, whereas a household would encounter it as a visit, a meeting, or an order to supply a worker. Examining this chain from enabling legislation to individual obligation prevents the mistaken assumption that enactment and enforcement were simultaneous events.

War administration built upon older forms of colonial coercion rather than creating pressure where none had existed. The police and bureaucratic hierarchy already had experience supervising political activity, regulating association, and assisting employers. Long before September 1939, tenant farmers confronted unequal property relationships, workers faced restrictive industrial discipline, and opponents of colonial authority could be arrested. What changed in the late 1930s was the mobilization of these institutions toward a progressively more comprehensive wartime purpose. The earlier statement that few Koreans experienced compulsion directly must therefore be read narrowly: the mass, state-directed recruitment programs had not yet reached their later scale, not that colonial rule had become noncoercive.

Authorities also sought to mobilize emotion and identity. Speeches, posters, workplace meetings, school ceremonies, and neighborhood associations presented wartime sacrifice as a duty owed by loyal imperial subjects. The Korean League for National Spiritual Mobilization and later mass organizations linked slogans about national service to concrete actions such as collection drives, labor details, and savings. Their success could not be measured by attendance alone: those who appeared at a rally might do so out of conviction, fear, habit, or the need to avoid suspicion. Administrative records reporting enthusiastic participation often described the result officials wished to display, not a reliable cross-section of private opinion.

There were practical limits to central control. Ministries competed for scarce personnel, transport, and fuel; mines and factories demanded workers of different ages and skills; the colonial authorities had to consider agricultural harvests and rural unrest. An order to send men to Japan could deprive a household of the labor required to plant or thresh crops. Officials sometimes adjusted implementation to meet competing demands, while other officials imposed quotas with considerable harshness. These tensions explain why the wartime system developed through successive measures and why experience differed among provinces, employers, and dates. It was not an administrative machine operating without friction.

Contemporary documents should consequently be read at several levels. A law establishes what the government authorized; a circular shows how one office interpreted it; a village register or labor roster may show who was selected; and testimony describes what selection meant to the person concerned. None alone can establish the full story. When those sources diverge, the discrepancy may reveal a gap between formal policy and practice rather than an error that must simply be discarded. This method provides the evidentiary foundation for the labor and military sections that follow.

The National General Mobilization Law (1938) and the Wartime Labor Ordinances, 1939–1944

Creator: Japanese government and Diet; ordinances issued by the cabinet under imperial authority · Date: law promulgated April 1938; National Service Draft Ordinance 1939; later measures 1942–1944 · Type: Statutes and administrative ordinances

Context: These texts were written in Tokyo to govern the empire’s war economy. They were not written for Koreans specifically, and their application to Korea depended on additional measures and on colonial administrators.

Paraphrase (not a quotation):

In summary, the 1938 law declares that in wartime the government may control human and material resources so that the nation’s total strength can be used most effectively for national defense. It authorizes imperial ordinances governing labor, wages, goods, and industry. Later ordinances use this authority to define who may be required to perform designated work and what penalties may follow for refusal.

Analysis: The law shows how broad enabling authority was converted into specific obligations by separate ordinances. Comparing it with the 1939, 1942, and 1944 measures lets students see that “mobilization” meant different legal arrangements at different times.

Limitations: Statutes record intended authority, not practice. They do not show how local officials selected workers, whether quotas were met by persuasion or pressure, or how families experienced them.

Perspective: The perspective is that of the metropolitan government. Korean perspectives appear only indirectly, if at all.

Division I · Section 62.2

Food, Rationing, Agricultural Deliveries, Savings, and Wartime Household Hardship

For most Korean families, the first experience of total war was not a draft notice but a demand for rice, metal, and money. As Chapter 58 explained, colonial agriculture had long been oriented toward supplying Japan, and tenancy left many cultivators with small margins. Wartime policy intensified these pressures. Officials set delivery quotas for grain, enforced price controls, and used compulsory or heavily pressured sales to the state. A poor harvest in 1939 in parts of southern Korea made the strain more severe, and later years brought shortages of fertilizer, labor, and draft animals as men and resources were redirected. The distinction matters: the colony produced more for the empire, but that did not mean more was available to Koreans. Rationing for urban consumers developed in the early 1940s, and rural households that grew food often found themselves unable to retain enough.

Other demands were more visible. Savings campaigns encouraged or required households to deposit income in postal or bank accounts and purchase war bonds, often administered through neighborhood associations. Metal collection drives asked for household brassware, utensils, and other items, and officials requisitioned materials for war industries. Clothing, fuel, and everyday necessities became scarcer, while inflation and price controls complicated markets. Neighborhood groups also used inspection and recordkeeping, so refusing to contribute was socially and sometimes officially risky.

Households responded in varied ways. Many reduced consumption, substituted cheaper grains or foraged foods for rice, and shifted to barter or informal exchange. Some hid portions of harvests or goods, while others sought safer wages by sending a family member to work in a factory or to Japan. These strategies show agency under pressure, but they should not be read as evidence that hardship was mild: families chose among constrained options. The consequences were uneven. Landowners and officials with connections often fared better than tenants and urban poor, and regional differences in harvest and proximity to industry produced varied experiences.

The wartime food economy rested on a conflict between imperial procurement and household subsistence. Korean rice and other agricultural products had long been incorporated into Japanese markets, yet many cultivators paid rent, taxes, and other obligations before calculating how much remained for their own use. A delivery target imposed upon an estate or district did not bear equally upon every household. Landowners might possess stores, cash, or administrative contacts; tenant families depended upon access to land and the disposition of a landlord. In years of scarcity, the same official procurement quantity could have very different consequences for those two groups. Administrative totals therefore cannot be read as measures of nutritional security.

Rationing changed the relationship between food and money. A person might have wages but still be unable to purchase a sufficient ration through official channels, while someone in a rural area might possess grain yet face pressure to deliver it. Distribution systems depended upon household registration, local officials, and access to shops; the implementation of ration entitlements could vary across urban and rural districts. Substituting barley, millet, vegetables, and other foods for rice was not merely a culinary change but a practical adjustment to scarcity and allocation. The poorest families had the fewest resources with which to absorb a bad harvest, the departure of an adult worker, or an unexpected illness.

Forced or strongly pressured savings should be distinguished from ordinary financial planning. Officials encouraged deposits, bonds, and contributions as signs of loyalty, and employers could withhold part of a worker's wages for designated accounts. A balance recorded on paper did not necessarily mean funds were readily available when a family needed them. Campaign targets, account records, and later claims about missing deposits must be examined together. For a household sending a worker away, the difference between promised remittances and money actually received could determine whether children remained in school or whether land and possessions had to be sold. Savings were thus simultaneously a tool of state finance and an experience of restricted household control.

Women often assumed additional responsibilities when men left for work or service. They managed planting and harvesting with fewer hands, cared for children or elderly relatives, and participated in neighborhood collections while also performing wage labor where opportunities existed. Elderly family members and adolescents could take on duties previously performed by absent adults. The burden was not uniform: households with livestock, relatives nearby, or land of their own had options unavailable to households dependent on daily wages. This gendered reorganization of labor should not be confused with the separate and distinct systems of women's industrial mobilization and military sexual slavery examined in Division IV.

Evidence of informal trading, concealed grain, and noncompliance complicates the impression created by public campaign reports. Local newspapers were censored, and wartime officials had reasons to emphasize discipline and success. Memoirs and oral histories provide descriptions of improvisation but may blend different years of worsening shortage. Historians test memories against harvest reports, prices, household registers, and local administrative notices when available. What emerges is not a single, uniformly starving population but a society in which official demands increasingly narrowed the range of choices available to families already divided by wealth, region, and status.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Household Under Delivery Quotas

Historians reconstruct household experience from official delivery and rationing records, local administrative documents, newspapers filtered by censorship, and later oral histories and memoirs. Each has limits: official records measure compliance, censored press promotes campaigns, and recollection after many decades may compress or reorder events. Taken together, they show households weighing food, labor, and risk, deciding whether to deliver, conceal, or sell produce, and whether to send a son or daughter to work elsewhere.

Division I · Section 62.3

Students, Youth, Schools, and the Mobilization of Civilian Society

Schools were among the institutions most directly reorganized by the war. Chapter 60 examined the ideological content of assimilation education; this section concentrates on what students were required to do. From the late 1930s, schools devoted growing time to physical drill, collective exercises, and assigned labor such as clearing land, collecting materials, assisting farms, and working in factories. By 1943–1944 the school year was increasingly interrupted by labor service, and measures issued in 1944 reorganized students into labor units for work in agriculture and industry. The reduction of regular instruction affected both secondary and higher education.

Teachers, principals, and local officials were instrumental. Japanese and Korean teachers organized labor details, kept attendance, and reported on students’ participation. Youth organizations offered a parallel structure for training and group activity. Families faced difficult tradeoffs: a child’s labor service might contribute meals or income, reduce supervision, or expose a young person to hazardous work, while refusing participation could harm the family’s standing with officials. Girls and boys experienced these demands differently, as Section 62.13 discusses for young women.

The longer-term consequences included interrupted schooling, disrupted expectations about careers, and a generation whose adolescence coincided with labor service and the threat of conscription. As Section 62.10 shows, the eligibility of students for military recruitment in 1943–1944 turned schools from sites of labor mobilization into sites of recruitment, linking this home-front section to Division III.

Student mobilization operated through institutions that were supposed to prepare young people for adult lives but increasingly used them as an immediately available labor force. School authorities could assemble pupils by class, assign a task, supervise movement, and report completed hours in ways difficult for employers recruiting adults across villages. The form of labor varied from collecting scrap and assisting agricultural production to work at industrial sites. A school activity called patriotic service could therefore range from a short supervised campaign to dangerous, sustained work during a period when ordinary instruction was sharply reduced. Students' ages, educational level, location, and the year of mobilization must be specified before their experiences can meaningfully be compared.

The chronology of regulation was cumulative. School-related patriotic labor arrangements existed by 1938, while the rapid expansion of wartime industry and the deteriorating military position produced intensified student deployment in 1943 and 1944. The foundation's chronology records an April 1944 student mobilization headquarters regulation and an October 1944 student work ordinance. These dates are distinct from the military laws governing male student recruits. Formal orders demonstrate widening authority, but school journals and local records are needed to determine how much classroom time was displaced in particular places. Students could be assigned labor through school organization without becoming soldiers or military civilian employees.

Teaching staff became intermediaries between state directives and their pupils. A principal could face an instruction to supply a certain number of workers or complete a designated project; a classroom teacher then had to make attendance and discipline work in practice. Japanese and Korean educators did not possess equal authority under the colonial system, and their responses varied. Some repeated the official rhetoric enthusiastically; others sought to protect pupils within their limited room for maneuver. The existence of an attendance register tells us who was recorded, but not why individual students complied or how teachers privately regarded the assignments.

The effects were educational as well as physical. Time spent working could mean missed examinations, delayed qualifications, and loss of training for occupations that required sustained study. Wartime scarcity of paper, fuel, and equipment further narrowed the resources available to schools. Access to advanced education was already highly unequal, so interrupted secondary and higher education fell upon a relatively small and socially differentiated population, while elementary pupils and youths outside school were mobilized through different organizations. The distinction guards against treating the experience of educated urban students as representative of all Korean adolescents.

Girls' schooling was affected by the demands for industrial work and by fears surrounding the security of young women recruited away from their communities. These anxieties were heightened by the coexistence of distinct recruitment systems, even though the Women's Volunteer Labor Corps was institutionally separate from military comfort stations. Families could attempt to withdraw children, arrange work nearby, seek administrative exemptions, or comply despite misgivings; not every strategy was available to everyone. Students' experiences were therefore embedded in household calculations about safety, credentials, food, and the likely response of officials.

By the final years of the war, school mobilization and military recruitment increasingly overlapped for young men. The autumn 1943 student enlistment campaign used educators and institutions to reach candidates who had previously anticipated careers outside the armed forces. Student labor and student military service must nevertheless be kept separate in chronology and statistics. The central historical transformation was that schooling lost much of its protective separation from the state's immediate wartime demands. A generation crossed into adulthood under conditions in which classrooms, factories, barracks, and military recruitment offices were linked by the same governing apparatus.

Division II · Section 62.4

Wartime Labor Recruitment, 1939–1942

The first stage of organized Korean labor mobilization to Japan began in 1939. Japan’s cabinet adopted a labor mobilization plan that included allocations of Korean workers for coal mines and other industries, and from the autumn employers were permitted to recruit Koreans under approved quotas. In this phase, companies sent recruiters, often accompanied by or working through local officials, to designated counties in Korea. Employers set targets; the Government-General approved and allocated them; and local administrations assisted with publicity, selection, and arrangements for travel.

Official descriptions emphasized contracts, wages, and defined terms of employment, commonly two years. Recruiters promised pay, housing, and food, and sometimes described work in more attractive terms than workers later found. Documented accounts describe deception about conditions, pressure from township officials to meet quotas, and travel arranged in groups under supervision. Some recruits went because they needed wages and judged the offer worth the risk; others felt they could not refuse once selected. Some experiences, therefore, resembled labor migration under unfair conditions, while others involved clear deception or pressure. Both existed within the same program, and historians must avoid collapsing them.

Important constraints applied once workers arrived. Companies controlled housing, food, and communication, and employment terms often restricted leaving. Wages were subject to deductions and compulsory savings, a pattern that created later disputes over unpaid wages (Section 62.19). The line between recruitment and compulsion was therefore not a single point but a gradient shaped by local officials, quotas, deception, and the practical impossibility of leaving without consequences. The program also should be distinguished from the earlier voluntary migration that had already brought hundreds of thousands of Koreans to Japan.

The recruitment phase must be situated alongside, but not merged with, earlier Korean labor migration. Before 1939, people had moved to Japan for factory work, construction, trade, education, and family reasons within an unequal colonial empire. The new wartime procedure placed employers' requests within an officially approved allocation plan. Coal mines, industrial manufacturers, and construction enterprises identified desired numbers; colonial officials designated recruiting areas; and teams organized travel to particular worksites. This procedure made recruitment part of the state's production strategy even where a worker signed an employment agreement or believed the promised wages offered an opportunity.

The conditions under which an agreement was made are as important as its formal wording. For a tenant family carrying debts, an advertised wage in Japan could appear attractive despite uncertainty about the work. Recruiters could exaggerate earnings or describe less dangerous occupations than those actually awaiting workers. In other cases police or local officials pressured men already selected to comply. The line between consent and coercion should not be decided from the mere existence of an application form. A meaningful inquiry asks whether the worker knew the destination and conditions, could refuse without punishment, and retained the practical ability to leave or recover withheld wages.

Travel itself demonstrates the increasing coordination of the program. Workers were gathered in groups, escorted to ports and railheads, and transported through established colonial routes. A roster might give a name, place of origin, destination, and employer, creating an invaluable record for later family searches. It generally says little about the conversation at home before departure. Company logs and official passenger lists can establish that a movement occurred without proving that every individual in the group agreed to it freely. Conversely, a later account of coercion should be examined in relation to the particular recruiting office, year, company, and individual circumstances.

Once employment began, the company often exercised extensive control over the worker's life. Housing in managed quarters, ration distribution, wage deductions, compulsory deposits, and restrictions on movement created forms of dependence not visible in a simple description of paid work. The promised contract period mattered less if the worker could not leave safely or if the employer refused to release savings and documents. Some workers tried to escape; others remained because families needed remittances, because they feared punishment, or because there was no feasible route home. A contract could therefore coexist with conditions properly analyzed as coercive labor.

Historical statistics for this phase should distinguish departures from Korea, arrivals at a Japanese port, workers employed at a given date, and individuals who moved between worksites. Adding those measures together can count one person several times. The same caution applies when sources use 'recruitment' as a bureaucratic label: it identifies a channel of dispatch but does not automatically describe the freedom exercised by the individual. The shift in 1942 to official placement did not begin coercion from nothing. Rather, it transferred more of the selection and enforcement process directly to the colonial administrative hierarchy.

Division II · Section 62.5

Government-Directed Labor Placement, 1942–1944

In 1942, as the Pacific War intensified shortages, the system shifted. Rather than leaving recruitment to companies, authorities adopted kan assen (official placement or government mediation), under which the Government-General and its local organs took over the selection and dispatch of workers. Labor associations and administrative offices coordinated with employers’ requests, and local officials were assigned numerical targets to produce.

The shift mattered for how obligation worked. Township and county offices compiled lists, summoned men, and organized transport in groups. Police backed these efforts, and penalties or social pressure could fall on families and villages that failed to produce workers. Quotas increased, and administrative demand now reached into places that had resisted private recruiters. Although the program was not yet general legal conscription, its practical compulsion grew substantially, as officials faced consequences for shortfalls and workers faced consequences for refusal. Korean workers and families described, in later testimony, selection by officials, sudden notices, and limited ability to decline, though historians must treat individual accounts with attention to time, location, and how the person was selected.

The structure also drew Korean intermediaries, including township heads and clerks, into carrying out orders. They worked under pressure of their own and varied in how they exercised discretion. Understanding the system requires attention to this chain of responsibility without reducing it either to Tokyo’s intent alone or to the individual actions of local officials alone.

Official placement changed the practical relationship among employer, local government, and worker. Companies still needed labor and retained an interest in identifying suitable employees, but the task of supplying people increasingly fell to a state-directed administrative system. An employer's shortage could become a request passed through government offices, then a local quota, and finally a summons delivered to a named person. This transfer mattered because villagers were already subject to officials who controlled permissions, records, and other aspects of public life. Refusal of a government demand was not the same social act as declining a private recruiter on a street or in a market.

Local administrators needed information about the men within their districts. Household registers, age categories, occupation lists, and personal contacts could be assembled into selection procedures. Authorities might favor men regarded as physically fit, already employed in similar work, or lacking some recognized exemption. Yet the selection process was not necessarily transparent: a family could discover a departure date with little time to prepare, and officials under quota pressure could stretch or ignore guidelines. The family of a selected man might petition, seek replacement, or conceal him. Such actions are historically significant not because they defeated the system uniformly but because they expose the friction between administrative demand and family survival.

The increased involvement of police sharpened that pressure. Officers could summon men, investigate absences, and support recruiters against evasion, while the threat of consequences for a household made resistance expensive. The degree of physical force varied by place and case, and particular accusations require identifiable sources. Nonetheless, a system in which a township office designates people for dispatch without a freely revocable agreement is qualitatively different from ordinary labor mobility. The absence of the final general conscription order before 1944 did not prevent serious practical compulsion in the official-placement years.

The needs of the sending community and the destination employer often conflicted. A district required workers for crops, fishing, roads, and household support; mines and factories elsewhere demanded men whose labor could not be replaced easily. The movement of young adults could impose additional work on women and older relatives and deprive local enterprises of skilled hands. Officials faced pressure to fulfill quotas even as they were also held responsible for maintaining agricultural production. Regional variation reflected this collision of objectives, not merely differing personal enthusiasm for the war.

Placement generated documentation that is especially useful for reconstructing the geography of wartime labor. Dispatch lists, travel permits, police correspondence, company personnel books, and later death records can be compared to track a person from a village to a workplace. None is complete on its own. A roster may count dispatches and exclude those who escaped before embarkation; a company record may omit an earlier recruitment path; a death notice may misstate a Korean name. This evidence problem becomes acute when families later try to locate missing relatives or recover unpaid earnings.

By 1944, government placement had established the social and logistical infrastructure that large-scale legal requisition could use: designated categories of people, quotas, gathering points, transport, and supervision. The final phase of labor conscription was therefore both a legal escalation and an extension of practices already familiar in many Korean communities. Distinguishing the phases permits historians to describe this escalation precisely instead of declaring all mobilization either voluntary until 1944 or uniformly conscripted from 1939.

Division II · Section 62.6

The Expansion of Compulsory Labor Conscription in 1944–1945

The third phase brought the broadest legal compulsion. The National Service Draft Ordinance (Kokumin Chōyōrei), established in 1939, had been applied narrowly to Koreans at first. In September 1944 its general application to Koreans was implemented, allowing officials to requisition workers directly, direct transfers, and restrict departure from assigned work. This is the point at which labor mobilization became formally compulsory on a large scale, rather than being carried out largely through recruitment and placement.

Historians therefore distinguish the ordinance’s original legal authority in 1939 from its expanded application in 1944. Treating 1939 as the date when all Koreans were conscripted for labor would be inaccurate, as would the claim that no legal compulsion existed before 1944. Practice often preceded formal law, and the formal law ratified and extended a system already pressing hard on Korean households. By late 1944 shipping losses and air attacks limited the number of workers who could be sent to Japan, and more laborers were assigned to work within Korea and to military construction projects. These changes mean that the geography of labor shifted along with its legal form.

Laborers under these orders faced reduced ability to leave jobs and penalties for absence. Yet resistance, escape, and evasion persisted, as did attempts to negotiate with employers. The consequences of the system are examined in the workplace case studies that follow.

The National Service Draft Ordinance originated in 1939 and was extended in limited ways within the empire before its broad application to Korean laborers in September 1944. The difference between promulgating an ordinance, extending its jurisdiction, and actually applying its compulsory powers to a large population is fundamental. A statute may contain authority long before an office has the personnel or transportation needed to enforce it. In Korea the earlier recruitment and placement programs had already supplied extensive records and administrative routines, so the new stage could build upon machinery that was both familiar and capable of reaching into individual households.

Direct requisition altered the worker's formal legal position. Instead of being presented chiefly as a company recruit or a person placed through official mediation, a man could be required under state authority to undertake assigned work. Rules governing movement, attendance, and departure could be enforced against him as obligations rather than interpreted simply as disputed contractual terms. Employers continued to benefit and might administer daily conditions, but the backing of the state became more explicit. Calling the practice a draft does not erase the distinct identity of the civilian labor program: it was not the same as compulsory military service imposed on Korean men that year.

The timing created substantial logistical problems. Japan's shipping losses and the danger of Allied submarine and air attacks made movement between Korea and Japan increasingly hazardous. At the same time, the need for coal, steel, transport facilities, and military construction became more urgent. Authorities therefore faced the contradictory task of moving people toward industrial targets while protecting or maintaining the transport system that allowed those targets to function. More workers could be kept or redirected within Korea for construction and industrial work, and some scheduled movements failed or were changed. The existence of an official quota is not proof that the quota was fulfilled or that all those selected reached their destinations.

Korean communities did not respond uniformly. Some men hid, moved away from the area of registration, or sought work that might appear to provide protection; others complied to avoid punishment or because their families could not afford a confrontation. Locally influential households could sometimes negotiate more effectively than poorer ones. Evasion figures must be understood cautiously because records frequently registered noncompliance from the state's perspective, without explaining whether the individual was ill, absent, in hiding, or already serving elsewhere. The system's coercive character is evident in the nature of the legal requirement and the documented pressures, but individual trajectories remain diverse.

Work assignments differed sharply in risk. A person requisitioned to an industrial plant within Korea could be subject to long shifts, strict discipline, and air-raid danger yet remain geographically closer to family. A person transported to a Japanese mine or a distant military project faced different risks of transport, communication, death, and nonreturn. Uniform claims about all drafted workers obscure those differences. Research based on surviving records must therefore specify location, workplace type, dates of assignment, and whether an individual actually worked at the named destination.

Labor conscription continued into Japan's final military crisis, but the system ended abruptly with surrender, leaving substantial unfinished records and outstanding obligations. Workers' release from service did not guarantee that employers could produce back wages, that savings accounts could be recovered, or that ships would carry them home. The administrative power that had gathered and moved people dissolved faster than the practical problems it created. These consequences link the 1944–1945 draft directly to the later history of missing persons, stranded communities, and contested payments.

Division II · Section 62.7

Korean Forced Laborers in Japan: Mines, Factories, Shipyards, and Construction

Korean laborers in Japan worked in coal and metal mining, steel and metal production, shipbuilding, munitions and aircraft-related factories, and construction projects, including tunnels, airfields, and dams. Conditions varied by employer and site, but documented patterns include long working hours, heavy and dangerous tasks, company-controlled housing in barracks, limited and monotonous food, and supervision that restricted movement. Wages were often reduced by deductions for food, housing, savings, and remittances, and in many cases workers received less than they had expected or nothing they could take home. Coal mining was especially hazardous, with accidents, cave-ins, and gas explosions. Workers also faced discrimination in assignments and treatment, and punishment for perceived infractions or escape attempts.

Escape attempts occurred across sites and were often punished. Workers who escaped faced difficulty surviving without papers, language fluency, or support, though some found shelter among Korean communities in Japan. Communication with relatives was limited and censored, and when a worker died, notification might be delayed or incomplete. These conditions created the large category of missing and unreturned workers discussed in Section 62.19.

Recent scholarship shows how historians reconstruct individual cases. A 2024 study of Korean forced laborers in Hokkaido by Duró and Palmer combined workplace and municipal records, survivor accounts, and the recovery of remains to rebuild histories of people who died far from home. Such work demonstrates that no single source suffices: documents may list names and dates without conditions, testimony may describe conditions without precise records, and physical remains can confirm that people died and were buried while leaving open how and why.

Coal mines present one of the clearest examples of how industrial priorities shaped working lives. Underground labor demanded physical strength and familiarity with dangerous equipment, yet newly arrived workers often received limited training and were assigned difficult tasks. Accidents could arise from unstable workings, ventilation failures, machinery, exhaustion, or inadequate supervision. Coal was indispensable to shipping, power generation, and factories, so production targets often encouraged long shifts despite increasing shortage of food, equipment, and skilled labor. A mine record might count a worker's output while omitting illness or injury unless it interfered with production.

Manufacturing workplaces had different hazards. At foundries and steel works, extreme heat, burns, and industrial accidents threatened workers; at shipyards and construction projects, heavy materials, machinery, heights, and hazardous weather could be serious risks. Some Korean workers arrived with previous skills and found themselves assigned differently from inexperienced recruits. Others were moved between projects as military demand changed. Racial discrimination could affect wages, housing, treatment by supervisors, and opportunities for advancement, but company practices were not identical. Precise evidence requires comparisons within particular industries, places, and years.

Living quarters mattered almost as much as the worksite. Dormitories or barracks organized around company control made it easier to distribute food, supervise attendance, and restrict movement. Workers separated from their home communities often lacked a safe means of appealing to authorities. Language barriers, censorship, and postal interruption limited communication with families. Even where records showed wage payments, deductions for housing, provisions, transport, and savings could reduce the money an individual actually handled. A promising wage stated at recruitment might therefore offer little real support to relatives in Korea.

Employers' need for labor also generated a continuous struggle over absences and escapes. Company memoranda sometimes classified departures as desertion, while a worker might regard leaving as the only way to escape abuse or obtain wages. Police involvement, the absence of travel papers, and the possibility of losing savings made departure risky. Korean communities already established in Japan could sometimes provide language assistance, shelter, or contacts. The prevalence of unauthorized departure must be established from identifiable records and should not be inferred from one dramatic example.

The Hokkaido case investigated by Ágota Duró and David Palmer demonstrates how workers can be recovered from administrative anonymity. Their 2024 article combines the history of wartime forced labor with efforts to locate graves and return remains. The article's published corrigendum modified wording to protect a research source and explicitly stated that the findings were unchanged. For historical purposes the significant point is methodological: a company list may preserve a name, a temple register a burial, and testimony a recollection of conditions; only comparison can determine whether they refer to the same person and what happened.

Death and disappearance figures should not be extrapolated casually from one site to all workers in Japan. Some worksites were exceptionally dangerous; others retained better records; some recorded accidental death without reliable information about illness, violence, or subsequent movement. Bodies and cremated remains could be transferred or left behind with incomplete labels, and the names of Korean workers were sometimes rendered inconsistently in Japanese records. The historical task is therefore both quantitative and individual: to understand the system's scope while identifying real people whose families may never have received an accurate account of their fate.

Another original-record example comes from the Sado Mine on an island in the Sea of Japan, rather than Hokkaido. Hye-kyung Jung’s study of Mitsubishi Mining’s Korean tobacco distribution lists identifies roughly 494 entries in surviving rosters dating from 1944–1946, while citing a larger, separately compiled total of 1,519 Koreans mobilized to the mine during 1939–1945. The ration lists show how workplace administration documented particular men, but their partial coverage makes them unsuitable as a complete count of laborers. By pairing this example with the Hokkaido burial research, students can see why ration records, employment lists, testimony, and memorial registers answer different questions about the same coercive wartime labor system.

Division II · Section 62.8

Forced Labor across the Empire: Korea, Manchuria, Sakhalin, and Military Construction

Labor mobilization was not limited to the Japanese home islands. Within Korea, mobilized workers staffed mines, factories, and construction sites, and as shipping to Japan became dangerous in 1944–1945, more labor was assigned locally, including to military construction. In Manchuria, Korean workers took part in industrial, mining, and construction projects, adding to the large Korean population already living there. On southern Sakhalin, known to Japan as Karafuto, Korean workers were assigned to coal mines and related work, and by 1945 tens of thousands of Koreans lived on the southern half of the island.

The chapter distinguishes categories carefully. Civilian industrial workers were employed by companies or public projects. Military civilian employees (gunzoku) worked for the armed forces in noncombatant roles, including construction and transport, under military control. Uniformed soldiers and sailors belonged to separate systems examined in Division III. Sources and statistics for these groups differ, so figures for “mobilized Koreans” that combine them require caution.

Sakhalin became especially significant because of what followed. After the Soviet Union took over the southern island in August 1945, Japanese residents were repatriated in the following years, while most Koreans were excluded by repatriation policy and the developing diplomatic division over their status, despite not having automatically lost Japanese nationality in August 1945; many remained for decades, a case examined in Section 62.18.

The Korean Peninsula itself was a major theater of industrial mobilization. Factories, mines, railways, and military installations demanded labor, while the need to preserve agricultural output competed with the same supply of workers. Northern industrial districts were strategically important for metals, chemicals, electricity, and military production, but industrial facilities in southern provinces and near transport nodes also absorbed labor. Workers assigned near home could remain subject to coercive conditions; geographic proximity did not itself make a labor assignment voluntary. Conversely, departures to Japan were not the only measure of wartime manpower extraction from colonial Korea.

Manchuria requires a different analytical vocabulary. Large Korean communities were present there before 1937 through migration, settlement, and political displacement, and their members lived within the changing institutions of Japanese-dominated Manchukuo. Wartime industrial and construction projects drew some people into coercive labor, but it is misleading to describe every Korean resident or worker in Manchuria as a person dispatched from Korea through the 1939–1944 programs. Distinguishing established migrants, wartime movers, military personnel, and forced laborers is essential to calculating scale and explaining what the war meant for local households.

Southern Sakhalin, called Karafuto under Japanese rule, exposes the dependence of labor policy upon frontier resources. Coal mining attracted migrants before the war, and wartime authorities subsequently sent additional Korean workers through recruitment and coercive schemes. Harsh climate, distance from Korea, company-managed settlements, and dependence on maritime transport complicated workers' ability to communicate or leave. By 1945 the Korean population was estimated in the tens of thousands. Numbers describing all Koreans living on the island are not interchangeable with the number mobilized under any single labor ordinance or with later figures for people left behind.

Military construction and military civilian employment should be distinguished from ordinary corporate labor contracts. Korean personnel could be placed under armed-forces authority to build roads, airfields, or defensive works, move equipment, supply units, or guard prisoners. Their formal status was not that of uniformed combatants, but military command could shape their living conditions and obligations. The term gunzoku covered different occupations, and surviving statistics may define it differently. Students should therefore ask whether a particular source counts soldiers, labor conscripts, civilian military employees, or other groups before combining reported totals.

Geography also shaped the probability of return. A worker within Korea might leave a workplace and walk or travel to relatives after surrender; a person in Japan depended on ports and ships; a person in Burma or on a remote Pacific island faced military collapse and long-distance movement; and people in Sakhalin encountered Soviet occupation and new rules of departure. The categories used to describe wartime service often continued to affect the processing of returnees, compensation, and recognition. A map of mobilization must therefore contain both outward movements and the uncertain routes home.

There is a further problem of changing political sovereignty. Areas described in Japanese sources as one imperial administrative space did not remain under one government after August 1945. Retrospective national borders can conceal wartime routes and misunderstand the circumstances in which people were stranded. The map study below should accordingly be read as a study of changing control over Korea, Japan, Manchuria, Sakhalin, and the wider war zone, not as a static map of modern countries.

Division III · Section 62.9

The Korean Army Special-Volunteer System, 1938–1943

Military service for Koreans began not with conscription but with a limited volunteer system. An ordinance issued in February 1938 established the Army Special-Volunteer System, with training beginning that spring. Korean men were not subject to the military service law that applied to Japanese men, so the army’s opening of enlistment to Koreans was a selective measure. Japanese military leaders were initially cautious: they questioned Koreans’ reliability, worried about arming a colonized population, and had no pressing need for large numbers of Korean recruits in the late 1930s. A small, controlled intake allowed them to test the idea and to publicize imperial equality without committing to universal service.

Eligibility rules, education requirements, and screening limited applicants, and training took place in dedicated facilities under strict supervision. Yet the program attracted large numbers of applicants relative to the number accepted. Motives were mixed. Some applicants sought employment and status in a society that offered Koreans few paths; some responded to the argument, advanced by some Korean elites and by officials, that military service would earn equal treatment; and some applied under pressure from police, township officials, and schools, which were evaluated on participation and could make applications difficult to refuse. Scholarship on Korean military service, including Brandon Palmer’s research, shows how the system combined administrative pressure with individual choice and calculation.

Numbers require care. Applications greatly exceeded acceptances, and published figures for those who actually entered service through this system by 1943 are on the order of tens of thousands at most, a small fraction of the later total. Historians therefore warn against treating the “volunteer” label as proof either of enthusiastic support or of uniform coercion. As the debate box in Section 62.11 explains, the evidence supports a graduated picture.

The special-volunteer scheme illustrates the paradox of Japanese colonial assimilation. Wartime rhetoric increasingly presented Koreans as fellow imperial subjects, yet the armed forces treated them as a distinct population requiring selection, surveillance, and special preparation. Japanese men had long been subject to conscription; Koreans did not enter the same institutional pathway in 1938. The army's hesitation reflected doubts about political loyalty, language, and the consequences of training colonized subjects to use weapons. The selective program thus served both a manpower purpose and a propaganda purpose: accepting some Koreans could be publicized as imperial inclusion without removing the unequal structure of military command.

Applications did not all arise from the same circumstances. Paid military employment offered some young men a possible route away from rural poverty or insecure civilian prospects. Others imagined that service might establish claims to fuller civic rights or social recognition. For men facing school officials, police, or local administrators who demanded applications, the calculation could be quite different. Individuals could hold several motives simultaneously. A person might resent colonial rule while believing enlistment offered the least dangerous or most advantageous option available, or he might share the political commitments of pro-imperial Korean organizations. Such complexity does not turn the colonial system into a relationship between equal partners.

The discrepancy between applications and admissions is exceptionally revealing. In his study of Korean service, Brandon Palmer reports more than 800,000 army applications during 1938–1943 but 17,664 army enlistments under the special-volunteer program. A Korean institutional compilation gives a somewhat different army special-volunteer total of 16,830. These are not figures to average or silently reconcile: each comes from an identified source and may reflect differences in counting accepted, trained, or inducted men. The strikingly high application total cannot be taken as a plebiscite on colonial rule, because applications were solicited through propaganda and administrative pressure.

Training was intended not only to impart skills but to enforce discipline and linguistic and political conformity. Recruits encountered Japanese-language commands, army hierarchy, physical drill, and the expectation of loyalty to the emperor. The training regime also communicated the limits of the claimed equality: Korean enlisted personnel generally served within a command structure dominated by Japanese officers. Soldiers' experiences varied by unit and posting, and it would be misleading to infer the treatment of every recruit from one memoir or disciplinary incident. Military regulations document expected conduct, while letters, recollections, and service files help reconstruct what happened in practice.

The program had consequences beyond the men who were accepted. Applicants who were rejected or never enlisted might still have undergone examinations, interviews, neighborhood pressure, and public campaigns. Their families participated in decisions about lost household labor, danger, and potential wages. Korean elites who publicly endorsed enlistment did not necessarily speak for a population with diverse experiences and limited political freedoms. After liberation, surviving soldiers and advocates would be assessed under sharply altered political circumstances. A historian must therefore distinguish what a person did under colonial conditions from the meanings later attached to that action.

By 1943 the volunteer system had ceased to be a stable compromise between military caution and limited colonial inclusion. The expanding war exposed serious manpower shortages and encouraged new student and naval programs, followed by compulsory service. The 1938 system had prepared instructors, publicity methods, and screening procedures that could be reused, but it had not made Korean soldiers equal in rank or status. Its history is best understood as a transitional institution that made the military reach of Japan's colonial state deeper while retaining the inequalities that distinguished imperial subjects.

Division III · Section 62.10

Student Volunteers, Naval Recruitment, and Military Preparation in 1943

As the war turned against Japan in 1943, manpower needs grew and recruitment widened. A naval special-volunteer system was established in 1943, separate from the army’s program, giving Koreans entry to the navy under comparable conditions. In the autumn of 1943 authorities launched a student volunteer campaign aimed at educated young men in Korea and Japan, including university and college students, who had previously been exempt from conscription as students in Japan but, as Koreans, had not been subject to it at all.

The campaign relied on institutional pressure. Schools, officials, newspapers, and community leaders encouraged applications, and students who declined risked social and official consequences. Some prominent Korean intellectuals publicly urged young men to enlist; their motives and their later reputations became subjects of postwar debate, introduced in Chapter 60. Families reacted in varied ways, from fear and attempts to arrange evasion to acceptance. Higher education was disrupted as students left for training, and the campaign made clear that a general military obligation was approaching.

It is important to distinguish three programs: the army’s earlier special-volunteer system, the 1943 student recruitment measures, and the naval system. They overlapped and used similar rhetoric, but they had different eligibility, institutions, and numbers. In the same year the military service law was amended to apply to Koreans, preparing the 1944 introduction of conscription.

The student campaign of 1943 was exceptional because it targeted men whose anticipated futures were often academic or professional. Korean students in higher education, whether in Korea or the Japanese home islands, had invested in credentials that were difficult to obtain within a colonial system of unequal educational opportunity. The military now treated that education as a pool of available personnel. A young man might face pressure from a dean, a newspaper campaign, a local association, and relatives who feared the consequences of refusal. Some accepted the argument that service could help Korea's status within the empire; others sought to complete their studies or avoid deployment. Neither acceptance nor refusal can be interpreted without these pressures.

The timeline must be separated into distinct administrative acts. The Japanese state decided to extend conscription to Koreans, while the 1943 changes to the military-service framework prepared the process scheduled for the following year. Meanwhile a special student enlistment drive operated in the autumn of 1943. The forced-mobilization foundation records applications from late October into November, followed by examinations in December and induction of a student cohort in January 1944. This recruitment process was not identical to the general conscription examinations initiated in April 1944. The dates matter because a student who entered service in January belonged to a different administrative pathway from a man inducted under the new general draft in September.

The naval program, also inaugurated in 1943, answered another manpower need. Training and postings in the navy were administered separately from army enlistment, and categories such as sailor, military employee, or industrial worker at a naval installation must not be merged. Brandon Palmer reports approximately 90,000 applications for the naval special-volunteer system and 2,000 actual navy enlistments in the group counted by that program; a broad total for Koreans serving in the navy across the war is a different statistic. The difference is not an inconsistency if the dates and categories are kept clear.

Publicity campaigns relied on selected Korean intellectuals, teachers, and community figures to argue that service demonstrated advancement or gratitude to the empire. These statements were produced under censorship and amidst differing individual motives, including commitment, ambition, fear, and opportunism. The chapter need not reproduce the extended history of collaboration treated elsewhere, but it should show how endorsement by recognizable Korean voices made state pressure more effective. Posters and published appeals are evidence of official argument and its authorized supporters, not automatically proof that the larger student population embraced the war.

Military preparation further intruded into campus life. Drills, inspections, and administrative registration made educational institutions legible to the recruiting authorities. A school could be judged by the number of students forwarded into military pathways, creating incentives for officials to pressure those who hesitated. Students might seek medical exclusions, change enrollment status, try to defer recruitment, or enter service. Even where records survive, the choices denied to those who never made an application can be difficult to recover. The experience of these students is a reminder that 'application' and 'voluntary decision' are not synonymous categories.

Families understood that the new measures threatened more than a planned career. Overseas deployment could endanger a son's life and cut communication, while loss of a skilled young adult could undermine siblings' education or parents' support. Women in the household sometimes took over income-earning or agricultural work. At the same time, propaganda portrayed enlistment as an honorable route to imperial participation. The contradiction between coercive institutional demands and language of personal aspiration reached its clearest expression when the selective student and naval programs were superseded by broader compulsory enlistment.

Division III · Section 62.11

The Introduction of Compulsory Korean Military Service in 1944

Compulsory service followed a distinction between law and implementation. The extension of the military service law to Koreans was decided in 1943, but its practical implementation took place in 1944. The first general conscription examinations were held beginning April 1, 1944, and the first conscripts entered service from September 1944. Registration established who was eligible, medical examinations selected men, and induction notices assigned them to units and training. The process reached large numbers of young men in a short time, and noncompliance could lead to penalties and pressure on families.

Within the armed forces, Korean conscripts served in a hierarchy marked by racial inequality. Officers and senior noncommissioned ranks were overwhelmingly Japanese, and Koreans faced discrimination, harsh discipline, and suspicion, as well as language and cultural pressures. Individual responses ranged from compliance and attempts to excel, to quiet evasion, desertion where possible, and, in some cases, contact with resistance groups. Reasons for serving, complying, or resisting differed widely and cannot be reduced to a single motive.

Military conscription and labor conscription were separate systems. Their legal bases, administrative processes, and statistics differ, and totals from one cannot be added to the other. Both involved compulsion, but treating them as one program would obscure how families and individuals experienced two different demands, sometimes within the same household.

The move to a general draft originated in Japanese military planning before the first Korean conscription examinations. A May 1942 decision envisaged extending compulsory service, while the necessary legal and administrative arrangements followed in 1943. Korean men then faced a more systematic process of registration, medical evaluation, classification, and assignment. The first general examinations began on 1 April 1944, and initial conscripts entered service beginning 1 September. Those dates distinguish the legal preparation of conscription from its practical imposition. They also help explain why a soldier listed as serving in 1943 was not necessarily a conscript under the new general system.

The colonial state described the draft as evidence that Koreans were becoming full imperial subjects, but the political implications were deeply contradictory. The right to determine imperial policy or elect those who controlled the army was not extended on the same basis. Korean men could now be compelled to serve a state that denied Korea sovereignty. The obligation to fight did not carry equal protection from discrimination in daily military life. This disparity undercuts an interpretation of the 1944 law as uncomplicated political equality, although officials promoted it in precisely that language.

The draft reached families through a sequence of procedures rather than a single act of force. Age and household registers identified possible recruits; notices required attendance at examinations; medical officers and boards classified fitness; and units received inductees for training. An individual could comply at one stage and attempt to avoid another. Households sought information about sons' whereabouts and possible assignments, often with limited success. A register recording a man as examined is not proof that he served, just as the designation 'eligible' is not equivalent to induction or battlefield deployment.

Estimates of service must preserve those distinctions. The Foundation for Victims of Forced Mobilization lists a minimum military-combatant total of 209,279 in a compilation based on Japanese official records and a Korean investigative committee. Its component categories include army special volunteers, student volunteers, army conscripts, and naval personnel. The total does not automatically include all Korean military civilian employees, workers in factories, or men who applied but were never enlisted. Brandon Palmer's figures for particular volunteer systems differ slightly from some institutional tabulations; an accurate text displays those figures with attribution and does not combine their denominators.

Experience after induction also varied. Some men spent much of their service in training or garrison duties; others were transported to combat zones or assigned hazardous tasks. They served in an armed force whose command language, disciplinary rules, and senior ranks were predominantly Japanese. Korean soldiers could suffer both the risks experienced by soldiers generally and colonial discrimination within their units. Available memoirs do not represent the whole service population, and evidence about particular acts of resistance or collaboration must remain tied to individual cases rather than generalized to all conscripts.

At surrender, the draft's short duration did not make its consequences slight. Newly inducted men could be far from home, uncertain of their legal status, awaiting transport, or exposed to danger in collapsing garrisons. Documents needed to confirm service might be lost, and families lacked information. The difference between the 1938 volunteer system and the 1944 conscription system survived in later disputes over responsibility and compensation. The historical analysis should preserve both the shared setting of colonial coercion and the differences between programs.

Historical Debate

How Should Historians Distinguish Voluntary Enlistment, Institutional Pressure, Labor Conscription, and Direct Coercion?

The question: Can the same word, “volunteer,” cover a young man who applied for status, one who applied because officials expected it, and one who was told his application was required? How should historians classify programs whose legal form and lived reality diverged?

Approach A — Legal categories as the primary distinction: Some scholars emphasize the legal difference between voluntary programs, in which application was formally required, and conscription or labor conscription, in which refusal was legally punishable. This keeps programs distinct and prevents totals from being merged.
Approach B — Degrees of pressure within and across categories: Other scholars emphasize that colonial conditions, administrative pressure, and the consequences of refusal made even formally voluntary programs partially coerced, and that practical compulsion in labor programs preceded the law. They argue that a spectrum of compulsion better describes experience.

Evidence: Ordinances and statistics support the first approach; testimony, school and township records, and evidence of quotas support the second. Most historians combine the two: they keep legal categories distinct while recognizing that choice was constrained.

Points of disagreement: Disagreement concerns interpretation and terminology rather than the underlying events: where to draw lines, which words to use, and how to weigh individual agency against institutional pressure.

Why it matters: Terminology affects how responsibility, compensation, and memory are discussed, and how individuals who served in different capacities are judged.

Division III · Section 62.12

Korean Soldiers and Military Civilians across Japan’s War Fronts

Korean men served in China, Southeast Asia, the Pacific, and in Japan itself, though conscripts inducted in late 1944 had little time to reach distant fronts before the war ended. Published totals for Koreans in the army and navy are commonly given at roughly two hundred thousand, with additional military civilian employees in the range of well over one hundred thousand, but such figures combine categories that should be kept separate and vary between sources. The groups included uniformed soldiers and sailors, military civilian employees (gunzoku) such as construction and transport workers, interpreters, and other auxiliaries.

One notable group were guards recruited in 1942, as military civilian employees, for camps holding Allied prisoners of war in Southeast Asia, numbering roughly three thousand. They were recruited under offers of pay and short-term contracts and placed in a command structure controlled by Japanese officers. After the war, some of these guards were prosecuted by Allied tribunals for mistreatment of prisoners; sources give figures in the range of well over a hundred convicted and a smaller number executed. Assessing responsibility requires distinguishing individual acts, the authority of Japanese commanders, the guards’ low rank and the pressures of recruitment, and the fact that abuse of prisoners was documented as a widespread practice in these camps. Neither blanket condemnation nor blanket exculpation fits the evidence, and many Korean guards were themselves treated harshly by their superiors.

Casualties, surrender, and captivity added to the complexity. Some Korean soldiers died in combat or from disease; others surrendered and were held by Allied or Soviet forces; some were held as war criminals; and many returned home to a divided and unstable peninsula. These later experiences are considered in Section 62.18.

The Japanese military's category of Korean personnel included more than those carrying rifles as members of combat units. Uniformed men might serve in infantry, engineering, transport, communications, and support assignments, while civilian employees attached to military institutions worked in construction, logistics, interpretation, and camp administration. These roles were governed by different recruitment channels and regulations, although everyday life could bring them under similar orders. A history based only on army enlistment lists would therefore leave out many Koreans whose labor sustained military operations; a history that called all of them soldiers would create a different distortion.

Assignments spread across China, Manchuria, Southeast Asia, Pacific islands, and Japanese military installations. The location mattered for food, climate, language, exposure to combat, and prospects of return. Personnel in a base within Korea could remain in some contact with family; a Korean employee in a remote occupied territory might be isolated by military censorship and failing transport. Military reversals after 1942 made supply more difficult, and some personnel experienced shortages, disease, or combat while doing work nominally classified as noncombatant. Official occupational labels cannot substitute for an examination of the conditions at the actual posting.

Korean prisoner-of-war camp guards are a particularly difficult but necessary subject. Some were recruited as military civilian employees, including groups trained in Korea before dispatch to camps handling Allied prisoners. The foundation's chronology identifies the 1942 mobilization of more than three thousand men for this type of service. Guards could be directed by Japanese superiors and participate within an institution structured by racial and military hierarchy. Yet coercive recruitment does not in itself determine whether an individual committed an unlawful act against a prisoner. Historical responsibility must be assessed through orders, witness evidence, disciplinary relationships, and the actions of named people.

After Japan's defeat, Allied tribunals prosecuted some Korean guards or other Korean personnel for war crimes. Scholarship discussing these trials records a figure of 148 Koreans convicted in the broader group of trials it analyzes, rather than 148 convicted prisoner guards alone. Within that total, individual judgments and the nature of the charge varied. The precise scope of a published tally is therefore indispensable. Some defendants argued that Japanese officers had constrained their choices; prosecutors examined their personal treatment of prisoners. A careful account must neither erase abuse suffered by prisoners nor assume that colonial status automatically absolved every act committed by an individual.

The fate of servicemen after the surrender was not uniform. Some returned directly; others were detained or interrogated, and some faced imprisonment, disappearance, or long delays in repatriation. Family members sought to learn whether missing men were dead, wounded, detained, or simply unable to communicate. Administrative records might use Japanese spellings of Korean names or preserve only a unit number. These difficulties help explain why precise identification became a major postwar task and why totals that combine 'dead,' 'missing,' and 'unrepatriated' may conceal different outcomes.

The problem of military service also reveals the limits of a binary moral vocabulary. Some Korean participants acted under immediate compulsion, others hoped to improve their circumstances, and a few exercised authority within colonial institutions. Japanese imperial policy created the system; individual conduct within it remains a distinct subject for historical investigation. Maintaining both levels of analysis permits a fuller explanation of how people from a colonized society were drawn into a war directed by an empire that denied their homeland independent political choice.

Division IV · Section 62.13

Women Workers, Girls, and the Women’s Volunteer Labor Corps

Women had long been central to Korean agriculture and, since the 1920s, to textile and other factory work. Wartime intensified those roles as men were mobilized: women took on heavier farm labor, worked in factories producing textiles and war goods, and took part in civilian organizations, sewing and collection drives, and nursing-related duties. Women worked for wages when employment was available, but they also faced low pay, long hours, and, in many cases, pressure to participate in neighborhood and school-based activities.

In 1944, the Women’s Volunteer Labor Corps (Joshi Teishintai) was established to organize unmarried women for labor service, with an ordinance in August 1944 and implementation in Korea during that year. Students and young women were organized into units sent to factories and workplaces. Experiences varied: some women worked locally and returned home; others were sent far from home to factories in Japan under demanding conditions; and parents feared for their daughters’ safety. Documented accounts describe both economic motives and institutional pressure from schools and officials.

The Labor Corps was not the same institution as the military’s system of sexual slavery. Its purpose was labor for industry, and the historical record documents the separate purposes and administration of the two systems. Yet rumors, parental fear, and deceptive recruitment by agents made the two seem connected in the public mind, and some women who were deceived with promises of factory or nursing work were taken into comfort stations instead. Careful analysis requires maintaining the institutional distinction while acknowledging how fear and deception linked the systems in experience.

Women's wartime work was much broader than any single government labor corps. Women cultivated fields, managed households without absent male relatives, worked in textiles and other industries, and joined neighborhood production and collection campaigns. These activities involved different forms of remuneration, pressure, and location. A woman laboring on her family's farm under increased procurement demands should not automatically be described as a member of a specific state labor unit. Conversely, industrial employment that paid wages did not always allow a worker to refuse reassignment or leave an abusive workplace freely.

The Women's Volunteer Labor Corps expanded female labor mobilization within the state's broader wartime policy. An August 1944 ordinance provided a formal administrative framework for organizing designated women for work. The official term 'volunteer' is historically misleading when school authorities, police, or local officials used institutional pressure to secure recruits. Some women joined hoping for wages or training, while others were pressured by teachers or feared repercussions if they refused. Recruitment conditions changed across time and place, and it would be incorrect to assert that all members entered under a single coercive procedure.

Factory labor exposed women to an unfamiliar combination of opportunity and restriction. Traveling to an industrial center could provide income and skills not readily available in a rural household, yet it could also mean separation from relatives, discipline in company housing, long shifts, hazardous machinery, and control of movement. Evidence from an individual workplace must establish the extent of force, wage payments, and conditions rather than infer uniform abuse from the name of the institution. An accurate account acknowledges the diversity of female labor experiences without obscuring the authority structures under which women were mobilized.

Age and family position influenced vulnerability. Young unmarried women, including students and recent school leavers, were particularly affected by recruitment through educational and local administrative channels. Families worried about transportation, lodging, contact with daughters, and the possibility that an advertised work opportunity concealed a different destination. Parents might seek exemption, early marriage, different employment, or withdrawal from school, but these tactics depended on resources and officials' willingness to cooperate. Wartime rumors therefore had real consequences even where particular rumors could not be corroborated as descriptions of an institution's formal purpose.

The distinction from military sexual slavery must remain explicit. The Women's Volunteer Labor Corps organized industrial and related labor; the military comfort-station system organized coerced sexual access for troops under different administrative arrangements. Because deceptive promises of ordinary employment were among documented means of recruiting women into comfort stations, families could associate the two in their fears. This does not justify treating the two institutions as identical, or claiming without evidence that industrial corps members were routinely transferred into sexual slavery. The task is to identify the precise recruitment path and destination in each documented case.

Postwar memory also complicates terminology. A survivor of industrial mobilization may recall being called a volunteer even while describing a compulsory assignment, whereas a survivor of sexual slavery may describe a false employment offer without ever having belonged to the Labor Corps. Oral histories require attentive preservation of the witness's own terms and an independent account of official categories. The two histories intersect in gendered vulnerability, colonial authority, and deceptive recruitment, but they should not be merged into one unexamined narrative.

Division IV · Section 62.14

The Japanese Military Sexual Slavery System: Origins, Recruitment, and Administration

The system of military comfort stations began in the early 1930s, with facilities in Shanghai, and expanded substantially after the outbreak of full-scale war in China in 1937 and as Japanese forces spread through Southeast Asia and the Pacific. Military authorities were involved in establishing and managing many stations, regulating their operation, and arranging transport of women. Brokers and private operators ran some stations under military supervision or approval. Women came from many places, including Korea, China, Japan, Taiwan, the Philippines, Indonesia, and elsewhere, and Koreans represented a significant portion of those documented, though historians disagree about proportions.

Recruitment in Korea occurred through several mechanisms documented in testimony and records: deception, such as false offers of well-paid factory, restaurant, or nursing work; fraudulent contracts and debt; sale or trafficking by relatives or brokers; abuse of authority by officials and police; and, in some cases, direct coercion. Recruiters, brokers, local officials, shipping and railway networks, and military authorities all had roles. Evidence differs by region and period, and historians debate how often each method occurred. Disagreement about particular mechanisms, however, does not remove the documented fact of military involvement.

Japan’s government acknowledged this in the 1993 Kōno Statement, issued by the Chief Cabinet Secretary after an official investigation. It recognized that the military was involved, directly or indirectly, in the establishment and management of comfort stations and the transfer of women, and that recruitment and life in the stations were, in many cases, against the women’s will. As a retrospective official finding rather than a wartime record, the statement is itself a historical source whose later interpretation and political use belong to Chapter 88.

The comfort-station system should be understood as an institution of imperial warfare rather than as a collection of disconnected private establishments. It developed in association with Japanese military expansion in the early 1930s and grew in scope after the full-scale invasion of China in 1937. Military authorities authorized, requested, regulated, and in many places assisted the operation of stations. The degree of direct military administration varied, but military involvement included such matters as transport permissions, access to installations, oversight, and medical examinations. Japan's official investigation in the early 1990s documented involvement in establishment, management, and movement of women.

Recruitment procedures were not uniform. Private agents could offer apparently ordinary jobs as nurses, servers, or factory workers, then move women into stations through deception. Debt and contractual arrangements could be used to control movement, and families facing poverty could be vulnerable to brokers. Testimonies also describe intimidation and direct force in particular cases. The relative incidence of these methods is debated because surviving records are fragmentary and because women were recruited from different territories under differing local conditions. Explaining this diversity is not a retreat from identifying the military's responsibility for the system's operation.

Travel routes were part of the machinery of coercion. Korean women might be taken from a home district by rail and ship, accompanied by recruiters or operators, and brought to a city, garrison, or field station distant from any reliable means of return. Once there, formal rules could govern soldiers' access, pricing, medical inspection, and the station's relationship with military commands. Such detailed regulation is evidence of institutional organization, not proof that the women controlled their lives. Their practical freedom depended on coercive conditions, debt claims, security, language, and the possibility of leaving, not merely on the wording of a permit or contract.

The system served military purposes defined by the occupying authorities. Commanders sought to manage soldiers' sexual behavior and regulate access to civilians, while operators could profit from transactions. Officials sometimes justified stations through claims about preventing disease or sexual violence outside the facilities. Such rationalizations do not establish that those aims were achieved and cannot excuse the coercion, repeated sexual exploitation, and harm inflicted on the women within stations. Historians must distinguish contemporaneous official justifications from evidence about what the institutions actually did.

Korean women were not the only victims. Women from China, Taiwan, the Philippines, Indonesia, and other occupied territories were subjected to related forms of exploitation, while recruitment and living conditions varied by region. Korea's significance lies in the scale and pathways through which colonial administrators, brokers, and military authorities could draw women from the peninsula into the empire's wider war zone. A comparative perspective must retain differences rather than assume that an account from Burma, China, or a Pacific island represents every station. Such comparison also shows that the violence belonged to a broader imperial system rather than to a unique characteristic of one nationality.

The 1993 statement by Chief Cabinet Secretary Yōhei Kōno followed a Japanese government investigation and acknowledged direct or indirect military involvement, coercive recruitment in many cases, and the women's suffering. This is a valuable retrospective official finding, but it is not a substitute for wartime records or individual testimony. Historians also examine the 1992 Japanese inquiry, which identified governmental involvement in station establishment, management, surveillance, and issuance of documents. The precise administrative mechanisms remain matters of detailed historical study; denial of institutional involvement is inconsistent with the findings of these official inquiries and the surviving evidence.

Division IV · Section 62.15

Korean Women in Military Comfort Stations: Confinement, Abuse, and Survival

Korean women were taken to stations in China, Southeast Asia, the Pacific, and other territories under military control, often far from home and unable to speak the local language. Testimony and documents describe restricted movement, guarded or controlled premises, debts and payment systems that were used to bind women, repeated sexual violence, and exposure to disease, injury, and psychological harm. Soldiers’ visits were regulated, but regulation did not protect women from abuse. Escape was difficult and dangerous, and many women died; the number is unknown.

Survival took many forms and should not be reduced to victimhood alone. Some women formed bonds with others in the station, some negotiated for small improvements or communicated with those outside, and some escaped or were left behind as the military retreated. At the end of the war, women faced the problem of returning: transport was uncertain, some were abandoned where they were, some were held by Allied forces, and many feared stigma, family rejection, or shame, so that numerous survivors remained silent for decades or did not return at all.

This textbook handles testimony with dignity and accurate attribution. It does not reproduce graphic detail, since the historical analysis does not require it, and it avoids sensationalized description. It treats the women as historical actors who endured a documented system of military-controlled sexual violence.

For a woman taken far from Korea, physical location compounded the restrictions imposed by the station system. She might be separated by language, transport networks, guarded premises, and unfamiliar geography from people able to assist her. Even when a station was nominally run by a private operator, military authorities controlled much of the surrounding environment and the movement of soldiers and supplies. The existence of stated fees or accounts did not demonstrate freedom to refuse sexual access, recover earnings, or depart. Debt, withheld documents, threats, and fear of retaliation could operate alongside formal regulations and occasional limited privileges.

Survivor testimony describes repeated sexual violation, disease, untreated injuries, inadequate rest, and psychological harm. Experiences differed among stations and over time, and responsible historical writing should not invent or amplify details to produce dramatic effect. The evidence supports grave and systematic exploitation without requiring a single identical experience for all women. Medical inspections, when documented, frequently served military goals of controlling infection and maintaining troop access rather than securing women's autonomy or well-being. Describing these procedures as health protections without asking whose interests they served would reproduce the perspective of military administration.

An individual's means of coping might include forming relationships with other women, seeking assistance from someone nearby, holding onto a personal object, learning to navigate a new environment, or finding chances to communicate with family. Some attempted to leave or escaped under specific circumstances. These acts show human agency within constraint, not an absence of coercion. The fact that a survivor negotiated for food, money, rest, or safety should never be used to infer meaningful freedom over the central conditions of her confinement. Historical interpretation must distinguish survival strategies from consent.

The worsening military situation made conditions still more precarious. Retreating units could abandon women without supplies or safe passage. Movement between stations, bombardment, fighting, and the breakdown of command created exposure to additional violence and displacement. A survivor might be left in a territory where local people associated her with the occupying army, even though she had herself been exploited by that army. Other women were encountered by Allied forces, held in temporary custody, or transported as displaced persons. The end of military administration did not automatically restore social security or provide a route home.

Return to Korea introduced a different set of obstacles. Shipping and documentation were scarce; some women had lost contact with family, and others feared the consequences of disclosure in communities shaped by stigma surrounding sexual violence. Many remained silent for years or decades. Silence should not be interpreted as the absence of an experience or as proof of a precise event. Survivors' later decisions about public testimony were shaped by family circumstances, evolving political movements, and their own judgments about whether speaking would produce recognition, danger, or further humiliation.

The public testimony of Kim Hak-sun in 1991 became a turning point in the historical and political visibility of survivors, but it did not create the underlying history. Women had lived with these experiences for decades, and some had spoken privately or in less visible settings before they gained broad recognition. Oral-history projects, court testimonies, and documentary research subsequently brought many accounts into relation with one another. A textbook should identify survivors as people with individual lives and voices rather than use them merely to illustrate a single abstract category. The ethical obligation to avoid gratuitous detail is compatible with explicit acknowledgment of the system's violence.

Division IV · Section 62.16

Testimony, Military Documents, and the Historical Reconstruction of Sexual Slavery

Historians reconstruct the system from several kinds of evidence, each with strengths and limits. Japanese military records, including regulations, orders, and transport and administrative documents, establish institutional involvement; many were destroyed at the war’s end or remain incomplete. Allied interrogation reports, produced by American and other forces who captured women and soldiers, provide contemporaneous descriptions written by outsiders. Survivor testimony, first given publicly in 1991 when Kim Hak-sun spoke out, and thereafter by many others, provides first-person accounts recorded decades later. Court records and government investigations, including Japan’s 1993 inquiry, add retrospective findings. Corroboration across these types strengthens interpretation.

Estimates of the number of victims differ widely, with scholars proposing ranges from tens of thousands to considerably more. The reasons include destruction and incompleteness of records, differing definitions of who counts and where, the difficulty of identifying women across diverse territories, and the silence of many survivors. This textbook does not adopt a single figure. Disagreement about numbers, and about the proportions recruited by different methods, does not erase the documented military involvement and coercive conditions.

The documentary record is unusually difficult because the institution combined military bureaucracy, private intermediaries, secrecy, and the destruction of records at Japan's defeat. Orders concerning transportation, medical inspection, provision of facilities, and relationships with military units reveal structures that ordinary business records might conceal. Yet the survival of one order does not establish how consistently it was obeyed across every region. Historians must distinguish what a command authorized, what a local official reported, and what witnesses experienced. A persuasive reconstruction uses several source types that constrain and illuminate each other.

The Japanese government's 1992 investigation and the Kōno Statement of August 1993 provide particularly significant retrospective evidence. The former identified governmental involvement in establishment and management of stations and the handling of personnel and documentation. The latter explicitly recognized military involvement and recruitment against women's wishes in many cases. These findings were produced decades after the war, drawing on surviving official records and interviews, so their strength and limits should be analyzed as those of a later investigation. They are not themselves transcripts of every wartime transaction, and they cannot supply a reliable total for all women exploited.

The 1944 Office of War Information report is more precisely identifiable than the original manuscript states. It was Japanese Prisoner of War Interrogation Report No. 49, dated 1 October 1944 and associated with T/3 Alex Yorichi and a Psychological Warfare Team attached to U.S. forces in the India–Burma theater. It summarized interrogations conducted between 20 August and 10 September 1944 at Ledo Stockade after the capture of twenty Korean women and two Japanese civilians near Myitkyina. The report was an English-language intelligence product, not an unmediated record written by the women. Its racist and sexualized introductory characterization cannot be adopted as neutral historical description.

The report's account of recruitment and station operation has documentary value, yet its authors selected information for a psychological-warfare purpose. Translation, the presence of operators, questions chosen by interrogators, and the circumstances of captivity shaped what entered the record. A recent study in the American Historical Review reassesses the report's production and subsequent interpretation within the wartime information struggle. Its significance is methodological: an Allied report can document exploitation and simultaneously reflect the observer's prejudices or incomplete understanding. Different passages should be compared with contemporaneous Japanese records and later first-person testimony rather than treated as a definitive description of women's status.

Testimony creates another kind of evidentiary challenge. A person's recollection decades later may be detailed about lived conditions while uncertain about the date or administrative classification of an event. That is not a reason to dismiss it. Historians compare testimony across interviews, locations, and surviving documents, identify points of agreement and divergence, and avoid pretending that exact numerical precision is always possible. The fact that many victims died before their stories were recorded creates a survival bias, while stigma and poverty could limit who spoke publicly. A documentary archive constructed primarily from officials' paperwork has a different bias.

Numerical estimates are particularly uncertain. Sources can count registered survivors, known stations, named individuals, transportation records, or broader inferred populations; none is the same as a comprehensive wartime census. Researchers have proposed widely differing totals, and political debates have often detached figures from their definitions. It is responsible to state that there is no securely established comprehensive total and to specify the denominator whenever a number is used. Uncertainty about a universal count does not make the institution's coercive structure or the documented suffering uncertain.

Historical comparison should end with a distinction between proof of a system and proof of a particular incident. The former can be established through converging regulations, administrative records, military inquiries, and numerous testimonies; the latter often requires close attention to one location, witness, time, and available corroboration. A source window that identifies the creator, date, perspective, and weaknesses of an interrogation report teaches more than a dramatic excerpt offered without provenance. It also models careful treatment of a subject on which public memory and scholarship remain politically contested.

Koreans in Japan’s Final War: Air Raids, Hiroshima, Nagasaki, and Wartime Deaths

In 1944–1945 conditions deteriorated across the empire. Shortages worsened, labor demands intensified, and Allied bombing devastated Japanese cities and industrial regions. Korean workers lived in barracks and worked in factories and mines that were frequent targets, and Korean residents of Japanese cities shared the dangers of raids, though air-raid shelters and relief were often less available to them. Deaths from bombing, accidents, malnutrition, and disease occurred among forced laborers, long-term Korean residents, soldiers, and families, but they were not systematically recorded by category, so totals cannot be stated reliably.

Koreans were also among those exposed to the atomic bombings of Hiroshima on August 6 and Nagasaki on August 9, 1945. Published estimates commonly place the number of Koreans exposed at about seventy thousand, and the number killed in the tens of thousands, but both figures are uncertain because the populations affected included workers, residents, soldiers, and families whose presence was inconsistently recorded. Survivors faced injury and illness, difficult return, and in later decades limited access to recognition and medical support, a subject for later chapters. The distinctions among laborers, long-term residents, and military personnel matter because their circumstances, documentation, and later treatment differed.

Allied bombing affected Korean residents of Japan in ways that cannot be reduced to a single casualty total. Some Koreans were recent wartime labor recruits housed near factories, docks, and mines; others belonged to long-established urban communities and had families in Japan. Large air raids damaged workplaces, housing, transport, and food distribution. A worker might survive the bombing of a plant but lose shelter, employment records, and the possessions needed for a journey home. Discrimination and poverty could affect access to protection and recovery, although precise conditions varied locally and must be grounded in specific testimony or municipal records.

The atomic bombings created especially severe challenges of recognition and counting. Hiroshima was bombed on 6 August 1945 and Nagasaki on 9 August. Koreans living or working in those cities were among the dead and injured. Estimates often cited by Korean survivor organizations suggest approximately 70,000 Koreans exposed across both cities and about 40,000 deaths, with a further division between returnees and survivors who remained in Japan. These are historically influential reconstructions, not the result of a complete contemporaneous census of Korean victims. Different studies count deaths over different periods and use changing estimates of the exposed population.

A recent peer-reviewed study of Korean atomic-bomb survivors emphasizes precisely this evidentiary difficulty. Researchers can study registered survivors and later death records, but registration decades after the bombing necessarily excludes many who died earlier or were never identified. A medical cohort is therefore not the same thing as a 1945 census. Moreover, a death attributed to radiation illness decades later presents a different problem from a person killed in the immediate blast. Without these distinctions, a single number may conceal rather than illuminate the human cost of the bombings.

The term hibakusha describes people affected by the atomic bombings, but legal categories of survivor recognition developed after the war and were not equally accessible to everyone. Korean survivors could return to a homeland divided between occupation authorities, remain in Japan amid uncertain status, or move elsewhere. Documentation, language, citizenship, and residence could shape later eligibility for care. These complications should be introduced here as wartime consequences while leaving the extended history of postwar legal and diplomatic claims to later chapters.

Deaths in the broader mobilization system also resisted easy calculation. A laborer killed in an industrial accident, a military civilian who disappeared in Southeast Asia, a resident killed in an air raid, and a soldier who died in combat belonged to distinct administrative categories. Some records were destroyed during bombings or at surrender; others were kept by companies and military units using inconsistent spellings of Korean names. Counting all 'wartime Korean deaths' would require clearly defined populations, territories, and periods. A textbook should therefore identify reliable individual and institutional evidence rather than print a falsely precise imperial total.

The final months of war brought an additional humanitarian crisis through collapsing food distribution, disrupted medicine, and the breakdown of transport. Survivors often faced illness, bereavement, and the disappearance of documents establishing wages or service. These harms did not stop on the day Japan announced its surrender. They shaped the ability of workers and families to return, to find one another, and eventually to make claims about what had happened during the war.

Japan’s Surrender, Demobilization, Repatriation, and Stranded Koreans

Japan’s surrender on August 15, 1945 ended the war but not the dislocation. Military formations were disbanded, and Korean workers left mines and factories, often abruptly and sometimes without back pay. Transport was scarce: ports were damaged, ships were few, and allied and Japanese authorities improvised arrangements. Many Koreans in Japan waited for passage, and a number traveled by their own means. Early repatriation was a mixture of organized returns and individual efforts. A tragic episode, the explosion of the Ukishima Maru off the Japanese coast on August 24, 1945 while carrying Korean workers, killed a number of passengers; its death toll, commonly given in the hundreds, and its causes remain disputed.

Outcomes differed by place. In Japan, a large majority of Koreans returned during 1945–1946, but several hundred thousand remained, for reasons including established lives, family ties, lack of property or prospects in Korea, and the uncertain situation there; their legal status became a long-term issue. In Manchuria, Koreans faced civil war and shifting authorities, and many returned while others stayed. Korean soldiers and military employees returned from various fronts, some after captivity, some after trial.

Sakhalin presents the starkest case. After Soviet forces took the southern half of the island, Japanese residents were repatriated in the late 1940s, while most Koreans were not included; the policy treated Koreans differently from ethnic Japanese even though formal nationality changes occurred later, and no Korean government secured their general return at the time. Commonly cited estimates put the number left at roughly forty thousand. Many remained for decades, separated from relatives in Korea, a situation that was only partly resolved much later.

The announcement of surrender on 15 August 1945 ended the authority through which Japanese officials had justified wartime mobilization, but it did not instantaneously supply ships, money, or reliable information. Industrial employers were losing contracts, military units were dissolving, and ports and rail links were damaged or overwhelmed. Workers could leave a site only to confront the problem of how to travel safely, where to obtain food, and whether family members were alive. Some had their wages or identity documents in company custody. Others had been moved between assignments without a complete record that relatives could use to locate them.

Repatriation was a process, not a single centrally planned movement. Japanese, Allied, and local authorities established arrangements as transportation became available, while many Koreans sought private or improvised passage. Those leaving Japan differed in circumstance: a recently dispatched unmarried worker might have immediate reason to return, whereas a family established in Osaka or another city might have livelihoods and children whose futures were tied to Japan. A majority of the roughly two million Koreans in Japan in 1945 returned during the postwar period, but a substantial population remained. This was not a straightforward division between free choice and compulsion: insecurity in Korea, social ties, and practical access to transport all mattered.

The sinking of the Ukishima Maru on 24 August 1945 shows the danger of the return journey. The ship was carrying Koreans from northern Japan when it sank near Maizuru. Japanese official records commonly state a toll in the hundreds, but estimates of passengers and casualties have remained disputed, and interpretations of the precise circumstances have been contested. The event should be presented as a documented maritime tragedy without adopting an unsupported assertion about deliberate sinking or an exact death toll as indisputable fact. Families' difficulties locating passengers and establishing who died demonstrate the wider weakness of wartime and postwar recordkeeping.

Southern Sakhalin followed an especially consequential path. The Soviet advance in August 1945 transferred the territory from Japanese to Soviet control. Repatriation agreements and practice subsequently privileged the return of ethnic Japanese residents, while tens of thousands of Koreans were left without comparable arrangements. Ki-young Choi's historical study discusses an estimate of roughly 43,000 Koreans remaining after the war. That number describes a postwar population, not a total of Korean workers dispatched in 1944 or an exact measure of people who wished to leave. The intersection of Soviet labor needs, Japanese responsibility, Korean political fragmentation, and early Cold War diplomacy prolonged their separation from relatives.

The circumstances of Sakhalin Koreans also changed over time. Some had come earlier as migrants, some had been recruited or forced to work in the mines, and some were family members born or raised on the island. Their experience cannot be reduced to that of a single wartime draft cohort. The Soviet administration reorganized work and residence, while decisions about nationality and possible migration were shaped by political change in Northeast Asia. A fuller account of their decades-long struggle for repatriation belongs to postwar history, but the essential fact here is that the collapse of the Japanese Empire did not restore equal freedom of movement to everyone it had displaced.

Koreans in Manchuria, China, Southeast Asia, and military camps elsewhere encountered other regimes and dangers. The Soviet entry into the war, the surrender of Japanese armies, Chinese civil conflict, and Allied custody affected routes and timing. Some people were detained for investigation or war-crimes proceedings, and others became refugees or remained in communities where they had lived before the war. These trajectories remind students that liberation in Korea was not one identical event experienced simultaneously by all Koreans abroad. The location of a person on 15 August 1945 decisively shaped what freedom could mean in the months that followed.

The change in Korean political status further complicated repatriation. Japanese nationality law and the legal status of former colonial subjects did not transform everywhere on the same day; the postwar status of Koreans living in Japan underwent additional changes under occupation and later treaty arrangements. It would be anachronistic to explain every 1945 decision as if the subsequent nationality settlement were already fixed. The accurate approach is to trace concrete transport policies, Soviet restrictions, Allied occupation rules, and the emerging Korean political authorities as they developed, rather than project later legal categories backward.

Unpaid Wages, Missing Workers, Survivor Testimony, and the Legacies of Total War

The end of the empire left a legacy of unresolved material and human consequences. Many workers returned without wages that had been withheld, deposited, or lost through deductions, and savings and pensions held in Japanese accounts or institutions were often inaccessible. Others did not return at all: workers who died at their sites, were killed in raids, or disappeared. Remains were buried near workplaces, in temples, or in unmarked graves, and many have not been identified or returned. Injured workers and veterans lived with disability, and survivors of labor, military service, and sexual slavery carried trauma that was often unspoken.

Families were separated for years or permanently, and in a divided peninsula the destinations of returnees, and the families they sought, might lie in different occupation zones. Responsibility and compensation became disputed questions among governments, companies, and survivors. Those disputes, including postwar agreements, court cases, and memorial movements, are treated in later chapters, particularly Chapter 88, and are not resolved here. What this chapter establishes is why they arose: the wartime systems left records that were incomplete, populations that were dispersed, and claims that were never settled at the time.

The collapse of a mobilization system did not settle the claims created within it. Employers and officials had deducted wages for food, accommodation, compulsory savings, and remittances; some workers had been promised payments upon completion of service or upon return to Korea. At surrender, a person might possess no more than a work identity card or a memory of the promised amount. Companies closed facilities, transferred accounts, or lost records. A sum recorded as paid by an employer is not proof that a worker received it freely or could withdraw it. Historians reconstruct unpaid earnings by comparing payroll ledgers, deposit records, receipts, testimony, and later claims.

Compensation questions contain several distinct kinds of obligation. Unpaid wages are earnings for work already performed; compulsory savings are sums withheld or deposited; unpaid benefits and pensions depend on different rules; and claims for injury, death, or unlawful coercion invoke still other evidence and legal principles. Combining these categories in a single figure obscures both their historical origins and the obstacles faced by families. A worker seeking a balance in a deposit account had a different documentary task from a family trying to establish that a missing relative died at a particular workplace.

Missing-person inquiries were often complicated by wartime movement. A man could be recruited in Korea, transported to a Japanese mine, reassigned, and then leave after surrender without having his destination recorded in any one archive. A company might use a Japanese rendering of his name; a temple might retain a burial record with a different spelling; a family's letter might identify only a locality or employer. Duró and Palmer's study of Korean graves in Hokkaido demonstrates why the recovery of remains requires painstaking identification rather than a simple comparison of totals. The act of locating a body or ashes can restore a name to a person otherwise recorded only as part of a labor quota.

Survivor testimony adds information that official accounting often excludes. Workers can describe food, punishment, illness, escape, pay practices, relationships with supervisors, and the experience of returning home. Women who survived military sexual slavery may describe recruitment and confinement otherwise absent from military paperwork. Former servicemen and families of the missing provide evidence about military posting, letters, and nonreturn. Such testimony must be handled with respect and evaluated carefully, recognizing the effects of trauma, memory, time, and interview setting while avoiding the mistake of treating administrative silence as evidence that suffering did not occur.

Statistics remain useful when their units are explicit. A count of departures is different from a count of unique people, persons currently employed, deaths at selected sites, or survivors registered decades later. A soldier total should not automatically incorporate military civilian employees; a number for Koreans in Japan includes established migrants and families as well as wartime workers; and the number of people left on Sakhalin after 1945 is not a direct measure of forced dispatches to the island. Comparison should begin by writing down the population, geographic area, date range, and record-making institution represented by each figure.

The long-term historical significance reaches beyond financial claims. Families lived with uncertain deaths and absent relatives; survivors carried injuries and memories; Korean communities abroad confronted new political boundaries; and the aftermath entered disputes among Korean, Japanese, Soviet, and other authorities. Later negotiations and court proceedings belong to subsequent chapters, particularly the discussion of Korean–Japanese memory politics, but they did not arise independently of the wartime system. They began in workers' ledgers, army records, interrupted letters, and families' unanswered questions.

This chapter's central conclusion is therefore twofold. Japan's wartime demand for resources transformed colonial rule into an increasingly comprehensive set of obligations imposed on people with little power over the policies that directed their lives. At the same time, the consequences cannot be reconstructed through a single category or a single statistic. Preserving distinctions among recruitment, placement, labor draft, military enlistment, industrial women's labor, and military sexual slavery enables a history that recognizes systemic responsibility and individual experience without sacrificing either to oversimplification.

Evidence Comparison

Official Mobilization Statistics versus Individual Records and Testimony

Official statistics record what administrators counted: quotas, dispatches, or inductions, for particular programs, years, and territories. They are useful for scale and change over time, but they measure administrative categories, may omit unrecorded movement, and do not describe conditions or consent. Totals that combine programs, territories, or military and civilian categories can mislead.

Individual labor and service records (rosters, contracts, death and burial registers) allow historians to identify named people and workplace outcomes. They survive unevenly and reflect employers’ and officials’ purposes.

Survivor testimony provides experience, conditions, and context that records omit, but it is recorded long after events, from those who survived and spoke, and requires corroboration.

Why the comparison matters: Each source type answers different questions. Statistics show magnitude, records identify individuals, and testimony reveals experience. Any statistic must be read with attention to exactly which population, period, territory, and category it measures.

Cause and Effect

Why Koreans Were Mobilized on This Scale

Long-term causes

Colonial rule had given Japan administrative reach over Korea’s people, land, and economy, and a population already tied to Japanese industry through migration.

Short-term causes

The 1937 war in China and the 1941 Pacific War created labor and manpower shortages as Japanese men entered the army.

Effects

Hundreds of thousands moved to Japan; many more worked within the empire or served in the military; households suffered scarcity and separation.

Long-term consequences

Unpaid wages, missing workers, stranded populations, and unresolved claims continued well after 1945.

Historical complexity: Mobilization resulted from empire’s demand, colonial administration, and local implementation together. No single decision or actor explains it.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From Colonial Subordination to Total Mobilization

What changed: Obligations expanded from exhortation and production quotas to recruitment, placement, and legal conscription for both labor and the military. What remained continuous: The colonial hierarchy that placed Koreans under Japanese officials and made their obligations unequal to their rights. When: Change was gradual until 1942 and accelerated sharply in 1943–1944. Who felt it most: Rural households, young men, students, and women whom officials and recruiters could reach. Why it matters: The pattern shows total war intensifying, rather than replacing, existing colonial structures.

Historical Vocabulary
Comfort Station and Military Sexual Slavery

Definition: “Comfort station” (ianjo) was the military’s euphemistic term for facilities where women were made available to soldiers. Many historians and survivors describe the system as military sexual slavery because of the coercion and confinement involved.

Historical context: The terminology is contested in public debate; this textbook uses the euphemistic term only when quoting its historical usage and otherwise describes the conditions directly.

Why it matters: Names shape understanding of responsibility and experience.

Potential misunderstanding: The term “comfort” describes the military’s view, not the women’s experience.

Chapter Review and Historical Thinking Checkpoint

Synthesis. Japan’s total war drew Koreans into several overlapping systems. At home, households met delivery quotas, rationing, savings drives, and school labor demands. In labor, programs moved from company recruitment (1939) to official placement (1942) to broad labor conscription (1944). In the military, a limited volunteer system (1938) widened through student and naval recruitment (1943) into conscription (1944). Women worked in farms and factories and were mobilized through the Women’s Volunteer Labor Corps, while a separate military system of sexual slavery confined and abused women in many territories. Surrender in 1945 left unpaid wages, missing workers, stranded communities, and unresolved claims. The sections of this chapter are best understood as distinct systems with different legal bases, whose statistics and experiences must not be merged.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined what Japan’s total war meant for Koreans between 1937 and 1945. Its central argument is that mobilization worked through multiple overlapping systems, each with its own legal basis, administrators, and consequences, and that no single word, whether “voluntary” or “forced,” describes them all.

The mobilization framework began with the 1938 National General Mobilization Law, an enabling statute that authorized control of labor and resources through later ordinances. The law’s practical effects depended on those measures and on the Government-General, provincial and local officials, police, and employers who carried them out.

On the home front, Korean households faced delivery quotas, rationing, savings campaigns, and metal collection. Production for the empire did not mean more food for Koreans. Households responded through substitution, informal exchange, and efforts to protect resources, within tightly constrained options. Students were redirected to labor service and, from 1943, became targets of military recruitment.

Labor mobilization passed through three phases: company recruitment from 1939, government-directed placement from 1942, and broad labor conscription from September 1944. Compulsion grew in each phase, and practice often preceded formal law. Workers in mines, factories, shipyards, and construction faced controlled housing, long hours, dangerous work, deductions from pay, discrimination, and limited ability to leave. Labor also extended to Korea, Manchuria, and Sakhalin.

Military service followed a separate track: a limited army volunteer system from 1938, naval and student recruitment in 1943, and conscription examinations from April 1944 with induction from September. Applications under volunteer systems were shaped by administrative pressure and colonial inequality. Korean personnel served as soldiers, sailors, and military civilian employees, including guards of Allied prisoners, whose responsibility requires careful, individual assessment.

Women worked in agriculture and industry and were organized in 1944 into the Women’s Volunteer Labor Corps. That institution was distinct from the military’s system of sexual slavery, though deception and fear connected them in popular experience. The comfort-station system involved military authorities, brokers, and officials in recruiting, transporting, and confining women through deception, coercion, and abuse of authority.

Historians reconstruct that system from military records, a 1944 Allied interrogation report, survivor testimony beginning publicly in 1991, and later government inquiries, including the 1993 Kōno Statement. Each source has limits, and disagreement over numbers and specific recruitment methods does not erase documented military involvement and coercive conditions.

In 1944–1945, air raids, shortages, and the atomic bombings killed Koreans among others; estimates of deaths remain uncertain. Japan’s surrender produced disbanded units, chaotic departures, scarce transport, and varied outcomes: return for most Koreans in Japan, continued residence for several hundred thousand, and long-term stranding for most Koreans on southern Sakhalin.

The consequences of mobilization persisted in unpaid wages, missing workers, unreturned remains, disability, trauma, and family separation. Postwar disputes over responsibility and compensation, treated in later chapters, grew from these unresolved legacies.

Finally, the chapter has shown why evidence matters. Statistics measure administrative categories, records identify individuals, and testimony conveys experience. Reading them together, and specifying exactly what each measures, allows historians to describe a system that was neither uniform nor reducible to one label.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows what an imperial strategy meant for the people on whom it fell. Chapter 61 explained why Korea was valuable to Japan’s expansion; Chapter 62 shows how that value was extracted through quotas, notices, recruiters, and orders that reached into villages, schools, and homes.

It also shows why careful categories matter. Reducing wartime experience to one word, “voluntary” or “forced,” would erase differences among workers, conscripts, volunteers, women, and civilians, and would confuse distinct legal systems and statistics. Keeping distinctions makes it possible to take both the evidence of coercion and the evidence of constrained choice seriously.

The consequences carried beyond 1945. Unpaid wages, missing workers, stranded communities, and unresolved claims entered the postwar world just as Korea faced liberation, occupation, and division, subjects of Chapters 63 through 66. They also became enduring questions in Korean–Japanese relations, explored in Chapter 88.

Finally, the chapter shows how historians work with incomplete evidence: reading statutes beside testimony, statistics beside burial registers, and wartime reports beside later memory. Understanding the war’s human cost depends on using each carefully and honestly.
