Silhak and New Ways of Understanding Society

Korean History: Ancient Kingdoms to a Divided Peninsula

Chapter Opening

In the winter of 1765 Hong Dae-yong, a Seoul scholar who held no significant office, joined a Joseon diplomatic mission to Beijing as a companion of his uncle, a member of the delegation. He went partly to see the Qing capital and partly to meet Chinese literati, and he later wrote about the journey in both Classical Chinese and Hangeul. Nearly a century later, in 1861, the cartographer Kim Jeong-ho published a woodblock-printed map of the entire peninsula in twenty-two folding volumes. Between those two moments lay a body of scholarship on land, agriculture, geography, history, astronomy, and technology that is conventionally grouped under the name Practical Learning, or Silhak. What did these scholars actually do, and how much did their ways of knowing change the society around them?

Chapter 37 examined how kings and factions competed for political authority between the mid-seventeenth century and 1800. This chapter turns to the intellectual life that unfolded alongside that competition. It asks how scholars in later Joseon investigated the problems of a society recovering from invasion, commercializing, and encountering new information from the Qing, Japan, and, indirectly, Europe. It follows their proposals for land reform, their studies of farming, settlement, and territory, their histories of the Korean past, their maps, and their responses to Western astronomy and mechanics.

The chapter's central subject is also a historiographical problem. The label Silhak is widely used in textbooks, museums, and popular writing, and it often carries a heroic story: a band of reform-minded thinkers, shut out by a rigid Neo-Confucian establishment, anticipated Korean modernity. Historians have increasingly questioned that story. The scholars discussed here differed greatly in their politics, their social positions, and their proposals; most were deeply Confucian; some served in government while others lived in rural retirement; and the grouping itself was largely constructed in the twentieth century. This chapter takes the label seriously as an object of study without assuming that it describes a single movement.

The chapter proceeds in a mostly chronological sequence. It begins with the conditions after the seventeenth-century wars that encouraged new kinds of inquiry, then examines the concept of Silhak itself. It treats the land-reform proposals of Yu Hyeong-won and Yi Ik, the Northern Learning scholars who looked to the Qing as a source of practical knowledge, the literature on agriculture and rural life, human geography and cartography, historical and evidential scholarship, and engagement with Western learning. A section on Jeong Yak-yong, whose writings cut across nearly all of these fields, is followed by a discussion of how scholars related to the court and why so few of their proposals were fully implemented. The chapter closes with the legacy of this scholarship and the ways later generations remembered it.

The evidence for this subject is largely textual and largely elite. It consists of treatises, essays, travel diaries, anthologies of collected writings, gazetteers, maps, and compilations produced by educated men, most of them yangban or, in a few important cases, jungin or secondary-son (seoeol) scholars. Many works circulated in manuscript for decades before printing, and some were printed long after their authors died; Yu Hyeong-won's major work, for example, was written in the seventeenth century and printed only in 1770. Dating, authorship, and textual transmission therefore matter. The voices of farmers, artisans, merchants, and women appear mainly through the observations of others, and the chapter marks where this limitation shapes what can be said.

The geography of the chapter extends well beyond Seoul. Yu Hyeong-won wrote in rural Jeolla Province, Yi Ik lived near Ansan in Gyeonggi Province, Hong Dae-yong and Park Ji-won travelled to Beijing and Manchuria, Jeong Yak-yong spent nearly two decades in exile in Gangjin on the southwestern coast, his brother Jeong Yak-jeon studied marine life while exiled on the island of Heuksan, and the epigrapher and calligrapher Kim Jeong-hui was sent to Jeju. Learning, in other words, was produced in provincial villages, island exile, and the roads to the Qing capital as well as at court.

Finally, the chapter prepares the ground for what follows. Chapter 39 examines commercial expansion, which formed part of the economic setting for these scholars' arguments. Chapter 40 treats religion, including the Catholic community that grew out of contact with Western books. Chapter 41 turns to literature and popular culture, and Chapter 42 to family and status. Chapter 43 and the chapters after it show what became of the scholarly tradition when the political order of the nineteenth century took shape.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain the conditions in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, including wartime damage, commercial recovery, and contact with the Qing, that encouraged new kinds of inquiry.
  2. Distinguish the premodern meaning of "practical learning" from the twentieth-century historiographical category Silhak.
  3. Compare the land-system proposals of Yu Hyeong-won, Yi Ik, Park Ji-won, and Jeong Yak-yong, and identify what they shared and where they differed.
  4. Analyze the Northern Learning scholars' arguments for learning from the Qing and explain how those arguments related to continuing Ming loyalism.
  5. Describe the agricultural and rural-economy literature of later Joseon and assess its connection to actual farming practice.
  6. Trace developments in human geography, historical geography, and cartography, including the work of Yi Jung-hwan, Jeong Yak-yong, and Kim Jeong-ho.
  7. Evaluate eighteenth- and nineteenth-century historical and evidential scholarship, including treatments of Gojoseon, Balhae, and Silla epigraphy.
  8. Distinguish Western science and technology as practical knowledge from Catholic doctrine in Joseon debates over Western Learning.
  9. Interpret a primary source from a Northern Learning scholar, identifying its purpose, perspective, and limits.
  10. Assess why many scholars' proposals were not implemented, weighing political, fiscal, social, and intellectual constraints.
  11. Evaluate competing historians' interpretations of Silhak, including claims that it anticipated modernity.
  12. Connect the intellectual developments of this chapter to political developments in Chapter 37 and to the nineteenth-century crises examined in later chapters.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did concerns about land, taxation, and rural livelihood become central topics of scholarship after the seventeenth-century wars?
  2. Was "Practical Learning" a coherent movement, a loose collection of individuals, or a later scholarly construction?
  3. How could scholars who revered the Ming still argue that Joseon had something to learn from the Qing?
  4. How did these scholars understand the relationship between agriculture, commerce, and the proper order of society?
  5. What new methods of investigation, including travel, measurement, textual criticism, and examination of inscriptions, appeared in this scholarship?
  6. How did Joseon scholars distinguish useful Western science from what they regarded as heterodox Western religion?
  7. Why were so many reform proposals discussed, praised, and then left unimplemented?
  8. How did the social position of the scholar, whether officeholder, exile, provincial retiree, or secondary son, shape what he wrote?
  9. How have modern historians, in the colonial era and in both Korean states, used the memory of this scholarship for their own purposes?

Historical Context

The scholarly world of later Joseon rested on foundations laid earlier in the dynasty. Chapter 26 described the Neo-Confucian state and its examination system, and Chapters 27 and 28 examined the creation of Hangeul and the scientific achievements of Sejong's reign, which showed that the court had long treated astronomy, agriculture, medicine, and printing as proper concerns of government. Joseon's literati also had a living tradition of practical statecraft within Neo-Confucianism itself. Scholar-officials of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, such as Yi I (Yulgok) and the advocates of the Daedong tax reform, argued that moral cultivation had to be joined with concrete institutional reform. Much later scholarship grew from this tradition and cannot be separated from it.

The invasions of the 1590s and the 1620s–1630s, examined in Chapters 31 through 36, supplied the immediate impetus. The Imjin War destroyed cropland, registers, and records, and the post-war recovery required resurveying land, rebuilding tax rolls, and restoring the countryside. The Manchu invasions and the surrender of 1637 created a different problem: the kingdom's ruling class, schooled to regard the Ming as the center of civilization, now had to deal with a Qing empire that governed China. As Chapter 37 showed, loyalty to the Ming became both a moral stance and a political resource. The same elites who invoked that loyalty nevertheless sent envoys to Beijing several times each year, and the delegations carried back books, instruments, and information.

Economic and demographic recovery shaped the setting. Agricultural output, population, and market activity rose over the later seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, a development treated in Chapter 39. Rural communities spread rice transplanting and double-cropping in some regions, cash and cloth moved through markets, and the Daedong law converted tribute obligations into a land-based levy. These changes made existing land and tax arrangements appear inadequate to some observers, and they gave scholars concrete problems to analyze. At the same time, the elite grew crowded: educated families outnumbered posts, and as Chapter 37 explained, factional defeats removed entire lineages from office. Several of the scholars in this chapter, among them Yu Hyeong-won, Yi Ik, and Yi Jung-hwan, belonged to families or factions, especially the Southerners (Namin), that were excluded from central power for long periods.

Joseon intellectual life was also more varied than the label "Neo-Confucian orthodoxy" suggests. The state endorsed the interpretation of Zhu Xi, and scholars who departed from it could be denounced; Song Si-yeol and his followers, for example, condemned the Southerner Yun Hyu as a disturber of orthodoxy for reading the classics independently of Zhu Xi's commentaries. Yet debates within the orthodox tradition were lively, as in the early-eighteenth-century dispute over whether the nature of humans and animals was the same, and a small circle of scholars centered on Ganghwa Island, including Jeong Je-du, pursued the teachings of Wang Yangming privately. Practical Learning emerged from this plural, contested environment, not from a monolithic intellectual establishment against which a new school rebelled.

Section 38.1

Knowledge after the Wars: Reconstruction, the Qing, and New Information

The seventeenth century left Joseon scholars with an unusual combination of motivations. Practical necessity pressed in from every side: records had to be rebuilt, land resurveyed, famine managed, and the fiscal system restored. Intellectual and moral concern pushed in the same direction, since many literati believed that the catastrophe of the wars revealed a failure of governance that scholarship should help to repair. Out of this convergence came a body of writing that asked what institutions a good government required, and it asked the question with unusual specificity about land, taxes, and the military.

New information entered Joseon through several channels. The principal one was the Beijing mission. Joseon delegations traveled to the Qing capital several times each year, and their members, including official envoys, secretaries, interpreters, and attendants, purchased books, observed Qing cities, and met Chinese and Manchu officials and scholars. These missions were also commercial occasions, since merchants and interpreters accompanied them. A second channel was Japan: between 1607 and 1811 the court sent twelve missions to the Tokugawa shogunate, which gave participants direct knowledge of Japanese cities and agriculture. A third, indirect channel brought European knowledge by way of Chinese-language books, maps, and instruments produced by Jesuit missionaries in China. When Crown Prince Sohyeon was held in Shenyang and Beijing, he met the Jesuit astronomer Adam Schall and returned with books and instruments, though his early death in 1645 prevented him from becoming king, as Chapter 37 noted.

These channels did not produce a uniform reception. The Qing's Manchu rulers were widely regarded by Joseon officials as barbarian conquerors who had displaced a Chinese dynasty to which Joseon owed its civilization, and the court's ritual life continued to honor the Ming. At the same time, envoys reported the substantial wealth, productive agriculture, and technical capability they saw in Qing cities. The tension between ideological hostility and practical admiration is one of the central themes of the chapter. Scholars who argued for learning from the Qing had to explain how doing so was compatible with loyalty to the vanished Ming order, and the arguments they made are examined in Section 38.5.

Joseon was also developing its own reference literature. Yi Su-gwang's Jibong yuseol, completed in 1614, collected material on a wide range of subjects, including the geography of foreign countries and summaries of Western writings obtained from China. Heo Jun's medical compendium, the Donguibogam, completed in 1610 and printed in 1613, systematized medical knowledge for Joseon practitioners and drew on both Chinese sources and local materials. Such works show that interest in encyclopedic, practical, and foreign knowledge predated the writers most often labeled Silhak scholars, and they remind students that later Joseon intellectual life drew on continuous traditions.

Section 38.2

What Was "Silhak"? Concept, Name, and Historiography

The phrase silhak (實學), literally "substantive" or "real" learning, was not invented in the twentieth century. Premodern Korean, Chinese, and Japanese scholars used it to mean learning with real content, as opposed to empty rhetoric or mere exam-taking technique, and Neo-Confucian thinkers regularly used the term to describe their own studies. In that older sense nearly any serious Joseon scholar could claim to practice silhak. The modern category is narrower and more specific: it refers to a group of seventeenth- through nineteenth-century thinkers, usually including Yu Hyeong-won, Yi Ik, Park Ji-won, Hong Dae-yong, Jeong Yak-yong, and others, whose writings are said to share a concern with practical reform and empirical inquiry.

That modern category took shape in the early twentieth century, and its history is part of the subject. In the 1930s, during Japanese colonial rule, Korean scholars associated with what was called the Joseon studies movement edited and published the collected works of Jeong Yak-yong and others, partly as a way of asserting that Koreans had produced their own indigenous intellectual achievements. Colonial-era Japanese scholarship often portrayed late Joseon as stagnant and dependent, and nationalist historians answered by emphasizing internal vitality. After 1945 both Korean states adopted the category, for different reasons. North Korean historians generally treated the scholars as progressive precursors who reflected "sprouts" of capitalist development within feudal society, while South Korean historians in the 1960s and 1970s developed the "internal development" thesis, which looked for the roots of modernization in late Joseon rather than in imperial imposition. Chapters 57 through 60 and Volume IV return to the larger politics of historical memory.

Scholars have also developed a conventional internal classification, though it too is a modern construction. One group, the school of statecraft and institutional reform (gyeongse chiyong), is represented by Yu Hyeong-won, Yi Ik, and Jeong Yak-yong, who concentrated on land, taxation, officialdom, and law. A second, the school of utility and enriching the people (iyong husaeng), is represented by Hong Dae-yong, Park Ji-won, and Park Je-ga, who stressed commerce, technology, and learning from the Qing. A third emphasis on "seeking truth from facts" (silsa gusi), represented by Kim Jeong-hui and historians of evidential research, focused on textual criticism, epigraphy, and historical method. These groupings are useful for organizing the chapter, but they overlap, and many individual scholars belong to more than one.

In recent decades, historians have raised several objections to the older picture. They note that the scholars did not form a school, did not describe themselves as a group, and often disagreed; that most were committed Confucians who sought to realize the ancient sages' vision, not to depart from it; that some held office and shaped policy, rather than standing outside the establishment; and that the notion of an unbroken line from Silhak to modern reform risks reading the nineteenth and twentieth centuries back into the eighteenth. The next feature presents the vocabulary and the debate.

Historical Vocabulary
Silhak (Practical Learning)

Definition. Silhak (實學) is a Korean term meaning "substantive" or "practical" learning. In modern historiography it names a group of late Joseon scholars concerned with institutional reform, economic life, empirical inquiry, and engagement with knowledge from the Qing and the West.

Historical context. Premodern scholars used the term broadly for serious, content-rich scholarship. The narrower modern meaning crystallized in the twentieth century through nationalist historiography and was elaborated differently in North and South Korea after 1945.

Why it matters. The term organizes a large body of important scholarship, but it can also impose a unity that the evidence does not show.

Potential misunderstanding. Silhak is sometimes translated "pragmatism" or described as opposed to Neo-Confucianism. Neither is accurate: most of the scholars were Neo-Confucian in training and outlook, and their goals were typically Confucian restoration and the welfare of the people, not secular modernization.

Historical Debate

Was Silhak a School, a Tendency, or a Construction?

The question: Does the label Silhak describe a real intellectual movement in late Joseon, or is it a modern category that groups diverse scholars under a single name?

Interpretation A — A reformist current anticipating modernity: Scholars influenced by the internal-development thesis argue that these thinkers shared critical attitudes toward existing institutions, interest in empirical inquiry, and concern for the common people, and that together they formed a recognizable current that foreshadowed later reform. On this view, the label captures a genuine historical pattern.
Interpretation B — A heterogeneous set of individuals: Other historians stress that the scholars belonged to different factions and generations, wrote on different topics, seldom cited one another as a group, and sometimes held conflicting views. The grouping, they argue, was imposed retrospectively, and treating it as a school hides real differences.
Interpretation C — Continuity within Neo-Confucian statecraft: A third approach emphasizes that the concerns of these scholars, including land, taxation, and administration, continued earlier Neo-Confucian statecraft, and that the contrast with "orthodox" learning is exaggerated. On this view, Silhak is best understood as part of a broader, internally varied tradition of reformist Confucian thought.

Evidence: Historians draw on the scholars' own writings and correspondence, the dates and circumstances of printing, evidence of personal and teacher–student connections, and the twentieth-century editorial and publishing record that popularized the category.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns terminology and interpretation rather than the existence of the texts: whether shared themes amount to a movement, and whether to emphasize continuity or novelty.

Why it matters: If Silhak was a proto-modern movement, late Joseon appears as a society on the way to modernity until external pressure interrupted it. If it was a diverse set of Confucian reformers, late Joseon appears as a complex premodern society with its own intellectual dynamics.

Student task: Select two scholars discussed in this chapter and list what they share and where they differ. Then decide whether those similarities are enough to justify placing them under a single label, and explain your reasoning.

Section 38.3

Land, Reconstruction, and Yu Hyeong-won's Program of Reform

Land was the central problem of Joseon political economy. It was the principal source of revenue, the foundation of elite status, and the livelihood of most of the population. Over time, large estates accumulated in the hands of yangban families and powerful households, while many cultivators were tenants who paid a substantial part of their harvest as rent. Scholars therefore turned to land reform, and they usually did so by invoking classical models. The most important was the ancient Chinese "well-field" system described in the classics, in which land was imagined as divided into squares, with a communal plot at the center, so that every family had a fair allotment. Whether this system ever existed as described is doubtful, but for Confucian reformers it represented the ideal of an equitable agrarian order.

Yu Hyeong-won (1622–1673) produced the most comprehensive seventeenth-century proposal. He was born in Seoul to a family with important officeholders, but he withdrew from the exam path, settled in Buan in Jeolla Province, and devoted himself to study. His Bangye surok, written over many years, takes its title from the name of the rural location where he lived. It proposed a complete program: a public-land system (gongjeon) in which the state would hold land in principle and distribute it according to a formula tied to status and service; reform of the military and tax system; a restructured school and examination system based on recommendation and schooling; and rationalization of the bureaucracy. Yu criticized the hereditary transmission of slave status and the arbitrary privileges of the powerful. He did not propose abolishing the hierarchy of ranks but sought to fit it to a more orderly framework.

The work's history shows how much textual transmission matters. Yu died in 1673 and the manuscript circulated in limited circles; it was not printed until 1770, under King Yeongjo, almost a century after its author's death. The delay suggests how limited the immediate influence of such proposals could be, and it shows that reform ideas could be preserved by family, disciples, and the occasional interested official until a political moment made them useful. Yu's reputation grew mainly after the eighteenth century, when scholars including Yi Ik and Jeong Yak-yong read and praised him.

Why did Yu and others look to land redistribution when state capacity to enforce it was so limited? Part of the answer lies in the moral logic of Confucian political thought, in which a sound distribution of land was the foundation of a stable and virtuous society. Part lies in observation: the post-war countryside was full of abandoned plots, disputed holdings, and unregistered land. And part is political. For Southerner scholars excluded from power, writing a comprehensive reform program was a way to demonstrate seriousness of purpose and to propose an alternative to existing policy without holding office. These factors should not be treated as mutually exclusive.

Historical Vocabulary
Gongjeon, Jeongjeon, Yeojeon, Hanjeon, and Yeongeop-jeon

Definition. These are names for proposed land systems. Gongjeon ("public land") refers to Yu Hyeong-won's scheme of state-allocated land. Jeongjeon ("well-field") refers to the ancient ideal of nine equal squares. Yeojeon ("village land") was Jeong Yak-yong's proposal for village-level collective cultivation with the produce distributed according to labor. Hanjeon ("limited land") refers to proposals, including Park Ji-won's, to cap private landholding. Yeongeop-jeon ("livelihood land") was Yi Ik's proposal for a minimum holding that could not be sold.

Historical context. All of these were responses to concentrated landholding, tenancy, and tax inequity. They differed in how far they would disturb existing property and how much they relied on state enforcement.

Why it matters. Comparing them reveals a spectrum from radical redistribution to modest limits, and shows that scholars argued among themselves about feasibility.

Potential misunderstanding. These proposals were not socialist or communist programs in the modern sense. They sought to restore a Confucian agrarian order and generally preserved hierarchy and private household farming.

Section 38.4

Yi Ik, the Seongho Circle, and the Critique of Social Practice

Yi Ik (1681–1763) lived most of his life near Ansan in Gyeonggi Province, a scholar from a Southerner family that had been shut out of office in the political reversals described in Chapter 37. His brother had died in the aftermath of a political purge, and Yi himself abandoned any ambition for official career and devoted his life to study, teaching, and writing. His best-known work, the Seongho saseol (Seongho's Miscellaneous Essays), is a large, topical collection of notes on subjects ranging from the natural world and Korean and Chinese history to institutions and social issues. It was compiled over many years and edited by his disciples, which illustrates the collaborative production of learning in this period.

Yi Ik studied land, taxation, slavery, the examination system, and the economy. He proposed a minimum landholding (yeongeop-jeon) that could not be sold, leaving other land open to the market. The proposal was more modest than Yu Hyeong-won's, aiming to protect small farmers from being forced to sell, without requiring the state to redistribute land. He also criticized the heavy reliance on slavery, the effects of the examination system on the quality of officials, and the idleness of those who lived on others' labor. Like many of his contemporaries, he treated coin and money circulation as a subject of concern, discussing whether commerce harmed or helped agrarian society. His views were not uniformly favorable to markets, and he voiced worries about the effects of a money economy on farmers.

Yi's importance rests partly on his role as a teacher. His circle of disciples, sometimes called the Seongho school, included scholars of widely different outlooks. After his death the group became divided over the reception of Western learning: some members, such as those later associated with Catholic belief, have been described as the "Sinseo" (new Western) wing, while others, who rejected Catholicism, have been called the "Gongseo" (attacking the Western) wing. The groupings were fluid and have been refined by later historians, but they show how a single teacher's students could draw different conclusions from the same body of texts. Yi himself was interested in Western books on astronomy and geography but regarded Catholic doctrine with skepticism. Chapter 40 and Chapter 45 examine the Catholic community and its persecution.

Yi Ik's example also illustrates the limits of reading these scholars as outsiders. He worked within Confucian concepts and wrote for an audience of other literati. He did not seek to overthrow the hierarchy. The reformers' distinctive contribution lay in the persistence and specificity with which they examined concrete problems of livelihood and institutions, and in the willingness of some to criticize well-established practices, including the use of hereditary slavery and the way the examinations were administered. That criticism was real, but it occurred within a shared moral framework.

Section 38.5

Northern Learning: Beijing, the Qing, and Arguments for Utility

In the second half of the eighteenth century, a group of mostly Seoul-based scholars began to argue openly that Joseon should learn from the Qing. They are called the Northern Learning (Bukhak) scholars, and the name itself is pointed: "northern" had long meant the barbarian north in contrast to the civilized Chinese center, and the scholars used it to turn that hostility around. Prominent members included Hong Dae-yong, Park Ji-won, Park Je-ga, Yi Deok-mu, and Yu Deuk-gong. Several lived in or near the neighborhood around the white pagoda in Seoul and are often described as the Baektap circle. Hong Dae-yong and Park Ji-won came from Noron families and are often linked with the Noron intellectual debates over the nature of humans and animals, a reminder that this group belonged to the heart of the capital's elite.

Hong Dae-yong's Beijing journey of 1765–1766 was a milestone. Traveling as an attendant to his uncle, he sought out Chinese literati in Beijing, formed friendships with scholars from the Hangzhou region, and recorded conversations about learning, friendship, and philosophy. He wrote an account of the journey in Classical Chinese and also a Hangeul version, the latter a notable example of vernacular travel writing that Chapter 41 returns to. He also wrote a dialogue, the Uisan mundap (Dialogue on Mount Uisan), set at a mountain in Liaoning, in which a figure representing conventional learning debates a figure representing true learning. In it he argued that the earth rotates on its axis and that the universe has no single center, which undermined the traditional view that China occupied the center of the world. He is also reported to have constructed an astronomical instrument at his family estate in Chungcheong Province.

Park Ji-won's Yeolha ilgi (Jehol Diary) records his journey in 1780 to the Qing summer residence at Chengde, where the Joseon mission attended celebrations for the Qianlong emperor's seventieth birthday. The work combines travel observation, anecdote, satire, and analysis. Park admired Qing brick construction, wheeled carts, irrigation devices, and the circulation of goods, and he argued that Joseon should adopt them. He also pressed the case for expanding commerce and, in his writings on agricultural policy, proposed limiting landholdings (hanjeon). His satirical stories, such as the Tale of the Yangban, targeted pretension and idleness among the elite; Chapter 41 discusses them as literature.

Park Je-ga, a secondary son, traveled to Beijing in 1778 with Yi Deok-mu and wrote the Bukhak ui (Discourse on Northern Learning), a set of practical proposals for adopting Qing methods in vehicles, ships, construction, agriculture, and trade, and for abandoning customs he regarded as wasteful. Park argued that consumption circulated wealth and that a society that refused to use its resources impoverished itself. The next feature examines this work as a source.

How could these scholars reconcile such proposals with Ming loyalism? Their argument was that the Qing, whatever its origin, had preserved and used techniques that Joseon needed, and that a commitment to civilization meant adopting what was beneficial, wherever it was found. Some also noted that the Qing had patronized Chinese scholarship, and they met Chinese literati who were themselves transmitting classical learning. Others, particularly conservative Noron officials, were uncomfortable, since to honor the Qing's achievements risked abandoning the moral stance that justified Joseon's claim to be the heir of Chinese civilization. The court itself maintained Ming commemoration, as seen in Sukjong's altar for the Ming emperors in 1704. Jeongjo's campaign against what he regarded as unorthodox prose, which touched Park Ji-won and others, reflected related anxieties about imported literary styles. The tension between ideology and practice persisted, and it shaped how far these scholars' proposals could go.

Historical Vocabulary
Bukhak (Northern Learning) and Seohak (Western Learning)

Definition. Bukhak (北學) refers to the program of learning practical techniques from Qing China. Seohak (西學) refers broadly to learning that came from the West, whether mathematics, astronomy, geography, and mechanics, or Catholic Christianity.

Historical context. Both terms carried political weight. Bukhak inverted the old contempt for the northern barbarians, while Seohak was used by officials in two senses that were often distinguished: scientific knowledge, which many tolerated, and Catholic doctrine, which the court came to treat as heterodox and dangerous.

Why it matters. The two terms show how Joseon thinkers sorted foreign knowledge into acceptable and unacceptable categories, and how that sorting shifted after the persecution of 1801.

Potential misunderstanding. Joseon Western Learning was not a single package. A scholar could be fascinated by European astronomy while rejecting Christianity, and the court itself used Western-derived calendrical methods while prosecuting Catholics.

The treatise argues that Joseon is poor largely because it does not make full use of its resources: its roads and vehicles are inadequate, its ships and brick-making are underdeveloped, and its customs discourage consumption and trade. It holds that circulation of goods creates wealth, that learning practical techniques from the Qing is no betrayal of principle, and that Joseon should expand commerce, improve transport, and cultivate useful skills rather than cling to wasteful habits and empty display.

It also demonstrates how Northern Learning ideas reached the court through formal channels and not solely through private circulation.

The dating and the circumstances of its later submission rest on the standard scholarly account and on the edition used. As a work of persuasion, it presents Joseon's deficiencies selectively and presents Qing practices favorably.

The perspectives of farmers, merchants, craftsmen, and women are not represented, nor are those of conservative officials who regarded such proposals with suspicion.

Section 38.6

Agriculture and Rural Knowledge

Agriculture was the economic base of Joseon, and a long tradition of writing on farming predated the scholars of this chapter. The state had sponsored farming manuals since the early dynasty, as Chapters 28 and 30 noted, including the Nongsa jikseol of Sejong's reign, which compiled practices suited to Korean conditions. In the later period, interest in agriculture broadened. Some works were written by officials seeking policies to increase output, and others by country gentlemen concerned with managing their own estates and households.

Household and estate management literature illustrates the second type. Hong Man-seon's Sallim gyeongje, compiled in the early eighteenth century, set out advice on farming, household management, diet, and medicine, and it was later revised and expanded in the eighteenth century by Yu Jung-rim into the Jeungbo sallim gyeongje. These books combined technical knowledge with material about everyday living, and they show a readership interested in making rural estates productive and well managed. Much of the material came from what practitioners already knew, and the authors often claimed to be recording experience rather than inventing practice.

The state also invited proposals. In 1798 King Jeongjo issued an edict inviting officials and others to submit suggestions on agricultural policy. It drew numerous responses, among them Park Ji-won's Gwanong sopcho (Short Notes on Agriculture), submitted in 1799, which combined practical advice on farming with arguments about land policy, including the idea of limiting landholdings. The episode shows that the court could treat agricultural knowledge as a legitimate matter for public consultation, even if it did not follow the advice offered. It also shows the boundary between technical and political argument: proposals about crop techniques could be adopted without confrontation, while proposals about land ownership touched powerful interests.

The largest agricultural compilation of the period is Seo Yu-gu's Imwon gyeongjeji (Records of Rural Life Management), a multivolume encyclopedia on farming, crops, tools, food, household economy, medicine, and the management of rural estates. It was compiled during the first half of the nineteenth century and developed through the 1830s and 1840s. Seo came from a family of officials with a strong tradition of agricultural interest, and his compilation drew on Korean and Chinese texts as well as his own experience. Like many encyclopedic works of the period, it reflects the compiler's conviction that systematic collection of knowledge could serve the people's welfare.

Changes in actual practice also appear in the record, though with less clarity. Over the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, rice transplanting spread in many regions, a technique that increased yields and reduced weeding labor while exposing farmers to greater risk if rains failed. Irrigation reservoirs and embankments were maintained and expanded. New crops entered the countryside. The sweet potato, according to the traditional account, was brought from Japan by Jo Eom, an envoy on the mission of 1763–1764 who obtained planting material at Tsushima, although earlier awareness of the plant and attempts at cultivation are recorded, and treatises on its cultivation followed. Tobacco, introduced earlier, became widely grown as a cash crop, a development tied to the expansion of markets discussed in Chapter 39. How far written treatises caused such changes, as opposed to recording what farmers were already doing, remains an open question, since farmers themselves left few records.

Section 38.7

Geography, Settlement, and Cartography

Geographical writing in later Joseon combined several purposes: administrative description, historical inquiry, practical guidance, and the representation of space. Earlier geographical compilations, including the early-dynasty gazetteers and maps, had mostly been official. In the seventeenth century private scholars also began writing on the subject. Han Baek-gyeom's Dongguk jiriji of the early seventeenth century took a historical-geographical approach, investigating the locations of ancient states and territories, and this orientation toward historical geography became a durable tradition.

Yi Jung-hwan's Taengniji (c. 1751) represents a different approach. Writing for educated readers who might need to choose a place of residence, he surveyed the peninsula's regions and assessed them according to four criteria: location and terrain, economic livelihood or profit, the character and customs of the people, and the quality of mountains and waters. The work describes roads, markets, rivers, and communities across provinces, with attention to how easily a place could supply a living and how well it could be reached by water and road. It reflects the circumstances of its author, a Southerner who spent much of his life outside office and who wrote for families whose members could not rely on government service. Its emphasis on commerce and transport as factors in the desirability of a settlement shows how much the commercial landscape had changed.

Maps were equally important. Joseon cartography had a long history, including the early fifteenth-century world map discussed in Chapter 28. In 1603 a copy of Matteo Ricci's world map, produced in China, reached Korea, and in 1708 Sukjong ordered the court to make a copy. Such maps portrayed a world of continents and oceans in which China was one country among many. This knowledge circulated alongside a popular tradition of circular world maps, often called cheonhado, which showed China at the center surrounded by smaller lands and, in some cases, imaginary places. Both kinds of map coexisted, and Joseon viewers did not necessarily regard one as replacing the other.

Domestic mapmaking improved in the eighteenth century. Jeong Sang-gi, a Southerner scholar, produced maps of the peninsula that used a consistent scale based on a unit of one hundred ri, an approach that influenced later cartographers. Sin Gyeong-jun is associated with a systematic account of Korea's mountain system and road routes, and the Sangyeongpyo, a tabular description of mountain ranges linked with the idea of a continuous great ridge running from Mount Baekdu to the south, is traditionally attributed to him, although the attribution is debated. The tradition culminated in the work of Kim Jeong-ho, whose Cheonggudo of 1834 was an atlas and whose Daedongyeojido of 1861 was printed from woodblocks in twenty-two folding volumes which, when assembled, formed a large map of the whole peninsula. The map used conventional symbols for roads, post stations, fortresses, granaries, and other features, and it was printable and therefore reproducible, which distinguished it from earlier hand-drawn maps. Kim's life is poorly documented. A popular legend holds that he was imprisoned and that the printing blocks were destroyed as a result of the map's military sensitivity, but reliable contemporary evidence for the story is lacking, and historians treat it with caution.

Historical geography flourished as well. Jeong Yak-yong's Abang gangyeok-go (Study of Our Country's Territories), completed in 1811, examined the locations of ancient states and commanderies using textual sources, an enterprise that connected geography to the debates about Gojoseon and Balhae discussed in the next section. Historians treat this scholarship as significant because it used comparison of sources and local knowledge to correct older identifications, while recognizing that many conclusions have since been revised by archaeology.

Section 38.8

Historical Scholarship, Territory, and Evidential Learning

The eighteenth century also saw a significant development in historical writing. An Jeong-bok (1712–1791), a Southerner scholar who tutored members of the royal household, completed the Dongsa gangmok (Annotated Outline of Eastern History) in 1778. It narrated Korean history from Gojoseon through Goryeo using the form of a classical "outline and detail" chronicle, and it paid close attention to evaluating sources, correcting dates and place names, and comparing conflicting accounts. An Jeong-bok also argued about political legitimacy. He treated certain states as the proper line of succession (jeongtong), tracing it from Dangun and Gija to Mahan, then to Silla, and then to Goryeo, and he denied the same standing to others, such as Wiman Joseon, regarded as usurping. Gija, the sage said in Chinese tradition to have founded a state in Korea, is not accepted as a historical ruler by modern historians, and Chapter 6 discusses the evidence. The point for this chapter is that An's work combined critical method with a Neo-Confucian framework of legitimacy.

Other scholars compiled broad narratives. Yi Geung-ik's Yeollyeosil gisul was a private history of Joseon politics that drew on a wide range of sources, including private writings, and attempted to arrange the political history of the dynasty by topic and reign. Such works are valuable because they preserve material that would otherwise be lost, and they are limited by the partisan outlook of their compilers, a limitation familiar from the discussion of competing royal annals in Chapter 37.

Yu Deuk-gong's Balhaego (Study of Balhae), written in 1784, is especially important for the themes of this textbook. Yu argued that Balhae, the northern state founded after Goguryeo's fall (Chapter 16), belonged to Korean history and that Korean historians had neglected it. He proposed describing the period in which Silla and Balhae coexisted as one of "North and South States." The argument had implications for how Korean territory and history were understood, since it connected the Manchurian region to Korean historical memory and criticized the older tendency to view Silla alone as the heir of the Three Kingdoms. Modern historians have a more complicated view of Balhae's population and political identity, but Yu's work is a notable precedent for treating the peninsula and its northern surroundings together, and Chapter 1 used his term in introducing the period.

A different strand of scholarship drew on Qing evidential research (kaozheng, Korean gojeunghak), which emphasized close textual criticism and the use of inscriptions and material evidence. Kim Jeong-hui (1786–1856), who traveled to Beijing in 1809 and met leading Qing scholars, became the best-known Joseon practitioner. In 1816 he studied a stele on Bukhansan and identified it, on the basis of its inscription and comparison with other sources, as a monument marking an inspection by the Silla king Jinheung in the sixth century, which is generally accepted. The identification showed that inscriptions could supply evidence independent of later chronicles, and Kim and others went on to study other ancient steles. Kim was also a major calligrapher and a painter of austere landscapes. In 1840, after a political reversal at court, he was exiled to Jeju, where he remained until 1848, and in that time he painted Sehando (Winter Scene), usually dated 1844. His exile on Jeju is also a reminder that provincial and island regions were places where scholarship could be produced as well as places of punishment.

The methods of this scholarship deserve emphasis. Historians in this tradition distrusted unsupported statements, compared texts, and looked for physical evidence. Their conclusions reflected their assumptions as well: they worked with Confucian frameworks, relied on the Chinese textual tradition, and often sought to affirm Korean continuity and dignity. Their work therefore resembles a form of critical scholarship within an inherited tradition, not a rejection of that tradition. Archaeology, which would later supersede much of their geography, did not yet exist as a discipline.

Section 38.9

Western Learning, Astronomy, and Technology

Knowledge of European science reached Joseon indirectly, mostly through books written in Chinese by Jesuit missionaries and their Chinese collaborators. The impact was most visible in calendrical science, an area in which accuracy mattered to the state, since the calendar determined agricultural timing and the dates of rites. The reformed Chinese calendar developed with Jesuit participation was adopted by the Qing, and in 1653 Joseon decided to adopt it as well, a change advocated by Kim Yuk and put into effect the following year. Court astronomers had to learn new computational methods, and the episode shows that Joseon officials were willing to adopt Western-derived techniques when they promised better results.

Cosmology was more contentious. Hong Dae-yong's argument for the rotation of the earth and a decentered universe, mentioned above, drew on Western astronomical ideas as they had reached him through Chinese texts, and it challenged assumptions of a China-centered cosmos. The argument was speculative and circulated among a limited group; it did not reshape state astronomy. Others, including Yi Ik, read Western books on geography and astronomy with interest, and some scholars sought to reconcile new information with established frameworks. Choe Han-gi (1803–1877), working through book networks that extended to Beijing, later wrote extensively on Western geography and natural philosophy, drawing on translated works. The reception of Western science was therefore selective and uneven.

Technology presented a more practical test. The Hwaseong project, discussed in Chapter 37, brought together traditional craft knowledge, military treatises, and information about machinery derived from Chinese-language works that described Western mechanics. Jeong Yak-yong's writings on lifting devices, associated with the cranes used at the fortress, drew on one such work, a Chinese-language illustrated book on Western machines published in the seventeenth century. Historians, as Chapter 37 observed, treat the construction as a collective enterprise and caution against crediting a single inventor. Jeong also is credited by tradition with contributing to the design of a pontoon bridge across the Han River for a royal procession in 1789, though the evidence for his exact role is debated. Such examples show how imported technical knowledge could be adapted to specific projects under royal direction.

Medicine offered another field of practical learning. The Donguibogam remained the central Joseon medical text, and the era saw additional compilations for practitioners and households. Jeong Yak-yong wrote on smallpox and related subjects, and Jeong Yak-jeon's Jasan eobo, written in 1814 while he was in exile on Heuksan Island, catalogued the marine creatures of the surrounding waters, describing their appearance, habits, and uses. In his preface he states that he relied on the knowledge of a local islander, a rare acknowledgment in the period that the observations of fishing people could be a source of scholarly knowledge. In the later nineteenth century, medical thought continued to develop, and Lee Je-ma's constitutional medicine, described in the 1890s, represents a distinctive later strand.

A crucial distinction was between Western science and Catholic doctrine. Some scholars from the Seongho circle encountered Catholic texts and converted, and in the 1780s Korean Catholic communities formed. The court initially tolerated discussion of Western books but became alarmed at the refusal of Catholics to perform ancestral rites. As Chapters 40 and 45 explain, the persecution of 1801 killed or exiled many believers, including Jeong Yak-yong's brother Jeong Yak-jong. After 1801 the political risk of association with Western Learning increased sharply, and scholars who had written on Western topics became more cautious. This episode shows how a political event constrained intellectual possibility.

Section 38.10

Jeong Yak-yong: Synthesis, Exile, and Statecraft

Jeong Yak-yong (1762–1836), who used the pen name Dasan, is often treated as the greatest figure of the tradition. Born near Seoul in Gyeonggi Province into a Southerner family, he passed the examinations, entered the court, and became a favorite of King Jeongjo, who employed him in scholarly and practical tasks, including work at the Kyujanggak and on projects connected to Hwaseong. In the late 1790s he served in provincial posts, including as a county magistrate in Hwanghae Province, which gave him direct experience of local administration. Jeong's early contacts with Catholic ideas through his family and circle later exposed him to attack; his elder brother Jeong Yak-jong became a leading Catholic and was executed in 1801, and Jeong Yak-yong, after the persecution of that year, was sent into exile.

He spent about eighteen years in Gangjin, on the southwestern coast of Jeolla Province, from 1801 until 1818. This period, difficult as it was, proved intellectually productive. He wrote commentaries on the Confucian classics and studies of medicine and law, and he produced the first two of the three major works for which he is best known. The Gyeongse yupyo (Design for Good Government), begun in 1817 and left unfinished, proposed a systematic reorganization of government institutions. The Mongmin simseo (Admonitions on Governing the People), completed in 1818, is a handbook for local magistrates that describes in detail the duties of a county official and the abuses of clerks and local elites. He continued his scholarship after returning home, completing the thirty-volume legal treatise Heumheum sinseo (Treatise on Penal Administration) in 1819 and writing its final preface in 1822. It addresses the administration of criminal justice, with attention to the care needed in investigating cases. Together these writings became known as his major works on institutions, local administration, and criminal justice.

Jeong's land proposals evolved over time. He first advanced a village-based system (yeojeon) in which the villagers would farm collectively, record each person's labor, and divide the harvest accordingly after paying taxes. Later he argued that a well-field system (jeongjeon), implemented through state purchase of land, was more realistic. Both proposals aimed to tie land to those who worked it and to reduce rent-taking by landlords. They were not adopted. The Mongmin simseo had a different kind of influence: it circulated in manuscript and was read by officials and later reformers, and it preserved a detailed picture of how local government worked in practice, including extortion and the mismanagement of the grain-loan system, subjects that Chapter 44 revisits in connection with rural unrest.

Assessing Jeong requires balance. His writings are exceptionally wide-ranging and show a mind that combined classical learning, administrative experience, and empirical concern. He also remained a Confucian: his goal was a government that fulfilled the sages' ideals, and he accepted a hierarchical society. After his return from exile, he lived at his family home near the Han River in Gyeonggi Province, continuing to write until his death in 1836. His collected works were not widely known until their publication in the 1930s, and much of the modern image of Jeong as a proto-modern thinker comes from that later reception, which Section 38.12 discusses.

Section 38.11

Scholars, the Court, and the Limits of Reform

One of the most important questions about these scholars is why so many of their proposals were not carried out. A first set of reasons concerns politics. Land redistribution would have threatened the property of powerful families, including the families that dominated the court, and any monarch contemplating it would have needed strong political support and enforcement capacity that the state did not possess. As Chapter 37 showed, kings faced factional rivalries and legitimacy problems, and few were willing to risk a confrontation over property. Even so capable a ruler as Jeongjo pursued targeted initiatives, such as the Kyujanggak and Hwaseong, rather than sweeping redistribution.

A second set of reasons concerns administration and finance. Joseon relied on local clerks and magistrates to register land, collect taxes, and enforce rules, and the surviving evidence indicates that this system was vulnerable to abuse. A reform requiring accurate land surveys and careful enforcement would have depended on the very officials whose practices were at issue. The Equalized Tax Law discussed in Chapter 37 illustrates the pattern: the state could reduce a burden but not overhaul the underlying system.

A third set concerns the social position of the scholars. Many were outside the circles of power, and their advice reached the king only when an intermediary or a royal initiative brought it forward. Jeongjo's patronage opened some avenues: he employed Park Je-ga, Yi Deok-mu, Yu Deuk-gong, and Seo I-su at the Kyujanggak, called on officials and others for agricultural proposals in 1798, and used Jeong Yak-yong in practical projects. But patronage depended on a single monarch's disposition. When Jeongjo died in 1800 and the political situation changed, the scholars lost their protector. Jeong Yak-yong's exile and the cautiousness that followed the persecution of 1801 illustrate how vulnerable a scholar's position could be.

A fourth set concerns ideas themselves. The scholars did not agree on what to do. Proposals for restricting landholdings, for expanding commerce, for abolishing slavery, and for adopting Qing methods were sometimes in tension with one another, and conservative critics could point to those disagreements. Some proposals, such as the use of carts and bricks, faced practical obstacles of road conditions and cost. Others challenged beliefs about status or civilization that were widely held. The result was that influence took indirect forms: scholars shaped debate, trained disciples, preserved records, and occasionally influenced specific decisions, without achieving the structural change many of them desired.

It would be wrong, however, to conclude that the scholarship had no effect. It enlarged what could be discussed, improved the quality of knowledge about territory and history, and left a body of texts that later reformers and historians used. The relationship between this scholarship and the reformers of the late nineteenth century, examined in Chapters 49 and 50, is debated: some historians see continuity of ideas, while others stress the new circumstances created by treaty-port contact and imperial pressure and caution against a direct line of descent.

Cause and Effect

The Rise of Practical and Empirical Scholarship in Later Joseon

Long-term causes

A Neo-Confucian tradition of statecraft that joined moral cultivation to institutional reform; the state's long record of sponsoring practical knowledge; regular contact with Qing book markets and scholarly networks.

Short-term causes

The destruction of the wars and the need for reconstruction; the pressures of tax and land problems; commercial expansion; the exclusion of Southerner and secondary-son scholars from office, which gave them time and motive to write.

Immediate context

Mid-eighteenth-century consolidation under Yeongjo and Jeongjo, whose interest in scholarship, recruitment of talented officials, and compilation projects created openings for reform-minded writers.

Immediate effects

A large body of treatises on land, agriculture, geography, history, and administration; new methods such as travel observation and evidential research; limited, selective adoption of proposals.

Long-term consequences: The scholarship preserved detailed knowledge of Joseon society and territory, supplied a vocabulary of reform to later generations, and became in the twentieth century a resource for nationalist and developmental narratives. Historical complexity: No single cause explains it. Political exclusion, commercial change, contact with the Qing, and royal patronage interacted, and the outcome depended on individual talent and circumstance as much as on structure.

Everyday Life and Social History

Farmers, Fishermen, and Townspeople in the Scholars' Writings

The writings examined in this chapter were produced by educated men, but their subject matter was often the daily life of others, and these sources preserve valuable traces of that life. The Mongmin simseo, for example, records how county clerks and local power-holders extracted fees and mismanaged the grain-loan system, and it conveys the exposure of poor households to these abuses. Agricultural manuals describe techniques, tools, crops, and seasonal tasks, and household guides discuss food preservation, medicines, and the management of servants and tenants. Yi Jung-hwan's Taengniji notes which markets and river landings supported local trade and which regions offered a reliable living.

These sources must be read with attention to the observer's position. The authors were landowners, officials, or exiles who described cultivators from outside, and their accounts of rural conditions served arguments about reform. Exceptions are valuable. Jeong Yak-jeon's study of marine life acknowledges reliance on the knowledge of a local islander, and Jeong Yak-yong's long exile in Gangjin placed him among ordinary neighbors whose experience informed his observations of local administration. The calendar, adopted from reformed astronomy, also affected ordinary households, who relied on the officially issued almanac to time planting and ritual observance, although the extent to which they noticed differences in calendrical technique is unrecorded.

Women's roles in household production are visible in household guides, which treat textile work, food preparation, and medicine as part of the household economy, but women rarely appear as authors or as named participants in scholarly debates. Enslaved and bonded people (nobi) appear in the discussions of reformers who criticized the hereditary transmission of slave status, but the people themselves are seldom heard. Historians therefore reconstruct their conditions from legal records, household registers, and sources outside the scholarly treatises, as Chapter 42 explores.

Section 38.12

Legacy, Memory, and the Nineteenth Century

The generation of Jeong Yak-yong and Kim Jeong-hui marked the high point of this scholarship, and the decades after 1800 were less favorable. The political order of the nineteenth century, which Chapter 43 examines, was dominated by royal in-law families, and the patronage that Jeongjo had extended to reform-minded scholars largely disappeared. The persecution of 1801 and subsequent persecutions made Western Learning dangerous. Even so, scholarly work continued. Kim Jeong-ho's maps, Choe Han-gi's writings on geography and natural philosophy, and Seo Yu-gu's agricultural compilation all belong to the first two thirds of the nineteenth century. Knowledge production did not cease; it continued in more restricted conditions.

The reception of this scholarship in the late nineteenth and twentieth centuries shaped the label that students now encounter. Reformers of the 1880s and 1890s, treated in Chapters 49 through 52, did sometimes read earlier writers, but the circumstances of treaty-port contact and imperial pressure differed sharply from those of the eighteenth century. In the 1930s, under colonial rule, Korean scholars rediscovered and edited the writings of Jeong Yak-yong and others, presenting them as evidence of indigenous intellectual vitality. After 1945, the scholarship was claimed in different ways by the two Korean states: North Korean historiography generally stressed class and economic themes, while South Korean historiography in the developmental period stressed internal sources of modernization. Chapters 75 and 77 describe the states whose historians made these claims.

The use of this scholarship in modern memory carries a risk of distortion. Heroic portraits of individual thinkers as lonely visionaries ignore the collaborative, textual, and often elite character of their work. Popular accounts attribute inventions and achievements to single individuals, as in the claim that one scholar designed the Hwaseong machinery or the legend of the mapmaker's imprisonment. A more careful approach distinguishes what the sources establish, what later tradition added, and what remains uncertain.

The chapter's larger lesson concerns the nature of intellectual change. Late Joseon scholars did not abandon the Neo-Confucian framework; they stretched it. They introduced new methods of observation and criticism, widened the range of information they considered legitimate, and argued for institutional changes that seldom took effect. Their work shows a society capable of sustained, creative self-examination under real constraints. Understanding this scholarship, and the ways it has been remembered, prepares students for the chapters that follow, in which commerce, religion, literature, and family life are examined in the same period.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Knowledge and Scholarship in Later Joseon

What Changed

  • Scholars increasingly used direct observation, travel, measurement, comparison of texts, and inscriptions as evidence
  • The range of subjects treated as worthy of study widened to include commerce, technology, farming, maps, and foreign countries
  • Knowledge of the wider world, including Qing China, Japan, and Europe through Chinese books, entered elite discussion
  • Scholarship on Balhae, Gojoseon, and territory expanded the historical imagination beyond the peninsula's south

What Stayed the Same

  • Classical Chinese remained the main language of scholarship, though Hangeul was used for some travel writing and household guides
  • The Neo-Confucian framework and the ideal of a moral, agrarian order remained dominant
  • The status hierarchy and the dominance of the yangban in education and office persisted, with limited exceptions
  • Reform depended on the attention of the court and on the enforcement capacity of local administration

Pattern and timing: Change was gradual and accumulated over the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, with a peak in the late eighteenth century and a more constrained continuation in the nineteenth. Scholars on the margins of power, including provincial and secondary-son thinkers, experienced the tension between new ideas and old structures most sharply, while most farmers and artisans experienced the scholarship only indirectly. The pattern matters because it shows intellectual dynamism coexisting with institutional rigidity.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how scholars in later Joseon investigated land, agriculture, geography, history, astronomy, and technology, and how modern historians have organized that scholarship under the label Silhak. Its central argument is that this body of work was a significant, varied, and genuinely innovative tradition of inquiry that nevertheless operated within a Neo-Confucian framework and under serious political and administrative constraints, and that the label itself is a modern construction that both illuminates and distorts.

The conditions for the scholarship were set by the seventeenth-century wars, post-war reconstruction, commercial and demographic recovery, and regular contact with the Qing. Earlier traditions of Neo-Confucian statecraft and state sponsorship of practical knowledge supplied foundations. Factional politics, as Chapter 37 showed, excluded some scholars from office and gave them time and motive to write.

The term silhak existed in premodern usage with a broad meaning, and its narrower modern sense was shaped in the 1930s and after 1945 by nationalist and developmental narratives in colonial, North Korean, and South Korean historiography. Historians debate whether the label identifies a coherent movement, a diverse set of individuals, or a segment of broader Neo-Confucian reformism.

Yu Hyeong-won's Bangye surok offered a comprehensive program of land and institutional reform, but it circulated in manuscript and was printed only in 1770. Yi Ik's more modest proposal for a protected minimum landholding and his teaching at Ansan shaped a circle of disciples whose divisions over Western Learning illustrate the diversity of the tradition. Jeong Yak-yong later proposed village-based and well-field systems. None was adopted.

The Northern Learning scholars, including Hong Dae-yong, Park Ji-won, and Park Je-ga, argued that Joseon should learn practical techniques from the Qing, and they reconciled this stance with Ming loyalism by appealing to the welfare of the people and the preservation of civilization. They met resistance from conservative officials, and the court supported them selectively through patronage while policing literary and ritual orthodoxy.

Writers on agriculture produced manuals, household guides, and encyclopedias, and King Jeongjo's call for agricultural proposals in 1798 drew diverse responses. The relationship between written treatises and actual practice, including the spread of rice transplanting and new crops, remains difficult to establish because farmers left few records.

In geography, Yi Jung-hwan's Taengniji assessed regions as places to live, Jeong Yak-yong and others undertook historical geography, and mapmaking culminated in Kim Jeong-ho's Cheonggudo and Daedongyeojido. Joseon viewers also lived with both Ricci-derived world maps and circular cheonhado. Legends surrounding Kim Jeong-ho lack reliable support.

Historical scholarship developed critical method in An Jeong-bok's Dongsa gangmok, expanded the historical imagination to Balhae in Yu Deuk-gong's Balhaego, and used inscriptions in Kim Jeong-hui's study of the Silla stele. Western science, including calendrical astronomy, entered Joseon through Chinese-language books, and the court and scholars distinguished it from Catholic doctrine, a distinction that hardened after the persecution of 1801.

Jeong Yak-yong's writings during and after exile, including the Gyeongse yupyo, Mongmin simseo, and Heumheum sinseo, brought together much of the tradition's concerns. Most proposals went unimplemented because of political resistance, administrative weakness, dependence on royal patronage, and disagreements among the scholars themselves. The tradition nevertheless widened what could be debated and left texts that later generations read, and its memory, shaped by twentieth-century politics, requires careful distinction between evidence and legend.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter shows that later Joseon was a society that examined itself with unusual seriousness. After the wars, scholars measured fields, studied markets, traveled to Beijing, compared texts, read inscriptions, and mapped the peninsula. Their work complicates the older image of a rigid, inward-looking dynasty and also complicates the opposite image of a society on the verge of modernization. It reveals a Confucian civilization capable of critique and innovation within its own terms.

The chapter also demonstrates that knowledge has a social history. Who could study, travel, publish, or be heard depended on status, faction, region, and royal favor. Provincial retirees, exiles, secondary sons, interpreters, and fishermen contributed in different ways, and the records they left reflect their positions. Reading these sources well means asking whose voices are present and whose are missing.

The scholarship examined here shaped later developments in ways that are easy to exaggerate. It preserved detailed knowledge of territory, history, and administration, enlarged the range of acceptable inquiry, and left texts that later readers valued. Yet its proposals for structural change largely failed, and the line from this tradition to the reforms of the nineteenth century is contested. The chapter encourages students to resist both neglect and celebration.

Finally, the chapter illustrates how the past is made into usable history. The label Silhak carries the concerns of the 1930s, of the two Korean states after 1945, and of later scholarship that questioned it. Understanding that history of interpretation is part of understanding the period itself, and it prepares students for the following chapters on commerce, religion, literature, and family life, which examine the society in which these scholars lived and wrote.
