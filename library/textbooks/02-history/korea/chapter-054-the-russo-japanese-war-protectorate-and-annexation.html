The Russo-Japanese War, Protectorate, and Annexation

Korean History: Ancient Kingdoms to a Divided Peninsula

Chapter Opening

On the night of 17 November 1905, Japanese troops stood in the streets of Seoul while Korea's ministers were gathered in a hall of the palace compound to consider a document that would strip their government of the right to conduct its own foreign relations. The emperor did not approve it. By the early hours of the next morning, it had nonetheless been signed. This chapter asks how a state that had proclaimed itself an empire only eight years earlier came to that night, and what Koreans did in the five years that followed.

The agreement dated 17 November 1905 and concluded during the night of 17–18 November is known in Korean as the Eulsa Treaty, after the sixty-year-cycle name of 1905, and in Japanese diplomatic usage as the Second Japan–Korea Agreement. It carried no formal title, and Korean scholarship has long emphasized that Emperor Gojong never ratified it. It nevertheless placed Korea's external affairs in Japanese hands and created the office of Resident-General in Seoul. Understanding why it was possible requires moving backward to the war with Russia that had made Japan the dominant armed power on the peninsula, and forward to the agreements of 1907 and 1910 that dismantled the remaining institutions of the Korean state.

This chapter treats those years as a sequence of separate decisions rather than a single inevitable slide. Japan's military victory over Russia gave it extraordinary leverage, but leverage is not the same as legal title. Between February 1904 and August 1910 Japan and its Korean collaborators had to produce a series of documents: a protocol, an adviser agreement, a protectorate treaty, a domestic-administration agreement, memoranda transferring the judiciary and police, and finally an annexation treaty. Each required pressure, each met objection, and each marked a distinguishable stage in the removal of Korea's sovereign functions. The chapter follows those stages in the order in which they occurred.

The chapter also insists that Koreans were not bystanders. Emperor Gojong appealed to foreign governments and, in 1907, sent secret emissaries to The Hague. Officials wrote memorials and several took their own lives in protest. Newspaper editors, school founders, and association leaders built what is now called the patriotic enlightenment movement. Scholars and villagers raised the Eulsa righteous armies in 1906, and after the dissolution of the Korean army in 1907 a far larger and more socially diverse armed resistance fought Japanese forces across the peninsula until 1909–1910. Ordinary households in Daegu and elsewhere contributed to a campaign to repay Korea's national debt. Others, for reasons that ranged from fear and careerism to genuine ideological conviction, cooperated with Japanese authorities. The historian's task is to separate these responses rather than collapse them.

The evidence for this period is unusually rich and unusually contested. Japan's Foreign Ministry published diplomatic documents, and Japanese military and gendarmerie records survive, but they were written by the party imposing control. Korean court records, including the veritable records of the Gojong and Sunjong reigns, were compiled under Japanese supervision after annexation and must be read with that in mind. Contemporary Korean newspapers, memorials, letters, private chronicles such as Hwang Hyeon's Maecheon Yarok, American, British, and Russian diplomatic dispatches, and the reports of foreign journalists who visited the righteous armies all contribute. No single category settles the questions; the chapter compares them.

Chapter 53 examined the Korean Empire's modernization programs and its neutrality diplomacy through January 1904. This chapter begins where that policy was overridden. It closes with the signing and promulgation of the annexation treaty in August 1910, and leaves the structure and operation of the colonial Government-General to Chapter 55. The central thread is the progressive transfer of Korea's diplomatic, administrative, military, and legal authority, and the multiple efforts, armed and unarmed, to contest it.

Finally, the chapter asks students to be careful with the vocabulary of the period. Words such as protectorate, annexation, treaty, consent, and collaboration carry legal and moral weight, and their application to 1905–1910 remains contested between Korean and Japanese scholarship and within each. This textbook states clearly what the documents show, distinguishes the official language of treaties from the conditions in which they were concluded, and identifies where historians genuinely disagree.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Trace the sequence of agreements and acts of coercion by which Korea lost diplomatic, administrative, military, and legal autonomy between 1904 and 1910.
  2. Explain the origins of the Russo-Japanese War and why Korea's neutrality declaration could not prevent Japanese military occupation.
  3. Describe the principal campaigns of the war, from Chemulpo and the Yalu to Port Arthur, Mukden, and Tsushima, and assess their consequences for Korea.
  4. Distinguish international recognition of Japanese predominance from Korean consent, and informal understandings from binding treaties.
  5. Analyze the circumstances of the Eulsa Treaty and evaluate the competing arguments about its validity.
  6. Compare diplomatic appeals, public protest, patriotic enlightenment, economic mobilization, and armed resistance as distinct Korean responses.
  7. Interpret the Hague mission, the abdication of Gojong, the 1907 agreement, and the dissolution of the army as stages in a single crisis.
  8. Assess the social composition, geography, and fate of the righteous armies and the limits of Japanese statistical records.
  9. Evaluate collaboration, accommodation, and coerced cooperation as analytically separate categories.
  10. Contextualize An Jung-geun's assassination of Itō Hirobumi within Korean resistance and Japanese annexation policy.
  11. Analyze primary sources, including treaty paraphrases and protest documents, with attention to authorship, purpose, and limitation.
  12. Explain why annexation in 1910 was the result of identifiable decisions rather than the automatic consequence of victory over Russia.

Historical Context

By 1904 the Korean state had spent nearly three decades in an international environment it did not control. The 1876 Treaty of Ganghwa (Chapter 48) opened Korea to Japan, and the treaties that followed (Chapter 49) brought Qing, Western, and Russian influence into competition on the peninsula. The Sino-Japanese War of 1894–1895 ended Qing predominance (Chapter 51). The Gabo reforms and the murder of Queen Min in 1895 (Chapter 52) discredited Japan in the eyes of many Koreans and drove Gojong to take refuge in the Russian legation in 1896. In 1897 he proclaimed the Korean Empire (Chapter 53), seeking to demonstrate equal sovereign standing, and the Gwangmu reforms introduced railways, electricity, modern schools, and a reorganized military.

Russia and Japan, meanwhile, came to see Korea in strategic terms. Russia's lease of Port Arthur and Dalian after 1898, its railway construction across Manchuria, and its continued presence in Manchuria after the Boxer crisis of 1900 convinced Japanese leaders that Russian power might extend south into Korea. A Russian timber concession on the Yalu and the fortification of Yongampo in 1903 sharpened this concern. Japan, for its part, had acquired a close ally in Britain through the Anglo-Japanese Alliance of 1902, which reduced the risk that another European power would join Russia in a war.

Korea possessed few means of resisting being drawn into this rivalry. Its army was small and unevenly equipped, its finances depended on irregular revenues and foreign loans, and its monarch's diplomacy relied heavily on balancing outside powers. The Independence Club had been suppressed in 1898–1899, which removed an organized reform constituency from public life. Japan already controlled much of the country's external trade in the treaty ports and had built or financed telegraph and railway connections. When war came in 1904 it came to a state whose institutions were real but thinly defended.

Students should keep three analytical points in view. First, Korea was a participant in the events of 1904–1910 rather than simply a territory acted upon. Gojong, his ministers, local officials, scholars, soldiers, and ordinary households made decisions throughout. Second, Japanese policy was not monolithic: Itō Hirobumi, Katsura Tarō, Terauchi Masatake, the army, the Foreign Ministry, and Japanese nationalist societies held different views on timing and method. Third, the international order of the early twentieth century, in which imperial powers recognized each other's spheres of interest, gave Korea little access to external support. The chapter examines how these factors combined.

Imperial Rivalry, Korean Neutrality, and the Outbreak of War

Section 54.1

Russia, Japan, Manchuria, and the Strategic Importance of Korea

The war that transformed Korea's position grew from a bargaining failure between two empires over the northern Pacific coast of Asia. After the Triple Intervention of 1895 forced Japan to return the Liaodong Peninsula to China, Russia leased Port Arthur and Dalian in 1898 and built the Chinese Eastern Railway across Manchuria. When Russian troops occupied much of Manchuria during the Boxer crisis of 1900, they did not withdraw on the schedule Russia had promised. For Japanese strategists, a Russian-controlled Manchuria with a rail link to a warm-water naval base looked like a standing threat to the peninsula, which Japanese military planners had described for years as a dagger pointed at Japan.

Negotiations between August 1903 and February 1904 centered on a possible exchange. Japan proposed, in effect, that Russia's special position in Manchuria be recognized in return for recognition of Japan's predominance in Korea. Russia was willing to accept a Japanese interest in Korea but sought to limit it, proposing among other things a neutral zone in northern Korea and restrictions on the use of Korean territory for strategic purposes. Japan regarded these conditions as unacceptable and, equally important, saw Russian delay as a way of reinforcing Manchuria. Tokyo broke off diplomatic relations on 6 February 1904. Japanese leaders did not agree on every point of policy, and some, including Itō Hirobumi, had favored a negotiated settlement; but the cabinet and the army and navy leadership concluded that waiting favored Russia.

It is useful to distinguish three levels of explanation. At the level of strategic interest, both powers had real stakes in Manchuria and Korea. At the level of public justification, Japan described its cause as the defense of Korean independence and its own security, and Russia as the protection of its legitimate railway and commercial rights. At the level of concrete decision, Japan chose a pre-emptive naval strike, with a land expedition planned simultaneously for Korea. What is missing from every level is a Korean voice in the negotiations: Seoul was discussed throughout but was never a party to the talks.

This absence was itself significant. Korea's treaties with the powers gave it formal equality, but the Russo-Japanese negotiations treated the Korean government as an object of arrangement. That pattern would continue through the war, the Portsmouth settlement, and the 1905 treaty.

The diplomatic talks also suffered from an asymmetry of timing. Russia's ministers might believe that another season of railway work would improve their bargaining position, while Japan's generals feared that the same delay would make a future landing in Manchuria much more dangerous. There was no single, agreed Russian or Japanese view: officials weighed the risks of escalation differently, and neither country's public rhetoric perfectly described the confidential proposals exchanged through its diplomats. A rigorous explanation must therefore avoid treating the outcome as the operation of geography alone. Leaders interpreted incomplete information, judged the credibility of concessions, and decided that their interests could no longer be secured by bargaining.

For the Korean government, the negotiations exposed a serious defect in the international order that Gojong had tried to enter as an equal sovereign. Recognition of Korea's independence did not give Korea a veto when stronger states proposed arrangements affecting its territory. A Russian neutral zone along the northern peninsula, a Japanese right to use Korean facilities, or an acknowledgment of another state's “special interests” could each be discussed without Korean authorization. Students should compare the diplomatic language of Korean independence with the practical exclusion of Korean representatives from decision-making. That contrast, rather than a supposed lack of Korean awareness, explains why diplomacy left the Korean Empire particularly exposed when Japan and Russia abandoned negotiations.

Section 54.2

The Korean Empire's Neutrality Declaration and Its Diplomatic Limits

Chapter 53 examined Gojong's neutrality initiative in detail. In brief, on 21 January 1904 the Korean government announced its neutrality in any Russo-Japanese war, and Korean diplomats sought acknowledgment from the powers. The declaration drew on precedent: Gojong had followed earlier proposals for the neutralization of Korea, and in this period he hoped that the powers' mutual suspicion might protect the peninsula. Several governments acknowledged receipt, and the statement was circulated through Korea's legations and through the Chinese treaty port of Chefoo (Yantai), from which it was relayed to Europe.

This chapter is concerned with why the policy failed. Neutrality is an effective status only when belligerents have reason to respect it. Japan had concluded that its war aims required unimpeded use of Korean territory, ports, telegraphs, and, above all, the corridor from Chemulpo and Seoul to the Yalu. Russia had, in Japanese eyes, already compromised Korean neutrality through its Yongampo timber enterprise and fortifications. Neither power regarded Korean declarations as binding in the face of what it judged to be military necessity.

Korea also lacked the means of enforcement. Its army was small, its coastal defenses were negligible, and its treasury could not support a field force that could compete with either belligerent. Some Korean officials favored closer alignment with Russia; others, including some court figures, concluded that Japan would prevail and that accommodation would be safer. These divisions in the court meant that the government could not present a unified position.

Neutrality therefore operated as a declaration of intent rather than a defensible fact. It remains historically significant because it demonstrates that the Korean government attempted to use the instruments of international law available to it. It also makes later Japanese claims that Korea had agreed to cooperate in 1904 harder to read as simple consent, since the Korean government's declared position was the opposite.

The distinction between neutral intention and neutral enforcement had operational implications at the ports. A government declaring neutrality should ordinarily regulate belligerent warships' use of its harbors and prevent military forces from turning its territory into a base. Korea could communicate such rules but could not compel the Japanese army, supported by the Japanese navy, to observe them. Foreign diplomats in Seoul were also constrained by the policies of their home governments; sympathy for the Korean emperor did not amount to authority to provide armed protection. The absence of a multilateral enforcement mechanism left neutrality dependent on the very powers whose conduct it sought to restrain.

Neutrality failed without becoming meaningless. Its documentary record establishes that Korea did not merely invite military occupation and subsequently regret the bargain. It provided Gojong's government with a basis for later protest, and it supplies historians with a chronological test: Japan's landings and the February protocol followed a contrary Korean declaration. The argument should not be pushed into a claim that every Korean minister held the same view, or that neutrality could have prevented the war under different circumstances. Rather, the failure demonstrates how a recognized sovereign state's declared policy could be overridden when other states controlled the means of enforcement.

Section 54.3

February 1904: The Outbreak of the Russo-Japanese War

On the night of 8–9 February 1904 Japanese destroyers made a torpedo attack on Russian warships anchored outside Port Arthur, damaging several vessels. Japan declared war formally on 10 February, after hostilities had begun, a sequence that Russian officials and some observers criticized and that Japanese officials defended as consistent with practice. Admiral Tōgō Heihachirō's Combined Fleet then sought to contain the remaining Russian Pacific Squadron within the harbor.

Naval control was the key to the whole campaign. Japan needed to move armies across the sea to Korea and southern Manchuria and to keep them supplied by sea. If the Russian squadron at Port Arthur or the cruisers at Vladivostok could interdict transports, the strategy would fail. The opening attack did not destroy the Russian fleet, but it gave Japan the initiative, and the Russian decision to keep its ships mostly inside Port Arthur allowed the Japanese Army to land in Korea in the first weeks of the war.

The land plan involved a sequence. A first force, the army that became the First Army under Kuroki Tamemoto, was to land in Korea, secure Seoul and the road north, and advance to the Yalu. A second army would then land on the Liaodong coast to isolate Port Arthur. Korea's role in this plan was to provide ports, roads, labor, and a base. Japan was already prepared to treat Korean sovereignty as subordinate to those requirements.

Russia was not unprepared in a general sense but had a very long supply line. A single-track Trans-Siberian Railway, with the Lake Baikal section incomplete until later, constrained reinforcement. Russian commanders also disagreed about whether to defend forward or to withdraw and trade space for time. These conditions, together with Japan's naval initiative, shaped the first months of the war and explain how Japanese troops could enter Korea almost unopposed.

The sequence of the first attacks requires care because war at sea and war on Korean territory began together. Japanese vessels attacked the Russian squadron outside Port Arthur; other Japanese ships and transports operated off Chemulpo, where Russian vessels were in an international anchorage. Japan's planners intended the opening blows to reduce naval interference with troop movement, not to win the whole war at once. A damaged Russian battleship still represented a potential threat if repaired, while intact cruisers could threaten transports and isolated shipping. Thus the Japanese navy had to maintain a blockade and patrol system for months after its successful opening attack.

The Japanese decision to advance by way of Korea also made logistical sense. Chemulpo offered a landing point connected to Seoul; from the capital, the route north through Pyongyang and the Yalu opened onto southern Manchuria. Supplies still had to be carried by human labor, carts, and incomplete transport links. Transport corridors were not empty lines on a campaign map: Korean communities lived along them and provided, voluntarily or under pressure, food, animals, land, and labor. An account focused only on admirals and generals misses the dependence of expeditionary warfare on these local resources. It also misses the political significance of Japanese military access before the Korean government had formally agreed to such use.

Section 54.4

Japanese Military Occupation and the February 23 Protocol

Japanese troops came ashore at Chemulpo (present-day Incheon) on 8–9 February and moved on Seoul within days. Their presence in the capital, and the Japanese minister Hayashi Gonsuke's demands on the court, set the conditions for negotiation. Pro-Russian figures such as Yi Yong-ik, who had been influential in court finance, were removed from the scene, and Korea's ministers were told that Japan expected cooperation. The Korean Foreign Ministry, headed on the Korean side in these weeks by Yi Ji-yong, entered talks with Hayashi.

The result, signed on 23 February 1904, was the Japan–Korea Protocol. In summary, the Korean government pledged to trust Japan and follow its advice on improving administration; Japan pledged to ensure the safety of the Korean imperial house and to guarantee Korea's independence and territorial integrity; Korea agreed to facilitate Japanese military operations; Japan received the right to occupy strategically necessary sites; and neither party was to conclude agreements with a third power contrary to the protocol without mutual consent.

The protocol's wording contains an obvious tension. It affirmed independence and territorial integrity while granting a foreign army freedom to occupy territory and a foreign government the right to give "advice" that Korea promised to accept. Japanese diplomats presented it as an alliance of necessity; Korean scholars have generally described it as an agreement concluded under military occupation. Both descriptions rest on the same facts. Japanese troops were already in Seoul. The Korean government had declared neutrality weeks earlier. Gojong's ability to refuse was severely limited, though the record suggests that he and some ministers sought to soften the provisions.

The practical consequences followed quickly. Japan built a military administration around its occupation, stationed what became the Korea Garrison Army, and in mid-1904 asserted military-police authority in the Seoul area, including press censorship and punishment of interference with military communications. Land for military use, including areas of Yongsan on the southern edge of Seoul, was requisitioned. Korean laborers were mobilized for railway construction, and some who resisted or abandoned the work were punished. The protocol thus served as the legal framework for a military occupation that preceded and exceeded its terms.

The occupation was not a uniformly administered regime established on a single day. The Japanese headquarters for troops stationed in Korea was organized during March 1904 and reached Seoul in April, while operational field forces marched north toward the Yalu. The stationed troops secured transport, garrisons, and communications behind the front. Their jurisdiction expanded as commanders claimed exceptional powers to protect military infrastructure. By the summer the occupation authorities were asserting military-police authority in parts of Seoul and northern Korea, and later proclamations widened the geographical reach of these powers. Both Japanese archival descriptions and Korean historical accounts show why it is inaccurate to place a fully developed military-police structure at the moment of the February landing.

For Korean villagers, the language of military necessity could prove more consequential than the published promise of independence. Requisitioned land was not equally burdensome to a large landowner, a tenant whose household depended upon one field, and an urban landlord compensated for premises used as a storehouse. Railway construction could create wages while also generating compulsory labor and displacement. Under military regulations, ordinary interference with a rail line or telegraph could be treated as a threat to army operations, exposing civilians to punishment far beyond local policing practice. These mechanisms explain how Korea's territorial sovereignty was weakened well before protectorate status was announced in 1905.

Primary Source Window I

The Japan–Korea Protocol, 23 February 1904

Creator: negotiated by Japanese minister Hayashi Gonsuke and Korean Foreign Ministry officials led by Yi Ji-yong · Date: 23 February 1904 · Type: bilateral protocol; the text below is a paraphrase of the provisions, not a verbatim translation

Context: The protocol was concluded two weeks after Japanese landings at Chemulpo and while Japanese troops were in Seoul. Korea had publicly declared neutrality on 21 January.

Paraphrase:

The two governments agree to maintain permanent and firm friendship. Korea will place full trust in Japan and adopt Japanese advice on improvements in administration. Japan will firmly guarantee the safety and repose of the Korean imperial house, and will guarantee Korea's independence and territorial integrity. If either is endangered, Japan will take necessary measures, and Korea will facilitate Japanese action, which may include occupying places for strategic purposes. Neither party will conclude, without the other's consent, an agreement with a third power contrary to the protocol.

Analysis: The text shows how Japan wished its role to be understood: as guarantor, adviser, and ally. It also reveals the mechanism of later control. The terms "advice" and "necessary measures" were open-ended, and the occupation clause allowed military convenience to define the limits of Korean territory's availability.

Limitations: The text alone cannot show whether Korean officials accepted it freely. Corroboration requires Korean court records, Hayashi's reports, and the context of occupation.

Perspective: The document represents the diplomatic language of the two governments. It does not represent Korean villagers affected by requisition, Russian interests, or Korean officials who opposed it.

  1. What does the protocol say Japan will guarantee, and what does it ask of Korea?
  2. Who created it and under what military circumstances?
  3. What purposes might Japanese diplomats have had in guaranteeing independence in the text?
  4. What can historians learn about Japanese strategy from the occupation clause?
  5. What can they not establish from the text alone about Korean consent?
Section 54.5

The August 1904 Agreement and the Beginning of Adviser Government

The first Japan–Korea Agreement, finalized on 22 August 1904 (after the first two articles were concluded on August 19), moved from military access to administrative supervision. Korea agreed to employ as financial adviser a Japanese subject recommended by the Japanese government, and as diplomatic adviser a foreigner recommended by Japan, and to consult Japan in advance about treaties with other powers and about concessions or contracts with foreign nationals. The Korean signatory was the acting foreign minister, Yun Chi-ho.

The financial adviser was Megata Tanetarō, a Finance Ministry official who took up his post in late 1904. The diplomatic adviser was Durham White Stevens, an American who had served in the Japanese legation in Washington and who now acted as Japan's channel into Korean foreign affairs. Their positions were within the Korean government, and the Korean emperor formally employed them. Yet the practical result was that decisions on revenue, borrowing, currency, and external relations could not be taken without their concurrence, and they reported to Tokyo.

The adviser system illustrates a general technique. Japan did not need to abolish Korean institutions to take control of their function. A Korean Finance Ministry continued to exist, but Megata's office could examine budgets and direct reform. Megata's 1905 currency reform, which replaced older coinage with a new system under the control of the Japanese Dai-ichi Bank, benefited the Japanese financial system and disrupted many Korean merchants and banks. Stevens, for his part, later became notorious in Korea for public statements praising Japanese rule; in March 1908 he was shot in San Francisco by two Korean residents, Jang In-hwan and Jeon Myeong-un, and died of his wounds.

The agreement was also a precedent. It was presented as an arrangement for assistance, and it left Gojong formal authority. But it set the principle that Korea must consult Japan before conducting foreign relations, which the Eulsa Treaty would turn into transfer. Two other episodes from 1904 show Korean opposition. When a Japanese private entrepreneur, Nagamori Tokichi, demanded wide rights to develop uncultivated land in Korea, a public campaign, led by organizations in Seoul, forced the demand's withdrawal. The episode demonstrated that Korean public opinion was active and that Japanese officials would sometimes retreat rather than provoke a general reaction.

The diplomatic record gives a particularly clear demonstration of the relationship between signatures and power. The agreement was concluded in stages in August, and a Japanese diplomatic communication to Washington presented the arrangement as an innocuous improvement of the February protocol. Its explanation insisted that existing Korean treaty obligations remained valid and that consultation over concessions would prevent imprudent contracts. On the Korean side, however, requiring prior consultation effectively removed the ability to choose between competing foreign bidders without Japanese approval. A provision framed as technical advice thus gave Japan a gatekeeping function over Korea's future sources of revenue and international influence.

The fate of the financial system made that power visible beyond the cabinet. Currency reform changed the value and acceptability of coins that merchants and households used to settle debts, pay wages, and accumulate savings. Firms holding currency rejected or discounted under the new arrangements could suffer even as a more regular monetary system served long-distance commerce. The financial adviser was not an isolated private expert: he worked within the political and banking networks of an occupying power. It is therefore necessary to distinguish the potential administrative value of monetary reform from the unequal terms of its adoption. A policy could increase standardization while transferring practical control over money and credit outside Korean sovereign institutions.

Historical Vocabulary
Protectorate, Resident-General, Neutrality, Suzerainty, Annexation

Protectorate describes a relationship in which a stronger state assumes responsibility for the external relations, and sometimes more, of a weaker state that retains formal existence. Resident-General was the title of the Japanese official who headed the protectorate administration in Seoul from 1906. Neutrality is a state's status of non-participation in a war, which depends on recognition and enforcement by others. Suzerainty refers to a hierarchical relationship in which a state acknowledges a superior while retaining internal rule; the Qing–Joseon tributary relationship is the usual example in East Asian history, and Japan's wartime and postwar use of the idea in Korea differed from it. Annexation is the incorporation of one state's territory and population into another.

Why it matters: A protectorate is not a colony. Between 1905 and 1910 the Korean government and emperor existed, but control passed in stages; annexation then abolished the state itself. Potential misunderstanding: The term "protectorate" is Japan's own diplomatic framing and presents control as protection. Korean usage often emphasizes "Eulsa" and the forced character of the arrangement.

Division II · Sections 54.6–54.11

The Russo-Japanese War: Campaigns, Logistics, and Military Victory

Section 54.6

Chemulpo, Japanese Landings, and the Korean Theater

The first land and sea action of the war on the Korean coast occurred at Chemulpo on 9 February 1904. A Japanese squadron under Rear Admiral Uryū Sotokichi entered the anchorage, where the Russian cruiser Varyag and the gunboat Koreets lay in a harbor shared with the warships and merchant ships of neutral powers, and demanded that they leave. When they sortied they were engaged and badly damaged; the Russian crews returned to the harbor, and the Varyag was scuttled and the Koreets destroyed. Japanese transports landed troops at Chemulpo in the same period without Russian interference.

The Korean theater's military importance lay in transport. Chemulpo gave access to Seoul, and from Seoul a road and, soon, a railway line led north toward Pyongyang and the Yalu. Japanese engineers pushed to complete the Seoul–Pusan line, which began operation in 1905, and the Seoul–Sinuiju line, which was completed in 1906 and relied heavily on military construction. Telegraph lines were taken under Japanese control. Seoul itself became the staging point for the First Army's movement north, and its garrisoning established the pattern of a permanent Japanese military presence.

For Koreans, the arrival of an army meant requisition. Land was taken for camps, rail routes, and roads; draft animals, grain, and labor were demanded; and Korean laborers were put to work, sometimes under compulsion, on railway and road construction. Korean officials were obliged to supply local guides and carriers. Japanese military regulations punished interference with telegraph lines, railway construction, and military supply, and there are documented cases of executions for such offenses. Villagers and officials along the route experienced the war as a series of demands from an army that was nominally an ally.

The Korean government attempted to maintain its administrative functions, and many local officials cooperated because they judged that obstruction would bring punishment. Reports to the court and the press of the period show both cooperation and complaint. The Korean theater was not a battlefield in the sense of the Manchurian campaigns, but it was a war zone in the sense that mattered to the people who lived along its roads.

The opening episode at Chemulpo also helps students see how the law and custom of international ports interacted with armed force. Russian ships were anchored in a harbor also used by neutral foreign warships. As Japanese transports arrived, the Russian commanders confronted a choice between remaining in the harbor and accepting an ultimatum or attempting to fight out into a sea controlled by a superior Japanese squadron. Their decision led to the destruction or scuttling of the Russian vessels and to the evacuation of survivors by neutral ships. The presence of neutral naval observers did not produce a diplomatic arrangement capable of protecting the Korean harbor from military use.

For the Korean court, Japanese control of the port and the railway corridor transformed international war into a domestic emergency. The movement of soldiers from Chemulpo to Seoul created pressure before the February 23 protocol had been signed. Korean magistrates had to accommodate troop movements and the requisition of transport, and foreign residents in the treaty ports observed rapidly changing rules. The historian can reconstruct the naval action from ship reports, but the costs imposed on carriers, food sellers, and farmers are less evenly documented. Keeping these evidentiary differences visible prevents the campaign narrative from confusing the abundance of military sources with the relative importance of the people they record.

Section 54.7

The Yalu River Campaign and the Russian Retreat from Korea

By April 1904 the Japanese First Army, commanded by General Kuroki Tamemoto, had advanced through Pyongyang to the south bank of the Yalu, the river that forms the boundary between northwestern Korea and Manchuria. The Russian Eastern Detachment under General Mikhail Zasulich held positions on the northern bank near Jiuliancheng and Hushan (Tiger Hill), opposite the Korean town of Uiju. The Yalu was wide and, in spring, difficult to cross, and the Russian commander had expected to use the line to delay Japan.

Japanese success depended on reconnaissance, concealment, and superior concentration. Over several days Japanese engineers prepared bridging equipment and positioned artillery under cover, while the army moved forces to cross at several points. On 30 April and 1 May the Japanese forced the crossing, took the Russian positions after a fight in which Russian artillery was outflanked, and compelled the Russians to withdraw. Japanese casualties were substantially lower than Russian losses in this battle, though precise figures vary by source.

The battle carried a significance beyond its scale. It was the first major land engagement of the war, and a victory by an Asian army over a European army was noted widely. For Korea, it ended any Russian military presence on the peninsula. From May 1904 the war was fought in Manchuria, and Korea ceased to be an active front. The consequence was ambiguous: the immediate danger of Russian operations on Korean soil passed, but Japan could now treat Korea as a rear area and consolidate its position without distraction.

The Yalu also illustrates a point about historical memory. The campaign was a Japanese success purchased with relatively modest losses, which fed confidence in Japanese military methods. It encouraged Japanese planners to believe that the war could be won through rapid decisive action, an expectation the siege of Port Arthur and the later battles in Manchuria would test severely.

Crossing the Yalu was an engineering and supply operation before it was a battlefield success. The river's channels, islands, and seasonal conditions required reconnaissance and the assembly of bridging and boat equipment. Japanese commanders concentrated their artillery and staged demonstrations to conceal the principal crossing point, while Russian forward positions were dispersed across a broad front. Japanese coordination exploited this separation, although Russian forces were not simply surprised without warning. A useful battlefield analysis distinguishes the destruction or retreat of specific Russian formations from the larger claim that Japan had eliminated Russian power in Manchuria. The latter remained a much broader military contest.

For Korean civilians north of Pyongyang, the approach of the army turned roads and settlements into supply corridors. Local officials confronted competing claims on food and transport in a region that had only recently begun adapting to new imperial administrative institutions. Military maps tend to represent bridges and ferries as neutral physical facts; in practice they were points where labor, boats, timber, and animals had to be acquired. The Yalu victory removed the immediate Russian field presence from the Korean peninsula, but it did not restore Korean authority over the route now used by Japan's advancing troops. That distinction is vital to the chapter's continuing account of territorial control.

Section 54.8

The Liaodong Campaign and the Siege of Port Arthur

While the First Army advanced to the Yalu, Japan landed a Second Army under General Oku Yasukata on the Liaodong Peninsula in May 1904. Its task was to cut Port Arthur off from the Russian forces to the north. Oku's army took the Nanshan position, a narrow isthmus fortified by the Russians, in a costly assault on 26 May, and then occupied Dalian, which gave Japan a base. A Third Army under General Nogi Maresuke, formed to besiege Port Arthur itself, then took over the main effort in the south.

Port Arthur was a fortress with permanent fortifications, trenches, machine guns, and artillery, and the Japanese infantry assaults in August and later were extremely costly. The siege became an industrial confrontation: both sides used heavy artillery, mines, and trench warfare, and Japan eventually brought heavy naval guns ashore. The turning point was the capture, after repeated assaults, of Hill 203 (203 Meter Hill) in early December 1904, which gave Japan an observation post from which it could direct fire on the Russian fleet in the harbor. Most of the remaining Russian Pacific Squadron was destroyed or crippled by this fire.

The Russian commander, General Anatoly Stessel, surrendered the fortress on 2 January 1905. Casualties on both sides were severe. Japanese losses, usually estimated in the tens of thousands killed and wounded, were among the highest of the war, and disease and exposure added to the toll. The exact numbers vary by definition and source, and students should treat any single figure with caution.

The siege's significance was threefold. It destroyed Russia's Pacific naval base and squadron. It freed the Third Army for the northern campaign. And it showed that modern fortifications and firepower could impose enormous costs on attackers, a lesson that Japanese commanders would absorb unevenly. For Korea the connection was indirect but real: the fall of Port Arthur ended Russia's ability to counter Japan in the south and moved the central contest to Manchuria and the sea.

Port Arthur demonstrated why a force that could win a series of open battles might nevertheless be stopped for months by fortified terrain. The garrison combined permanent defensive works with field trenches, barbed wire, minefields, and artillery positions. The Japanese commanders initially sought relatively rapid assault but learned that infantry advancing against prepared positions could suffer severe losses without opening a decisive breach. Siege techniques became progressively more systematic as engineers dug approach works, heavy guns were brought forward, and defenders' lines were attacked from several directions. The important lesson is not simply that the Japanese soldiers were persistent, but that the character of the fighting changed as modern firepower made frontal attacks increasingly costly.

The fight for Hill 203 and the bombardment of the harbor illustrate the connection between land and sea operations. An observation point overlooking the harbor allowed artillery fire to be corrected against ships previously sheltered from direct view. Once the remaining squadron ceased to be an effective fleet, Port Arthur lost much of its value as a Russian naval base. Estimates of killed and wounded vary because some sources count only formal siege operations, while others include preliminary assaults, subsequent sickness, and the actions of separate armies. The chapter should therefore report categories when using figures rather than combining incompatible totals. The siege also had a political effect: its duration demonstrated that Japan's victory was expensive and left Japanese leaders searching for a favorable peace before the exhaustion of their own resources became decisive.

Section 54.9

Liaoyang, the Shahe River, and the Struggle for Manchuria

In Manchuria the main Russian army, under General Aleksey Kuropatkin, faced the Japanese Manchurian armies, which were commanded by Field Marshal Ōyama Iwao with Kodama Gentarō as chief of staff. The two sides met at Liaoyang in late August and early September 1904. After several days of fighting, the Russians withdrew in good order toward Mukden. The Japanese had won the field but had not destroyed the Russian army, and their own losses were heavy.

Kuropatkin's strategy was to avoid decisive defeat and wait for reinforcements brought by rail, on the expectation that Russia's greater reserves of manpower would eventually prevail. Japan's strategy was to win quickly before its resources ran out. In October the Russians counterattacked along the Shahe River, a battle that produced heavy casualties on both sides without a decisive result. In January 1905 a further Russian offensive at Sandepu failed to change the balance.

These battles illustrate the logistical asymmetry. Russia was far from its centers of supply, with a single-track railway whose capacity was limited, but its reserves were larger. Japan fought close to its sea lines but had a smaller population and a stretched treasury, financed in part by foreign loans arranged in London and New York. Each side could win battles but struggled to convert them into collapse of the opponent.

For Korea this phase mattered chiefly because of its diplomatic implications. Japan's repeated costly victories made its leaders anxious to secure political gains while the military situation was favorable. They therefore used 1904 and 1905 to cement their position in Korea through the agreements described in other sections, rather than waiting for the end of the war to define their aims.

The fighting around Liaoyang and the Shahe did not conform to a simple series of annihilating Japanese victories. At Liaoyang, Japanese commanders attacked prepared positions, while Kuropatkin chose withdrawal in part to preserve his army for later operations. Retreat allowed Russian formations to remain in existence, even as it surrendered territory and damaged confidence in command. The Shahe fighting ended without the complete destruction of either force. Both armies then faced weather, extended supply routes, and the difficulty of feeding large formations in a region whose transport system had been transformed into a battlefield.

Communications technology did not eliminate this friction. Japanese battlefield orders could be delayed by damaged lines or the movement of headquarters; Russian reinforcements arriving over the Trans-Siberian route still needed to reach their assigned formations and obtain ammunition and food. The same railway network that made mass warfare possible also concentrated strategic vulnerability at a few junctions. Students should compare commanders' intentions with what their armies could actually sustain. In this respect the Manchurian battles help explain why victory over Russia required not only battlefield success but diplomacy. Neither combatant possessed infinite reserves of money, rail capacity, soldiers, or public patience.

Section 54.10

The Battle of Mukden and the Military Balance in 1905

The largest land battle of the war began in late February 1905 around Mukden (Shenyang). Japanese armies, now reinforced by Nogi's Third Army, advanced against a Russian force that was larger in some estimates and held a long defensive line. After roughly three weeks of fighting, the Japanese forced a Russian withdrawal and entered Mukden on 10 March. The Russians retreated north in considerable disorder, and Kuropatkin was replaced as commander in chief.

Mukden is often described as a Japanese victory, and tactically it was. Yet it did not end Russian resistance. Casualties on both sides were very large, on the order of tens of thousands each and exceeding a hundred thousand when combined, with Russian totals generally estimated as somewhat higher than Japanese; but estimates differ and should be treated as approximate. Japan's reserves of manpower were nearly exhausted, and its ammunition and finances were strained.

It is important to distinguish tactical victory from strategic exhaustion. Japan had won each major battle and had destroyed Russia's southern position, but it could not march to Moscow, and Russia could continue the war in theory by drawing on its huge reserves. Neither side could sustain unlimited offensive operations. In Russia, military defeat coincided with the revolution of 1905, which further weakened the government's ability to continue. In Japan, leaders recognized that a prolonged war would be hard to finance.

This balance explains the diplomacy that followed. Japan wanted a settlement that confirmed its gains, including recognition in Korea, while it still held the initiative. Russia, with a domestic crisis and a fleet on its way to the Far East, was willing to talk but not to pay indemnity. Both sides were therefore receptive to outside mediation, and the decisive naval action of May would shape its terms.

At Mukden, concentration of military force increased the scale of the battle without removing the ordinary problems of command. Long fronts were difficult to supervise, units lost contact in the dust and winter conditions, and reports of movements on a flank could arrive after the opportunity for a counterstroke had passed. Japanese armies attempted to envelop Russian positions; the Russian command withdrew to preserve what remained of its forces. The campaign thereby demonstrated a paradox of modern warfare: an army could be defeated and compelled to abandon a major city without being destroyed outright.

Casualty estimates must make their denominators explicit. Widely cited modern assessments place Russian casualties around ninety thousand and Japanese around seventy-five thousand, but contemporary reports frequently mixed dead, wounded, prisoners, and missing in different combinations. These are enormous losses even when a single total is uncertain. The armies' medical and transport systems were overwhelmed by the numbers requiring treatment, and reinforcements could not immediately restore exhausted units to full effectiveness. For Japan, Mukden strengthened diplomatic leverage but made another offensive costly. Russia could still fight on paper, yet its army was damaged and its domestic political crisis deepening. The combined effect was to make a negotiated settlement increasingly rational for both governments.

Section 54.11

The Battle of Tsushima and the Destruction of Russian Naval Power

After the loss of the Pacific Squadron at Port Arthur, Russia sent its Baltic Fleet, under Admiral Zinovy Rozhestvensky, on a voyage of many months around Africa and through the Indian Ocean to the Far East. The fleet, with a mixture of modern and older ships and crews of uneven training, aimed to reach Vladivostok. Admiral Tōgō Heihachirō's Combined Fleet waited in the Tsushima Strait, the waters between Korea and Kyushu, through which the Russians were most likely to pass.

On 27 and 28 May 1905 the two fleets fought in the strait. Japanese ships had better gunnery, higher speed, and effective use of coordinated maneuver, and they concentrated fire on the Russian battleships. Most of the Russian fleet was sunk, captured, or interned; a very small number of ships reached Vladivostok. Russian dead and captured numbered in the thousands, while Japanese losses were comparatively small. Rozhestvensky himself was captured. The details of ship counts and casualties differ among sources, but the scale of the Russian defeat is not in doubt.

Tsushima settled the question of maritime supremacy. Russia could no longer threaten Japanese sea communications or contest Japanese transport of forces to Korea and Manchuria. It also changed the diplomatic setting: Japan, which had been financially stretched, now had unquestioned control of the sea, and President Theodore Roosevelt could offer mediation with both sides recognizing that Japan held the dominant position.

The strait lay off the Korean coast, and Korean fishermen and coastal communities experienced the war's noise and aftermath. But Korea's connection to the battle was strategic rather than local: it removed the last practical obstacle to Japan's treating the peninsula as its sphere. In the same period, in early 1905, Japan also issued a notice incorporating the islets known in Korean as Dokdo into Shimane Prefecture. Korea disputes the legal meaning and historical context of that act, a subject discussed in Chapter 88; here it is noted as a Japanese wartime incorporation notice; its historical and legal significance is treated in Chapter 88.

Tsushima was not merely the triumph of one tactical maneuver. Japanese commanders had concentrated their ships near likely approaches to the strait, used scouting reports to locate the approaching Russian formation, and trained crews in rapid gunnery and coordinated movement. The long Russian voyage had imposed mechanical wear, irregular opportunities for repair, and an exhausting routine on mixed crews. These conditions did not determine the outcome independently, but they compounded the disadvantage once the two fleets met. The battle demonstrates the relationship between preparation and decisive operational action: the Japanese navy exploited a chance it had created by maintaining command of the surrounding waters.

The defeat also mattered for diplomatic perceptions outside East Asia. Russia's naval projection into the western Pacific had failed, while Japan's claim to great-power status was strengthened by a victory observed across the world. Yet the result did not itself transfer any Korean legal authority to Japan. That happened through separate negotiations and instruments later in 1905. The distinction is essential: sea control supplied the capability to impose a protectorate; it did not establish Korean consent to one. The contemporary Japanese incorporation notice concerning the Liancourt Rocks should be mentioned, if at all, in neutral terms and not treated as a legal determination of the later territorial dispute reserved to Chapter 88.

Division III · Sections 54.12–54.16

The Peace Settlement and the Creation of the Protectorate

Section 54.12

The Treaty of Portsmouth and Japan's Ascendancy in Korea

President Theodore Roosevelt offered mediation in the summer of 1905, and delegations led by Komura Jutarō for Japan and Sergei Witte for Russia met at Portsmouth, New Hampshire, beginning in August. The principal disputes concerned indemnity and the island of Sakhalin. Japan eventually dropped its demand for an indemnity and accepted the southern half of Sakhalin; the treaty was signed on 5 September 1905. News of the terms provoked riots in Tokyo, where many Japanese had expected a larger indemnity.

For Korea, the key provision was that Russia recognized Japan's paramount political, military, and economic interests in Korea and agreed not to obstruct the measures of guidance, protection, and control that Japan might consider necessary there, subject to the principle of equal opportunity for other nations' commerce. In practical terms this removed Russia, the one power that had contested Japan's position on the peninsula, from the field. The treaty also transferred Russia's leases at Port Arthur and Dalian and its southern Manchurian railway rights to Japan.

Korea was not a party and was not consulted. This is not a trivial point. The treaty was an agreement between two states about a third, and it created obligations only for the parties. It did not authorize Japan to deprive Korea of its independence, and indeed its language spoke of guidance, protection, and control, not annexation. Nor did it substitute for Korean consent, which Japan would still have to seek, or manufacture, in November. International recognition of Japan's predominance and legitimate Korean consent were different things, and Japan's later treaties with Korea were necessary precisely because the first did not supply the second.

The Portsmouth negotiations also reveal the limits of Japanese victory. Japanese delegates sought recognition of their position in Korea, rights in southern Manchuria, and a financial indemnity, but the Russian government refused to pay an indemnity and resisted other claims. President Roosevelt's mediation helped produce a compromise that neither public considered an unqualified triumph. In Japan, disappointment over the peace terms contributed to the Hibiya disturbances; in Russia, the treaty limited its Pacific position without destroying its broader state power. The bargaining shows why military outcomes do not map automatically onto the terms of peace.

Korea's exclusion deserves separate attention. The treaty concerned, among other subjects, another sovereign state's future, yet no Korean representative was admitted to decide the issue. Russian acknowledgment of Japan's predominant interest in Korea was a promise between Russia and Japan. It could weaken Korea's options by removing a potential outside protector, but it could not, simply by its own text, establish that the Korean emperor had agreed to surrender foreign relations. Japan subsequently had to impose a separate agreement on Korea. Students can trace the progression from a strategic outcome, to recognition by another power, to coercive demands on the Korean government. Those were connected acts, but they were not interchangeable.

Section 54.13

Great-Power Diplomacy and the International Acceptance of Japanese Predominance

Portsmouth was the last of several steps by which other powers accepted Japan's position. On 29 July 1905 U.S. Secretary of War William Howard Taft, traveling in Tokyo, met Prime Minister Katsura Tarō. The record of their conversation, known as the Taft–Katsura memorandum, reports that Katsura disclaimed Japanese designs on the Philippines and that Taft expressed the view that Japanese suzerainty over Korea would be a logical consequence of the war and would contribute to peace. The memorandum was a record of conversation, not a treaty; Taft had no authority to bind the United States by it, and it did not authorize annexation. It became widely known only in the 1920s, and historians debate its weight. It does show that Japanese leaders believed they had American sympathy.

The revised Anglo-Japanese Alliance of 12 August 1905 was a binding agreement, and it went further. Britain recognized Japan's paramount interests in Korea and its right to take such measures of guidance, control, and protection as it considered necessary, in return for Japanese support of the British position in India. This was formal treaty recognition by a major power. Germany and France had their own interests elsewhere and did not contest Japan's position. Korea's treaty partners, in effect, were agreeing with each other, and with Japan, about Korea's future.

Korean diplomats were aware of these developments. Gojong and officials in Seoul appealed to American and other representatives, relying on the clause in the 1882 Korean–American treaty promising good offices. A Korean petition carried by Yi Seung-man (Syngman Rhee) and Yun Byeong-gu to Washington in August 1905 was received politely, but the American government indicated that such petitions had to come through official channels, which Japan now controlled. The American legation in Seoul was among the first to close after the November treaty.

The result was an international environment in which no power was prepared to contest Japan's actions. This did not make those actions legitimate in Korean eyes, and it did not make them legally sound in the view of later scholars. It did mean that Korea's most plausible route to resisting, through foreign pressure on Japan, was effectively closed.

The Taft–Katsura record is especially vulnerable to oversimplification. It was a memorandum of conversation, not a negotiated treaty text ratified by the United States and Japan. Taft and Katsura discussed the Philippines, Korea, and regional security, and their exchange signaled accommodation of Japan's intentions in Korea. It should not be described as a document by which Washington formally ceded Korea or authorized annexation. The revised Anglo-Japanese Alliance had a different character: it was a publicly concluded alliance between governments and acknowledged Japan's special position, although its language likewise did not grant authority on behalf of the Korean people.

The diplomatic effect was cumulative rather than instantaneous. Japan's leaders could see that the leading outside governments were unlikely to oppose a protectorate militarily. Britain was occupied with imperial security and its alliance with Japan; the United States had priorities in the Philippines and the Pacific; Russia had been defeated; and other governments were unwilling to force a confrontation over Korea. Korean diplomats understood this narrowing of options, which helps explain the urgency of Gojong's appeals after November 1905. In source study, students should set official treaty articles alongside private memoranda and Korean protests. Only by distinguishing their legal forms can they avoid overstating what the great powers actually promised.

Historical Debate I

Was Japan's Acquisition of Korea Predetermined by Victory over Russia?

The question: Did victory in 1905 make the loss of Korean sovereignty effectively certain, or did further decisions, divisions among Japanese leaders, and Korean actions leave real alternatives?

Interpretation A — Victory made annexation highly probable: Some historians emphasize the strategic logic of Japanese policy since the 1890s, the great-power acceptance of 1905, and the absence of any force capable of reversing the outcome. On this view, differences among Japanese leaders concerned timing and method.
Interpretation B — The outcome required further contested decisions: Others stress that Japan needed separate coercive acts in 1905, 1907, and 1910, that leaders such as Itō Hirobumi favored indirect control, that the cabinet decided on eventual annexation only in 1909, and that resistance forced Japan to commit major military resources.

Evidence: Japanese cabinet decisions, Itō's statements, diplomatic dispatches, and the sequence of treaties inform both readings.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns causation and the weight of contingency, not the sequence of events.

Why it matters: If annexation was predetermined, resistance appears futile in advance; if it was contested, Korean choices and Japanese divisions become historically meaningful.

Student task: Identify evidence from Sections 54.12–54.40 that supports each interpretation, and explain which you find more persuasive.

Section 54.14

Japanese Pressure on Gojong and the Korean Cabinet

With Russia and the other powers aligned, Japan's leaders decided in the autumn of 1905 to convert wartime influence into formal protectorate status. Itō Hirobumi, then president of the Privy Council and the most senior statesman available, was sent as special envoy. He arrived in Seoul on 9 November and was received by Gojong on 10 November, when he delivered a letter from the Japanese emperor. In the following days he and Hayashi Gonsuke presented the proposed agreement and pressed for assent.

Japanese troops in the capital were a constant presence. The Korea Garrison Army, commanded by General Hasegawa Yoshimichi, had deployed units in and around the palace area. Gojong refused to commit, citing the need to consult his ministers and, in some accounts, public opinion. Several ministers, including Prime Minister Han Gyu-seol, opposed the agreement, while others were willing to negotiate over terms. Pak Je-sun, the foreign minister, was in a difficult position: his office was the one that would ordinarily handle the agreement, and he was formally responsible for refusal or approval.

The divisions within the Korean cabinet mattered. Yi Wan-yong, then minister of education, and others, including Yi Ji-yong, Yi Geun-taek, and Gwon Jung-hyeon, were prepared to accept an agreement if modifications were made, and over the course of the negotiations they became the Japanese side's principal Korean interlocutors. Their motives are debated. Some historians emphasize personal ambition and alignment with Japan; others stress their judgment that resistance would bring worse outcomes. The surviving evidence does not fully resolve the question, and the results of their actions are treated separately from their motives.

By the evening of 17 November, Japanese diplomats had assembled the cabinet, including Han Gyu-seol, for a meeting at the palace. Gojong was not present. What happened in that meeting is the subject of the next section.

Japan's demands were accompanied by political theater as well as military force. A senior Japanese envoy could appeal to the language of Korean reform, emphasize Japan's international obligations, and remind ministers that the armed forces surrounding the capital were Japanese. These messages worked together: coercion reduced the space for disagreement, while assurances offered officials a rationale for compliance. Korean ministers did not respond uniformly. Some sought to protect administrative positions or believed that cooperation might preserve a reduced monarchy; others rejected the proposed loss of foreign relations. The emperor's objections posed a particular problem for the Japanese side, because agreement presented as Korean consent was less persuasive without the monarch's authorization.

The documentary record must be approached with an eye to purpose. Japanese ministerial reports could portray a cabinet majority as sufficient authority; Korean memorials could describe pressure and betrayals; later histories might sharpen ambiguous statements into fixed declarations of loyalty or treachery. These are not reasons to suspend judgment about military coercion, which is well documented. They are reasons to specify the procedural questions: who authorized negotiations, who signed and affixed seals, whether the emperor approved, and how different officials understood the consequences. The events of November 17 should be reconstructed as a political struggle under occupation, not reduced to a list of five names.

Section 54.15

November 17, 1905: The Eulsa Treaty and the Seizure of Diplomatic Sovereignty

The meeting took place in the Jungmyeongjeon hall of the Gyeongun Palace (now Deoksugung) on the afternoon and evening of 17 November. Japanese troops had been posted in the streets and around the palace. Hayashi and Hasegawa were present with Itō. Itō questioned the ministers one by one. Han Gyu-seol, the prime minister, said he opposed the agreement, as did the ministers of finance and justice. Others gave qualified answers. Itō interpreted ambiguous replies as assent, and the Japanese side later counted a majority. Han was reported to have been removed from the room for a time. The agreement was concluded, with the seals of the ministers, in the early hours of 18 November, though it is dated 17 November.

In outline, the agreement provided that Japan's Foreign Ministry would henceforth control and direct Korea's external relations and affairs; that Japanese diplomatic and consular officials would protect Korean subjects and interests abroad; that Korea would conclude no international agreements except through Japan; that Japan would station a Resident-General in Seoul, charged mainly with diplomatic affairs, and residents at open ports; and that Japan would maintain the dignity and welfare of the Korean imperial house. The Korean ministers regarded as having assented, Pak Je-sun, Yi Ji-yong, Yi Geun-taek, Yi Wan-yong, and Gwon Jung-hyeon, are known in Korean memory as the five Eulsa traitors.

Several features of the documentary record have been central to later argument. The text bore no title. Gojong did not give his approval and, so far as the evidence shows, the instrument was not ratified in the form that Korean law and international practice would ordinarily require. Accounts of the use of the foreign minister's seal differ, with Korean narratives alleging that it was taken and Japanese accounts denying that. The presence of troops is well attested; the exact manner in which individual ministers were pressed is described differently in different sources.

The Eulsa instrument deserves close attention because it separated a state's legal personality from its ability to exercise it. Korea retained its emperor, ministers, courts, and internal offices, but a government unable to dispatch accredited representatives or independently conclude treaties could no longer decide its own international commitments. Japanese officials would present overseas representation as a service protecting Korean interests, yet the new arrangement allowed Japan to select which Korean interests to advocate and which agreements to conclude. A protectorate thus preserved institutional appearances while transferring one of sovereignty's core functions to another government.

The evidence of coercion and the debate over validity should remain analytically distinct. The presence of troops, the pressure on ministers, and the emperor's recorded opposition are historical propositions that can be investigated through contemporary sources. Whether an instrument concluded in those circumstances satisfied the legal requirements of the time is an additional argument involving authority, procedure, ratification, and the international law then applicable. Later treaties and governmental positions have added further layers of interpretation. Students should not be asked to infer validity solely from the appearance of a signed document, nor to suppose that a declaration of illegality resolves every historical question about the actions of individual officials. A careful chapter explains the documented circumstances first and the legal positions second.

Historical Debate II

How Should Historians Interpret the Legality and Legitimacy of the Protectorate Treaties?

The question: Were the 1905 and 1907 agreements valid, invalid from the start, or valid by the standards of their day but illegitimate by later ones?

Interpretation A — Invalid from the outset: Korean scholarship has generally argued that coercion of the ministers, the absence of imperial ratification, the missing title, and the use of troops made the agreements void. Some international law commentators have cited the 1905 case in discussions of coercion of state representatives.
Interpretation B — Valid under the standards of the time: Some Japanese officials and legal writers of the period argued that the agreement was concluded by authorized ministers, that international law then tolerated pressure on weaker states, and that other powers had recognized it. Later Japanese governments have taken positions that avoided conceding original invalidity.
Interpretation C — Distinguish legality from legitimacy: Others argue that historians can document coercion and Korean rejection without resolving a formal legal question, and that the 1965 normalization treaty's wording about earlier agreements being already null and void has been read differently in Seoul and Tokyo.

Evidence: Cabinet accounts, Japanese diplomatic reports, Gojong's protests, foreign observers, and treaty texts.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns interpretation and the applicable legal standard rather than the facts of Japanese military presence.

Why it matters: The question affects how colonial-period history is understood and how later diplomacy between South Korea and Japan has been conducted.

Student task: Distinguish the documentary evidence of coercion from the legal arguments, and explain what additional evidence could clarify the question.

Section 54.16

The Residency-General and the Destruction of Independent Foreign Relations

After the agreement, Japan moved quickly to implement it. The Japanese legation in Seoul was converted into the Residency-General, which began operating in February 1906, and Itō Hirobumi, who had come as an envoy, became the first Resident-General, taking up the post in Seoul in March 1906. The office's formal remit was diplomacy, but Itō was also given authority over the Japanese military in Korea and, in practice, wide influence over Korean policy. Japanese residencies replaced consulates at the treaty ports.

The practical effects were immediate. Korean legations abroad were recalled or closed, and Korean diplomats lost their function. Foreign governments withdrew their ministers from Seoul and replaced them with consuls: the American legation was the first to close, in late November 1905, and others followed within weeks. For these governments this was a pragmatic recognition of a changed status. For the Korean court it removed the channels through which Gojong had previously sought foreign support.

The meaning of protectorate status was thus specific. Korea retained an emperor, a cabinet, ministries, a currency, and an army, and it was still formally an independent state. But it could not make treaties, receive or send envoys in its own name, or speak for itself in international forums. Because Itō believed that an existing government could be a more efficient instrument than direct rule, he left Korean offices in place while increasing the number and influence of Japanese advisers.

This distinction between formal retention and practical control shaped everything that followed. It gave Japan a means to describe its policy as reform and protection, and it gave Koreans a government that, in their eyes, could no longer be trusted. The same distinction also explains why Gojong and others tried to use the remaining space, secretly and openly, to contest the arrangement.

The Residency-General's presence changed the everyday meaning of diplomacy in Seoul. Foreign representatives who had previously conducted business with the Korean Foreign Ministry now received communications through Japanese institutions, and the Korean government's ability to protest actions abroad was narrowed. Korean legations faced closure or loss of independent function. The resident-general's authority was initially defined with reference to foreign affairs, but that restriction proved unstable because railway contracts, customs revenue, police arrangements, and the appointment of officials could all be represented as matters touching foreign relations or security.

The protectorate also needed cooperation from the existing Korean administrative apparatus. A Japanese resident-general could not personally supervise every county magistrate, revenue office, or school; he depended on Korean ministers, local officials, interpreters, and newly placed advisers to transmit orders. This created opportunities for bargaining, evasion, and resistance as well as collaboration. It also created a mechanism for gradual transfer of authority: an office could keep its Korean name while a Japanese official acquired control over its decisions. Chapter 55 will examine direct colonial institutions after annexation; the problem here is how protectorate authority grew while Korea formally remained a separate state.

Evidence Comparison

Japanese Treaty Explanations versus Korean Protests and Foreign Reports

Historians reconstructing the Eulsa agreement compare several families of evidence. Japanese official accounts, including the Foreign Ministry's published documents and the reports of Hayashi and Itō, describe the agreement as concluded by Korean ministers after discussion and in the Korean interest. Korean accounts, including Gojong's later statements, newspaper editorials such as the Hwangseong Sinmun's mourning editorial, and the testimony of dissenting ministers, describe coercion and the lack of imperial approval. Foreign diplomatic reports, from American, British, and other observers, record the troop presence and the closing of legations, and generally accepted the agreement as a fait accompli.

None of these categories is neutral. Japanese officials had an interest in presenting consent; Korean writers in presenting resistance; and foreign diplomats had interests that favored accommodating Japan. Where all three agree, as on the date, the presence of troops, and the text's provisions, the facts are secure. Where they diverge, historians weigh the position of each witness.

Division IV · Sections 54.17–54.21

Korean Resistance to the Protectorate, 1905–1907

Section 54.17

Gojong's Diplomatic Protests and the Rejection of Protectorate Status

Gojong never approved the Eulsa agreement, and from the weeks after its signing he sought to make that known abroad. Because the Residency-General now controlled Korea's formal communications, he relied on informal channels: foreign missionaries and advisers, Korean agents traveling abroad, and letters sent in secret. The American Methodist missionary and educator Homer Hulbert, who had long served as an informal intermediary, carried an appeal from Gojong to the United States in late 1905 and early 1906. Other messages were sent to European monarchs and to the press.

The core of these appeals was that the agreement lacked the emperor's consent and was therefore not valid, and that the signatory powers should honor the commitments in their own treaties with Korea, notably the "good offices" clause of the 1882 Korean–American treaty. Gojong also argued that Japan had violated its own earlier promises, in the 1904 protocol and in the 1895 Treaty of Shimonoseki, to respect Korean independence.

The reception was poor. The governments concerned had already accepted Japan's position at Portsmouth and in the Anglo-Japanese Alliance, and they regarded the matter as settled. Some officials and journalists were sympathetic, and the appeals contributed to a body of foreign writing critical of Japanese policy, including accounts by Hulbert and by British journalists. But sympathy did not become action. Japan, for its part, learned of at least some of the appeals through its own surveillance, and the attempt hardened Itō's view that Gojong could not be trusted to accept the protectorate.

The episode matters for two reasons. It shows that Gojong did not acquiesce, and it establishes a pattern of secret diplomacy that culminated in the Hague mission of 1907. It also shows the limits of that approach: an emperor who had lost control of his own diplomatic channels was left dependent on intermediaries whose reach was small.

The emperor's private and public messages were not identical in form or audience. Public proclamations could affirm dynastic dignity without directly challenging the Japanese authorities who controlled communications; confidential letters could be more explicit about coercion and lack of consent. Delivering them presented a practical problem. An envoy carrying a letter abroad might be stopped, challenged over credentials, or denied access to officials whose governments already accepted Japan's authority over Korean diplomacy. Even when a message reached its destination, diplomatic acknowledgment did not necessarily translate into support. A foreign minister might record a Korean protest while continuing official relations with the Japanese representative.

These failures should be judged against the alternatives that Korean officials actually possessed, not against an imaginary power to command great-power assistance. A public declaration of war against Japan would have been militarily ineffective; unqualified acquiescence would have abandoned the government's claim to independent authority. Secret diplomacy preserved evidence that Korea objected, created channels later used by independence activists, and ensured that the protectorate was not universally represented as a voluntary bargain. The Hague mission of 1907 developed this strategy on a more public international stage, but its roots lay in the emperor's early protests.

Section 54.18

Memorials, Public Protest, and the Crisis of Political Legitimacy

News of the agreement spread within days. Officials submitted memorials to the throne demanding rejection of the agreement and punishment of the ministers who had signed it. Confucian scholars and retired officials, including Choe Ik-hyeon, wrote or traveled to Seoul to petition. Shops in Seoul closed in protest, students demonstrated, and crowds gathered near the palace. On 20 November the Hwangseong Sinmun published an editorial by Jang Ji-yeon, widely known by its title in Korean, mourning the day on which the nation had been lost. The paper's issue was seized and its editor imprisoned for a time.

A number of officials took their own lives. Min Yeong-hwan, a senior court official and relative of the late queen, died on 30 November 1905, leaving written messages addressed to the Korean people and to foreign diplomats. Jo Byeong-se, another senior official, and several others also killed themselves. Their deaths were interpreted in different ways. To many Koreans they were acts of loyalty and protest and were commemorated as such; popular tradition surrounded Min's death with stories, including the claim that bamboo grew in the room where his blood-stained clothes were kept, which is a legend rather than documented fact. Some contemporaries and later writers considered them an inadequate form of action; others saw them as a catalyst for wider mobilization.

It is useful to distinguish symbolic protest from organized political resistance. Memorials, closures, and suicides expressed rejection and moral claims, and they were meaningful in Confucian political culture, in which a loyal official's remonstrance carried legitimacy. But they did not by themselves create an organization capable of confronting Japanese power. The crisis was one of political legitimacy: the cabinet that had signed the agreement lacked the confidence of much of the public, while the emperor who had refused it lacked the means to act.

That gap, between a government that could be pressured and a society that rejected the result, would shape the next phase. It pushed energy toward newspapers and schools on the one hand and toward armed action on the other.

Protest took forms conditioned by the status and education of the participants. Scholar-officials appealed to the throne through memorials written in the vocabulary of loyal remonstrance. Urban groups held meetings, read newspapers, and circulated petitions that reached people beyond the official class. Some officials resigned rather than work under the new arrangements, while others believed that remaining in government gave them a better chance to defend limited Korean interests. These choices should be compared without assuming that resignation always entailed greater personal sacrifice or that continued service always meant support for Japanese policy.

Min Yeong-hwan's death became a powerful symbol because public accounts interpreted it as a final remonstrance against the treaty. The meaning of such an act was shaped through mourning, print, and the retelling of personal conduct. Its emotional force did not supply organizations with weapons or diplomatic leverage, but it could enlarge the constituency of resistance. This section should connect public protest to the emerging patriotic enlightenment movement, whose organizers argued that education, institutions, and collective economic discipline were essential to political survival. The tension between immediate opposition and long-term strengthening recurs across the chapter.

Section 54.19

Newspapers, Schools, Associations, and the Growth of National Resistance

A second form of response was long-term and institutional. Newspapers had grown rapidly since the 1890s, and during the protectorate they became a principal means of shaping opinion. The Daehan Maeil Sinbo, founded in 1904 by the British journalist Ernest Bethell and the Korean editor Yang Gi-tak, was protected from Japanese censorship by Bethell's British nationality and extraterritorial status, and it published sharply critical editorials in Korean and, for a period, in other editions. The Hwangseong Sinmun, founded in 1898, was another leading voice. Japan responded with the newspaper law of 1907 and later publication controls, and Bethell was prosecuted in a British consular court in 1908, though he continued to be a target of Japanese complaint until his death in 1909.

Associations and schools expanded alongside the press. The Daehan Jaganghoe (Korea Self-Strengthening Society), founded in 1906 with Yun Chi-ho as its first president, promoted education, industry, and public discussion, and it was succeeded after 1907 by the Daehan Hyeophoe. The Sinminhoe (New People's Society), organized in 1907 by An Chang-ho, Yang Gi-tak, and others as a secret society, aimed at building a new national leadership. Hundreds of private schools were founded by Christian missions, local notables, and reformers, among them Osan School and Daesong School, and they taught modern subjects together with patriotic content. In 1908 Japan issued a private school ordinance to regulate them.

This movement is often called the patriotic enlightenment movement. Its premise was that Korea's loss of sovereignty reflected weakness in education, industry, and civic organization, and that self-strengthening was both a moral duty and the necessary route to independence. Critics of the movement, then and later, argued that it underestimated the immediate military and diplomatic situation; defenders argued that it built the human foundations on which later independence efforts depended. Its leaders had varied views, some stressing gradual preparation, others quietly supporting armed resistance. Its political significance was real even though it could not stop the loss of sovereignty.

The press provided a new political infrastructure rather than merely publishing declarations. Editors selected which foreign developments to explain, printed appeals and letters, and connected readers in Seoul with organizations in provincial towns. Newspapers differed in language, audience, and degree of exposure to censorship. Foreign ownership or association could sometimes complicate direct Japanese interference, although it did not guarantee that a newspaper could escape pressure. Teachers and school founders likewise used classrooms to spread knowledge of international affairs, Korean history, and the language of national obligation. Associations brought merchants, students, intellectuals, and officials into forms of organization less dependent on royal command.

There was no simple separation between “modernization” and “resistance.” A government-controlled railway could move troops into a province while also carrying printed newspapers, commercial goods, and passengers who debated independence. A school established through private funds could train clerks who later entered Japanese-supervised administration or activists who opposed it. Such outcomes depended on political choices and changing institutions, not on technology itself. Japanese authorities increasingly recognized the political potential of print and education and responded through regulation and censorship. The movement's achievements should therefore be assessed not only by its inability to prevent annexation in 1910, but also by the durable organizational capacities it created.

Section 54.20

The Eulsa Righteous Armies: Choe Ik-hyeon and Min Jong-sik

The righteous armies (uibyeong) had precedents in the sixteenth-century wars against Japan (Chapter 34) and in the resistance of 1895–1896 after the murder of Queen Min (Chapter 52). In 1906 they appeared again, led mainly by Confucian scholars and retired officials, and are known as the Eulsa righteous armies. Their leaders interpreted the protectorate in terms of loyalty to the king and defense of civilization, and they recruited followers through scholarly networks, lineage ties, and local authority.

In Chungcheong province, Min Jong-sik, a former official, raised a force in the spring of 1906 and occupied the fortress town of Hongju in early June. Korean government troops and Japanese forces retook the town within days, and Min was arrested. In Jeolla province, Choe Ik-hyeon, a prominent scholar in his seventies and a figure of long standing in the political opposition, raised an army in June 1906 at Taein with his disciple Im Byeong-chan, and attracted several hundred followers. When the force advanced toward Sunchang, it was confronted by Korean government troops. Reportedly, Choe declined to fight fellow Koreans, and he was captured and sent into exile on Tsushima, where he died in January 1907. His death in exile became a powerful symbol.

Not all leaders were from the scholarly elite. In the Taebaek mountains and the southeast, Shin Dol-seok, a commoner, led a force in 1906 that operated in Gyeongsang and Gangwon, and it showed that resistance was not confined to the gentry. Still, in 1906 the movement was relatively small and localized, dependent on traditional leaders and with limited weapons, mostly older firearms and local arms.

The differences from the later movement are important. The Eulsa armies were led largely by Confucian elites, were few in number, and lacked trained soldiers. After 1907 the movement became larger and more socially diverse, with former regular soldiers contributing military skill and weapons. Treating the 1906 movements separately recognizes their distinct character and their role as the first armed response to the protectorate.

The Hongju campaign demonstrates how a localized righteous-army coalition could briefly challenge Japanese-backed authority. Min Jong-sik brought together scholars, former officials, and recruits with varying military experience. The capture of a town or the dispersal of a police detachment could create political momentum, but holding a fortified position against better-equipped forces required ammunition, defensive preparation, and a reliable command structure. Japanese reinforcements and their ability to control the principal routes greatly constrained what the insurgents could retain. The movement's military problem was not a lack of courage but the disparity between mobilizing a crowd and sustaining an armed field force.

Choe Ik-hyeon brought a different kind of authority. As a prominent Confucian scholar and former official, he could give an uprising moral legitimacy by framing resistance as fidelity to rightful political order. Yet his networks did not represent all Korean society, and his concept of loyalty did not necessarily coincide with the politics of younger urban nationalists. The earlier Eulmi righteous armies of the 1890s provided precedents, but the situation after 1905 was new: the target was a protectorate increasingly controlled by a foreign administrative structure. This section should mark that transition without preempting the broader participation of disbanded soldiers after 1907.

Historical Vocabulary
Eulsa Treaty, Jeongmi Treaty, Uibyeong, and Patriotic Enlightenment

Eulsa Treaty is the Korean name for the November 1905 agreement, after the year Eulsa (1905) in the sexagenary cycle; Korean usage often adds words meaning "forced" or "protectorate." Jeongmi Treaty names the July 1907 agreement, after the year Jeongmi (1907). Uibyeong (righteous army) refers to irregular forces raised in defense of the country; the term carried associations of loyalty and Confucian duty. Patriotic enlightenment (aeguk gyemong) names the movement that sought to restore national strength through education, press, and associations.

Why it matters: These terms are drawn from Korean usage and carry judgments. Japanese official terms, such as Second Japan–Korea Agreement, are less loaded but present the events as routine diplomacy. Potential misunderstanding: "Righteous army" does not mean a single force; the units varied in leadership, aims, and composition.

Section 54.21

The Limits and Possibilities of Korean Resistance before 1907

By the spring of 1907, Korean responses had not restored independence. Petitions to the throne had reached a monarch with no power to act. Foreign appeals had been politely received and ignored. Public mobilization had produced demonstrations but no sustained organization. Patriotic education was a long-term strategy that could not yield immediate results. The armed resistance of 1906 was suppressed within months. The question for historians is why.

Part of the answer was organizational. The Korean opposition lacked a unified leadership, a shared strategy, and a safe base. Reformers, Confucian scholars, officials, and the emperor held different views about what should be done and about each other, and the Independence Club's suppression had left few experienced political organizers. Part was the overwhelming asymmetry of power. Japan had just defeated a great European empire and had troops, a navy, a modern police, control of telegraphs, railways, and finance, and the diplomatic backing of Britain and tacit acceptance by the United States.

It would be wrong to conclude that the efforts were meaningless. They established the repertoire from which later movements drew: a language of legal and moral protest, a press tradition, a network of schools and associations, and a model of armed resistance. They also forced Japan to adjust its methods. But in early 1907 the protectorate stood, and Gojong faced a choice between acceptance and another, riskier effort. He chose the latter, and that decision led to the confrontation of the summer.

It is useful to measure the several forms of resistance against different objectives. A petition might aim to influence the emperor, persuade a minister to resign, or preserve a written record of opposition. A newspaper campaign might seek public solidarity or funds; a school might aim to train a generation capable of competing economically and politically; an armed group might seek to remove Japanese units from a district. Treating them all as failed versions of the same strategy obscures why participants chose different methods and why an individual could support more than one. Their lack of central coordination was a political weakness, but it also reflected a society whose institutions had been deliberately fragmented.

Japanese policy exploited this diversity by threatening some activists, tolerating others temporarily, and promising positions to officials prepared to cooperate. The protectorate could survive a wave of memorials if foreign governments refused to act; military detachments could defeat isolated insurgent groups if they obtained intelligence and reinforcement. Nevertheless, the preservation of documentary objections, organizational links, and local armed traditions would influence later movements. Chapter 54 should recognize the limits of immediate effectiveness without portraying the participants as historically insignificant simply because their contemporary objectives were not realized.

Section 54.22

The Hague Secret Emissaries and Korea's Appeal to International Law

The Second International Peace Conference met at The Hague from June to October 1907. Gojong decided to send a delegation to present Korea's case. He chose three emissaries: Yi Jun, a former prosecutor; Yi Sang-seol, a former senior official; and Yi Wi-jong, a Korean diplomat who was then in Russia. They carried credentials in the emperor's name and traveled in secret, leaving Seoul in April 1907 by way of Vladivostok and the Trans-Siberian Railway. Hulbert assisted in the planning and later in the effort to publicize the mission.

The mission's legal argument was that the Eulsa agreement was invalid because Gojong had not approved it, and that Korea, as a sovereign state, had a right to participate in the conference and to be heard. The emissaries reached The Hague in late June. The conference's organizers declined to seat them, accepting the position that Korea's foreign relations were controlled by Japan. The Russian president of the conference and the Dutch hosts declined to receive them officially, and delegates of the powers that had acquiesced in the protectorate refused to intervene.

The emissaries then turned to the press. They contacted journalists, and the English journalist W. T. Stead, who published a daily conference newspaper, gave them sympathetic coverage. On 9 July Yi Wi-jong, who spoke French, delivered an address to an international press gathering that presented Korea's case. The coverage did not alter the diplomatic position, but it embarrassed Japan and drew attention to the issue.

Yi Jun died at The Hague on 14 July 1907. Japanese accounts attributed his death to illness, and some contemporary reports mentioned a carbuncle or similar infection. Korean tradition has often described his death as a deliberate act of protest, including suicide. Historians treat the cause with caution: the contemporary evidence does not allow certainty, and later narratives were shaped by the symbolic importance of the story. What is not in dispute is that the mission failed in its diplomatic objective and that Japan used it as a pretext for further action.

The Hague mission offers a particularly clear test of international institutions. The Peace Conference dealt with matters of arbitration, war, and diplomatic procedure, but the Korean emissaries were prevented from acting as a recognized delegation because the governments controlling the conference's admission process did not accept Korea's separate standing under the protectorate. The problem was therefore not that Korea lacked an intelligible legal argument; it was that access to the diplomatic forum depended on states unwilling to challenge Japan. Newspaper interviews and public speeches could make the argument visible to an international audience without giving it a formal place in the proceedings.

The mission also produced divergent memories. Some narratives emphasize the courage of the envoys and the sacrifice represented by Yi Jun's death; others investigate the travel arrangements, credentials, conversations with diplomats, and the limitations of contemporary reports. The responsible approach is to preserve both the documented political effort and uncertainty concerning the exact circumstances of his death. Later dramatizations should not be silently converted into eyewitness fact. Nor should the absence of a successful conference resolution be mistaken for proof that the envoys were fraudulent representatives. Their failure illustrates the gulf between a sovereign's claim to send emissaries and the international recognition necessary to have them heard.

Section 54.23

The Forced Abdication of Gojong, July 1907

Japan learned of the mission early and responded with anger. Itō and the Japanese government treated it as a breach of the Eulsa agreement, and Japanese leaders in Tokyo, including Prime Minister Katsura and the elder statesmen, considered how to respond. Some urged a harsh response, including declaring war or annexation; Itō preferred to use the crisis to tighten control, and it was agreed that the emperor must be pressed to give way.

Yi Wan-yong, who had become prime minister in May 1907, and other cabinet members, including Song Byeong-jun, called on Gojong to take responsibility and to step aside in favor of the crown prince. Itō's own role in the details is debated: Japanese statements suggested that the initiative came from the Korean cabinet, while Korean historians have generally treated Itō and the Japanese government as the real source of pressure, which contemporary diplomatic records tend to support. Gojong resisted.

The outcome was a transfer of authority announced on 19 July 1907, in a form that Gojong's supporters disputed. The emperor was said to have delegated authority to the crown prince, a step that stopped short of formal abdication; a ceremony on 20 July was reported to have been conducted without the emperor's presence, using substitutes. Accounts of the details differ, and the question of whether Gojong ever freely abdicated remains part of the debate over the legitimacy of the process. What is clear is that he was removed under heavy pressure and that the pressure came in the context of the Hague mission.

Crowds gathered in Seoul in protest, and there were attacks on pro-Japanese officials and clashes with police and Japanese troops in the following days. The Korean cabinet and Japanese forces suppressed the demonstrations, but the public reaction showed that the emperor's removal was widely seen as illegitimate.

The documentary sequence of abdication should be described as a process, not a moment easily reduced to one date. A delegation of executive authority was approved or announced around 19 July, with a proxy ceremony on the following day. A subsequent instrument treated the transfer as ending Gojong's reign, and the ministers soon addressed the crown prince as emperor. Sunjong's formal enthronement came on 27 August. Each step had a different procedural and symbolic function, and the lack of free royal consent lies at the center of the controversy. A dated decree can be genuine evidence that an act was publicly announced without proving that the emperor willingly approved it.

The political significance was immediate. The Korean emperor had tried to use diplomatic channels outside Japanese supervision, so his removal communicated that defiance could cost a ruler his office. It also made the cabinet's apparent acceptance of Japanese conditions more important: Japan could claim that Korea's own ministers were carrying out the succession. The resulting street protests demonstrated that many Koreans did not share that claim. Students should therefore compare the official sequence of decrees with reports of coercion and unrest, rather than substituting either for the other.

Section 54.24

Sunjong's Accession and the Reconfiguration of Imperial Authority

Gojong's son Sunjong succeeded to the throne, with a formal accession ceremony in August 1907, and adopted the reign era name Yunghui. Gojong became the retired emperor and remained in Deoksugung, while Sunjong moved the court to Changdeokgung in the autumn. The new emperor was in poor health and had little independent political role. In December 1907 Itō arranged for the young crown prince Yi Un (Yeongchin), a half-brother of Sunjong, to go to Japan for what was described as education.

Retaining an emperor, rather than abolishing the monarchy, was useful to Japan. It maintained the appearance of continuity and allowed measures to be issued in the name of the Korean throne, which could be presented as legitimate. The rewards of cooperation, including titles, stipends, and positions, were offered to members of the royal family and the elite. The court became increasingly ceremonial, with decisions made in the cabinet under the Resident-General's direction.

The arrangement also carried a risk. The existence of Gojong as a living former emperor in the capital meant that a focus of loyalty remained, and the lines of authority were blurred in the minds of many Koreans. The consolidation of Japanese control in 1907 therefore proceeded alongside efforts to restrict contact between the former emperor and political figures.

The accession arrangements also altered the location and visibility of monarchy. The Korean court retained its ceremonies and hierarchy, but decisions on policy were increasingly transmitted through a cabinet and Residency-General that operated under Japanese direction. The imperial household became both an institution of prestige and a resource to be controlled. Royal family appointments, travel, education, palace access, and ceremonial expenditures could be used to reward compliance or prevent political communication. The removal of the young Yi Un to Japan for education demonstrated how the future of the dynasty itself could be managed through ostensibly protective arrangements.

Sunjong's era name, Yunghui, was adopted in early August before the formal enthronement later that month. It is useful to distinguish this symbolic act from the earlier transfer of executive authority. The new emperor was not simply powerless in a personal sense; he presided over institutions that had been progressively deprived of independent authority. By continuing to issue measures in his name, the protectorate government could draw on dynastic legitimacy while restricting the channels through which the monarch might resist. This helps explain why Japan maintained an imperial figure for three more years before abolishing the Korean state in 1910.

Section 54.25

The July 24, 1907 Agreement and Japan's Control of Domestic Government

While the court was in turmoil, Itō presented a new agreement, signed on 24 July 1907 by Prime Minister Yi Wan-yong and Itō. Known in Korean as the Jeongmi Treaty or the Seven-Article Treaty, and in Japanese as the Third Japan–Korea Agreement, it extended Japanese authority from external affairs to domestic government. In summary, Korea agreed to follow the Resident-General's guidance in administrative reform; to obtain his prior approval for laws and important administrative acts; to separate judicial administration from ordinary administration; to obtain his consent for the appointment and dismissal of senior officials; to appoint Japanese persons recommended by him to Korean official posts; and not to employ foreigners without his consent. The 1904 adviser agreement was superseded.

A related memorandum, whose terms were not made fully public at the time, provided for the appointment of Japanese subjects as vice-ministers and for further steps including the reorganization of the army, and these provisions were put into effect quickly. The seven ministers associated with the agreement, Yi Wan-yong, Yi Byeong-mu, Go Yeong-hui, Song Byeong-jun, Jo Jung-eung, Yi Jae-gon, and Im Seon-jun, are known in Korean memory as the Jeongmi seven traitors.

The difference between the formal provisions and their practical enforcement was substantial. On paper, the Resident-General gave guidance and approval. In practice, Japanese vice-ministers in each ministry held the decisive administrative functions, and Korean ministers could not act without them. The 1907 agreement thus completed the structure of adviser government, turning advisers into officials with authority within the Korean state.

The July agreement extended the earlier adviser system into the core of domestic decision-making. Under its articles, the Korean government was obliged to obtain the Resident-General's consent before issuing significant laws or administrative measures; senior appointments required concurrence, and Japanese nationals could be recommended for Korean offices. Unlike a purely diplomatic protectorate, this structure made it difficult for Korean ministries to act independently even on matters not directly involving foreign governments. The formal responsibility of ministers remained, but the authority to approve their decisions rested elsewhere.

Implementation depended on personnel. Japanese vice-ministers and officials entered departments responsible for administration, finance, judicial affairs, and policing. Policy could be controlled through a sequence of seemingly ordinary signatures, budgets, appointments, and reports. This is why the chapter must give administrative mechanics almost as much attention as dramatic events at the palace. A government that cannot hire officials, authorize expenditures, or promulgate regulations without external agreement has lost much of its domestic autonomy. The treaty's provisions, not simply its label, show the depth of that transformation.

Section 54.26

The Dissolution of the Korean Imperial Army, July–August 1907

The Korean army had been reduced in size after 1905, to a force of some thousands, but it remained the only organized Korean armed body. Japan's leaders saw it as a potential source of resistance and as an unnecessary expense. An imperial rescript of 31 July 1907 ordered its reorganization and the discharge of most units, retaining only a small guard force. The order was issued in Sunjong's name, though it had been prepared by the Japanese side.

Implementation began on 1 August. Officers and men of the Seoul units were assembled at the training ground at Hunryeonwon, told to come without arms, and informed of the dissolution. Soldiers were offered lump-sum payments scaled by rank, and some units complied. The response of Korean soldiers was not uniform. Some accepted discharge; others were enraged at the loss of their profession and at the humiliation. Provincial units, scattered across the country, would be dissolved in the following weeks.

The consequences were far-reaching. The state lost its remaining capacity for organized defense, and several thousand trained men, many with weapons training and some with grievances, were released into society. The decision to dissolve the army immediately after Gojong's removal reflected a calculation that quick action would prevent organized resistance. It had the opposite effect in many places: the dissolution became the proximate cause of the largest wave of armed resistance of the protectorate years.

Military dissolution was not a simple administrative economy measure. The imperial army had already been reduced, and Japan had military forces deployed across the peninsula. Yet even a comparatively small Korean regular force represented a competing source of armed authority and a potential rallying point for the public. Disbanding it removed an institution whose officers might claim loyalty directly to the Korean emperor. The order presented the decision as royal policy, but it followed coercive cabinet arrangements and Japanese military plans. An account that focuses only on payroll or modernization would miss the sovereignty issue: the capacity to command an independent armed force is a practical attribute of statehood.

For soldiers, the moment of dissolution also had a personal meaning. They were deprived of career, income, organizational identity, and a recognized mission of national defense. Some accepted payments because they had dependents or saw no viable alternative. Others concealed weapons, joined fellow soldiers in resisting, or entered irregular bands after returning to their home districts. The outcomes cannot be reduced to a single patriotic response. More precise source criticism is needed for numbers, since an official return may list soldiers dismissed but not the number who subsequently joined an armed unit, and later accounts may assume that all disbanded soldiers became fighters.

Section 54.27

Armed Resistance in Seoul and the Disbanded Soldiers

On 1 August 1907 Pak Seung-hwan, a battalion commander of the Guard, shot himself after being ordered to carry out the dissolution. His death, according to accounts from the time, inflamed the soldiers under his command. The First Battalion of the First Regiment of the Guard and elements of the Second Regiment seized weapons and ammunition, and fought Japanese troops in and around central Seoul, in the area near Namdaemun. The Korean soldiers fought with determination but were outgunned and outnumbered, and Japanese reinforcements overcame them. Japanese reports listed Korean dead in the dozens and several hundred prisoners; Korean sources give different counts, and all figures should be treated as approximate. Japanese casualties were considerably smaller.

Resistance did not end in the capital. In the following days garrisons at Wonju, on Ganghwa Island, and in other provincial towns rose against dissolution, in some cases led by junior officers, and fought Japanese units before dispersing into the mountains. Many of the soldiers who survived took their weapons and their training to join or form righteous armies.

These soldiers transformed the character of the resistance. They brought modern rifles, drill, knowledge of tactics, and organizational experience that the scholar-led forces of 1906 had lacked. They also brought a different social composition: they were often from commoner backgrounds, and their presence in the movement opened it to wider groups. This is the reason that 1907 marks a transition in the history of Korean resistance, from a movement of local elites to a widespread armed struggle.

The Seoul fighting illustrates the military value and limits of professional training. Soldiers familiar with rifles, unit command, and the storage of ammunition could sustain resistance longer than an untrained gathering, but they still faced forces with superior numbers, heavier weapons, and access to reinforcement. Urban combat around guard barracks, streets, and gates imposed risks on civilians and complicated the movement of both sides. Contemporary casualty returns were produced for military reporting purposes; they need not capture the full effect on households or all of those who fled or were detained after the clashes.

The longer-term effect was organizational. The scattering of regular soldiers moved military knowledge into the countryside, where preexisting righteous-army networks offered shelter and recruits. The resulting combinations differed by region. Former soldiers might command units, act as instructors, or serve alongside scholar-leaders whose political legitimacy rested on Confucian education. The encounter could produce tension over tactics and hierarchy, but it also broadened the movement's social base. This is a more historically useful explanation than the claim that the 1907 revolt was simply a spontaneous reaction to an unpopular imperial decree.

Division VI · Sections 54.28–54.32

The Righteous-Armies War and the Suppression of Armed Resistance

Section 54.28

The Jeongmi Righteous Armies and the Broadening of Armed Resistance

From the autumn of 1907 armed groups appeared in nearly every province. Participants included disbanded soldiers, Confucian scholars and local notables, farmers, miners, laborers, hunters, merchants, and, in some regions, members of earlier religious and popular movements. Some units numbered in the hundreds; many were small bands of a few dozen. Leaders ranged from former officers to scholars to commoners with local authority.

Recruitment depended on village and lineage networks, on shared grievance, and on the presence of men with weapons skills. Weapons came from the dissolved army, from captured Japanese equipment, from older stocks, and from arms smuggled across borders; supplies of ammunition were a constant problem. Local communities provided food, shelter, information, and sometimes labor, and in some places those who refused were pressured to contribute. The movement relied on the countryside's mountainous terrain, which made pursuit difficult.

It is important not to describe these formations as a single unified national army. They had no central command, overlapping and sometimes conflicting aims, and varied tactics. Some groups fought primarily for local defense, others for the restoration of the emperor and the old order, and still others for what they described as national independence in more general terms. The movement is better understood as a wave of related resistances with shared opposition to Japanese control. Foreign journalists, notably the British correspondent F. A. McKenzie, visited some of the groups in 1907 and photographed them, and their accounts and images are important evidence, though they reflect the viewpoint of the visitor.

After the disbandment, righteous-army units combined older legitimating traditions with the practical experience of soldiers and the grievances of commoners. Some commanders issued proclamations in the name of loyalty to the emperor; others appealed more directly to national independence. Local fighters might have joined because a gendarmerie post abused their village or because requisition had disrupted livelihood, without possessing an elaborate theory of sovereignty. These motives were compatible in action but not identical in ideology. The composition of any unit could change substantially as recruits arrived, supplies failed, or leaders were captured.

The movement's military form also varied. Small detachments attacked patrols and communications lines, while larger coalitions attempted coordinated advances. A force operating near its own villages could obtain intelligence and shelter, but it also exposed those villages to reprisals. A scholar with a famous name could mobilize supporters who did not have sufficient rifles, while an experienced soldier might command weapons but lack the wider networks needed for food and political support. The chapter should explain both the potential and the fragility of this coalition before presenting the attempt to concentrate forces for an advance on Seoul.

Section 54.29

The Thirteen-Province Allied Righteous Armies and the Attempt to Advance on Seoul

In late 1907, leaders from several provinces attempted to coordinate their forces. Yi In-yeong, a former official who had led forces in Gyeonggi and Gangwon, was chosen as commander of a coalition known as the Thirteen-Province Allied Righteous Army, and Heo Wi, a Gyeongsang-born officer and former soldier, served as a principal military figure. The plan was to concentrate forces near Seoul, attack the Japanese garrison, and compel the foreign legations and the Residency-General to reconsider.

In early 1908 vanguard units under Heo Wi advanced to within a short distance of Seoul, reaching the area east of the Dongdaemun gate. Japanese forces counterattacked, and the advance collapsed. The failure had several causes. The allied forces were not assembled on the expected scale, in part because of difficulties of communication and travel in the winter. Command was divided; Yi In-yeong left to observe mourning for his father, a decision reflecting Confucian obligation that also weakened the coalition. Supplies were short and ammunition limited, and the Japanese had far better weapons, intelligence, and organization.

The episode demonstrates both the ambition and the limits of the movement. It showed that Koreans from different provinces could cooperate, and that the capital was within the reach of armed groups, but it also showed that a conventional assault on a modern army was beyond their capacity. After 1908 the movement turned increasingly to guerrilla methods. Heo Wi was captured in the middle of 1908 and executed in October; Yi In-yeong was captured in 1909 and executed.

The proposed advance on Seoul exposed the problems of coalition warfare. Forces from different provinces gathered under leaders who shared an immediate target but differed in status, military experience, and local loyalties. To coordinate an attack, commanders needed agreement on timing, routes, ammunition distribution, and the command that would direct the vanguard. Japanese surveillance and the need to supply a substantial force near the capital made secrecy difficult. The failure should therefore not be explained merely as a single decision by one commander. Even a more unified command would have confronted fortified positions, rail-borne Japanese reinforcements, and a severe shortage of modern ammunition.

The coalition also reveals a social limit within an expanding movement. Prominent units associated with commoner commanders were not necessarily treated as equal partners by scholar-official leaders who retained traditional status assumptions. Shin Dol-seok's experience is an example of the tension between a broadened recruiting base and older hierarchies of command. This does not justify describing all scholars as exclusionary or all commoners as a unified democratic faction; it does show that resistance to foreign control did not automatically erase domestic divisions. Students can use the attempted advance to assess the relationship between military coordination, legitimacy, and social composition.

Section 54.30

Regional Campaigns, Local Leadership, and Civilian Participation

The movement was strongly regional. In Gangwon and Gyeonggi, mountainous terrain near the capital supported groups that harassed Japanese communications. In Chungcheong, scholars and former officers led several units. In the northern provinces, including Pyeongan and Hamgyeong, hunters and mountain people were prominent; the hunter Hong Beom-do, active in the northeast, became widely known. Shin Dol-seok continued operations in the Taebaek region until his death in 1908.

The strongest and longest-lasting resistance was in Honam, the southwest (Jeolla provinces). Leaders such as Jeon Hae-san, An Gyu-hong, and Sim Nam-il organized units that operated in the hills, along the coast, and in rural market districts. Yi Gang-nyeon, by contrast, fought chiefly in Gangwon, Chungcheong, and northern Gyeongsang, not as a Honam commander. Honam combined dense agricultural population, rough upland areas, a tradition of local opposition, and a sizeable rural elite, and it was less accessible to Japanese forces than the corridor from Seoul to Pusan. The details of individual leaders' careers and fates vary across sources and should be consulted in specialized studies.

Civilian participation took many forms. Villagers gave information, food, and cover; some joined for a season and returned to their fields; women supplied food and carried messages; and in many places communities were divided about whether to support the fighters or to avoid reprisal. The availability of weapons and the presence of trained men determined where groups could sustain themselves. These regional patterns show that resistance depended as much on local conditions and social relationships as on any central direction.

Regional geography must be used precisely. Yi Gang-nyeon fought chiefly in the central and eastern regions—around Jecheon, Danyang, the Gangwon mountains, and northern Gyeongsang—rather than as a commander of the Honam movement. His capture and execution in 1908 illustrate the pressure on these mountain networks. Shin Dol-seok's operations were associated with Yeonghae and adjoining eastern districts; Hong Beom-do organized hunters and other supporters in the northern frontier zone. By contrast, An Gyu-hong's unit arose in Boseong in Jeolla, where his background as a farm laborer demonstrates the movement's reach beyond prestigious literati families. Jeon Hae-san belongs among the Honam commanders. Naming each leader in the right region matters because tactics and local alliances cannot be understood if every commander is placed in the southwest.

Honam's prolonged fighting also had a distinctive social economy. Coastal mobility and inland wooded hills offered places for a unit to withdraw, but farms and market towns still had to supply food. Local informants could guide an army away from a patrol or disclose its position to the gendarmerie. Japanese military pressure increasingly targeted the relationships connecting fighters to household support, rather than only confronting armed groups in open battle. The eventual destruction of the regional networks is therefore inseparable from the coercion experienced by civilian communities. Local histories and court records can recover individual careers, but they must be assessed alongside the interested categories used in Japanese operational reports.

Section 54.31

Japanese Counterinsurgency and the 1909 Southern Suppression Campaign

Japan responded with military and police operations. The Korea Garrison Army, reinforced in 1907–1908, conducted sweeps in the affected provinces, supported by the gendarmerie, which was expanded and used intelligence networks, informants, and Korean auxiliaries. Japanese forces burned villages suspected of sheltering fighters, seized property, arrested family members, and executed captured leaders. Surrender offers and amnesties were used alongside force.

The most systematic effort was the operation conducted in Honam in September and October 1909, known in Japanese records as the South Korea Pacification Operation (in Korean usually rendered as the great southern suppression). Japanese units, with naval and gendarmerie support, divided the region into sectors and swept through it, closing escape routes by land and sea. Japanese reports claimed that several hundred fighters were killed and that over a thousand were captured or surrendered, and that the operation broke the organized resistance in the region. Korean sources emphasize the destruction of villages and the civilian suffering.

Statistics must be treated with care. Japanese military and gendarmerie compilations, widely cited, reported on the order of 2,800 engagements and roughly 140,000 participants in the righteous-army movement between August 1907 and June 1911, with substantial losses that vary by category and compilation; different compilations cover different years, define participants and engagements differently, and sometimes count civilians as combatants. These records were made by the force doing the suppression, which had an interest in demonstrating success and in classifying opponents as bandits. They offer a measure of the scale but not a reliable census, and Korean estimates, based on different definitions, have not resolved the discrepancies.

The surviving records of the southern suppression campaign should be described with explicit geographical and temporal limits. One report covering the South Jeolla area from late August to October 1909 gives about 420 deaths among identified righteous-army members and approximately 1,687 people arrested or surrendering. Another Korean historical synthesis cites 103 leaders and 4,138 fighters killed or captured across a differently framed campaign. These figures cannot be added together: their regions, dates, categories, and reporting authorities differ. Japanese reports also sometimes classified local supporters as combatants, creating uncertainty about who was included. The magnitude of repression is clear, but an exact single national death toll is not established by these returns.

The tactical logic was to separate irregular fighters from the villages and terrain that sustained them. Military columns and gendarmerie detachments converged on designated areas, closed routes of retreat, interrogated residents, and searched households for arms, food stores, and suspected associates. Such methods could reduce the ability of insurgents to re-form without requiring a decisive battle against every remaining unit. They also imposed penalties on households that had supplied fighters, been forced to supply them, or simply lived in a suspected district. The campaign's documented results should therefore be analyzed as evidence of both military suppression and a wider system of civilian coercion.

Section 54.32

The Human Cost of Resistance and the Movement beyond Korea's Borders

The costs fell heavily on farming households. Supporting an armed group meant giving up food and shelter that families could not spare, and being suspected of doing so exposed them to reprisal. Villages were burned, men were arrested or killed, and families were displaced. Women and children bore the burden of supporting households, fleeing, or surviving in destroyed communities, and some were themselves arrested. The effects on the countryside, in lost harvests, population movement, and social division, lasted beyond the fighting.

As Japanese pressure intensified, surviving fighters and leaders increasingly crossed the northern borders into Manchuria (the Jiandao area) and the Russian Maritime Province, where Korean communities had grown through earlier migration. Figures such as Yi Beom-yun, Choe Jae-hyeong, and Yu In-seok became associated with armed efforts in the Maritime Province, and An Jung-geun was linked to these networks. These communities offered sanctuary, funds, and recruits. The later development of independence armies in these regions is the subject of Chapter 59; here it is enough to note that the end of large-scale resistance within Korea did not end armed opposition but shifted its geography.

The civilian experience can be reconstructed through a combination of official punitive reports, private diaries, claims about destroyed property, and later local testimony. No single genre provides an adequate count. A military commander might record rifles seized or men described as bandits killed, yet never list the number of households unable to plant the following crop. A family might remember a burned storehouse or an arrest without knowing which unit ordered it. The historian should place these sources alongside one another rather than treating the surviving numerical record as a complete representation of suffering.

Migration was not an instantaneous, uniform response. Some fighters crossed the Yalu or Tumen, while others moved within Korea, entered ordinary occupations, or surrendered. The borderlands already contained Korean farming and trading communities formed during earlier migrations, so newly arriving activists encountered institutions and networks that predated the 1907–1909 crisis. Resources were uncertain: support might come from relatives, local merchants, religious groups, or émigré organizers, but these communities also lived under Russian and Chinese authorities with interests of their own. The movement's geographical shift thus created new possibilities and new constraints. Keeping that distinction permits Chapter 54 to explain the origin of later cross-border resistance without preempting Chapter 59's treatment of the independent armed formations that followed.

Division VII · Sections 54.33–54.37

Protectorate Administration, Economic Control, and Competing Political Responses

Section 54.33

The Residency-General, Japanese Advisers, and Administrative Penetration

The Residency-General expanded from a diplomatic office into a governing structure. Beginning in 1906 it created its own departments and residency offices across the country, and from 1907, under the July agreement, Japanese vice-ministers and officials entered Korean ministries and provincial administration. The numbers of Japanese officials in the Korean government grew from a handful of advisers to several thousand by 1910, though exact figures vary. Japanese police, including the gendarmerie, took on functions across Korea.

Korean ministers continued to hold office, and some, including Yi Wan-yong, worked closely with the Resident-General, but real authority in ministries lay increasingly with Japanese officials. Provincial governors remained Korean, with Japanese advisers attached. It is important to distinguish this protectorate administration from the colonial Government-General that would follow annexation: under the protectorate, Korean institutions continued formally to exist and the Resident-General's authority rested on agreements; the Government-General abolished the Korean state and governed directly, as discussed in Chapter 55.

Protectorate administration can be examined at several levels. In Seoul, the Resident-General's office dealt with central ministers and regulated appointments, legislation, and major policy. Regional residency offices monitored local decisions and transmitted instructions. Japanese officials appointed within Korean departments handled specialized tasks such as revenue accounting, policing, judicial procedure, and transportation. Korean officeholders continued to produce paperwork, and residents might still approach a Korean magistrate with a tax or property dispute. But the official's effective discretion was increasingly circumscribed by the adviser or superior authority who had to approve the result.

The expansion of this apparatus also altered the nature of political responsibility. A Korean minister could be blamed publicly for a policy that Japanese officials had compelled, while Japanese authorities could describe the same decision as an act of the Korean government. This dual structure made foreign control less visible in the formal wording of decrees than in the administrative chain that produced them. It depended on interpreters, clerks, and minor officials as much as on senior policymakers. Distinguishing administrative subordination from direct colonial rule is essential: Korea remained formally separate under the protectorate, but the institution retained in name was increasingly deprived of independent authority.

Section 54.34

Finance, Railways, Communications, Courts, and the Transfer of Practical Authority

Control of finance came first. Megata's 1905 currency reform and the designation of the Japanese Dai-ichi Bank as the government's treasury agent placed revenue and currency under Japanese influence, and loans from Japan increased Korea's indebtedness. A central bank, the Bank of Korea, was established in 1909 and took over many of these functions. In April 1905 an agreement transferred Korea's postal, telegraph, and telephone services to Japanese administration, and the Seoul–Pusan and Seoul–Sinuiju railways, built or financed under Japanese military and commercial direction, came under Japanese control.

Judicial and police authority followed. In July 1909 a memorandum entrusted Korea's judicial and prison administration to Japan, and in June 1910, on the eve of annexation, police administration was also transferred. Japanese judges and prosecutors entered the courts, and Korean courts increasingly applied rules set by the Residency-General. By 1910 the Korean government had lost control of revenue, communications, transport, courts, police, and the army.

It is important to maintain a distinction here. The railways, telegraphs, banks, and courts represented genuine modernization, and some Koreans benefited from new employment and services. But the question of who owned and directed these institutions mattered: the infrastructure was designed to serve Japanese military and economic purposes, and the Korean government's capacity to make independent policy disappeared as it lost control of them. Economic modernization and loss of sovereign control proceeded together.

The movement of financial power can be traced through a concrete sequence. Adviser supervision affected budgets and borrowing, currency reform altered the relationship between Korean commerce and Japanese banking, and new controls over revenue and contracts narrowed the state's ability to pay for independent projects. Railway and telegraph networks were assets for trade, but they were also military instruments, enabling Japanese forces to concentrate, communicate, and supply isolated garrisons. Transfer of control over these systems thus reduced the Korean government's freedom both to govern and to resist.

Judicial and police changes followed their own chronology. Japanese influence expanded through advisers, inspectors, and gendarmerie before being formalized in agreements that transferred additional authority in 1909 and 1910. A Korean court could continue to hear cases while the qualifications of its judges, appointment of officials, and execution of decisions came increasingly under Japanese supervision. Students should see that sovereignty consists of routine administrative capacities as well as spectacular diplomatic acts. By the eve of annexation, the Korean state possessed the external symbols of a government but could no longer securely command many of the institutions through which government acts.

Section 54.35

The National Debt Repayment Movement and Patriotic Economic Mobilization

In early 1907 a civic campaign began in Daegu, led by Kim Gwang-je, a publisher, and Seo Sang-don, a merchant. Korea owed a large debt to Japan, commonly given as about thirteen million won, and the campaigners argued that repaying it would remove a pretext for Japanese control and demonstrate national solidarity. They proposed that Koreans give up smoking for several months and donate the savings, and that people contribute directly.

The movement spread quickly. Newspapers, especially the Daehan Maeil Sinbo, publicized it and collected donations, and associations and local groups organized fundraising across the country. Women played a prominent part, with women's groups forming and donating rings, hairpins, and savings; this participation was notable for its public character. Students, merchants, officials, and rural people contributed small sums.

The financial results were small in relation to the debt, and the movement was constrained by Japanese suspicion; Yang Gi-tak was arrested in 1908 on charges relating to the handling of donated funds and was later released. Its political significance exceeded its money. It showed that large numbers of Koreans were willing to make collective sacrifices for sovereignty, it linked urban and rural populations, and it expressed a distinct conception of resistance through economic nationalism. It also demonstrates that the loss of financial independence was widely understood as the loss of sovereignty.

The National Debt Repayment Movement shows how economic action became a form of political participation. Its organizers argued that a national financial burden could be met through collective contributions rather than dependence on foreign creditors and the policy choices those creditors might demand. Merchants sponsored appeals, newspaper editors publicized subscriptions, and residents who could not contribute large sums offered smaller amounts. The movement's prominent recommendations to reduce tobacco consumption and personal expenditure made the problem accessible to households outside official circles. Women formed groups and raised money through savings and the sale or donation of valuables, demonstrating that civic action was not restricted to enfranchised men or officeholders.

The financial results should not be confused with the political significance. Contributions were too small relative to the state's debt and the Japanese control of the financial apparatus to restore Korean autonomy. Fundraising also encountered disputes over management and surveillance of organizers. Yet the campaign provided practical experience in voluntary association, public accounting, and national fundraising. It showed that people could act collectively in the name of a national community when the state itself could not exercise independent policy. The chapter should place this movement alongside education, newspapers, and armed resistance as a different, but historically serious, attempt to preserve sovereignty.

Section 54.36

Iljinhoe, Collaboration, Accommodation, and Competing Visions of Korea's Future

Not all Koreans opposed Japanese policy. The Iljinhoe, founded in 1904 by Song Byeong-jun and Yi Yong-gu, supported Japan during the war and later advocated close association with Japan. Yi Yong-gu had been associated with the Donghak movement (Chapter 46), and the Iljinhoe drew many members from followers of that tradition and from other groups. Many members were drawn by the prospect of protection, status, or employment; some held genuine beliefs that Korea needed Japanese tutelage to modernize. Japanese advisers and nationalist figures supported and funded the society.

Donghak's leadership under Son Byeong-hui renamed the movement Cheondogyo in 1905 and expelled Iljinhoe leaders in the following period, so it would be wrong to portray Donghak or Cheondogyo adherents as supporters of annexation. In December 1909 the Iljinhoe publicly issued a petition calling for the merger of Korea and Japan, which met widespread condemnation; the attempted assassination of Yi Wan-yong in December 1909 by Yi Jae-myeong shows the hostility the issue provoked.

Analytically, it is useful to distinguish several categories: coerced accommodation, in which people complied from fear or necessity; careerism, in which individuals sought personal advancement; ideological support, in which some believed that Japanese guidance was beneficial; and organized collaboration, in which societies and officials actively promoted Japanese aims. These categories overlap, and motives are often difficult to establish from the surviving evidence.

The Iljinhoe was not simply a group that followed Japanese instructions without internal politics. Its leaders justified cooperation using arguments about order, reform, protection, and Korea's future. Some drew on networks associated with religious and social movements; others saw opportunities in the offices, resources, and influence available through the protectorate. The association's public endorsement of annexation gave Japanese proponents evidence they could present as Korean support, but its membership and leadership did not represent a freely consulted national electorate. Nor did its claims cancel the protests of Koreans who opposed annexation.

Analysis of collaboration requires attention to different degrees of agency. A minister who promoted the annexation settlement, a clerk who retained a job in a supervised department, and a villager who provided food to a passing patrol did not necessarily share motives or enjoy equivalent choices. Some conduct involved ideological support, some strategic calculation, and some fear. Historians should avoid both the presumption that all cooperation was voluntary and the equally untenable claim that coercion erased responsibility among officials who actively supported the removal of Korean sovereignty. The same distinctions help students understand why the language of “collaboration” became politically charged in later national memory.

Section 54.37

Everyday Life under the Protectorate, 1905–1910

For residents of Seoul, the protectorate meant the presence of Japanese soldiers and police, new regulations, and increased Japanese settlement. Merchants in the treaty ports faced both opportunities and competition from Japanese traders with preferential access to credit and customs; the 1905 currency reform hurt many Korean merchants. Railway workers and laborers found employment but under harsh conditions and often under Japanese supervisors. Students and teachers in private schools worked under growing regulation, and newspapers faced censorship.

In the countryside, farmers experienced taxation and land policies, requisitions during the war, and, in areas of resistance, violence from both sides. Uncertainty was a pervasive condition: people did not know whether the emperor retained any authority, whether the protectorate was permanent, or what annexation might bring, though rumors of it circulated. Differences of class, region, and occupation produced different experiences, and some Koreans did benefit from new opportunities.

The experience of a household depended heavily on place and occupation. A family in the capital might encounter Japanese troops at a railway station, new police regulation of meetings, and newspaper discussion of changing treaties. A farming household near a railway line might instead experience land requisition or the loss of carts and labor to military transport. A teacher could continue presenting lessons but face restrictions on textbooks and associations. A merchant might gain customers from increased movement through a port while finding that access to bank credit, contracts, or currency exchange favored Japanese competitors. These contrasts reveal why the visible growth of infrastructure cannot by itself be used to claim equal economic benefit.

Women and children appear less often in treaty and military histories, but they were central to the costs of both resistance and accommodation. When an adult was arrested or joined a righteous-army unit, others supplied the household's labor, maintained trade or farming, or sought relatives' protection. In districts subject to punitive searches, the distinction between a fighter and a supporter could be imposed by an armed patrol, not established through an impartial hearing. Communities also contained disagreement: one family might contribute to resistance while a neighbor feared retaliation or expected employment through Japanese enterprises. The range of these choices should be reconstructed without inventing representative testimony for people whose voices are not directly preserved.

In 1904 its men may have been called for labor on the railway and its grain for military supply. In 1905 a village near a garrison might have lost fields to a military site. Section 54.38

An Jung-geun and the Assassination of Itō Hirobumi at Harbin

An Jung-geun was born in 1879 in Hwanghae province and educated in the Confucian tradition and in the Catholic faith, which he adopted as a young man and in which he took the baptismal name Thomas. After 1905 he engaged in educational work, helping to run schools in Pyeongan province, and then moved to the Russian Maritime Province in 1907, where he joined the armed resistance networks active among Korean communities. He took part in a cross-border raid into Hamgyeong province in 1908, which ended in defeat, and in early 1909 he and several comrades pledged themselves to the cause in a ceremony in which they cut off a finger.

On 26 October 1909, An shot and fatally wounded Itō Hirobumi on the platform of the railway station at Harbin, where Itō had arrived for talks with the Russian finance minister. An also wounded several members of Itō's party. He did not attempt to flee and was arrested by Russian officials and turned over to the Japanese. Itō died within a short time. It is important to note that Itō was by then a former Resident-General: he had resigned the office in June 1909 and been succeeded by Sone Arasuke, though he remained a senior statesman as president of the Privy Council.

An was tried at the Japanese court at Lüshun (Port Arthur) in February 1910 and sentenced to death. He was executed on 26 March 1910. At his trial and in his statements he declared that he acted as a Korean officer of the righteous army and asked to be treated as a prisoner of war, and he listed grounds for his act that included the Eulsa agreement, the removal of Gojong, and the killing of Koreans. While in prison he began writing On Peace in East Asia, an unfinished work proposing cooperation among Korea, China, and Japan, including joint institutions; Japanese authorities did not allow him to complete it. His stated objective was to stop what he regarded as Japanese aggression and to restore Korean independence in a framework of regional peace.

Later national memory has made An a major figure in both Koreas and a controversial one in Japan, where views of Itō and of the act differ. Historians distinguish An's own stated aims from the symbolic roles later assigned to him, and note that Itō's own views on annexation were more complicated than the later narratives of him as its architect suggest, a subject taken up in the next section.

An Jung-geun's political identity cannot be explained solely by the single moment at Harbin. His earlier work included education, organization among Korean communities beyond the peninsula, and involvement with armed resistance. His claim to have acted as an officer of a Korean independence force mattered because he sought to frame the shooting as an act in an international political conflict, not as an ordinary personal murder. Japanese judicial authorities rejected that characterization, and the resulting trial record is simultaneously evidence of his own political arguments and a record produced by the state prosecuting him.

His views on East Asian peace also complicate the assumption that opposition to Japanese empire required opposition to all regional cooperation. He argued for an East Asian order that could resist Western imperial power but did not accept Japanese domination of Korea as the means of achieving it. The unfinished character of his writings imposes limits on claims about a fully developed program. The historian should distinguish what survives in his statements from ideas later attributed to him. Itō's assassination had a powerful symbolic effect, but the July 1909 Japanese policy decision makes clear that annexation planning preceded the event. The killing cannot serve as a sufficient causal explanation for Japan's decision to abolish Korea's independent statehood.

Section 54.39

Japan's Decision to Annex Korea and the Politics of 1909–1910

Japanese policy on eventual annexation had been developing before Harbin. Itō had favored indirect control through the protectorate and had been wary of the costs and risks of absorbing Korea, though he did not rule out annexation in principle at a future time. Others, including Prime Minister Katsura, Foreign Minister Komura, and the army, pressed for a firmer commitment. In July 1909 the cabinet adopted a policy, approved by the emperor, that annexation of Korea should be carried out at a suitable time, and it began to prepare. Itō had resigned as Resident-General in June 1909 and was succeeded by Sone Arasuke. Historians debate how far Itō's death altered the timing; most see it as a factor that strengthened advocates of annexation rather than as the cause of the decision, which was already in motion.

The international setting was favorable. Britain, the United States, and Russia had acknowledged Japanese predominance, and further understandings with Russia in 1907 and 1910 and an agreement with China in 1909 over the Jiandao area reduced the risk of external objection. Domestically in Korea, the main armed resistance had been largely suppressed by late 1909. In May 1910 Terauchi Masatake, the army minister, was appointed Resident-General, and he arrived in Seoul in July with the task of completing the process.

Korean political actors responded in different ways. Yi Wan-yong and his cabinet collaborated with the planning, while the Iljinhoe's petition provided a pretext of Korean demand. Many other Koreans opposed annexation, and Japanese authorities restricted public assembly and the press and, in advance of the August 1910 announcement, sharply restricted public meetings and reporting. The decisive point is that annexation was not the expression of free Korean consent but a Japanese decision implemented through a cooperating cabinet and under heavy surveillance and the presence of troops. Opposition within Japan was limited, though some commentators and politicians questioned the cost.

The July 1909 annexation policy demonstrates the difference between a strategic objective and the timing of its execution. Japanese cabinet members could agree that Korea should ultimately be annexed while differing over when public announcement would be least costly. They had to consider international reactions, the suppression of armed resistance, the continued presence of a Korean imperial household, and whether Korean ministers could be persuaded or compelled to cooperate. The months before August 1910 were therefore a period of implementation planning rather than an unbroken improvisation after Harbin.

The Japanese government also sought appearances of procedural continuity. A treaty signed by a Korean prime minister and a Japanese resident-general would permit Japan to present annexation as a transfer of sovereign rights rather than a unilateral military proclamation. Korean administrative offices still existed and could be instructed to transmit the arrangement, but they operated under the protectorate's coercive structure. The Iljinhoe's advocacy could be publicized while opposing newspapers and associations faced constraints. These are the conditions that made “Korean approval” a contested historical claim. Governmental procedure mattered politically, but the existence of procedure did not prove that the country's population had freely consented to annexation.

Section 54.40

August 1910: The Annexation Treaty and the Extinction of Korean Sovereignty

Terauchi presented a draft treaty to Yi Wan-yong in mid-August 1910. After a cabinet discussion and a meeting at the palace, in which Sunjong was pressed to authorize the arrangement, the treaty was signed on 22 August by Terauchi and Yi Wan-yong, and its existence was kept secret for a week. Later accounts sometimes claim that Sunjong’s consort tried to conceal an imperial seal, but such an anecdote cannot establish the legal history of the treaty. The surviving royal edict and its seals are a firmer documentary basis for examining the disputed authorization process. Seoul was filled with troops and police in the interval, and public meetings were banned. The treaty was promulgated on 29 August.

In summary, the treaty's articles provided that the Korean emperor completely and permanently ceded all rights of sovereignty over Korea to the Japanese emperor, and that the Japanese emperor accepted the cession and agreed to the annexation. The former Korean imperial family was to receive titles of honor and annual stipends, and a number of Koreans were to be rewarded with titles and payments. The name Korea was replaced by Joseon in Japanese usage and Seoul renamed Gyeongseong, and Terauchi became the first Governor-General when the Government-General was established on 1 October.

The distinction between the treaty's formal legal presentation and the political reality is fundamental. The text presents a voluntary cession by an emperor to another emperor. The surrounding facts include the military presence, the prior removal of Gojong, the dissolution of the army, the transfer of police power, the silencing of the press and associations, and the secrecy of the signing. Great powers raised no objection, and notified governments accepted the annexation. Hwang Hyeon, the scholar-official and chronicler, took his own life in September 1910 after writing that the country had been lost, among a number of such suicides.

The final legal sequence needs to be separated into cabinet deliberation, signing, concealment, and promulgation. Japanese and Korean officials discussed the draft in August, the instrument was signed on August 22, and it was publicly promulgated on August 29. The Korean imperial edict associated with promulgation has itself become an object of legal and historical study: researchers have pointed to differences between the seals appearing on Korean and Japanese instruments and have debated the implications for required royal authorization. These documentary issues are more useful to students than an unverified story that Sunjong's consort concealed the seal. The existence of a popular anecdote may be noted as memory, but it cannot substitute for analysis of surviving papers.

The act of promulgation also created a new administrative reality. Japanese authorities transferred power to a Government-General whose organization is the opening subject of Chapter 55. Former Korean officials, soldiers, and residents faced different immediate consequences, and the ending of the empire did not erase the social networks, legal claims, or memories through which independence continued to be asserted. A careful chapter should describe the loss of sovereignty precisely without prematurely narrating the following decades of colonial policy. The decisive question at this point is how a treaty framed as voluntary cession acquired force under a system already controlled by the recipient power.

Historical Vocabulary
Sovereignty, Coercion, Ratification, Promulgation, and International Recognition

Sovereignty is the supreme authority of a state over its territory and affairs. Coercion is the use of force or threat to compel action; in treaty law it concerns pressure on a state or its representatives. Ratification is a state's formal confirmation of a treaty after signature. Promulgation is the public announcement that makes a law or treaty effective. International recognition is acceptance by other states of a political arrangement.

Why it matters: Each term identifies a different question about 1905–1910: whether Korea freely agreed, whether the proper authority approved, whether the act was announced, and whether other powers accepted it. Potential misunderstanding: Recognition by other powers is not the same as consent by the state concerned.

Section 54.41

The End of the Korean Empire: Loss of Sovereignty, Historical Interpretation, and Continuity

Between 1904 and 1910 the Korean state lost its functions in sequence. In 1904 it lost control of its territory for military purposes and of the use of its ports and roads. In 1904–1905 it lost control of finance and the choice of advisers. In 1905 it lost diplomatic sovereignty. In 1907 it lost the emperor in his existing form, domestic administrative autonomy, and the army. In 1909–1910 it lost the judiciary and the police, and in August 1910 it lost its existence as a state. Each stage rested on a different instrument, and each involved distinct acts of coercion and cooperation.

Resistance failed to prevent this for reasons that combined organization and power. Diplomatic appeals met powers that had accepted Japanese predominance; public protest and enlightenment work were long-term strategies that could not alter immediate military facts; armed resistance, though extensive, was fragmented and outgunned; and a cooperating minority gave Japan the Korean signatures it needed. Yet resistance did impose costs, forced adjustments, and created traditions.

What survived annexation was substantial. Korean society continued, with its families, villages, schools, religious communities, and press traditions. Networks of educators and associations, memories of the righteous armies, the example of An Jung-geun, and the idea that the 1905–1910 agreements were illegitimate persisted and fed the independence movement of the next decades. The Provisional Government of 1919 (Chapter 56) would assert continuity with the Korean Empire. The legacy of the period also remains disputed in contemporary diplomacy, including over the validity of the treaties.

The loss of sovereign functions was not distributed evenly over six years. A Korean diplomat lost practical authority in 1905 when the foreign ministry's functions were transferred; a soldier could still serve in an imperial uniform until the dissolution of 1907; a local clerk might continue handling documents bearing a Korean office's name even as a Japanese adviser acquired effective control. A farmer might first recognize the new regime through requisition, taxation, policing, or repression rather than through a treaty announced in Seoul. This chronology of experience helps students understand why the final annexation was simultaneously a formal rupture and the culmination of changes already underway.

Historical interpretation must preserve both resistance and contingency. Korean diplomacy did not fail because sovereignty was merely symbolic, and armed resistance did not fail because the fighters lacked conviction. Japan possessed advantages in military organization, international recognition, finance, and control of transport that Korean opponents could not overcome separately. Yet decisions about cooperation, suppression, diplomatic protest, and the pace of annexation remained decisions. The surviving institutions and networks would later supply resources for independence movements, but those later outcomes should not make the actors of 1904–1910 appear to have known the future. Chapter 54's final lesson is how the destruction of statehood occurred through a series of contested measures, not through historical inevitability.

Section 54.42 · Review

Chapter Review and Historical Thinking Checkpoint

Chapter Summary

This chapter has explained how a Korean Empire proclaimed in 1897 became a Japanese possession in 1910. Its central argument is that the transformation was progressive and contested: Japan's victory over Russia gave it military leverage and international acceptance, but Korean sovereignty was removed through a sequence of separate decisions and instruments, each involving coercion, Korean intermediaries, and great-power acquiescence.

The Russo-Japanese War grew from rivalry over Manchuria and Korea. Gojong's January 1904 neutrality declaration could not restrain a Japanese army that landed at Chemulpo and occupied Seoul in February. The Japan–Korea Protocol of 23 February 1904 gave Japan wartime access and a claim to advise Korean government, and the agreement of 22 August 1904 placed Japanese-recommended advisers in finance and diplomacy.

The war's campaigns, from the Yalu and Port Arthur to Liaoyang, Mukden, and Tsushima, established Japan's military and naval superiority while straining its resources. Korea served as a staging ground and rear area, with consequences for communities subjected to requisition and labor demands. Victory at Tsushima made it possible for Roosevelt to mediate the Treaty of Portsmouth, in which Russia recognized Japan's predominant interests in Korea.

International recognition of Japan's position, through the Taft–Katsura memorandum, the revised Anglo-Japanese Alliance, and Portsmouth, was distinct from Korean consent. The Eulsa Treaty of November 1905 was concluded under military deployment, without Gojong's approval, and transferred Korean foreign relations to Japan. The Residency-General under Itō Hirobumi put the protectorate into effect, retaining Korean institutions while extending Japanese influence.

Koreans responded in several ways. Gojong appealed secretly to foreign governments; officials sent memorials and some took their own lives; newspapers, schools, and associations built the patriotic enlightenment movement; and scholars such as Choe Ik-hyeon and Min Jong-sik raised the Eulsa righteous armies. These efforts did not restore independence, owing to organizational weakness and overwhelming asymmetry of power.

The crisis of 1907 compressed the destruction of Korean autonomy. The Hague mission failed diplomatically but provoked the forced removal of Gojong. The agreement of 24 July extended Japanese control to domestic government, and the dissolution of the army on 1 August triggered fighting in Seoul and a wider wave of armed resistance. Disbanded soldiers transformed the righteous armies into a larger and more socially diverse movement, which Japan suppressed through counterinsurgency, culminating in the 1909 southern campaign. Japanese statistics on these operations are valuable but must be read critically.

The protectorate years also saw administrative penetration, control of finance, railways, communications, courts, and police, the National Debt Repayment Movement, and organized collaboration by groups such as the Iljinhoe. Collaboration, accommodation, and coercion must be distinguished rather than assumed. An Jung-geun's assassination of the former Resident-General Itō in October 1909 entered a Japanese decision to annex that had been adopted in principle in July.

The annexation treaty was signed on 22 August 1910 and promulgated on 29 August, presenting as voluntary cession what the surrounding record shows to have been the end of a coerced sequence. The historical debates over its validity continue. What survived was Korean society, networks of resistance, and a lasting claim of illegitimacy, which shaped the independence movement and which Chapter 55 continues in its account of colonial rule.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it explains how a sovereign state was removed, step by step, in a period when the rules of the international system allowed stronger powers to settle the fate of weaker ones among themselves. The sequence of 1904 to 1910 shows how military force, diplomatic recognition, financial control, and local intermediaries combined, and why no single act can account for the outcome.

It also shows that Korean people responded in ways that deserve to be studied on their own terms. Emperors, officials, scholars, soldiers, journalists, teachers, merchants, women's groups, and farmers acted on different assumptions and with unequal means. Their failure to prevent annexation does not make their choices unimportant; it makes them the foundation of what followed.

The period shaped later developments in several directions. It supplied the grievances, institutions, and memories on which the March First Movement and independence organizations drew (Chapters 56 and 59). It produced a continuing dispute over the legal status of the treaties that has affected relations between Korea and Japan into the present. And it raised questions of collaboration and responsibility that both Korean states would address in their own ways.

Within this textbook, the chapter closes Part IX and the long crisis of sovereignty that began with the opening of 1876. Chapter 55 begins from the point at which the Korean Empire no longer existed, and examines how a colonial state was built on the ground that this chapter has described.
