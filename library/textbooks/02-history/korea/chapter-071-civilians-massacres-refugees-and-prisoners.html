Civilians, Massacres, Refugees, and Prisoners

Korean History: Ancient Kingdoms to a Divided Peninsula

Chapter Opening

In the early hours of June 28, 1950, South Korean engineers destroyed the Han River bridges at Seoul while civilians, soldiers, and vehicles were still crossing them. The explosion cut off the army's own retreat, killed people on the spans, and left hundreds of thousands of residents on the northern bank. Within days the same population would be sorted by a new occupying authority; within months it would be sorted again by the returning one. The question this chapter asks is what the war looked like from the position of the people who had no control over any of these decisions.

The preceding three chapters followed the Korean War as a sequence of campaigns: the invasion and the defense of the Pusan Perimeter (Chapter 68), the Incheon landing and the advance toward the Yalu (Chapter 69), and the Chinese intervention and the long war of attrition that followed (Chapter 70). Those chapters necessarily described the war through armies, commanders, and fronts. This chapter changes the vantage point. It asks what the same events meant for the farmers, clerks, teachers, fishermen, merchants, schoolchildren, and mothers who lived in the path of the fighting, and it asks what happened to the men and women who were captured. The chronology is the same, but the historical subject is different: the Korean population itself.

The scale of what happened to that population can be stated only with caution. Korea in 1950 was densely settled, and its people were not spectators to a contest between armies. Roughly twenty million people lived in the south and perhaps nine to ten million in the north. The front line crossed the peninsula several times in twelve months, so that many communities lived under two, three, or even four different authorities in succession. Most scholarly estimates place total Korean deaths in the war, civilian and military combined, in the range of two to three million, with civilians forming a majority, but these figures rest on incomplete registration, contested definitions, and, in the case of political killings, decades of suppression. This chapter therefore does not adopt a single national death toll. It attributes each figure to the investigation, official body, or scholar that produced it, and it explains what that figure does and does not count.

Violence against civilians in the Korean War had several distinct sources, and the chapter insists on keeping them distinct. South Korean police, military police, and army units executed detainees, including people who had never been charged with any offense. American forces killed refugees in at least one documented incident and, more broadly, adopted policies toward refugee movement that endangered civilians. North Korean authorities and local allies arrested and killed officials, police, landowners, and political opponents during the 1950 occupation and retreat. Armed local organizations of both political persuasions settled scores in villages where hostilities predated the war. Bombing, artillery, and urban fighting killed and displaced people on both sides of the front. These were different acts with different perpetrators, different chains of command, and different evidentiary records, and treating them as interchangeable would erase the very distinctions that make accountability and understanding possible.

The evidence also differs enormously from one topic to another. Some of the most important civilian histories were buried for decades. In South Korea, anti-communist governments suppressed discussion of the killings of detained leftists and of civilians accused of collaboration, and survivors' families were often stigmatized. Only after democratization, and especially through the work of the Truth and Reconciliation Commission (2005–2010), did systematic investigation begin. In North Korea, state narratives of American atrocities were elevated into national memory while evidence of killings by local North Korean allies and authorities was excluded. American archives preserved material on refugee policy that became widely known only after journalists and historians returned to them at the end of the twentieth century. Historians of the war must therefore ask not only what happened, but also who produced each record, who was able to speak, and who was silenced.

The chapter is organized in five divisions. The first examines the successive refugee crises, from the flight from Seoul in June 1950 to the winter evacuations of 1950–1951 and the camps and relief efforts that followed. The second examines killings by South Korean state institutions and by American forces. The third examines the North Korean occupation and the local civil conflicts that it intensified, using the Sinchon massacre as a case study in the evaluation of competing atrocity narratives. The fourth turns to bombing, hunger, disease, and the social experience of women, children, and divided families. The fifth follows prisoners of war and civilian internees through capture, the camps on Geoje Island, and the contest over repatriation that culminated in Syngman Rhee's release of prisoners in June 1953. The fifth division ends with a comprehensive review that returns to the problems of evidence and memory.

The chapter's central argument is that the Korean War was fought not only between organized armies but through competing projects of political control over the Korean population. Rapid changes in territorial control exposed civilians to repeated displacement, suspicion, detention, collective punishment, and violence. Security campaigns, occupation policies, local reprisals, bombardment, and the treatment of prisoners converted a military conflict into a social catastrophe whose consequences persisted long after the guns fell silent in July 1953. The questions of what happened, who was responsible, and how we know are the work of the sections that follow. Chapter 72 will take up the armistice negotiations and the prisoner-exchange agreements; this chapter explains what happened to prisoners and why their treatment became so controversial.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why the geography of a densely populated peninsula, rapid front-line movement, and rival claims to sovereignty made Korean civilians especially vulnerable.
  2. Trace the successive waves of refugee movement from June 1950 to 1951, distinguishing flight from bombing or occupation from the decisions of authorities to permit or prevent movement.
  3. Analyze the origins, operation, and documented consequences of South Korea's preventive detention system, including the National Guidance Alliance and the Bodo League killings.
  4. Reconstruct the No Gun Ri incident from documentary evidence and survivor testimony, distinguishing established killings from disputed casualty totals and command responsibility.
  5. Assess the character and variation of North Korean occupation policy and the killings during the occupation and retreat, using contemporaneous and retrospective evidence.
  6. Evaluate competing atrocity narratives, using Sinchon as a case study, and distinguish the occurrence of mass killing from the identification of perpetrators and the political uses of memory.
  7. Explain how political classification, denunciation, and alternating occupations turned local conflicts into cycles of reprisal.
  8. Describe the effects of bombing, shortages, disease, and the destruction of infrastructure on everyday life, and analyze the perspective of the bombed population as distinct from operational objectives.
  9. Interpret the experiences of women, children, and families, recognizing both suffering and agency.
  10. Compare the treatment of prisoners by North Korean, Chinese, and UN forces, and explain the conditions of captivity and the violence within the camps on Geoje Island.
  11. Analyze the dispute over voluntary repatriation, including the problem of coercion in the recording of prisoners' preferences, and the significance of Rhee's June 1953 release.
  12. Distinguish contemporary documents, official investigations, scholarly estimates, eyewitness testimony, and unverified allegations as categories of evidence about wartime violence.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why were civilians targeted by governments, security organizations, armies, and irregular forces in a war that is often described primarily in terms of military campaigns?
  2. How did repeated offensives and withdrawals create successive waves of refugees, and who had the means and permission to flee?
  3. How did political suspicion transform ordinary civilians into alleged collaborators, insurgents, or enemies of the state, and what role did class, family, and local rivalries play?
  4. How should historians weigh a retrospective truth commission finding against a contemporary document, a survivor's testimony, or an official state narrative?
  5. Why must responsibility for civilian killings be assessed incident by incident rather than attributed to "both sides" in general?
  6. How did bombing, shortages, disease, family separation, and forced mobilization affect everyday life, and how does that picture differ from the one visible in operational military histories?
  7. Why did prisoner-of-war policies, detention camps, and repatriation become humanitarian and political controversies, and what does it mean to say a prisoner's choice was free?
  8. Why did many massacres remain unacknowledged for decades, and how have later investigations changed public understanding?
  9. How did temporary wartime separations become permanent divisions of families and communities?

Historical Context

The civilian experience of the Korean War cannot be understood without the five years that preceded it. Liberation in August 1945 (Chapter 64) released political energies that had been suppressed under Japanese rule, and the occupation and division that followed (Chapters 65 and 66) organized those energies into rival camps. In the south, conflicts over land, tenancy, police personnel who had served the colonial government, and the future shape of the state produced strikes, uprisings, and a large-scale counterinsurgency. The Jeju uprising of 1948 and the Yeosu-Suncheon rebellion of that year left tens of thousands of civilians dead or displaced, established patterns of suspicion and collective punishment, and gave the new Republic of Korea a National Security Law (December 1948) under which membership in leftist organizations became a crime. In the north, land reform, nationalization, and the reorganization of society under the Korean Workers' Party redistributed property and created new categories of political reliability, while landlords, Christians, former officials, and others identified as hostile faced pressure, detention, or flight to the south. Perhaps several hundred thousand northerners had already moved south before June 1950.

By the eve of the war, then, both Korean states had built administrative apparatuses that classified residents by political reliability, and both contained populations that had reasons to fear the other. This is the essential background for the political violence of 1950. When the front line moved, local officials, police, and armed organizations did not encounter strangers; they encountered neighbors whose prior affiliations, family histories, and property they often knew in detail. The war's speed also mattered. The North Korean army captured Seoul in three days, reached the Nakdong River within weeks, and was thrown back across the peninsula in the autumn, so that the same villages could be occupied, liberated, and reoccupied within half a year.

Three features of the international setting shaped the humanitarian outcome. First, the United Nations Command, led by the United States, assumed responsibility for the military defense of South Korea and, with it, a set of obligations toward civilians that its commanders understood unevenly; the Geneva Conventions of 1949 existed but had not been ratified by the principal belligerents, and the United States and the United Nations Command asserted that they would follow their humanitarian principles. Second, the air power available to the United Nations Command was overwhelming and, from late 1950, largely unopposed over the north outside the northwest corner of the peninsula. Third, there was no neutral administrative machinery capable of protecting civilians in occupied territory, and the International Committee of the Red Cross found its access constrained by every belligerent government and especially by the northern side.

Finally, the evidentiary landscape is itself part of the historical context. Contemporary records on civilian matters are often fragmentary, and the more politically sensitive the subject, the more likely it is that records were destroyed or never created. In South Korea, wartime security forces rarely kept formal records of executions of detainees; later knowledge depends on survivor accounts, exhumations, and the archives of foreign observers and of the truth commissions. In North Korea, access to archives remains tightly restricted, and many records relevant to occupation policy survive mainly in captured documents taken by American forces in 1950 and later made available to scholars. These asymmetries explain why this chapter repeatedly asks what kind of source supports each claim.

Section 71.1

The Korean War as a Civilian Catastrophe: Death, Displacement, and Historical Evidence

Any account of civilian suffering in the Korean War begins with a problem of numbers that cannot be fully solved. Published estimates of total Korean deaths in the war vary widely, and most fall somewhere between two and three million people, but such totals combine very different populations: soldiers killed in battle, civilians killed by bombing and shelling, civilians killed in political executions, people who died of hunger and disease linked to displacement, and people who simply disappeared. Different studies count different categories, different time periods, and different geographic areas. South Korean government statistics compiled during and after the war, North Korean figures that are rarely documented, estimates based on postwar census comparisons, and reconstructions by individual scholars do not measure the same thing, and they cannot responsibly be added together or substituted for one another. This chapter therefore avoids adopting any single nationwide civilian death toll. Where a figure appears, the text states whether it represents confirmed victims, identified remains, an official estimate, a later scholarly reconstruction, or an unverified allegation.

The distinction between combatants and noncombatants is itself difficult to maintain in a civil war. A man conscripted into the North Korean army in occupied territory might be reported as a soldier by one authority and as a forced laborer by another. A farmer who cooked for soldiers, a clerk who kept his post under occupation, a teacher who taught the curriculum issued by the new authorities, or a villager who joined a local committee might be called a collaborator, a victim, or a combatant depending on the observer. Part of the argument of this chapter is that these labels were often products of the political struggle rather than neutral descriptions. The legal and moral category of civilian does not depend on political sympathy. Membership in an organization, or suspected sympathy with an enemy government, is different from direct participation in hostilities, and the historical record shows that many people who were killed in 1950 were killed for the former rather than the latter.

Counting the dead is further complicated by the state of Korean population records. The census system inherited from the colonial period and reorganized after liberation was incomplete, household registers (hojeok) were destroyed or lost in many localities, and the movement of millions of people made it impossible to say whether a person absent from a register was dead, missing, relocated, or abducted. Families sometimes did not report deaths because they feared political consequences, particularly when a relative had been killed as a suspected leftist. The distinction between wartime deaths and subsequent demographic loss is also blurred: infants and elderly people who died of hunger and disease in refugee shelters in 1951 were counted differently from those killed in an air raid, even though the cause was the same war. The consequence is that every estimate of civilian deaths has a wide margin of uncertainty, and some categories, especially executions carried out secretly, will probably never be counted completely.

Geography and speed made the Korean population unusually exposed. The peninsula is roughly one thousand kilometers long but narrow, and its principal cities, railways, highways, and ports lie along a handful of corridors. In 1950 most of the population lived in rural villages or small towns whose roads and bridges were also the routes of armies. The front line did not advance steadily across empty territory; it swept across inhabited farmland and towns, then reversed. The North Korean army reached the Nakdong River by August, the United Nations forces reached the Yalu River region by October and November, and the Chinese counteroffensive drove them back below Seoul by January 1951. The result was that the experience of invasion, occupation, liberation, and reoccupation, which in most wars affects a limited border zone, reached nearly every region of the peninsula in a matter of months. A further consequence was that each belligerent government claimed sovereignty over the entire peninsula and treated the population of recaptured territory as potential traitors, so that the arrival of one's own side could be as dangerous as the arrival of the enemy.

The evidence in this chapter falls into several categories. Contemporary records—military messages, diplomatic cables, newspapers, and relief reports—are close to events but reflect their authors' restricted access and institutional interests. Official investigations assemble documentary, testimonial and sometimes forensic evidence; they too have mandates and evidentiary limits. Survivor testimony restores experiences omitted from records but requires attention to memory and later political pressure. Exhumations may establish deaths and burial patterns without identifying every perpetrator. Scholarly reconstructions test these sources against one another. Unverified allegations remain claims rather than findings. The chapter asks readers to distinguish these forms of evidence before evaluating attribution or casualty figures.

Section 71.2

The First Refugee Crisis: June–September 1950

When the North Korean army crossed the 38th parallel on June 25, 1950, the first civilians to flee were those closest to the border, in Uijeongbu, Dongducheon, and the northern approaches to Seoul. By June 27 the flow of people toward the south had reached the capital, where the government's own radio broadcasts assured residents that the army was holding. The government and National Assembly in fact began leaving that day. On the night of June 27–28, the retreating army moved across the Han River, and in the early hours of June 28 the main bridges were blown up. The decision was taken by South Korean military authorities to prevent the pursuing North Korean tanks from crossing, and it was carried out before many civilians and military units had crossed. The number of people killed on the bridges has been estimated by different sources at several hundred, with some accounts giving higher figures; no precise tally exists. A South Korean engineering officer, Colonel Choi Chang-sik, was executed for the bridge demolition in September 1950 and was posthumously exonerated by a later review, a case that illustrates how blame for the military's own decisions could fall on individuals while the decision's civilian consequences went unexamined.

After the bridges fell, residents who were able to do so crossed the Han by boat, by swimming, or by improvised ferries operated by boatmen who charged for the crossing, while many hundreds of thousands remained in the city. The reasons for remaining were varied and are well documented in survivor accounts: families that had no means of transport, the elderly and sick who could not walk, people who believed the government's reassurances, people who feared leaving property, and some, though by no means all, who expected to live under the new authorities without harm. The fall of Seoul on June 28 therefore left a large civilian population under North Korean control. Some who remained were later accused by the South Korean government of having chosen to stay, a charge that confused circumstance with political choice, as Section 71.8 explains.

From late June, refugees moved south along the principal rail and road corridors, passing through Suwon, Osan, Cheonan, Daejeon, and Daegu. The movement was largely by foot, and families carried what they could, often on their heads or backs or on A-frame carriers (jige), with oxcarts for those who had them. Trains were requisitioned for military use, and many people clambered onto the roofs of cars or the sides of locomotives. The government's own relocation, first to Daejeon and then to Daegu and Busan, set a model that others followed: officials, police, and prominent families moved first and by better means, and the poor, rural, and elderly moved later or not at all. Even in these early weeks, the unequal opportunity to evacuate was visible, a pattern that recurred in each later crisis.

The consequences for military operations were real, and historians do not need to deny them in order to defend refugees. Columns of civilians on the same roads used by retreating Republic of Korea and American units slowed the movement of troops and supplies, hindered the organization of defensive lines, and, as Section 71.7 shows, created fears that infiltrators could pass through refugee crowds. The military authorities of both the Republic of Korea and the United Nations Command responded by attempting to control refugee movement: setting up checkpoints, restricting the roads, and in July establishing policies to prevent refugees from crossing front lines. It is essential not to leap from the fact that refugees interfered with operations to the assumption that refugees were enemy combatants. North Korean infiltration through refugee columns did occur and was documented, but the great majority of the people on the roads were farmers, laborers, and families seeking safety. The policy and practice of treating refugee crowds as security threats is a historical problem of its own, and its most extreme consequences are examined in Section 71.7.

By late July and August, the South Korean government and United Nations forces held a shrinking area in the southeast, bounded by the Nakdong River. This territory contained not only the original population of the region but hundreds of thousands, and by some estimates more than a million, refugees from the north and west, in addition to the families of soldiers and officials. Villages absorbed relatives and strangers; schools, temples, warehouses, and churches were used as shelters; and the cities of Daegu and Busan filled beyond capacity. Food became increasingly difficult to obtain, and the dense settlement of displaced people in the perimeter contributed to the outbreaks of disease discussed in Section 71.14. At the same time, the fighting along the Nakdong, including artillery, air attacks, and the shelling of villages, injured and killed civilians who had nowhere further to flee, and the destruction of towns such as Waegwan and the fighting around Pohang, Yeongdeok, and Masan added to the toll.

Not everyone fled, and those who remained in occupied territory had their own experience of the summer. In the areas under North Korean control, civilians faced the political organization described in Division III: people's committees, public meetings, registration, requisitions, mobilization for labor and military service, and arrests of persons identified as police, officials, or enemies of the new order. Their choices were constrained by fear of the occupation authorities and, increasingly, fear of what would follow when their own government returned. In the perimeter, meanwhile, the Republic of Korea's authorities were carrying out mass detentions and executions of suspected leftists, as Division II describes. The first refugee crisis was thus not only a flight from the enemy but also, for some, a flight from or into the reach of their own government.

Section 71.3

Winter Flight, Hungnam, and the Great Displacements of 1950–1951

The Incheon landing and UN advance in autumn 1950 reversed some civilian movement: displaced families returned to damaged homes, while northern communities came under a new authority. This fragile expectation of peace ended when Chinese forces intervened, first in October and then in the major November offensives. The retreats from northwestern Korea and the Changjin (Chosin) Reservoir region triggered another major displacement, this time across northern Korea and toward ports where evacuation by sea was possible (Chapters 69–70).

Northern civilians who fled in these weeks had varied reasons. Some had held positions or property that made them targets of North Korean authorities and expected punishment on the return of those authorities. Some had been anti-communist activists, Christians, landowners, or relatives of officials who had been killed or detained. Some had experienced the United Nations advance, including bombing and ground fighting, and wished to escape further destruction. Others, especially in the northeast, were driven by the sudden approach of the winter and the rumor of what the Chinese army would do. Many were simply following relatives or neighbors. The decisions were difficult and rapid. Families were divided by the speed of events, with members at work, at school, or in other towns when a decision to leave was made.

The most documented single episode of this northern flight is the evacuation from Hungnam, on the northeast coast, in December 1950. As X Corps of the United Nations Command withdrew from the northeast, its commanders organized the movement of troops and equipment by sea from Hungnam between roughly December 15 and December 24. Civilians gathered in the port in large numbers, and a portion of them were taken aboard ships. The U.S. Naval History and Heritage Command's operational account reports that approximately 91,000 Korean civilians were evacuated by sea from Hungnam, alongside approximately 105,000 military personnel. These figures should be attributed to that specific operational account. They represent people who were taken aboard evacuation ships and do not describe all Korean civilians who fled from the north, a much larger group, including many who traveled overland, by fishing boats, or by other routes. One commonly told episode involves the cargo ship SS Meredith Victory, which is reported to have carried approximately 14,000 refugees on its final voyage from Hungnam, arriving in southern ports on or after Christmas Day; the account is widely cited but depends on the ship's own reports and the memories of those aboard, and the count is approximate. Civil-affairs officers and Korean interpreters and community leaders are credited with pressing for civilians to be included in the evacuation, and the final departure of the ships was followed by the demolition of the port facilities.

The distinction between a refugee's decision to flee and the separate decision of authorities to permit boarding, crossing, or settlement was crucial at Hungnam as elsewhere. At the port, military authorities determined who could embark and in what order, and the priority was the evacuation of troops and military equipment. Civilians were taken aboard to the extent that space was available and that officers decided to make it so. Many who reached the waterfront were left behind, and some of those who could not board returned to their villages or tried to continue along the coast. People who were evacuated by ship were then moved to ports in the south, including Busan and, importantly, Geoje Island, where large numbers of northern refugees were temporarily settled; these were not the same Geoje facilities as the prisoner camps discussed in Division V, but they shared the island, and the same island's population would be transformed twice in two years.

The fall of Pyongyang to the Chinese and the retreat of the United Nations forces across the 38th parallel, followed in January 1951 by the Chinese and North Korean offensive that again threatened Seoul, produced the next displacement. As the offensive approached in the first days of January, the government and the United Nations Command decided to abandon Seoul. The evacuation of January 4, 1951, known in Korean as the "1·4 retreat" (il-sa hutoe), was carried out in severe winter. Many residents who had remained in or returned to Seoul crossed the Han River on ice or on pontoon crossings and on the few available bridges, and moved south along roads that were frozen and congested. A substantial share of the city's population left, though not all, and Seoul changed hands again. Many left on foot and without adequate clothing, food, or shelter; exposure and hunger killed some, and the overall hardship was extreme. Having experienced the summer's occupation and the autumn's reprisals, residents had strong reasons to expect a repetition, and the memory of those events shaped the decision to flee a second time.

Escape in winter depended partly on class, health, kinship and official permission. Those with money or connections were more likely to obtain vehicles or ship places; poor households, the sick, elderly people and families caring for small children often walked or remained behind. The hardship of the journey continued after arrival in overcrowded southern settlements. Estimates of northerners settling in the south during the war and its immediate aftermath range widely—often about 650,000 to more than one million—and sometimes include migration from 1945–1950. Figures must be linked to the period and registration method used.

Section 71.4

Refugee Camps, Relief Organizations, and the Struggle to Survive

Refugees in the south found shelter in an improvised and unevenly supplied system. In the first months, the South Korean government, local officials, and community and religious groups provided what they could: schools, warehouses, temples, churches, railway stations, and open ground became shelters. As the displaced population grew, the government attempted to register refugees, issue identification and ration cards, and direct them toward settlements. Registration served several purposes: it enabled the distribution of food and relief, but it also allowed authorities to monitor who had arrived from where, to screen for suspected infiltrators, and to control movement. For refugees, registration was both a path to assistance and a source of anxiety, since political suspicion could attach to where a person had come from or what he or she had done.

International relief arrived through several channels. In the first year the United Nations Command's civil-assistance functions were handled by military organizations, evolving into the UN Civil Assistance Command, Korea (UNCACK), under the Eighth Army, which coordinated supply of food, medicine, and relief goods for civilians in territory under UN control and attempted to organize public health measures. The United Nations also established a reconstruction agency, the UN Korean Reconstruction Agency (UNKRA), in December 1950, though its large-scale reconstruction work was constrained by the continuing war. Alongside these official bodies, voluntary agencies, many of them church-related, supplied clothing, food, and medical care, and Korean churches, Buddhist temples, and neighborhood organizations also carried out relief. In practice, military priorities determined much of what was available, since military transport and supplies came first, and relief organizations frequently complained of shortages and delays.

Camp conditions were harsh. Shelters were overcrowded, with families living in tents, makeshift huts of straw mats and scrap, or the rooms of the already full homes of relatives. Sanitation was poor, with limited water supply and latrines, and winter cold added a new danger. Food distribution fell short of need: rations were irregular, often consisting of grain supplied by the military or the government in quantities insufficient for a family, and many refugees supplemented them through labor, begging, or small commerce. Malnutrition and infectious diseases, which are discussed more fully in Section 71.14, spread easily in the crowded shelters. Refugee settlements near Busan, Daegu, Masan, Jeju, and Geoje were among the largest, and they developed into long-lasting neighborhoods; hillside shantytowns in Busan and elsewhere, many established in this period, remained for decades and became part of the urban landscape.

Work was one route to survival. The Korean Service Corps, organized to supply United Nations forces, recruited large numbers of Korean men to carry ammunition and supplies up steep slopes on A-frame carriers; these laborers worked in dangerous conditions near the front and many were killed or injured, a form of mobilization sometimes obscured in military histories. Other refugees worked for the military as laundresses, cooks, interpreters, or day laborers, or sold goods in markets that grew around military bases and relief depots. Informal commerce flourished: markets in Busan, notably the Gukje market area, became centers of trade in salvaged goods, relief supplies, and military surplus. Schooling was interrupted, but in many refugee areas, open-air or tent schools were set up, and the government moved some educational institutions to Busan and other southern cities, so that some instruction continued even in wartime.

Access to assistance was unequal. Local residents and refugees competed for scarce resources, and tensions between hosts and newcomers are documented. Registered refugees could receive rations, while unregistered people sometimes received less; families with connections to officials or religious networks fared better than isolated individuals; and corruption in the distribution of relief goods was a recurring complaint. The disparity between the provisions available to the military and those available to civilians was stark. Meanwhile, the government's treatment of the Korean men of military age in the National Defense Corps, which in the winter of 1950–1951 resulted in the deaths of a large number of conscripts through starvation and exposure after officers diverted supplies, shows that the state's own agencies could contribute to mass civilian mortality; the number of deaths in this incident is estimated in the tens of thousands, and the scandal led to the dismissal and punishment of senior officers in 1951.

Section 71.5

Preventive Arrests, the National Guidance Alliance, and the Bodo League Massacres

The mass executions of detainees in the first months of the war grew out of institutions created before it. After the Yeosu-Suncheon rebellion of October 1948 and the adoption of the National Security Law in December of that year, the South Korean government pursued former members of the South Korean Workers' Party and affiliated organizations, many of whom had been legal participants in politics under the American military government. In April 1949 the government sponsored the creation of the National Guidance Alliance (Gukmin Bodo Yeonmaeng, commonly called the Bodo League). Its stated purpose was to "guide" or reorient former leftists, who would renounce their past affiliations, register with the organization, and in exchange avoid prosecution. In practice, enrollment was sometimes voluntary, sometimes pressed by local police and officials, and sometimes inflated to satisfy quotas or to enroll people with no leftist history at all. Reports from the period and later scholarship put the enrolled membership in the range of several hundred thousand, with figures around 300,000 frequently cited; the number is approximate and contested, since registration was uneven and many enrollees were not leftists in any meaningful sense, including peasants who had received food or fertilizer through programs associated with local committees.

The registers created by this process had a consequence that most enrollees could not have foreseen. When the war began, they gave police and military authorities lists of people already marked as politically suspect. In the first days after June 25, authorities across South Korea ordered the detention of listed persons and of others suspected of leftist sympathies. Documentary evidence from later investigations indicates that orders for detention and execution moved through the police and military command systems, though the precise paper trail varies from place to place and much of it was destroyed or never written. Detainees were held in police stations, prisons, warehouses, and other improvised facilities and were then taken, usually at night, to remote valleys, mines, wells, coastal cliffs, or the sea, where they were shot or killed by other means. In many documented cases, they were not tried, charged, or told of any accusation. The victims included Bodo League enrollees, people already in prison on political charges, including those convicted before the war and persons held on minor offenses, relatives of suspected leftists, and others who were simply swept up. Women and children were among the victims in some localities.

The responsible institutions are identified by the evidence of different cases, not by a single national finding. In many localities the principal actors were the police, particularly the district police and their intelligence sections; in others, military police, army counterintelligence, and military units were directly involved; in coastal areas the navy and marine units participated in some documented episodes; and local right-wing youth organizations assisted by guiding, guarding, or participating. The government at the highest level did not issue a single published order authorizing the killing of all detainees, and historians continue to examine how far the executions followed instructions from central authorities and how far they reflected the decisions of regional commanders and local officials acting within a framework of emergency and fear. What the evidence does establish is that the practice was widespread, that it occurred across many provinces, and that it was carried out by state institutions of the Republic of Korea. It also establishes that state officials did not subject the detainees to judicial process.

Regional variation was marked. Some localities experienced large executions within the first weeks of the war, in July, as the front line approached; others saw killings later in the summer or at the moment of retreat. In some places, local officials delayed or refused the killings, and the survival of detainees sometimes depended on the decision of one commander. In others, detainees held for weeks were released or survived because the front line shifted before orders were carried out. The geography of the killings corresponded partly to the geography of the rebellions and leftist strength before the war, partly to the military situation, and partly to the individual choices of officials. Jeju, with its recent history of the 1948 uprising and its suppression, saw executions of detainees in 1950 as well, according to later investigations; coastal and southwestern regions with strong prewar leftist organization also registered large numbers.

The scale of the Bodo League killings is one of the most disputed questions of the war's civilian history. The Truth and Reconciliation Commission (2005–2010) investigated numerous incidents and confirmed several thousand victims by name in the cases that families brought to it, while estimating that the actual number was far greater, in the tens of thousands at minimum. Scholars working with the Commission's results and other materials have proposed estimates for the total ranging from tens of thousands to well over one hundred thousand. The wide range reflects several uncertainties: the incompleteness of surviving records, the fact that many families never reported killings because of fear and stigma, the difficulty of distinguishing Bodo League executions from other killings, and differences in definition about which killings count. The responsible approach, as stated in Section 71.1, is not to select one total but to distinguish confirmed local findings, which are documented and specific, from nationwide estimates, which are reconstructions. It is also important not to aggregate the Bodo League figures with other categories, such as killings of civilians after the recapture of Seoul or deaths in other incidents, as if they were one number.

For decades, public discussion of these killings was suppressed in South Korea. Under the governments of the 1950s through the 1980s, the dominant narrative attributed civilian killings of 1950 to the North Korean occupiers, and families of victims of state killings were stigmatized as relatives of "reds" and sometimes subjected to surveillance or social exclusion under the guilt-by-association practices of the period. The 1960 April Revolution briefly opened space for bereaved families to organize and to seek the exhumation of remains, but the 1961 military coup under Park Chung-hee suppressed these efforts, and the leaders of bereaved families' associations were arrested. Only after democratization, the work of civil society groups, and the creation of the Truth and Reconciliation Commission under a 2005 law were systematic investigations possible. In 2007 and subsequent years, exhumations at sites such as Daejeon, Cheongwon, and others recovered remains and artifacts that corroborated witness accounts. The Commission also recommended that the state acknowledge responsibility, offer an official apology, and take measures for the victims and their families, and in several recommendations presidents of the Republic of Korea issued public apologies for particular state killings, though the government response to the Commission's recommendations was uneven.

Section 71.6

Prisoner Executions, Daejeon, and the Geography of State Repression

Daejeon, in the center of the peninsula, became in July 1950 the site of one of the best-documented episodes of state killing. As North Korean forces advanced toward the city in the first half of July, the South Korean government had already relocated, and the city was held by American and Republic of Korea forces for a brief defense before it fell on about July 20. In the days before the fall, military police and police units took prisoners from Daejeon prison and from other detention facilities, together with Bodo League detainees brought from surrounding districts, to sites outside the city, including the Golryeonggol valley, where they were shot and buried in trenches. Survivors, local residents, and later investigators have indicated that the killings occurred over several days in early to mid-July. The number of victims is uncertain; estimates commonly cited run from the low thousands to several thousand, and the Truth and Reconciliation Commission and exhumation teams have worked to document remains, identify individuals, and reconstruct the numbers.

Part of what makes the Daejeon killings well documented is that foreign observers saw them. An American military officer photographed executions at the site in July 1950, and the photographs, which remained classified for decades, were later located in American archives and became a central piece of evidence when they were made public in the 1990s and 2000s. They show lines of men beside trenches and soldiers carrying out the shootings, and they corroborate the accounts of survivors and local residents that mass executions occurred at the site under the direction of South Korean military and police personnel. The photographs, taken from a distance, do not by themselves establish the total number of victims, nor do they identify the persons shot or the command that ordered the executions. They do establish that the killings occurred, that South Korean security forces carried them out, and that at least some Americans were aware of them at the time. The extent to which American officials and military officers knew of the killings and the degree of their responsibility to protest, intervene, or report remain subjects of historical discussion; American archival records indicate that at least some officials were aware of and uneasy about the executions, but there is limited evidence that any serious effort was made to halt them.

It is necessary to distinguish the Daejeon killings of July from those that occurred in the same city two months later. In September 1950, as United Nations forces approached from the south and the Incheon landing threatened to cut the North Korean supply lines, North Korean forces and local allies retreating from Daejeon killed prisoners held in the city's prison and elsewhere, including South Korean police, officials, right-wing activists, and civilians, as well as some captured soldiers. These killings are discussed in Section 71.10. The sites and perpetrators were different, as were the victims in part; although some of the same buildings and locations were used, the documentary and forensic record must be read carefully to distinguish remains belonging to the July killings by South Korean forces from those belonging to the September killings by retreating North Korean forces. Treating them as one event, in either direction, has been a feature of political uses of the Daejeon history: governments and groups have at times emphasized one set of killings and ignored the other. The chapter's approach is to document both and to attribute each to the responsible actors on the evidence.

Executions of detainees were not confined to Daejeon. The geography of killing followed the geography of detention and the movement of the front: in the summer of 1950, as North Korean forces approached each region, local authorities in a large number of counties in North Chungcheong, South Chungcheong, North and South Jeolla, Gyeongsang, and Gangwon provinces, as well as Seoul and Gyeonggi, carried out executions of detainees, often in the days immediately before retreat. In the southeast, where the front stabilized, executions continued for weeks as detainees were taken from prisons and police stations. Along the south coast, in places such as the waters near Busan, Masan, and Geoje and in the regions of Ulsan and Pohang, there are documented killings of detainees, including cases in which victims were taken out to sea. In Jeju, according to later investigations, hundreds of persons held in custody were executed in August 1950, and in some cases those killed had been held since the earlier uprising. These sites are only selected examples, and the Commission's casework indicates that the true geography is much broader.

The concealment of burial sites was part of the pattern. Bodies were buried in trenches in valleys, mines, and fields, and in some places thrown into abandoned mines or wells, or dumped in the sea. Local people knew the locations, but under the political conditions of the following decades they could not safely speak of them or visit the graves. Some bereaved families attempted to collect remains in 1960, during the brief period of openness after the April Revolution, and in a small number of places they erected memorials or reburied remains, but in 1961 the military government ordered such monuments destroyed and, in some places, the remains scattered. The later exhumations of the 2000s and 2010s, which recovered remains and personal objects at multiple sites, represent the work of forensic teams, volunteer organizations, and families, and they have identified a small proportion of the victims by DNA matching. The slow pace of identification is a reminder of how little physical evidence survived for decades and how much of the early history rests on witness testimony.

Exhumations and witness testimony together give historians distinct kinds of evidence, and each has limits. Remains can show that large numbers of people were killed in a particular place in a way consistent with execution: for example, bound hands, bullet wounds, and the arrangement in trenches. They can often give approximate numbers, though commingled and fragmentary remains make exact counts difficult, and they cannot normally identify the individuals or the order under which they were killed without additional evidence. Witness testimony, by contrast, can identify who gave orders and who carried them out, but it is vulnerable to memory error, fear of retaliation, and the political uses to which accounts have been put. Historians strengthen their conclusions when forensic evidence, documentary records, and testimony converge, as they do in the Daejeon case, and they flag the limits where they do not.

Section 71.7

No Gun Ri and Civilian Deaths during American Military Operations

In the third week of July 1950, American ground forces were retreating before the North Korean advance through the central part of the peninsula, and the roads and rail lines of the region were thick with refugees. At the same time, commanders were concerned about North Korean infiltration, and there were documented cases of North Korean soldiers disguising themselves as civilians. In this environment, American units and their headquarters were attempting to establish rules for refugee movement. In late July, messages from higher headquarters, including from the 1st Cavalry Division, discussed restrictions on refugees crossing the lines and, in some, authorized or discussed the use of force against persons who persisted in doing so. The surviving documents differ in their wording and in the units to which they were addressed, and historians have debated how far they amounted to an order to fire on civilians, what discretion they allowed for women and children, and whether the units on the scene received them.

The incident now known as No Gun Ri (Nogeun-ri) took place near the village of that name in Yeongdong County, North Chungcheong Province, between about July 25 and July 29, 1950. Several hundred Korean civilians, many of them villagers who had been ordered out of their homes by American soldiers and by South Korean police, were on the move along the roads and a railway line when they encountered units of the 7th Cavalry Regiment, 1st Cavalry Division. Survivors report that the refugees were stopped and searched, that aircraft strafed the group on July 26, and that they then took shelter in the twin railway underpasses beneath a bridge, where American soldiers fired on them over a period of days. Survivors report that many people were killed in the underpasses, that others tried to escape at night, and that the shooting continued into the third day. Their accounts, taken together with the testimony of some American veterans of the 7th Cavalry who later spoke to journalists, have been central evidence for the incident.

The incident was little known outside the village for nearly half a century. Survivors petitioned the South Korean government beginning in the 1990s, and a claims process was begun, but the political environment and the lack of documentary evidence frustrated their efforts. In 1999, a team of Associated Press journalists published an investigation based on interviews with survivors and American veterans and on archival research, and the story drew international attention. The U.S. Department of the Army conducted a review and in January 2001 published a report, and the governments of the United States and the Republic of Korea issued statements, including an expression of regret by the American president. The Army's 2001 review acknowledged that U.S. soldiers killed or injured an undetermined number of Korean refugees near No Gun Ri, but did not establish a definitive death toll or conclude that a specific order to kill civilians was transmitted to the soldiers at the scene. It treated the incident as a tragic consequence of combat circumstances; its separate treatment of allegations about an air attack remained disputed. The U.S. report and the Korean investigation did not resolve every disagreement over responsibility or the sequence of fire. Survivors and many Korean scholars, along with some American historians, rejected that characterization, pointing to the testimony of soldiers and the documentary evidence of the refugee policy.

The documentary evidence is a central part of the historical argument. A July 26, 1950 letter from U.S. ambassador John J. Muccio to Assistant Secretary of State Dean Rusk reported military discussions of refugee-control policy (Primary Source Window II). It had been declassified earlier but drew substantial public attention in May 2006, when Associated Press reporting and Sahr Conway-Lanz's historical research brought it into the No Gun Ri debate after the 2001 inquiry. Other records, including messages of July 24 and 25 from the 1st Cavalry Division and other American commands, and the testimony of soldiers of the 7th Cavalry about the instructions they received, have been analyzed by historians. The Army's own review included documents and testimony that bear on these questions. What the documents establish, and what they do not, must be kept distinct. They show that in late July, some American commands adopted or discussed policies restricting refugee movement across the front, and that some contemplated or authorized fire on civilians who did not turn back. They do not, by themselves, establish that the particular soldiers at No Gun Ri received a specific order to kill refugees, nor do they show the exact wording of any such order as it was communicated on the spot. Some veterans have said that they were told to shoot, others have said they were not, and some have said that they fired in the belief that the group included enemy infiltrators. A distinction between policy and execution, and between a general atmosphere of fear and a specific instruction, is therefore necessary.

The number of victims is likewise disputed, and the different figures measure different things. The survivors' association has long claimed that several hundred people were killed. South Korean official recognition records maintained by the No Gun Ri Peace Park identify 226 victims: 150 dead, 13 missing, and 63 surviving with lasting injuries or disabilities. Recognition records concern identified claims; they are not a forensic count of every person who died. Estimates by survivors and some researchers are higher. The American Army's review did not establish a total. These figures should not be confused: the official recognition of victims by the Republic of Korea is a legal-administrative finding based on claims and evidence presented to the committee, and survivors have maintained that it undercounts. The absence of an agreed figure does not weaken the central finding that a large number of unarmed civilians, including women and children, were killed or wounded by American forces over several days. It is a reminder that numbers in this chapter must be read with their sources.

No Gun Ri was not the only case of American forces firing on refugees. Historians who have examined American military records, including Sahr Conway-Lanz and others, have shown that in the summer of 1950 and later, American air and ground units attacked groups of civilians on roads and in villages in a number of other incidents, and that policies of treating refugee groups with suspicion were applied at various times by multiple commands. The Army's own documents record that American aircraft were directed to attack groups of civilians believed to include infiltrators on the roads near the front, and later in the war, at times of heavy fighting, civilians in battle zones were killed by artillery and air attack. The scale of these killings has not been fully documented, and the evidence consists largely of scattered military records and testimony. The distinction between incidents that are documented and those that rest on allegation is important, and the chapter treats the No Gun Ri case as the best-documented example, not as a representation of all American operations.

Historical Vocabulary
Noncombatant Status, Distinction, Indiscriminate Attack, and Military Necessity

Definition. Noncombatants are persons not taking a direct part in hostilities, including ordinary civilians. The principle of distinction in the laws and customs of war requires belligerents to differentiate between combatants and civilians and to direct attacks only at military objectives. An indiscriminate attack is one that does not distinguish between military objectives and civilians, or that can be expected to cause civilian harm excessive in relation to the military advantage anticipated. Military necessity is a principle that permits measures needed to achieve legitimate military aims, but it does not authorize violations of the laws of war.

Historical context. In 1950 these ideas existed in treaty law, military manuals, and customary practice, though the 1949 Geneva Convention on the protection of civilians had not yet entered into general force, and the later Additional Protocols of 1977 had not been written. The belligerents in Korea invoked humanitarian principles selectively. Military officers justified restrictions on refugees by reference to security and necessity, and critics have answered that the presence of possible infiltrators did not remove the protected status of the civilians among them.

Why it matters. The vocabulary allows historians to ask separate questions: whether the people targeted were combatants, whether the attack could discriminate, and whether necessity can justify the harm. It also clarifies that a person's status does not depend on political opinion, ethnicity, or presumed sympathy.

Potential misunderstanding. Military necessity is not a general excuse. A fear of infiltration may explain why commanders adopted a policy, but explanation is different from justification, and the historian can analyze motive without endorsing it.

Section 71.8

Recapture, Collaboration Accusations, and Political Reprisals

When the Incheon landing and the breakout from the Pusan Perimeter in September 1950 returned Seoul and the southern provinces to South Korean control, the government faced a population that had lived under the North Korean occupation for as long as three months. Its response was to treat the question of who had cooperated with the occupiers as a matter of national security and loyalty. Seoul was recaptured at the end of September, and in the weeks that followed, the government established investigative bodies, including joint investigation headquarters involving police, military police, and army counterintelligence, to identify and punish those who had assisted the enemy. The word used for such persons was bu-yeok-ja, "collaborator" or "one who joined in the enemy's work," and its meaning was elastic. The government issued special measures for punishing collaborators, and the procedures classified suspects by degrees of culpability, with the most serious cases subject to trial by military and special courts and, in the early weeks, summary action.

The Truth and Reconciliation Commission has documented executions of unarmed civilians in Seoul and Incheon in the weeks after the recapture, including cases in which people were taken from detention, held by police or military units, and killed without trial. The victims included people accused of having served in North Korean-organized committees or worked for the occupying authorities, those suspected of leftist affiliation, and those denounced by neighbors. The Commission's case decisions identify police, military police, and army units as responsible in particular cases. These findings correspond to a pattern also documented in other districts, in which the return of the South Korean government was followed by arrests, interrogations, and in a significant number of cases executions of persons accused of collaboration. The scale is not known with precision. The documented cases are numerous, and the Commission has stated that the number of victims is certainly greater than those it was able to confirm, but a figure for the whole country cannot be responsibly given.

The conditions under which ordinary survival activities were redefined as political collaboration illuminate the process. A resident who had stayed in Seoul was frequently required to register with the new authorities, to obtain a ration, to attend public meetings, or to carry out assigned work such as cleaning, food preparation, or the transport of goods. Government employees, teachers, and others who were told to return to their posts faced a choice between obeying and risking punishment by the occupiers. People who had been compelled to join mobilization drives, to carry out labor, or to sign petitions were later accused of voluntary cooperation. In the investigation after the recapture, the question of whether the person had acted under compulsion was not always examined, and in the early weeks suspicion alone could be enough to lead to arrest. The line between coerced compliance and voluntary collaboration was a historical and moral problem that the security officials of the time were often unwilling to draw. The fact that the government itself had left the capital without evacuating most of the population made the accusation especially hard for those who had been unable to leave.

Accusations also depended on social relationships. Class, family background, and prior political records could determine who was scrutinized. A family with a member who had belonged to a leftist organization before the war, or who had joined the North Korean forces or a people's committee, could find all members suspected. Landowners and wealthier villagers who had suffered under the occupation, and who now returned to positions of influence, sometimes denounced those who had taken part in the North Korean land redistribution or who had held office. Neighbors with property disputes, family quarrels, or personal enmities could use the new situation to accuse their rivals. At the same time, many people protected neighbors by testifying to their good character or by hiding them, and the records show that a testimonial from a respected local figure could save a person. The evidence for such cases comes from survivor accounts, court records where they survive, and the investigations of the truth commissions, which generally agree that the accusation process combined genuine political conflict with opportunistic denunciation.

Reprisals were also carried out by local armed groups, including right-wing youth organizations and police auxiliaries, who in some places took the lead in arresting and killing people accused of collaboration, sometimes before regular authorities arrived and sometimes in coordination with them. Where these groups acted, they often drew on grievances that predated the war, including conflicts between landlords and tenants, between supporters and opponents of the 1948 uprisings, and between families that had suffered at the hands of leftists and those that had been targeted by the police. Such cases fall between state violence and local civil conflict, and Section 71.12 returns to them. The distinction matters for assigning responsibility: where the police, military police, or army units acted, the institutions of the Republic of Korea bear direct responsibility; where armed civilians acted on their own, the state's responsibility consists of its failure to restrain them and, in some cases, of its tolerance or encouragement.

Cause and Effect

How Political Classification and Repeated Occupation Produced Detention, Denunciation, and Reprisal

Long-term causes

Postwar conflicts over land, policing, and the future state; the suppression of the 1948 rebellions; the National Security Law; and prewar registers that sorted citizens by political suspicion on both sides of the 38th parallel.

Short-term causes

The rapid military collapse of June 1950; the departure of the government from Seoul without evacuating most residents; emergency security doctrines that treated suspected sympathizers as an immediate military threat.

Immediate trigger

The recapture of Seoul and the southern provinces in late September 1950, when authorities moved to identify those who had cooperated with the occupation and local organizations moved to settle scores.

Event or transformation

Ordinary survival activities, such as registering, receiving rations, or reporting to work, were reclassified as political crimes; suspicion became arrest, and arrest in many documented cases became execution without trial.

Immediate effects

Executions of unarmed civilians in Seoul, Incheon, and other districts; family flight to avoid accusation; a climate of denunciation; and, as the front moved again, renewed fear of reoccupation.

Long-term consequences

Decades of stigma for victims' families, suppressed public memory, and later truth-commission investigations; a legacy of mistrust between neighbors in many villages.

Historical complexity: No single factor produced these outcomes. Government policy, military security fears, local rivalries, individual choices, and the speed of the front all contributed. Responsibility must be assessed case by case, distinguishing orders from implementation and institutions from individuals.

Section 71.9

Civilian Life under North Korean Occupation

The North Korean administration of occupied South Korea lasted from a few weeks to about three months, depending on the region. It was neither improvised nor uniform. Documents captured by American forces in the autumn of 1950 and later made available to researchers show that North Korean authorities had prepared a political program for the territory they expected to hold. Military administration was exercised by the Korean People's Army in the first days, followed in most places by the formation of people's committees (inmin wiwonhoe) at the provincial, county, and village levels, and by the activation of local party and mass-organization structures. Many of the personnel of these bodies were local leftists, including people who had been released from prison, who had returned from hiding in the mountains, or who had been active in the South Korean Workers' Party before it was suppressed. In several regions, especially the southwest, where leftist networks had been strong before the war, the committees had a local base of support; in others, the authorities relied heavily on personnel brought from the north.

The occupation's social program was built around class categories. Within weeks the authorities announced the application of the land-reform principles they had implemented in the north in 1946, redistributing land from landlords to tenants and landless farmers, and the committees organized the registration of land and the allocation of plots. For many tenants and poor farmers the policy answered long-standing grievances, and some responded with genuine support. At the same time, the redistribution created opponents among landlords and their families, and the short duration of the occupation meant that new owners had no time to secure their claims before the front reversed, leaving them exposed to accusations of having profited from the occupation. The authorities also nationalized or took control of enterprises, banks, and government property, and they organized political education sessions, public rallies, and the distribution of propaganda. Residents were required to attend meetings, to register their households, and to participate in organizations for youth, women, farmers, and workers. For people already sympathetic to the new authorities, these provided opportunities; for others, they were obligations to be fulfilled with as little engagement as possible.

Arrests began almost at once. The categories of people targeted corresponded to the authorities' definition of enemies: police officers, officials of the Republic of Korea, members of the National Assembly and other political figures, members of right-wing youth organizations and the Bodo League's opponents, landlords, and people who had assisted the South Korean government. Prisons were reopened by the occupying authorities and filled with people from these categories, and some of those held were later killed, as Section 71.10 describes. At the same time, the occupiers took many prominent South Koreans north, including politicians, scholars, and professionals, a matter discussed in Section 71.16. Some people who had held political or professional positions were given the choice to cooperate. The authorities also released political prisoners held by the South Korean government, including many who had been detained since the rebellions of 1948–1949 and the early months of 1950, and these released prisoners were among those who took up positions in the new committees.

Mobilization was one of the most significant and coercive elements of the occupation. The North Korean government needed manpower to replace its heavy losses, and in the occupied territory it conducted what it called volunteer recruitment (uiyonggun in the sense of "righteous army" volunteers) for the army, with young men told to enlist or be treated as enemies. Evidence from the period and later testimony indicate that recruitment ranged from real volunteering by committed supporters to coercion, with pressure applied through committees, family threats, and rounding up of men. Some of those recruited were sent to the front with minimal training and were among the large number of South Koreans captured in the autumn by United Nations forces, many of whom later were held in prisoner camps and civilian internee compounds, as Division V explains. Requisitioning of food, draft animals, labor, and carts was also common, imposed to supply the army and a population under heavy strain, and a "wartime agricultural tax" in kind was collected in the countryside. Many farmers experienced the requisitions as burdensome and, because of the shortage of men and tools, as a threat to the summer and autumn harvest.

Responses among Korean communities varied widely and cannot be reduced to a single pattern. In some places, local people welcomed the occupation, particularly where leftists and poor farmers had suffered under the Republic of Korea's police and counterinsurgency; in others, especially among landowners, Christians, officials, and families that had lost members to leftist violence, the arrival of the North Korean army was met with fear, flight, and hiding. Most people occupied a middle position, complying, avoiding politics, and attempting to protect their families. Historians who study the period using memoirs, interviews, captured documents, and local records have shown that the initial reception in the first weeks, sometimes marked by public celebration organized by the committees, gave way to disillusion as the demands for labor, grain, and recruits grew and as American bombing and the failure of the offensive at the Nakdong became evident. The character of the occupation changed accordingly. As the KPA's military position deteriorated in August and September, the authorities intensified requisitioning and mobilization, imposed stricter controls, and became more suspicious of the population, so that the early period of political organization gave way to coercion and, in the final weeks, to the killings of the retreat.

Section 71.10

North Korean Executions, Prisoners, and the September Retreat

North Korean forces and the local authorities allied with them killed South Korean officials, police, right-wing activists, political prisoners, civilians, and captured soldiers during the occupation and especially during the retreat. These killings are documented by contemporary investigations, by exhumations carried out at the time and later, by witness testimony, and by captured documents, and the evidence for their occurrence is strong even where the numbers are uncertain. They were committed by identifiable organizations: units of the Korean People's Army, the political and security organs of the North Korean state, and local committees and armed local supporters. As with the South Korean killings, they require precise attribution to those responsible. They do not become less serious because other killings occurred, and they do not make the South Korean and American killings less serious either.

The timing and character of the killings varied. In the first weeks, the arrests and killings concentrated on people identified as enemies of the new order: police officers, officials, members of right-wing organizations, and landowners or others who had denounced leftists. Some were killed after quick people's trials, while others were killed without any proceeding, and some killings were carried out by local activists acting on personal initiative with the tolerance of the committees. In several documented cases, prisoners held in local jails were killed as the front line moved. The greatest concentration of mass killings, however, occurred in September 1950, when the North Korean position collapsed after the Incheon landing and the breakout from the Pusan Perimeter. As the retreating army and its local organizations withdrew northward, they killed prisoners rather than release them or leave them for the returning South Korean authorities. These killings took place in prisons and detention sites in a number of cities and towns.

Daejeon was the largest documented case. Before abandoning the city in late September, North Korean forces and their local supporters killed a large number of people held in Daejeon prison and in other locations, including South Korean police, officials, right-wing members, civilians, and some captured soldiers, and including American prisoners. The sites included the prison, the Sannae area, and other places where bodies were afterward discovered by returning forces. Estimates of the number killed in Daejeon at the time of the retreat commonly run to several thousand, but the figure is uncertain; the numbers used by South Korean authorities in 1950 and later varied, in part because of the difficulty of distinguishing remains of different killings, in part because some political authorities later had an interest in emphasizing the scale. In Seoul, prisoners at the Seodaemun prison and other facilities were killed or taken north as the occupiers withdrew, and thousands of South Korean civilians were forced to march north. Similar killings were documented in other cities and towns. In August 1950, North Korean soldiers killed a group of captured American soldiers near Hill 303 on the Nakdong front, and other killings of prisoners of war occurred, as Section 71.17 notes.

Contemporary investigations provide a range of evidence. After the recapture of Seoul and Daejeon, South Korean and American authorities, journalists, and observers of the United Nations Commission on Korea visited the sites, examined bodies, and took statements from survivors and relatives. The October 7, 1950 UNCOK report is a primary source of particular importance, because UN observers were present at several sites. It must be read with the limits of its production in mind: the observers had limited time, depended in part on South Korean officials and local residents for access and interpretation, and reported what they saw while still in the aftermath of fighting, with bodies not yet fully exhumed. Some contemporary claims of atrocities were exaggerated or reproduced rumor, and Cold War propaganda on both sides made use of the discovery of killings. The task for historians has been to separate verified mass killing from exaggerated or politically constructed claims. For the Daejeon and Seoul killings, the combination of contemporary observation, exhumation, and survivor testimony supports the conclusion that mass killings of prisoners and civilians by retreating North Korean forces and their local allies did occur, even though the exact numbers and some details remain uncertain.

Later historical research has refined this picture while confirming its core. Studies drawing on South Korean government records, captured North Korean documents, and testimony of local participants and survivors have shown that the killings were not uniform across the occupied territory. In some counties, committees limited killings or protected individuals; in others, executions were frequent. Some killings were carried out by local leftists settling prewar scores, with the committees' knowledge or encouragement; others were carried out on the orders of army and security units. The Republic of Korea's postwar governments used the killings extensively in anti-communist propaganda, sometimes inflating numbers or eliding the differences between North Korean killings and their own, and the South Korean state's own killings were excluded from public narrative until democratization. Both of these political uses should be recognized, and neither undermines the evidence that the North Korean killings occurred.

Section 71.11

Sinchon, Local Civil War, and Disputed Responsibility for Massacres

Sinchon, a county in South Hwanghae Province northwest of Seoul, is the setting of one of the most heavily commemorated, and most contested, massacres of the war. North Korean official history holds that in the autumn of 1950, during roughly fifty days of occupation by United Nations forces beginning in mid-October, American troops killed a very large number of civilians in Sinchon, and the official figure given by the North Korean state and its museum at the site is 35,383. The Sinchon museum, opened in the 1950s and expanded later, presents the event as a signature case of American atrocity, and the narrative has been used extensively in North Korean education and propaganda. Delegations from international organizations, including a commission of the Women's International Democratic Federation that visited North Korea in the spring of 1951, reported on atrocities including Sinchon, and the claims became part of a broader contest over the narrative of the war.

Korean scholarship, including Monica Hahn's 2013 analysis of the October 13 uprising and Daeyeol Yea's 2024 study of the local right-wing peace-preservation corps, has challenged the official North Korean attribution of the massacre exclusively to American forces. Researchers have found that Hwanghae Province had been a region of sharp political and religious conflict before the war, with a strong Christian community and an active anti-communist movement in the north's first years, and that, as the United Nations advance and the withdrawal of North Korean authorities in October 1950 created a political vacuum, local right-wing organizations and armed groups, some of them composed of people who had suffered under the North Korean regime or had been in conflict with the local communists, took revenge on people identified with the party and the government, including relatives. According to these studies, much of the killing in Sinchon was carried out by these local groups, with the American forces' role limited, disputed, or at most one of permissiveness, and some killings are attributed to the early weeks, in September and October, before and during the transition. The detailed sequence, numbers, and relationships to occupying forces remain debated; see Hahn (2013) and Yea (2024). It does not follow, however, that the mass killings were invented. The evidence from testimony, remains, and local accounts supports the conclusion that a large-scale massacre of civilians took place in Sinchon in the autumn of 1950, although its scale and the identity of the perpetrators are disputed.

Several kinds of evidence bear on the dispute, and they point in different directions. The North Korean official claim relies on state-produced accounts, the testimony of survivors recorded under state auspices, and the physical site of the museum and memorials. These sources establish that the event is deeply embedded in North Korean memory and that survivors exist, but because they were produced within a political system that permitted a single interpretation, they are less able to test alternative explanations. Korean scholars working on the basis of interviews in the south with people who left Hwanghae, of records from the period and of other documentation have found accounts that point to local right-wing perpetrators, but these sources too have their own limitations, since the people interviewed in the south were often those who had been on the anti-communist side and may have had reasons to emphasize or minimize particular actions. American military records of the period contain little direct information on events in Sinchon, and the extent of American forces' presence in the county during the period in question is part of the dispute. Contemporary observations by journalists and international delegations were produced in the context of the war's propaganda contests.

For this reason the Sinchon case is the chapter's central case study in how historians evaluate competing atrocity narratives. The first step is to separate questions: whether mass killing occurred, who the victims were, how many died, who carried out the killings, who gave or tolerated orders, and how the event has been used politically. The second is to ask what each kind of source can and cannot establish. The third is to refuse two tempting shortcuts: the shortcut of accepting an official state account because the event is real, and the shortcut of rejecting the event because an official account is propagandistic. A narrative can be both politically useful and substantially grounded in real events, and it can also attribute real events to the wrong perpetrators. The task of the historian is to hold these possibilities apart.

Section 71.12

Cycles of Revenge, Forced Allegiance, and Communities Divided by War

For many Koreans, the war's central experience was not the front but the repeated change of authority in one's own village. A county in the south could be held by the Republic of Korea in June, by the North Korean authorities in July and August, by the Republic of Korea and the United Nations Command again from late September, and for some, in January 1951, briefly by the Chinese and North Korean forces once more. Each change brought a purge of those identified with the previous authority. Those who had held office under the first order were arrested or killed by the second; those who had cooperated with the second were punished by the third. People in these communities faced an acute problem of survival: to remain neutral was often impossible, to support one side was dangerous when the other returned, and to flee was to abandon land and family.

Families were frequently divided by these changes. A household might contain a son conscripted by the North Korean army, a daughter working for the people's committee, a father who had been a village official under the Republic of Korea, and a relative in the police. When the front moved, the family was exposed to accusation on the basis of its least safe member. Hostage-taking was common: authorities arrested or threatened relatives to compel the surrender of suspected persons in hiding, or detained family members as collateral. Denunciation within villages was a means by which individuals and factions used the authorities to settle disputes; because both sides' security organizations relied on local informants, the accusations were not subject to careful verification. In some localities, neighbors who had lived side by side for decades became murderers and victims. The evidence for this is in survivors' testimony, in anthropological and historical studies of villages in the postwar period, and in truth commission case files. Heonik Kwon and other scholars have shown how the memory of these local killings structured village life for generations, including in the form of rituals and silence.

Insurgency and counterinsurgency compounded the problem. In the mountainous regions of the south, including Jirisan and the Jeolla and Gyeongsang provinces, armed leftist groups, composed of local people, survivors of earlier rebellions, soldiers who had been cut off, and North Korean agents, operated against the South Korean authorities and, in many places, controlled villages at night. The South Korean army and police responded with operations against these partisans and against the civilians suspected of supporting them. In February 1951, soldiers of the South Korean army's 11th Division, in operations in the Jirisan region, carried out killings of civilians in Geochang and nearby counties, including at the Sinwon-myeon area, where hundreds of villagers, among them women, children, and elderly people, were killed in a single episode. A military court heard cases arising from the incident in 1951, with convictions of officers, and later official investigations and legal measures have recognized the victims; the number recognized is in the hundreds, with a figure of 719 frequently given as the number of victims in Geochang, though the full set of incidents in the region involved other districts. The Geochang case illustrates the way in which counterinsurgency doctrine, treating villages as supporting insurgents, led to the killing of unarmed civilians by regular army units.

The categories imposed by each state did not capture the ambiguity of civilian conduct. The Republic of Korea classified residents as loyal, suspected, or collaborators; the North Korean authorities classified them by class background and political reliability, as supporters, neutrals, or reactionaries. A farmer who gave food to partisans under threat, a woman who cooked for soldiers who came to her door, a teacher who continued to teach, or a boy who carried a message might be treated by one authority as a hero and by the next as a traitor. The documentation, much of it assembled after the fact, reflects an abundance of cases in which people did what they thought necessary for the survival of their families. The historical point is not that conduct was morally irrelevant, but that the state categories did not distinguish between compelled acts and chosen ones, nor between combatants and noncombatants.

The war also intensified local conflicts that had begun earlier. Disputes over land and tenancy, over the colonial-period records of police and officials, over religion, and over the rebellions of 1948 and the counterinsurgency that followed, had divided villages before June 1950. The war turned those disputes into a matter of life and death by giving one side or the other the means of violence and the cover of a state's authority. Some historians describe this as a layered conflict: an international war, a civil war between two states, and a set of local civil wars within communities. The notion of local civil war helps explain why violence could be both highly organized and intensely personal, and why bereaved families have often known the identities of those responsible.

Historical Debate

How Should Historians Explain Civilian Massacres in the Korean War?

The question: Were the mass killings of civilians in 1950–1951 primarily the result of orders issued by central authorities, of local initiative by commanders, police, and civilian groups, of wartime emergency systems that suspended legal safeguards, or of a combination of these? The answer affects how responsibility is assigned and how states are held accountable.

Interpretation A — Central direction and state policy: Some scholars emphasize that the preventive detention system, the registers, the emergency laws, and the existence of security institutions designed for this purpose indicate planning and policy at the national level. On this view, even where written orders have not survived, the pattern across provinces and the existence of lists point to coordination, or at least to a policy of permissiveness set by senior officials.
Interpretation B — Local agency and decentralized violence: Others emphasize variation: some localities saw many killings and others few, depending on the decisions of local police chiefs, commanders, and civilian groups, and on local grievances predating the war. On this view, the central government created conditions and tolerated violence, but individual actors made the decisive choices and deserve attention as agents.
Interpretation C — Wartime emergency systems and combinations: A third approach stresses that in rapidly collapsing military conditions, security fears, fear of fifth columns, and the breakdown of legal order produced a system in which central policy, local initiative, and emergency improvisation interacted. This approach is compatible with elements of the first two and treats the question as one of degree and of mechanism.

Evidence: Surviving written orders are scarce; the registers and laws are documented; witness testimony and exhumations show patterns across regions; some commanders' decisions to spare or to kill are recorded in testimony. The evidence is stronger for the occurrence and pattern of killings than for the exact transmission of commands.

Points of disagreement: Disagreement concerns causation and interpretation more than the occurrence of killings: how much weight to give central policy versus local agency, and how to interpret the absence of explicit orders. It also concerns terminology, such as whether to describe events as policy, as emergency measures, or as massacres, and scale, where estimates differ.

Why it matters: If central direction is emphasized, accountability falls on senior officials and institutions; if local agency is emphasized, on commanders and perpetrators; if emergency systems are emphasized, on the legal and institutional frameworks that failed to restrain violence. These are not mutually exclusive, and the analysis may differ for the South Korean, North Korean, American, and local armed-group cases. Treating them as identical would obscure real differences.

Section 71.13

The Destruction of Cities: Air Raids, Bombardment, and Civilian Life

Chapter 70 treated the air war as a military strategy, examining the objectives set by United Nations Command planners, the aircraft and tactics employed, and the relation of bombing to the negotiating table. This section reverses the perspective. It asks what the bombing meant for the population beneath it, and it recognizes that the stated objective of an operation and its actual consequences for civilians are separate historical questions. Both are legitimate, and neither substitutes for the other. The operational records of the United States Air Force are essential for reconstructing intent, targets, and reported damage. They are not substitutes for Korean civilian testimony, which is far scarcer for the north, where the state controlled the recording of memory, and for the south, where bombing by friendly forces was a sensitive subject.

American-led air attacks on North Korean territory began in the first weeks of the war, concentrating at first on transportation targets, airfields, and industrial installations, and expanded after the Chinese intervention to include the systematic destruction of towns and villages that the command considered to be supply and communications centers. Figures cited in the secondary literature, which derive from Far East Air Forces and Navy statistics, put the total tonnage of bombs dropped on Korea in the war at roughly 635,000 tons, including more than 30,000 tons of napalm; these are operational totals, not measures of civilian harm, and they include attacks in the south and on front-line targets. Pyongyang was the object of repeated heavy raids, including those of July and August 1952, the largest of the air war. Other cities and towns in the north, among them Hamhung, Hungnam, Sinuiju, Wonsan, and numerous smaller places, suffered severe destruction. Historians who have worked with American and North Korean sources, including scholars such as Conrad Crane writing on airpower strategy and Bruce Cumings writing on the war's social effects, differ about the degree to which the destruction was a deliberate aim of policy or a by-product of attacks on military and transport targets, but they agree that by the war's end few buildings in many northern urban areas remained standing.

For civilians in the north, the bombing changed the organization of daily life. Urban populations dispersed into the countryside; families dug shelters and tunnels in hillsides; factories were moved underground or into mountain caves; and offices, schools, and hospitals were relocated to improvised quarters. The government mobilized residents for air-defense, firefighting, rebuilding, and repair, and it gave the experience of bombing an ideological meaning that became a pillar of North Korean political culture. People who lived through the war in the north later described the constant presence of aircraft, the fear of napalm, the loss of family members, and the experience of living in caves and trenches. These accounts, collected under official auspices, and a smaller number of testimonies gathered abroad or from defectors, are a vital but unevenly available source, and they need to be read with the political context of collection in mind.

Bombing of targets that supported the civilian economy was part of the campaign. In June 1952, American aircraft attacked the Suiho (Supung) hydroelectric station on the Yalu River and other power plants in the north, after the removal of earlier restrictions on attacking such targets. Official U.S. Air Force histories describe the attacks as a major strike on power generation, and they report that a very large share of the north's electrical capacity was put out of operation for a period. The consequences for civilians were indirect but significant: power shortages affected water supply, factories, rail transport, and any service that depended on electricity, and they contributed to the already severe conditions in cities. In May 1953, as the armistice negotiations continued, American aircraft attacked dams that controlled irrigation water for the rice fields, including the Toksan and Chasan dams. Air Force records confirm that the attacks produced extensive flooding that damaged rail and road transport and agricultural land, and they show that planners were aware that the destruction of the dams would affect the rice crop at a critical moment in the planting season. North Korean authorities mobilized very large numbers of people to repair the dams and the fields in the following weeks, and the episode became part of the argument, discussed in many histories, about the relation between aerial pressure and the armistice. The records are valuable for reconstructing operational intent and damage; for the experience of the people in the flooded valleys, they are limited.

South Korean cities were also devastated, though by different means. Seoul changed hands four times between June 1950 and March 1951. Its recapture in September 1950 involved intense urban combat, artillery fire, and air attack, and large parts of the city were damaged; the January 1951 evacuation and the subsequent recapture added further destruction. Incheon was bombarded before the September landing. Towns along the Nakdong front, including Waegwan and Hadong, were destroyed or heavily damaged in the summer fighting, and numerous villages were burned in the course of operations, including some deliberately burned by South Korean and American forces in counterinsurgency or as a measure to deny shelter to enemy forces. Civilians in South Korea were also killed by friendly fire and by the artillery and air attack on towns held by North Korean forces. Fighting in the central mountains and along the later stabilized front destroyed villages and fields and displaced the inhabitants, so that a belt of territory across the middle of the peninsula was largely depopulated by 1952.

The effect of the bombing on civilian mortality cannot be stated with precision. Estimates of civilian deaths in North Korea vary widely, from several hundred thousand to over a million, and they depend on assumptions about population loss, refugee flight, and the definition of war-related deaths. The figures are not based on registration and should be treated with caution. What can be said with confidence from the records of both sides is that cities were largely destroyed, that the infrastructure of the civilian economy was severely damaged, and that civilians were killed in large numbers by air attack. The legal and ethical assessment of the campaign, including questions of proportionality, the status of towns as military targets, and the use of incendiary weapons, has been debated by historians and legal scholars; this chapter's role is to establish the effects on the population, and it leaves to Chapter 70 the analysis of strategy.

Section 71.14

Hunger, Disease, Medical Care, and the Destruction of Everyday Infrastructure

The war disrupted food production at every stage. Spring planting in 1950 had barely finished when the invasion began, and in many areas the summer and autumn harvests were interrupted by fighting, the flight of farmers, the requisitioning of labor and draft animals, and the destruction of irrigation works and transport. Men were conscripted or mobilized by both sides, leaving women, children, and the elderly to carry out the work of cultivation. In the south the area of reliable agricultural production shrank to the southeastern corner in the summer of 1950, while the number of people to be fed increased; in the north, bombing destroyed storage and transport, and the attacks on irrigation dams in 1953 threatened the rice crop. Shortages of seed, fertilizer, and tools persisted. Food shortages were most severe for refugees, people in shelters, and urban populations cut off from the countryside, and they were compounded by inflation and the black market.

Disease followed displacement. In the crowded shelters and camps of the winter of 1950–1951, typhus, typhoid, smallpox, dysentery, and tuberculosis spread among malnourished and poorly clothed people. The United Nations Command and the Korean authorities organized mass vaccination and delousing campaigns, using DDT dusting and inoculations, and they credited these measures with limiting the spread of epidemic typhus; medical histories of the war, including the U.S. Army Medical Department's accounts, treat the public-health campaign as one of the successes of the civil-assistance effort, while recognizing that mortality among refugees and in institutions was high. Smallpox outbreaks persisted into 1951 and later. The evidence for the extent of the epidemics comes largely from military and relief agency reports, which counted cases and deaths among those they reached; the burden among people outside the camps and in the north is much less well documented.

Allegations about the deliberate use of biological weapons require separate treatment. In 1952, the North Korean and Chinese governments, with the support of the Soviet Union, charged that United Nations forces had dropped insects and other materials carrying disease on Korean and Chinese territory, and they publicized the claims through reports and delegations. The United States and the United Nations Command denied the charges and called for an impartial inquiry, which the other side did not accept. Former Soviet archival documents made public in 1998 describe fabrication of purported infection sites and evidence; historian Milton Leitenberg and Korean scholar Jeon Ye-mok (2021) draw on these and other records to argue that key 1952 allegations were orchestrated or fabricated. Jeon cautions that not every detail of the broader allegation history is resolved. There is no independently substantiated basis here for stating that American forces conducted the alleged germ-warfare campaign. See Jeon (2021) and Leitenberg's discussion of the archival documents. This is an example of a claim to test against the evidence rather than accept or dismiss solely on political grounds, and it illustrates the way atrocity claims became weapons in the war's contest for international opinion.

The loss of medical infrastructure was severe. Hospitals and clinics were destroyed, requisitioned, or abandoned; medical personnel were killed, captured, conscripted, or fled; and supplies of medicines were scarce. In the south, Korean physicians and nurses, working in makeshift facilities in Busan, Daegu, and Jeju, with support from Korean and international relief organizations and the military medical services, treated wounded civilians and refugees. International agencies, including the Red Cross societies of various countries, sent medical teams and supplies, and military hospitals sometimes treated civilians, though the priority was the treatment of troops. The disparity between the logistical resources available to the military and those available to the civilian population was marked: the armies, and particularly those of the United Nations Command, moved large quantities of food, medicine, and equipment, while civilians frequently had no access to the same resources. Water and sanitation systems in the cities were disrupted by damage and by the loss of power, and in the camps, shortages of clean water were a standing cause of illness. In the north, public health campaigns were organized by the state, which mobilized people for sanitation and vaccination despite the destruction, and some sources report that the state's centralized organization helped to contain epidemics; the evidence, from the state's own records, requires independent verification.

The consequences of hunger and disease were long-lasting. Children who suffered malnutrition in refugee shelters faced lasting health effects, and the loss of adults who died of preventable illness left households without workers. The interruption of farming in the middle of the peninsula, where the front line settled, deprived many families of their land for years, and the post-1953 mine and ordnance contamination of fields added a lasting hazard. The war's toll in these respects does not appear in battlefield casualty figures, and it is unevenly captured in demographic estimates. It is the clearest illustration of the argument that the war's social destruction was much larger than the number of people killed in direct violence.

Historical Vocabulary
Displacement, Forced Migration, Missing Persons, and Civilian Protection

Definition. Displacement is the movement of people away from their homes because of violence or its threat; those who remain within their country are today called internally displaced persons, while refugees in the strict legal sense cross an international border. Forced migration covers movement compelled by violence, coercion, or authorities' orders, including expulsion, abduction, and forced labor. A missing person is one whose fate is unknown to family and authorities. Civilian protection refers to the legal and institutional measures intended to shield noncombatants from violence and deprivation.

Historical context. In Korea in 1950 the terms "refugee" (pinanmin in Korean usage) and related words were applied to people fleeing the fighting within the peninsula, though the 38th parallel was not an internationally recognized border and both Korean governments claimed all of Korea. The 1951 Refugee Convention was drafted during the war and did not address the situation. Missing persons included people killed in secret, abducted, conscripted, or separated by flight.

Why it matters. The vocabulary distinguishes voluntary flight from coerced movement and clarifies that different categories of displaced people had different legal and political status, and different risks.

Potential misunderstanding. Modern definitions should not be read back mechanically. A person who "fled" in 1950 may have been following orders, avoiding conscription, escaping accusation, or being forced, and the sources do not always say which.

Section 71.15

Women, Children, War Widows, and Orphans

Women bore much of the burden of keeping households alive, and the evidence shows them acting as organizers, workers, and decision-makers and not merely as victims. With men conscripted, killed, imprisoned, abducted, or in hiding, women headed households, made decisions about whether and when to flee, and managed the transport of children, the elderly, and household goods across the front lines. In the refugee settlements they carried out the daily work of procuring food and fuel, cooking, washing, and caring for the sick. In the countryside they took over the planting and harvesting of the fields. In the cities and refugee towns they entered the market economy in large numbers, selling food, secondhand goods, and relief items, working as laundresses, domestic servants, and laborers for the military, and in some cases becoming traders who supported extended families. Women also served as nurses and health workers, as volunteers in relief organizations, and as teachers in improvised schools, and women's organizations and church groups ran relief programs. In the north, women were mobilized for agricultural and industrial labor as men went to the army, a transformation of the labor force that the state promoted. This record of agency does not diminish the suffering involved; it shows that the survival of families and communities depended on women's work.

The war also exposed women to sexual violence and exploitation. Testimony and documents indicate that soldiers of the several armies, as well as armed civilians, committed rapes and other sexual violence in the course of the war, though the scale cannot be measured because of underreporting, stigma, and the absence of systematic investigation. Accounts of rape during occupations and advances, of coercion of women in exchange for food or protection, and of the vulnerability of women traveling alone are found in survivor testimony, relief worker reports, and court and police records. Korean researcher Kim Gui-ok's work drawing on the ROK Army's 1956 Rear-Area War History (Personnel) and interviews documents army-operated “special comfort units” (teuksu wianbudae) established during the war and continuing into the early postwar years. Accounts include testimony of coercion, but the surviving evidence does not independently establish every woman's circumstances or a complete national tally. The subject requires further original-language archival and oral-history research (see contemporaneous reporting on Kim's documentary findings). The vulnerability of poor and displaced women to economic exploitation in the camp towns that grew around military bases is also documented, though it belongs more fully to the postwar history of the American military presence. These matters have been little discussed in public until recent decades, and the testimony available is limited by social stigma.

War widows faced particular hardship. Large numbers of women lost husbands to the fighting, to executions, to abduction, or to disappearance, and under the social norms of the period their situation was precarious. Widows in the south were often left without the support of the husband's family or land, and in cases where the husband had been killed as a suspected leftist, they faced stigma and surveillance as well as poverty. Some received assistance from government or charitable programs, but the support available was limited, and many supported children through petty commerce, domestic work, or day labor. Women whose husbands were missing, whether abducted to the north or killed in secret, lived with uncertainty that prevented remarriage or legal settlement of property.

Children experienced the war as a loss of parents, home, and school. Many were separated from their families in flight or by death, and some were left on the roads or in the care of strangers. Estimates of the number of war orphans in the south, commonly given at around one hundred thousand though uncertain, reflect the scale of the problem. Orphanages were established or expanded by Korean religious and charitable organizations, by foreign missionaries and relief agencies, and by chaplains and soldiers of the United Nations forces, who supported institutions near their units. Conditions in orphanages varied from the well-run to the severely overcrowded. In December 1950, at the time of the Seoul evacuation, the U.S. Air Force carried approximately one thousand orphans by air from Seoul to Jeju Island in an operation later called Kiddy Car; the figure is approximate and the episode is among the best-known examples of wartime child rescue. Some children worked as shoeshine boys, newspaper sellers, or street vendors, and others joined or were pressed into service with military units as messengers and laborers. Schooling was interrupted, but both governments gave education a high priority, and in the south, classes continued in tents, on hillsides, and in rented buildings. It is important to distinguish this wartime orphan relief from the large international adoption system that developed after the armistice, beginning in the mid-1950s, which has its own history and belongs to the postwar chapters.

Everyday-Life Study

The Decisions of a Displaced Household

Source label: The household described below is an analytical composite. It is not an actual family and not any survivor's recollection. It is constructed from patterns documented across survivor testimony, relief agency reports, the Truth and Reconciliation Commission's case records, and scholarly studies of wartime displacement, and each decision it describes corresponds to choices recorded in those sources. Students who wish to hear identifiable survivors should consult published oral-history collections and the testimony in the Commission's case files, listed in the source register.

Consider a household of the kind that appears repeatedly in the sources: a widowed or husband-absent woman, her children, and an elderly relative, living in a village or small town in a region that the front line is approaching. The first decision is whether to leave at all. Reasons to stay were real: the household's land, animals, and stored grain; the difficulty of moving an elderly or sick relative; the absence of any relative in the south; and uncertainty about which authority was safer. Reasons to leave included the proximity of fighting, rumors of arrests, a husband or brother already marked as a leftist or a police officer, or memories of an earlier occupation. The documented pattern is that households with someone who had held office, belonged to a political organization, or been identified as an opponent of the prevailing authority were more likely to leave, or to split, with some members going and others staying to protect the property.

The second decision concerned what to carry and how. Testimony commonly mentions rice or other grain, cooking pots, bedding, and documents such as household registers when they could be saved; items that could be sold, such as cloth, were prized. Choices were constrained by the capacity to carry loads on foot or on an A-frame, and by the knowledge that roads were dangerous, so that families often traveled at night or by side routes. The third decision concerned destination: toward relatives, toward a port, toward a town the authorities had designated. The fourth concerned work and subsistence on arrival: registering for rations, finding labor, selling goods in markets, placing children in schools or orphanages, or sending a child to live with relatives. The fifth, in winter, concerned whether to move again. In each case, documented households made decisions under severe constraint, with incomplete information, and with consequences that could not be foreseen.

Section 71.16

Divided Families, Abductions, Forced Movement, and the Destruction of Community Life

Many movements across the peninsula during the war were not voluntary. In the summer of 1950, North Korean authorities took a considerable number of South Koreans north when they retreated in September, including politicians, public officials, scholars, journalists, clergy, and technicians, along with others seized in the course of the occupation or marched north as prisoners. Members of the National Assembly, university professors, and well-known cultural figures are documented among them. A 1952 South Korean Ministry of Public Information list recorded 82,959 reported abductees, according to the National Memorial for Abductees during the Korean War, and associations of families have claimed higher numbers; the North Korean government has denied that abductions on this scale took place, and some of those who went north did so voluntarily, in sympathy with the northern government or to flee punishment. The figures therefore represent claims and lists based on family reports, not verified totals, and the proportion of forced and voluntary movement is not known. The fate of many abductees is uncertain; some died on the marches and in the first winter, others lived in the north, and a few were later able to communicate with relatives.

Movements in the other direction also involved coercion. During the northern retreat in late 1950 and the later evacuations, United Nations and South Korean forces moved some civilians from threatened areas, sometimes with consent and sometimes by order, including residents of islands and coastal areas and inhabitants removed from front-line zones. Political prisoners and persons classified as suspect were also moved. The two governments also pressed able-bodied men into service as laborers and porters, and the lines between evacuation, conscription, and detention were not always clear. The movement of civilians from the zones near the changing front, and the creation of depopulated belts, was part of military policy on both sides, with consequences for those moved.

The fragmentation of households was extensive. Families lost members in the confusion of flight, when children and elderly relatives were separated from the group on crowded roads, bridges, railway stations, and ports. Hungnam in December 1950 and the Han River crossings in January 1951 are among the places where the sources record families divided. Some members stayed behind to look after property or the elderly, intending to rejoin the others within days or weeks. Some were killed, conscripted, or imprisoned. Documents that would have helped trace relatives, including household registers, land deeds, and photographs, were destroyed or left behind, and many registration offices were burned. After the front stabilized, the land between the lines became a barrier that few could cross, and even letters could not pass. The losses affected the structure of the extended family and lineage, the organization of villages, and the practice of ancestral rites, which depended on knowing the location of graves and the fate of relatives.

The geographic consequence was the military demarcation line eventually established in 1953, which did not follow the 38th parallel but ran across the peninsula along the front as it had settled, so that some localities north of the parallel came under South Korean control and some south of it under North Korean control (Chapter 72). Families whose members were on opposite sides of the line when the fighting ended could not reunite, and there was no mechanism for reunion. Chapter 72 examines how the armistice and the demilitarized zone institutionalized this separation. Here it is enough to note that the changing front converted what was experienced in 1950 as a temporary wartime separation into a long-term division. In the decades that followed, the South Korean government registered separated families; a television broadcast in 1983 searching for dispersed family members led to the reunion of more than ten thousand families, and limited government-organized reunions began in 1985 and occurred intermittently thereafter. Official registers have listed more than one hundred thousand South Koreans seeking relatives in the north, and the great majority of registrants have since died. The experience of separated families belongs both to the history of the war and to the politics of the divided peninsula, and it remains an unresolved humanitarian issue.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From Prewar Political Division to Wartime Separation and Enduring Family Fragmentation

What Changed

  • Between 1945 and 1950, movement across the 38th parallel was difficult but not impossible; many northerners moved south and some southerners moved north, and letters and visits were still possible for some.
  • During the war, movement became forced, sudden, and often irreversible, through flight, evacuation, abduction, and conscription.
  • After 1953, the line, the demilitarized zone, and the ban on communication turned separation into a legally and politically enforced condition.
  • Over decades, memory of the separation moved from lived grief to the subject of state policy, public broadcasting, and commemoration.

What Stayed the Same

  • Political suspicion attached to people on the basis of origin, class, family, or kin on both sides of the line.
  • The importance of family and lineage as the basic unit of Korean social life gave separation a particular weight.
  • Governments on both sides claimed to represent all Koreans while limiting actual contact between them.

When and how did change occur, and who experienced it most directly? The change was abrupt for individuals, occurring in days of flight, and gradual for society, as the line hardened. Those who were separated experienced it most directly; people who remained together experienced greater continuity in household life, though many lost relatives. Why the pattern matters: It explains why the humanitarian consequences of the war outlasted the fighting and why separation became part of how both Koreas understood the division.

Division V · Sections 71.17–71.20 · 1950–1953 Prisoners of War, Captivity, Repatriation, and Historical Synthesis Prisoners and detainees became participants in the political struggle over Korea's future, not simply passive objects of a later exchange.
Section 71.17

Capture, Forced Marches, and Prisoner-of-War Conditions

Soldiers captured by North Korean and Chinese forces faced conditions that changed over the course of the war and differed by nationality, time of capture, and location. In the first months, the North Korean army's treatment of prisoners was often brutal. Documented episodes include the killing of a group of captured American soldiers near Hill 303 on the Nakdong front in August 1950, killings of wounded and captured soldiers during retreats, and the shooting of prisoners in Daejeon and elsewhere in September, mentioned in Section 71.10. In the autumn, as United Nations forces advanced, North Korean guards marched groups of American and other UN prisoners north in severe conditions, including the march that survivors later called the "Tiger" death march, during which a large share of the prisoners died of exhaustion, exposure, hunger, and execution of those who could not continue. The sources for these events are principally the accounts of survivors and the investigations by United States authorities after the war, and while the occurrence of the killings is well supported, the precise numbers in each case depend on those accounts.

Prisoners taken by the Republic of Korea's soldiers, in many cases, faced a different fate. South Korean soldiers captured by North Korean and Chinese forces were often treated as traitors and, in many cases, compelled to serve in the Korean People's Army, work as laborers, or undergo political instruction. The fate of South Korean prisoners is among the least documented parts of the war. When prisoner exchanges took place in 1953, fewer than ten thousand South Korean soldiers were returned, a number far below the totals of the missing claimed by the South Korean government, which has maintained that tens of thousands remained in the north. Reports from escapees in later decades, particularly from the 1990s onward, have described former South Korean prisoners kept in the north as laborers, including in mines, but these reports are fragmentary, and the number and fate of the individuals concerned cannot be established from open sources.

Chinese forces generally ran prisoner camps along the Yalu River in the northwest, where many American and other UN prisoners were held from late 1950. The first winter, 1950–1951, was by common account the deadliest, with severe shortages of food and medical care, harsh weather, and a high rate of death from disease and malnutrition. Over time the Chinese authorities introduced a policy they described as lenient, combined with a program of political instruction, study sessions, self-criticism, and incentives for statements and cooperation, and they sought propaganda value from prisoners' statements. Interrogation and coercion were used, including punishments for refusal to cooperate. Accounts by former prisoners, memoirs, and American government studies of the conduct of prisoners have been used extensively, and scholarship has examined both the abuses and the attempt to change prisoners' political views. By the end of the war, roughly seven thousand Americans were known to have been captured, and close to forty percent of them died in captivity, according to commonly cited figures; the numbers for other nationalities and for South Korean soldiers are less certain.

The United Nations Command held a much larger number of prisoners, and its obligations and practices require equal scrutiny. From the summer of 1950, American and South Korean forces captured North Korean soldiers, and after the Chinese intervention, Chinese soldiers. By 1952, the United Nations Command held on the order of 170,000 or more persons in its camps, including North Korean and Chinese soldiers and tens of thousands of people who were later classified as civilian internees. The Command stated that it would follow the humanitarian principles of the Geneva Convention on prisoners of war, and the International Committee of the Red Cross visited camps, hospitals, and civilian internment facilities, inspected conditions, and reported to the belligerents. The ICRC's historical account documents its visits and its work on family links, though its access was far greater in the United Nations Command's camps than in those of the other side, which allowed only limited or no access. Prisoners in United Nations camps were generally fed and housed, but the sources record overcrowding, shortages, the use of force by guards, and, as the next section shows, violence among the prisoners.

Verification of death tolls and conditions in camps with limited outside access remains difficult. For the camps of the North Korean and Chinese side, the evidence consists mainly of testimony from returnees, many of whom were in poor health and later interviewed by their own governments, and of documents captured or released later. For the United Nations camps, there are official records, ICRC reports, and prisoner testimony, but these too reflect the interests of their authors. The historian must compare the sources, note what each could observe, and be cautious about numbers. The comparison of prisoner mortality across the two sides is complicated by differences in the periods and populations covered, and it should not be reduced to a simple ranking.

Historical Vocabulary
Prisoner of War, Civilian Internee, Repatriation, Non-Repatriation, and Screening

Definition. A prisoner of war is a member of the armed forces or an associated militia captured by an enemy power, entitled under the Geneva Convention of 1949 to humane treatment. A civilian internee is a civilian held for security reasons, protected under a separate convention. Repatriation is the return of prisoners to their country of origin. Non-repatriation refers to the refusal of a prisoner to return, and the policy of not forcing return. Screening was the procedure by which the United Nations Command interviewed prisoners to learn their preferences about repatriation and to separate civilians from soldiers.

Historical context. The 1949 Convention provides for release and repatriation of prisoners without delay after the end of active hostilities, and the dispute in Korea was over whether this required returning prisoners who did not wish to go. In Korea, categories were blurred: many people held as prisoners were South Koreans pressed into the northern army, and many were civilians. The categories used by the camps' administrators shaped each person's fate.

Why it matters. Classification determined whether a person was treated as a soldier, a civilian, a returnee, or a refuser, and it determined the legal arguments each government made about the prisoner's future.

Potential misunderstanding. A recorded refusal to return is not automatically evidence of free choice; the conditions under which preferences were recorded, including coercion by captors and by other prisoners, must be examined in each case.

Section 71.18

Geoje-do, Prison-Camp Violence, and Ideological Struggle

The largest United Nations prisoner complex was built on Geoje Island, off the southern coast near Busan, beginning in early 1951, when the number of prisoners captured in the offensives of the previous months exceeded the capacity of the camps on the mainland. By 1952, the island held well over one hundred thousand prisoners in a set of compounds, crowded together under guard. The sheer size of the population, the limited number of guards, many of whom were poorly trained, and the shortage of American personnel for the camps made the administration of Geoje extremely difficult. The prisoners included North Korean soldiers, Chinese soldiers, and a large group of civilians and South Koreans who had been conscripted into the northern army or caught up in the fighting, some of whom were in fact not combatants at all.

Within the compounds, the prisoners organized themselves politically. Groups of committed Communists, some of them officers and party members, established control in some compounds, and groups of anti-Communists, some supported by or in contact with South Korean guards and security personnel and with agents associated with the Republic of Korea and the Chinese Nationalist government, controlled others. The leaders of each side imposed discipline by threats and force. Evidence from the period and later research records internal "courts" and punishments, beatings, forced participation in demonstrations and political study, and killings of prisoners accused of disloyalty, including those who wished to return home and those who wished to stay. Bodies of prisoners killed by other prisoners were discovered in compound latrines and elsewhere. Historians continue to debate to what extent the Communist organization on the island was directed from the outside, whether North Korean and Chinese leaders deliberately had officers surrender in order to organize resistance in the camps, and to what extent the movement arose from the conditions of captivity, since the evidence is partial and shaped by the sources' political positions.

Violence also came from the camp administration. A series of incidents between guards and prisoners, in which American and South Korean guards used force against demonstrating prisoners, resulted in many deaths. In February 1952, an effort to screen prisoners in one compound led to a clash in which dozens of prisoners and one American soldier were killed. In other cases, guards fired into compounds, killing and wounding prisoners. Contemporary American diplomatic records acknowledge that camp authorities were involved in inadequate supervision, in the forced political indoctrination of some prisoners, in abuse by prisoner leaders whom the guards tolerated or used, and in beatings and torture. It would therefore be misleading to claim that abuses in United Nations prison camps were committed entirely by one ideological faction. Prisoners were exposed to abuse by guards, by the leaders of both factions, and by the failure of the camp authorities to protect them, and responsibility for that failure lay with the United Nations Command and the officers in charge of the camps.

On May 7, 1952, a group of prisoners in Compound 76 seized Brigadier General Francis T. Dodd, the commander of the camp, when he went to the compound gate to speak with prisoners' representatives, and held him for three days. The prisoners demanded an end to forced screening and to what they described as mistreatment, and they asked that the camp be recognized as having been run improperly. The incident was reported internationally, and the United Nations Command's leaders in Tokyo and Washington faced the problem of how to respond. After negotiations conducted by Dodd's successor, Brigadier General Charles Colson, who signed a statement acknowledging that prisoners had been killed and wounded by guards and that screening would not be forced, Dodd was released on May 10. The Command repudiated the statement as made under duress, and both generals were later reduced in rank. Contemporary documents and later military histories need to be read together. The incident cannot be explained solely as propaganda manufactured by Communist prisoners, since the prisoners' complaints about killings by guards had a basis in the earlier events, and it cannot be explained solely as a spontaneous protest against mistreatment, since the prisoner leadership used it for political and propaganda purposes and to bargain with the Command.

The response was a reorganization of camp security under a new commander, Brigadier General Haydon Boatner, who broke up the large compounds into smaller units to reduce the leaders' control, replaced and reinforced guards, and moved prisoners. On June 10, 1952, American paratroopers entered Compound 76 to carry out the dispersal. In the fighting that followed, according to the commonly cited United Nations Command account, 31 prisoners were killed and 139 wounded, with one U.S. enlisted man killed and 14 other U.S. personnel wounded, according to the contemporary UN Command account (S/2774), reproduced in the United Nations Yearbook for 1952. The dispersal, together with the movement of civilian internees and the changes in guard practice, reduced the large-scale violence, though incidents continued. The camps continued to be a subject of controversy, including over the transfer of the prisoners to smaller islands and the continuing role of anti-Communist groups. The questions of responsibility for protecting prisoners, from both their guards and fellow inmates, are matters that the laws of war place on the detaining power. The events at Geoje show the consequences of failing to meet that responsibility.

Section 71.19

Voluntary Repatriation, Coercion, and the Struggle over Prisoners' Futures

The question of what should happen to prisoners at the end of the war became the main obstacle in the armistice negotiations in late 1951 and 1952, and it is a central problem of this chapter because it forced the belligerents to decide what a prisoner's consent meant. The Communist side argued that the Geneva Convention required the return of all prisoners without exception once hostilities ended. The United Nations Command, led by the United States, argued that prisoners should not be returned against their will, a position given weight by memories of the forced return of Soviet citizens after the Second World War and by the political and propaganda value of prisoners who declined to go home. The positions had human consequences: for the prisoners who wished to go home, the issue was whether they would be held back; for those who did not, it was whether they would be forced to return to governments they feared. The diplomatic dimensions, including the negotiations at Panmunjom and the agreement on a neutral commission to hold prisoners who refused repatriation, belong to Chapter 72.

Screening was the mechanism by which the United Nations Command attempted to determine preferences. In April 1952, interviews conducted by American and Korean personnel in the camps indicated that a large proportion of the prisoners, on the order of seventy thousand of the roughly one hundred seventy thousand then held, wished to return, a result that surprised and disappointed the Command and provoked the Communist side to accuse it of coercion. The figures included the civilian internees whom the screening had classified and who were later released. The reliability of these results is debated. The Communist negotiators argued that anti-Communist leaders, South Korean guards, and Nationalist Chinese agents had intimidated prisoners into declaring against return, and the historical record contains evidence of such pressure, including threats, beatings, and in some cases the tattooing of anti-Communist slogans on prisoners. At the same time, evidence also shows that many prisoners genuinely did not want to return, for reasons that included fear of punishment as traitors, political conviction, and, for South Koreans conscripted into the northern army, the desire to go home to the south. Both coercion and genuine preference existed, and they cannot be cleanly separated in individual cases. A recorded choice made in a compound under the control of a faction, in the presence of guards who had power over the prisoner, cannot be read as unconstrained, and the same applies to a choice made under the pressure of the opposite faction.

The International Committee of the Red Cross played a limited and difficult role. It visited camps, monitored conditions, and sought to assist the exchange of information, and it was asked by the parties at various points to take part in questions about prisoners. But the Committee was dependent on the access that belligerents granted, it did not control the screening, and it was cautious about taking sides in the dispute over repatriation. Its reports provide important evidence on conditions, but they could not resolve the question of consent. For prisoners, the Committee's presence offered some protection and a channel for communication, but not a guarantee against coercion.

Syngman Rhee's government opposed the proposed armistice settlement, which would have left the peninsula divided and, in its view, provided no assurance against renewed attack. On the prisoner question, Rhee's government objected to any arrangement that would hold anti-Communist North Korean prisoners under the control of a neutral commission including states friendly to the Communist side, or that might return them to the north. On June 18, 1953, as the negotiators approached agreement, Rhee ordered South Korean military police guarding the camps to release the North Korean prisoners who had declared against repatriation, and in the early hours of that day the guards opened the gates in several camps, including those at Busan, Masan, and Nonsan. The number released is variously reported. The June 18 U.S. diplomatic record reports approximately 25,000 initial escapes, while a subsequent Joint Chiefs account gives 25,131 by that afternoon; some Marine Corps retrospectives summarize the release as approximately 27,000 when discussing the broader operation. Those are source-specific figures and should not be presented as one identical measurement. Some of the prisoners were recaptured, others dispersed into the South Korean population, many of them with the help of South Korean civilians and officials. The human consequences included the severing of the prisoners' ties with the north and the end of their prospects of being held under UN protection, as well as the risks of living as unregistered persons in a wartime society.

The effect on the relationship between Rhee's government and the United Nations Command was severe. The American government, which had not been informed in advance, was angered by the unilateral action, which put at risk the negotiations and the authority of the United Nations Command over the prisoners. The Communist side protested and postponed the talks. American officials responded with a diplomatic effort to secure Rhee's acquiescence to the armistice, discussed in Chapter 72. The episode showed that the South Korean president was willing to use his control of the guards to override the allied command, and it showed that the prisoners' futures were being decided by governments in contest with one another and not by the prisoners themselves.

Primary Source Window IV

Syngman Rhee's June 18, 1953 Statement and Correspondence on the Release of Prisoners

Creator: Syngman Rhee, President of the Republic of Korea · Date: June 18, 1953 · Documents: his letter to UN Commander Mark W. Clark with an enclosed presidential press statement · Documentary source: Foreign Relations of the United States, 1952–1954, vol. XV, part 2, Document 607; for the separate account of the mass escape, see Document 606.

Context: Rhee's public statement asserted personal responsibility for ordering the release; his private letter to Clark explained why he had not notified the UN commander beforehand. The two texts must be read separately. Contemporary U.S. reporting placed approximately 25,000 escapes in the early hours of June 18, followed by smaller additional escapes; later retrospective accounts sometimes give higher cumulative figures.

Editorial paraphrase of the June 18 press release and accompanying letter (not a verbatim quotation): Rhee defended the release of anti-Communist North Korean prisoners as a matter of personal responsibility and as a response to their prolonged detention and possible exposure to further political screening. He acknowledged acting without prior agreement from the UN Command and directed local government officials to assist the released prisoners. In private correspondence he acknowledged that advance notice to Clark would have frustrated his plan. These statements reveal his justifications; they do not establish that every prisoner's wishes were uncoerced.

Analysis: The document reveals the president's rationale, his claim to independent authority over the prisoners' fate, and his use of the principle of non-forcible return to justify an act that also served his political objectives in opposing the armistice. It shows that Rhee presented the prisoners' consent as established, a claim that stood in tension with the evidence of coercion discussed above.

Limitations: It is a political statement by a party to the dispute and does not provide evidence of the prisoners' actual wishes, of the conditions in which they declared them, or of what happened to them afterward. It does not describe the release as the prisoners experienced it. The number of prisoners involved and the details of the operation come from other sources, which differ.

Perspective: The perspective of the South Korean head of state seeking to prevent an armistice he opposed. Missing are the voices of the prisoners, of the prisoners who wished to return, of the United Nations Command, and of the North Korean and Chinese governments.

Questions for students:

  4. What can historians learn from it about the relationship between the Republic of Korea and the United Nations Command?
  5. What can historians not establish from it about the prisoners' consent or about what happened to them afterward?

The release of June 1953 and the long dispute that preceded it demonstrate the wider historical implication of this division. Political captivity was part of the Korean War's conflict over allegiance, identity, and sovereignty. Prisoners were not merely held until the guns fell silent and then exchanged; their classification, their treatment, their political organization, and their preferences were themselves contested territory, and each government sought to use them in claims about the legitimacy of its own cause. For the prisoners, the contest meant coercion from several directions, and for many of them a future decided by others.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has reconstructed the Korean War through the experiences of civilians and prisoners, and it has argued that the war was fought not only between armies but through competing projects of political control over the Korean population. The peninsula's density, the speed with which the front moved, and the rival claims of two governments to sovereignty over all of Korea meant that civilians were exposed repeatedly to flight, suspicion, detention, and violence.

Division I traced displacement as a continuing process. The flight from Seoul in June 1950, the destruction of the Han River bridges, and the movement toward the Pusan Perimeter were followed by the northern retreat, the Hungnam evacuation of December 1950, in which about 91,000 civilians were taken by sea according to the U.S. Navy's account, and the January 1951 exodus from Seoul. Opportunities to flee were unequal, and authorities controlled boarding, crossing, and settlement. The refugee camps and relief efforts that followed combined official institutions, voluntary agencies, religious networks, and local communities, but they could not prevent hunger, disease, and unequal access to help.

Division II examined violence by South Korean state institutions and American forces. The National Guidance Alliance, created in 1949, produced registers that were used in 1950 for preventive detention and execution; police, military police, and army units killed detainees without trial in many localities, including Daejeon. The Truth and Reconciliation Commission confirmed several thousand victims in the cases it investigated and estimated that the real number was much larger; scholarly estimates of the national total vary widely and should not be reduced to one figure. At No Gun Ri in July 1950, American forces killed a large number of refugees, and documents show that refugee-control policies contemplating fire on civilians were circulating at the time, though the documents do not prove what the particular soldiers were ordered to do. After the recapture of Seoul, ordinary survival under occupation was reclassified as collaboration, with executions of unarmed civilians documented in Seoul and Incheon.

Division III turned to North Korea's occupation of the south and to local civil conflict. The occupiers organized people's committees, redistributed land, mobilized labor and recruits, and arrested officials, police, and political opponents; killings during the occupation and especially during the September retreat, as at Daejeon, are documented by contemporary observers and later research. The Sinchon case showed how historians weigh competing atrocity narratives: the North Korean official attribution of a massacre solely to American forces is challenged by Korean scholarship that points to local right-wing violence, but the occurrence of mass killing is not in doubt. Cycles of denunciation and reprisal, and counterinsurgency episodes such as Geochang in February 1951, show that the war was also a set of local civil wars.

Division IV examined bombing, hunger, disease, and social disruption. The air campaign destroyed northern cities and infrastructure, including power stations in 1952 and irrigation dams in 1953, and South Korean cities were devastated by repeated fighting. Women kept households and communities alive through labor, commerce, and caregiving, while facing widowhood and sexual violence; children were orphaned and displaced. Abductions, forced movement, and the changing front fragmented families and turned temporary separation into lasting division.

Division V followed prisoners. Captives of North Korean and Chinese forces suffered killings, forced marches, and deprivation, with conditions varying by time and place, while the United Nations Command held a much larger number in camps where political struggle among prisoners produced killings and coercion and where camp authorities bore responsibility for failures of protection. The Dodd incident of May 1952 and the dispersal of June 1952 illustrated the breakdown of control. The dispute over voluntary repatriation made prisoners' consent a political and historical problem, and Rhee's release of North Korean prisoners on June 18, 1953 showed that governments, not prisoners, were deciding their futures.

Across the five divisions, the chapter has insisted on specificity. South Korean, North Korean, American, and local armed-group actions had different perpetrators, chains of command, objectives, victims, and evidentiary records. Comparison is legitimate, but undifferentiated references to violence by "both sides" erase the distinctions that make accountability possible. Political sympathy did not make a person a combatant, and affiliation did not legally justify summary execution.

The chapter has also emphasized how knowledge about civilian suffering was produced. Contemporary documents, official investigations, survivor testimony, forensic evidence, and scholarly reconstructions each have strengths and limits. Many massacres remained unacknowledged for decades because governments on both sides suppressed or used them, and the work of truth commissions and scholars has changed public understanding. Numbers in this chapter have been attributed to their sources, and overlapping figures have not been added together.

Finally, the chapter has argued that the humanitarian consequences of the war outlasted the fighting. The mass killings, imprisonments, disappearances, and family separations left stigma, silence, and unresolved loss that shaped both Korean states' postwar politics. Chapter 72 takes up the negotiations, prisoner exchanges, the armistice of July 27, 1953, and the demilitarized zone that institutionalized the division.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it changes what the Korean War is understood to be. Campaign narratives describe lines advancing and retreating; this chapter shows what those movements meant for people whose homes lay in between. It also shows that the war's violence was not only external. Governments and armed organizations on the peninsula, with foreign forces beside them, pursued the political control of the population through detention, execution, displacement, and coercion, and the people they targeted were frequently defined by affiliation rather than conduct.

The chapter's larger lesson concerns evidence. The history of civilian suffering was shaped by who could speak and who could not. In South Korea, killings by the state were suppressed for decades; in North Korea, a state narrative of American responsibility crowded out other explanations; in the United States, records of refugee-control policy entered public discussion late. Truth commissions, exhumations, journalists, scholars, and survivors recovered much of what had been hidden, but not everything, and the numbers remain uncertain. Understanding this process is part of understanding the war.

The consequences extended into both Korean states' postwar politics. Anti-communism in the south and revolutionary memory in the north each drew on wartime violence, and each regime used the dead selectively. Families separated by the front became symbols in the politics of division. The treatment of prisoners foreshadowed the armistice negotiations of Chapter 72 and the questions of sovereignty and allegiance that remained unresolved after July 1953.

The work of investigation is not finished. South Korea's Truth and Reconciliation Commission, according to its own announcement, resumed work under a renewed mandate in February 2026; readers should consult its current publications, since that work continues beyond this book's endpoint. The task of reconstructing the experiences of those who had least control over the war, and of identifying the institutions and decisions that placed them in danger, remains a central obligation of any history of Korea.
