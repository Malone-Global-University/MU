Donghak and New Religious Movements

Korean History: Ancient Kingdoms to a Divided Peninsula

Chapter Opening

In the fourth lunar month of 1860, in a mountain valley near the old Silla capital of Gyeongju, a man in his mid-thirties, who had never held office and whose family stood at the margins of the yangban elite, reported that he had been addressed by Heaven. Within four years he would be executed on the order of the Joseon government. Within a generation, the religion he founded would help ignite the largest peasant upheaval in the dynasty's history. This chapter is about the first part of that story, which is too often reduced to a prelude: how Donghak began as a religion, what it taught, how it organized itself, and how it survived the death of its founder.

The man was Choe Je-u (1824–1864), later known to his followers by the pen name Suun. The place was Yongdam, a secluded site in the hills of Gyeongsang Province where his family had a modest holding and where his father, a local Confucian scholar, had once taught. According to Choe's own written testimony, composed within a year or two of the event, he suffered a bodily trembling that he could not explain, heard a voice that identified itself as the Lord on High, and was given a spiritual formula and a talisman with which to heal the sick and to teach. Historians cannot verify the supernatural content of this account, and this textbook does not attempt to. What historians can do is read the testimony as a document, trace what Choe made of the experience, and follow the consequences of his decision to teach.

Those consequences were considerable. Choe gave his teaching the name Donghak, "Eastern Learning," and set it deliberately against Seohak, "Western Learning," the contemporary Korean term for Catholic doctrine and Western knowledge. Yet Donghak was neither a defensive revival of Confucian orthodoxy nor a simple counter-Christianity. It drew on a vocabulary shared with Neo-Confucianism, Daoism, Buddhism, and popular religion, while asserting that each person carried the divine within, and that moral sincerity, disciplined prayer, and the recitation of a short incantation were open to farmers, artisans, women, and impoverished literati alike. It was composed in literary Chinese for educated readers and in vernacular Korean verse for a wider audience.

The government's response was suspicion, then repression. Provincial officials and local Confucian scholars regarded Donghak as heterodox, as a source of illicit gatherings, and, in an age of rural unrest and Western pressure, as a possible vehicle for rebellion. Choe was arrested briefly in 1862, arrested again in late 1863, and executed at Daegu in 1864. Yet the religion did not die with him. His successor, Choe Si-hyeong (1827–1898), led a community of believers into hiding in remote highland districts, preserved the texts, maintained the practices, and rebuilt a network of congregations that would later prove capable of mobilizing tens of thousands of people.

This chapter concentrates on approximately 1860 to 1876. It does not narrate the Donghak Peasant Revolution of 1894, which belongs to Chapter 51, nor does it reconstruct the wider agrarian grievances treated in Chapter 44, the Catholic persecutions and Western military encounters treated in Chapter 45, or the Daewongun's political program discussed in Chapter 47. It uses those chapters as background, and it points forward to later developments only to explain continuity. Its central claim is that Donghak must be understood first as a religion with its own theology, ethics, scriptures, and organization. Its later political importance grew out of that foundation and did not define it from the start.

The evidence is uneven, and students should keep that in view throughout. The most important sources are Choe Je-u's own writings, which were later gathered into the Donggyeong Daejeon and the Yongdam Yusa, but these were printed only in 1880 and 1881, long after his death, and the texts passed through devotional hands. Official records of the Joseon state, including the reports of provincial authorities and the proceedings of the court, preserve the government's view, shaped by criminal-law categories of heterodoxy and sedition. Donghak and, later, Cheondogyo tradition preserves the believers' view, but much of it was written down decades after the events. No single category of source is neutral, and part of the work of this chapter is to show how they can be read against one another.

The chapter has a further purpose within the textbook. Donghak is one of the best examples in Korean history of religious innovation under pressure: a movement that borrowed, adapted, and recombined existing materials into something new, while insisting that it was a distinctively Korean answer to a crisis of the age. Understanding it requires attention to theology and to social history, to the founder and to the ordinary believers, and to the difference between what a religion teaches and what later generations make of it. It prepares students for Chapters 47 and 48, which return to the court and to the opening of Korea, and for Chapter 51, where the networks described here appear on a very different scale.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain the religious, social, and international conditions of the mid-nineteenth century that made a new religious movement possible and appealing.
  2. Trace the life of Choe Je-u from his upbringing in Gyeongju through his spiritual search and the reported experience of 1860.
  3. Distinguish Choe's own description of his religious experience from later devotional and scholarly interpretations of it.
  4. Explain why the movement was called Donghak and why it was neither simply anti-Christian nor a revival of Confucianism.
  5. Analyze the central teaching of si cheonju and show how Choe's conception of Heaven developed during his lifetime and was later reformulated.
  6. Describe Donghak's practices of incantation, talisman use, healing, and discipline, distinguishing what believers held about them from what historical evidence can establish.
  7. Assess how far Donghak's teaching of human dignity challenged Joseon's status hierarchy, avoiding the projection of later egalitarian doctrines onto the 1860s.
  8. Trace the spread of Donghak and the formation of jeop congregations and local leadership.
  9. Explain the reasons for official suspicion, the arrests of 1862 and 1863, and the execution of Choe Je-u in 1864, distinguishing allegations from evidence.
  10. Evaluate the survival of Donghak under Choe Si-hyeong and the significance and limits of the Yeonghae uprising of 1871.
  11. Interpret competing scholarly views of Donghak as a religion, an ethical movement, and a vehicle of social mobilization.

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  2. How should historians treat a founder's report of a revelation whose supernatural claims cannot be verified?
  3. In what sense was Donghak "Eastern," and in what sense was it a response to the West rather than a rejection of everything foreign?
  4. How did Choe Je-u's ideas about Heaven change during his lifetime, and why does it matter not to read later doctrine back into 1860?
  5. Why did a teaching that emphasized the dignity of every person appeal across lines of status, and what did it leave unchanged?

  7. Why did the Joseon state regard Donghak as a danger, and how well did official accusations match what Choe's writings and practices actually show?

  9. What is lost when the early history of Donghak is told only as a prelude to 1894?

Historical Context

By 1860 the Joseon dynasty had governed the peninsula for nearly five hundred and seventy years. Its state ideology was Neo-Confucianism, examined in Chapter 26, which structured the examination system, the ritual calendar, family law, and the self-image of the yangban elite. Buddhism, deprived of state patronage since the early dynasty, survived in mountain monasteries and lay devotion. Shamanic rites, divination, geomancy, and healing practices continued in village and household life. Chapter 40 treats these traditions and the arrival of Catholicism; the point to retain here is that Joseon religious life was plural, that official orthodoxy and popular practice often overlapped, and that the state policed the boundary between them.

The mid-nineteenth century was a period of mounting political and fiscal stress. Chapter 43 explains how the monarchy weakened and how in-law families dominated court politics under the system known as sedo politics. Chapter 44 shows how corruption in the three revenue systems of land tax, military cloth tax, and grain loans pushed peasants into hardship and how popular unrest culminated in the major uprisings of 1862. These conditions did not produce Donghak mechanically. They did create an environment in which many people were open to new explanations and to new forms of community, and in which prophetic literature, rumors of dynastic change, and anxiety about the future circulated widely.

Foreign pressure added a second layer of anxiety. Catholic communities, treated in Chapter 45, had existed in Korea since the late eighteenth century and had been repeatedly persecuted. News from China, where Britain and France had defeated the Qing in the Second Opium War and occupied Beijing in 1860, reached Joseon through the tributary envoys and caused alarm. For observers in the countryside, the lesson seemed to be that the old civilizational center could be overwhelmed, and that Joseon might be next. Choe Je-u's own writings register this fear, and it forms part of the setting for his teaching.

Finally, Gyeongju and its region deserve notice. Gyeongju had been the capital of Silla (Chapters 10 and 14), and its landscape was thick with historical memory. In the nineteenth century it was a provincial center in Gyeongsang Province, a region of old yangban lineages, academies, and dense agrarian settlement, and one that would also be affected by the unrest of 1862. Choe Je-u belonged to this society but not to its upper tier, a position that shaped both his criticism of it and his appeal to people who stood outside it.

Section 46.1

Religious Change and Social Anxiety in Nineteenth-Century Joseon

It is tempting to imagine that Joseon religious life in 1860 was frozen, with an orthodox Confucian elite above and a passive peasantry below. The evidence points elsewhere. Neo-Confucianism was the language of the state and of family ritual, but it did not satisfy every spiritual need. Ancestral rites, which Confucian ritual codified, coexisted with household cults, spirit-propitiating ceremonies conducted by shamans, geomantic beliefs about the siting of graves and houses, and recourse to monks, diviners, and healers in times of illness. Many people moved among these resources without feeling any contradiction. Where the state intervened, it did so mainly to protect Confucian orthodoxy from what it called heterodoxy, and its criminal law, which drew on the Chinese code that Joseon had adopted as supplementary law, treated the spread of unorthodox teachings that misled the people as a punishable offense.

The nineteenth century intensified the demand for religious explanation. Harvests failed in some years, epidemic disease recurred, and the revenue system, as Chapter 44 shows, became an instrument of extortion in many districts. Court politics offered no remedy, and many literate people looked to prophetic and divinatory literature for signs of what was coming. Texts associated with the Jeonggamrok tradition, which spoke of a change of dynasty and the rise of a new ruling house, circulated in manuscript and by word of mouth, as did expectations of a coming age of renewal. The state regarded such literature as seditious, and prophetic claims had featured in earlier uprisings. This does not mean that every rumor was believed by every villager. It means that, for many, the present order seemed neither permanent nor just.

Foreign pressure sharpened the sense of disorder. The suppressed but persistent Catholic communities of Joseon showed that a foreign religion could take root in Korean soil, and the state's persecutions of 1801, 1839, and 1866, discussed in Chapter 45, revealed how anxious the court was about it. The Qing defeat in the Second Opium War and the Anglo-French occupation of Beijing in 1860 deepened that anxiety. For readers in the Korean countryside who learned of these events, the lesson was that the Chinese empire, the model of civilization for Joseon's elite, could no longer be counted on as protector. Later in the century, the slogan of defending orthodoxy and rejecting heterodoxy, associated with conservative Confucian scholars and treated in Chapter 47, would crystallize one response to this anxiety. Donghak was a different response.

Two cautions apply. First, "religious crisis" should not be taken to mean that existing traditions had collapsed. Confucian ritual, Buddhist devotion, and shamanic practice remained vigorous. The better claim is that a gap had opened between what these traditions offered and what some people, especially those outside the elite, were seeking: a way of understanding suffering, a form of community, and a sense of dignity. Second, the evidence for the mid-nineteenth-century religious mood is itself uneven. Official records speak about heterodoxy as a legal problem, Confucian literati write about it as a moral danger, and the testimony of ordinary believers survives mostly through the filter of later Donghak tradition. Historians must reconstruct the setting from these partial sources.

Section 46.2

Choe Je-u: Family Background, Education, and Spiritual Search

Choe Je-u was born in 1824 in the Gyeongju region to a family of the Gyeongju Choe lineage. Family tradition links the lineage to the Silla scholar Choe Chi-won, a claim that ties it to a famous literary past but that cannot be verified in detail. His father, Choe Ok, was a Confucian scholar with a local reputation for learning and for teaching, but he did not hold significant office, and the family belonged to what might be called the lower margins of the yangban order: educated, respectable by local standards, yet without the connections or wealth that sustained the great lineages. Most accounts add that Choe Je-u's mother was a widow who had remarried, and under Joseon rules the sons of remarried women faced restrictions on taking the civil service examinations and holding office. If this account is correct, Choe was barred in principle from the main route to official status, which would help explain why a man of his education never sought a career in government.

The sources for Choe's early life require care. The narrative that exists was largely written by later followers, who had good reasons to emphasize the hardships and spiritual seriousness of the founder's youth. The broad outline is nonetheless consistent: Choe lost his mother as a child and his father while still a young man, received a classical education in Chinese texts, married, and experienced the decline of his family's fortunes. Tradition adds that a fire destroyed the family home. After these reverses he spent a long period, often described as roughly a decade, traveling through different parts of the country, engaged in various occupations and observing the conditions of ordinary people. Later accounts say that he also investigated Buddhism, Daoist practices, divination, and the prophetic literature then circulating, as well as the teachings associated with the West. How much of this reconstruction reflects actual study and how much reflects later hagiography cannot be fully determined.

By the mid-1850s the outline becomes clearer. Choe settled for a time near Ulsan, where he continued the search for a religious answer to what he regarded as the disorder of the age. Donghak tradition reports that he undertook periods of intensive prayer in mountain retreats, including one on Cheoksan, and that a mysterious book was given to him by a Buddhist monk in 1855. Scholars treat the latter story with caution as part of the legendary framework that grew up around the founder, though it may preserve a memory that Choe was in contact with Buddhist circles. In 1859 he returned to the family property at Yongdam near Gyeongju and shortly afterward changed his name to Je-u, written with characters that can be understood to mean relieving the ignorant, an indication of how he now understood his purpose.

What emerges is not the portrait of a revolutionary or an antiquarian but of a serious, restless man from the periphery of the educated elite, formed by the Confucian classics yet excluded from the careers they were meant to serve, who had seen the hardships of rural life at first hand and who sought a response that was religious rather than purely political. That profile helps to explain both why Donghak spoke in a Confucian idiom and why it appealed to those who stood outside Confucian privilege.

Section 46.3

The Yongdam Revelation of 1860

On the fifth day of the fourth lunar month of 1860, which falls in late May by the Gregorian calendar, Choe Je-u reported the experience that he and his followers regarded as the founding of Donghak. According to his own account, he was seized by a bodily shuddering and a sense of cold, and he could not account for it or describe it. He then heard a voice speaking to him, which told him not to be afraid and identified itself as the Lord on High, whom people called Sangje. In the account, Choe asks why he has been addressed and is told that he has been given the way to teach people. In a significant exchange, Choe asks whether he should teach the Western way, and the answer is no. The voice instead says that it has a spiritual talisman and an incantation, which Choe is to use to heal the sick and to teach people, and that by doing so he will spread virtue through the world.

Several features of this account deserve attention. The first is its structure. It combines a physical crisis, an auditory revelation, a dialogue, and a commission, and these elements resemble patterns found in other religious traditions, including the experience of spirit possession and calling that Korean shamanic practice recognized. Some scholars have noted parallels with that tradition, while others emphasize that Choe's teaching differs from shamanism in its content and that he himself distinguished it from the practices of the shamans. The comparison is useful as long as it does not reduce the one to the other. The second feature is the explicit reference to Western teaching. The question of whether to follow the Western way shows that Choe understood his experience partly in relation to the Western religion that was spreading in Korea, and that the answer he reports defined his teaching as an alternative to it.

The third feature concerns the identity of the one who speaks. In the account, the voice calls itself Sangje, a traditional term for the supreme deity of ancient Chinese and Korean religious thought. In the following months and years Choe's language shifted, and by the time he composed his main prose writings he was describing a divine presence within the person rather than only a voice from above. This development, treated in Section 46.5, is one reason this chapter avoids treating the 1860 experience as a complete statement of later doctrine.

Historians face a methodological problem in handling such a report. They cannot confirm or disconfirm a supernatural claim, and they should not pretend to. What they can do is establish when and by whom the account was composed, how it relates to the other sources, how it was received, and what Choe did afterward. For about a year after 1860, according to the tradition, Choe tested the experience privately, practicing the incantation and the talisman, reflecting on their meaning, and observing their effects. In 1861 he began to teach. The importance of the experience for history lies in what it authorized: it gave Choe a sense of mission, it supplied the central practices of the movement, and it provided his followers with a sacred origin story that distinguished their teaching from the Confucian learning in which he had been trained.

Primary Source Window

Choe Je-u's Account of the 1860 Revelation, c. 1861

Creator: Choe Je-u (1824–1864) · Date: composed c. 1860–1861; first printed in the Donggyeong Daejeon in 1880 · Type: autobiographical religious testimony in literary Chinese, in the text known as the Podeokmun ("Spreading Virtue"), with related passages in the Nonhakmun ("Discussion of Learning")

Context: Choe wrote the account after he began to teach, in part to explain the origin and authority of his teaching to educated readers. The texts were circulated in manuscript among followers and were printed only after his death, under the supervision of his successor. Published scholarly translations, including excerpts available through Columbia University's Asia for Educators materials, allow students to read the passages in English.

Paraphrase: The following is a paraphrase of the content, not a direct quotation.

The author describes a period of social disorder and anxiety, including the fear that the Western powers that had overcome China might next threaten Korea. In the fourth month he is overtaken by trembling and a chill he cannot explain, and then hears a voice telling him not to be afraid and identifying itself as the Lord on High. He asks why he is being addressed and what he should teach; he asks whether he is to teach the Western way and is told that he is not. He is told that a spiritual talisman and an incantation have been prepared and that, if he heals people with the one and teaches them the other, he will help to spread virtue throughout the world.

Analysis: The account is valuable as evidence of how Choe understood and presented his own authority. It shows his anxiety about Western power and his decision to define his teaching against the Western way. It also shows a religion beginning with practices rather than doctrine: the talisman and the incantation come first, and the explanation follows. Finally, it shows the audience he addressed, namely literate readers who needed to be persuaded that the teaching was not mere sorcery or Western doctrine.

Limitations: The account cannot establish what, if anything, occurred beyond Choe's own report. It was written after the event, with the aim of persuading readers, and later editors may have altered wording. It tells us little about how ordinary believers understood the experience and nothing about what outside observers made of it at the time.

Perspective: The perspective is that of the founder, a literate man addressing an educated audience. The perspectives of followers who could not read Chinese, of Confucian opponents, and of provincial officials are not represented, and must be sought elsewhere.

Questions for students:

  1. What does the source say Choe experienced, and what was he told to do?
  2. Who created it, when, and under what circumstances?
  3. What purposes may have shaped the account, given its likely audience?
  4. What can historians learn from it about Choe's concerns and the setting of his teaching?
  5. What can historians not establish from this source alone?
Section 46.4

Eastern Learning in an Age of Western Learning

In 1861, as followers gathered and visitors asked what his teaching was called, Choe gave it the name Donghak, Eastern Learning. The name was a statement. Seohak, Western Learning, was the established Korean term for the body of teachings that had entered through China, including Catholic doctrine and Western science, and for the Korean Catholic community that practiced it. By calling his teaching Donghak, Choe placed it in explicit opposition to Seohak. In the Nonhakmun, he explains the name in simple terms: the teaching arose in the East, in Korea, and its author was born and received it there. He adds that its way is the Way of Heaven, so that the name Donghak refers to its place of origin and not to a limited geographic or ethnic boundary.

It would be a mistake to read Donghak as merely anti-Christian. The relationship was more complicated. Choe used the term Cheonju, Lord of Heaven, which Korean Catholics used for God, and some of his critics and later scholars have asked how far his religious language drew on the Catholic vocabulary then circulating in Korea. Choe himself rejected the connection. In the Nonhakmun he acknowledges that the Western teaching and his own spoke in similar terms about Heaven, but argues that they differ in what matters most: in his view the Western teaching lacked the incantation and the practice of divine presence that he taught, and in his judgment its prayers were directed too much toward individual benefit. Scholars continue to debate how much Choe knew of Catholic teaching and how far Catholicism shaped Donghak, and the evidence does not support a confident answer in either direction.

It would be equally wrong to read Donghak as a revival of Confucianism. Choe drew heavily on the moral vocabulary of Confucianism, including virtue, sincerity, reverence, and the ordering of human relationships, and Donghak believers generally continued to practice Confucian family ritual. But the foundation of his teaching was not the study of the classics. It was an experience of Heaven and a set of practices open to those who could not read. Choe also used terms from Daoist and Buddhist traditions and from popular religion, and he did not present his teaching as a restoration of any of them. He asserted that it was a new revelation suited to a new age. This claim of newness is one of the features that made Donghak both attractive to its followers and threatening to the guardians of orthodoxy.

Donghak's choice of an "Eastern" label also had a political meaning. In a period when the Qing empire had been humbled and Western powers were arriving in East Asia, the claim that Korea possessed its own source of spiritual and moral authority had obvious appeal. It should not be confused with the modern nationalism that developed later and that would find Donghak a congenial ancestor, as Chapters 53 and 56 discuss. In the 1860s the word "Eastern" reflected a sense of place and civilizational belonging more than a claim of national sovereignty.

Historical Vocabulary
Donghak (東學) and Seohak (西學)

Definition. Donghak, "Eastern Learning," is the name Choe Je-u gave to his religious teaching in 1861. Seohak, "Western Learning," was the contemporary Korean term for Western knowledge and, above all, for Catholic doctrine and the Catholic community.

Historical context. Seohak entered Korea from China from the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries and became, after the state's persecutions, a label for a forbidden religion. Donghak was named in explicit contrast to it, in an environment in which any association with Seohak could bring severe punishment.

Why it matters. The paired names show how Donghak understood itself and how its opponents were likely to misread it. They also help explain why official suspicion could attach to a movement that defined itself against the West.

Potential misunderstanding. "Donghak" is sometimes read as a nationalist or ethnic label, or as a simple rejection of Christianity. In 1861 it meant a teaching that arose in the East and claimed the Way of Heaven. The movement was renamed Cheondogyo, "Religion of the Heavenly Way," only in 1905.

Section 46.5

Si Cheonju: Serving the Lord of Heaven

The central formula of Donghak is si cheonju (侍天主), usually rendered "to serve" or "to bear" the Lord of Heaven. It appears in the twenty-one-character incantation, or jumun, that Choe taught his followers, in which the phrase is followed by lines about the harmony of Heaven's workings, about never forgetting, and about knowing all things. The word at the center, si (侍), can mean to wait upon, to attend, or to hold within oneself, and Choe offered an explanation of it that has been central to all later interpretation. In the Nonhakmun, he says in effect that si means that within each person there is a divine spirit, that outside there is a transforming energy, and that each person, recognizing this, does not separate from it.

This gloss makes si cheonju more than a form of reverence directed upward. It asserts that Heaven is not only above or beyond but present in the person and in the surrounding world, and that the religious task is to realize and maintain that presence. In Korean-language verse Choe uses the term Hanulnim, "Lord Heaven," which gave the teaching an accessible, personal address. Interpreters differ on how to classify this conception. Some stress that it preserves a personal deity who addresses the believer and hears prayer, others stress its immanence, and some describe it in terms that relate Heaven to the world without identifying them. The evidence of Choe's writings supports elements of each, and students should treat the labels as modern interpretive tools rather than as Choe's own vocabulary.

A crucial point, emphasized in recent scholarship, is that Choe's conception of Heaven developed during his lifetime. In the 1860 account the divine presence is largely external, a voice that speaks from outside and issues a command. In the prose works of 1861 the emphasis moves toward the spirit within the person and toward the transforming energy that pervades the world. The Korean verse composed in the same years combines a vivid sense of Hanulnim as one who speaks to and cares for the people with exhortations to cultivate the mind. These variations do not make the teaching incoherent; they show a thinker working out the implications of an experience while teaching and writing under pressure.

The later history of the teaching adds further layers. After Choe's death, Choe Si-hyeong developed a set of sayings that drew out the ethical implications of si cheonju, including the instruction to serve people as one would serve Heaven. In the early twentieth century, under Son Byeong-hui, the religion adopted the formula that humanity is itself Heaven and reorganized itself as Cheondogyo. These are important developments, and the Cheondogyo tradition often presents them as the unfolding of what Choe taught in 1860. Historians distinguish between Choe's own formulations and these later ones, not to diminish the later teachers but to preserve the chronology of how the ideas developed. Attributing "humanity is Heaven" to the founder in 1860 would be an anachronism.

Historical Vocabulary

Why it matters. It is the core of Donghak's teaching and the basis for its later ethical developments, including the principle of respect for every person.

Potential misunderstanding. Cheonju in Donghak is not simply the Christian God, and later Cheondogyo teachings such as "humanity is Heaven" should not be projected back onto Choe Je-u's original formulations.

Section 46.6

Mind, Vital Energy, and Moral Cultivation

If si cheonju states the relationship between Heaven and humanity, Choe's teaching on mind and vital energy explains how that relationship is realized in practice. The Chinese concept of gi (氣), vital or material energy, is common to Confucian, Daoist, and popular thought in East Asia, and Neo-Confucian philosophers had debated its relationship to principle and to the mind at length. Choe's incantation begins with jigi (至氣), "ultimate energy," which he describes as formless yet active, a pervasive force through which Heaven acts. To receive and hold this energy was, in his teaching, the meaning of the invocation that opens the incantation.

Moral cultivation, in Choe's account, depends on the state of the mind. His writings emphasize guarding the mind and rectifying the energy, a formulation that echoes the Confucian concern with the cultivation of the mind but redirects it toward the realization of Heaven's presence. They also emphasize sincerity, reverence, and trust as the conditions of religious practice, virtues that Confucian moral teaching also esteemed but that Choe tied to the practice of the incantation and to ordinary moral conduct. The transformation he describes is not a withdrawal from the world but a change in how one lives in it: honesty in dealings, respect within the household, and care for others.

Comparisons help to locate Donghak, but they must be made with care. With Neo-Confucianism, Donghak shares an interest in mind, energy, and moral cultivation, but it differs in giving priority to an experience of Heaven and to devotional practice rather than to textual study and the investigation of principle, and in opening that practice to people who could not read the classics. With Daoism, it shares the vocabulary of energy and a concern with health and long life, visible in the name of the talisman as an elixir, but it does not pursue immortality through alchemical or meditative methods in the manner of Daoist adepts. With Catholic theology, it shares a vocabulary of Heaven's Lord and a practice of prayer, but it lacks the structures of church, priesthood, and sacrament and does not teach the doctrines of incarnation or of a personal afterlife in the Catholic sense. Buddhist influence is visible in some terms and practices, though Choe did not present his teaching as Buddhist. Donghak is best understood as a recombination of these materials around a new center rather than a synthesis whose parts can be neatly assigned to their sources.

The social implication is significant. Because the path to Heaven ran through sincerity, reverence, and practice, it was in principle open to anyone willing to follow it. A farmer who could not read could chant the incantation, keep the discipline, and hold the faith with the same standing as a scholar. This democratizing implication did not abolish distinctions in the community, but it gave Donghak a different relation to learning than that of the Confucian schools, and it is one reason the movement attracted those who stood outside the educated elite.

Section 46.7

Incantations, Talismans, Healing, and Religious Discipline

For most believers the heart of Donghak was not doctrine but practice. The principal practice was the recitation of the jumun, the incantation received in 1860. It has two parts: a short invocation of eight characters, calling the ultimate energy to descend, and a main formula of thirteen characters, built around si cheonju. Believers recited it aloud, individually and in groups, as a form of prayer and a means of joining oneself to Heaven's presence. Its brevity and rhythm made it easy to learn without literacy, and its repetition built a shared practice among people who might otherwise have had little in common.

The second practice was the use of the yeongbu, the spiritual talisman said to have been given in the revelation. According to Donghak tradition and to hostile official accounts, it was a drawn or written sign which believers burned, mixed the ashes with water, and drank. Choe called it an elixir of the immortals, and reportedly said that he had tested it on himself. Sources associate its design with symbols drawn from the prophetic and popular tradition, including a doubled bow sign that appeared in the Jeonggamrok literature. For the state, the talisman was precisely the sort of object that characterized sorcery and heterodox practice; for believers, it was a vehicle of Heaven's healing power.

Healing was one of the most important reasons people came to Donghak. In a society where physicians were costly and often unavailable, and where epidemic disease recurred, a religion that promised healing through prayer and a talisman was attractive. Followers reported recoveries, and these reports spread the religion's reputation. Historians cannot assess the medical efficacy of the talisman and should not pretend to. They can note that illness in the period was understood in religious as well as physical terms, that practices such as prayer, the support of a community, and the expectation of recovery could affect how illness was experienced, and that the reputation for healing was a significant factor in recruitment. What believers held to be true about healing and what historians can verify are different matters, and this chapter keeps them separate.

Daily religious discipline gave Donghak its character as a way of life. Believers were expected to recite the incantation regularly, to keep their minds sincere and their conduct honest, and to observe the ethical norms of the community. Reports of Choe's gatherings also mention a sword song and associated movements, which Donghak tradition interprets as a spiritual exercise and which hostile officials later cited as evidence of martial intentions. The truth probably lies in the ambiguity of the practice: a rhythmic, collective performance that believers took as devotion and that outsiders could read as drill. As later sections show, such ambiguity was dangerous in a state that regarded unauthorized assembly with suspicion.

Section 46.8

Human Dignity, Social Hierarchy, and the Meaning of Equality

Joseon society, as Chapters 29 and 42 explain, was organized by hereditary status. Yangban, middle people, commoners, and the unfree nobi had different legal obligations and different degrees of access to education and office, and gender hierarchies shaped family and public life. Donghak arose within this order, and one of the questions historians ask is how far it challenged it. The question needs a careful answer, because a simple reply either overstates or understates the movement's significance.

The core of Donghak's teaching, that Heaven is present in each person who serves it, carried an implication of equal dignity before Heaven. Choe's own life supplied examples. Donghak tradition reports that he freed two female slaves of his household, taking one as an adopted daughter and the other as a daughter-in-law, though the source of this account is the devotional tradition and it should be treated with that qualification. His Korean-language verse was written in a form accessible to women and commoners, many of whom could read Hangeul but not literary Chinese (Chapter 27). The community's gatherings, according to hostile reports, brought together people of different status in a way that scandalized conservative observers. Women participated as believers, though the surviving sources for their experience are thin.

At the same time, Choe did not call for the abolition of the status system or for the dismantling of yangban privilege. His teaching continued to uphold Confucian family ethics, including the obligations between parents and children and between husband and wife, and Donghak families generally continued to practice Confucian ancestral rites. The principle of the equal presence of Heaven in all persons coexisted with the acceptance of social roles. Later Donghak teachers drew more radical conclusions: Choe Si-hyeong's emphasis on respect for all persons, including women, children, and servants, went further than anything attributable securely to Choe Je-u, and the abolition of status distinctions in 1894, discussed in Chapter 52, was the work of a reforming government and not of Donghak.

The historically careful position is therefore that Donghak contained the seeds of a theology of human dignity that could and later did support more egalitarian claims, that it appealed across status lines in practice, and that it did not in the 1860s articulate the modern doctrine of social equality. Reading it as such would impose twentieth-century categories on a nineteenth-century religion, and would also miss what was actually distinctive: a religious claim about the standing of every person before Heaven in a society that distributed standing unequally.

Section 46.9

Protecting the Country and Bringing Peace to the People

Two phrases in Choe's writings link his religious teaching to the condition of the country. Boguk anmin (輔國安民), "aiding the state and bringing peace to the people," names an aim that was conventional in Confucian political language. Gwangje changsaeng (廣濟蒼生), "widely rescuing the multitudes," expresses a broader salvific aspiration. Choe laments in his writings that he and his contemporaries lacked a plan to achieve the first, and presents his teaching as the means. The relationship among these ideas needs to be understood carefully.

For Choe, the route to social and political peace ran through moral and religious regeneration. If the people recognized Heaven within themselves, cultivated sincerity, and conducted themselves accordingly, the disorder of the age would be remedied. His writings express anxiety about the Western threat, citing the fate of China, and imply that a spiritually renewed Korea would be better able to resist it. They also contain, in the Korean verse especially, criticism of arrogance and selfishness among the powerful, though the surviving texts do not call for the overthrow of the king or the dynasty. Later Donghak tradition speaks of a coming new age, sometimes called the opening of a "Later Heaven," and expectations of renewal were certainly present in the movement's atmosphere, though how fully they were articulated by Choe himself is a matter on which scholars differ.

This combination explains why historians describe Donghak as both a religion and a social force without reducing it to either. The ethical program was explicitly religious, and its political implications were framed in the language of loyal service to the state. Yet a movement that promised to protect the country and rescue the multitudes, that gathered people across the countryside, and that spoke of a changing age could be read by officials as a threat, however loyal its own self-understanding. The gap between what Choe intended and what the state feared is central to the story told in the remainder of the chapter.

Section 46.10

The Donggyeong Daejeon and Yongdam Yusa

Choe Je-u composed his writings between 1860 and 1863, in two registers that reflect two audiences. For educated readers he wrote in literary Chinese, the language of the Confucian classics and of Joseon administration. These prose and verse works include the Podeokmun, which narrates the revelation and the mission; the Nonhakmun, a dialogue on the nature of the teaching; and the Suddeokmun, on the cultivation of virtue; along with shorter texts, poems, and ritual prayers. Later gathered, they form the Donggyeong Daejeon, "Great Compendium of Eastern Scripture." For a broader audience, including many who could not read Chinese, he composed verse in Korean in the gasa form, a rhythmic, narrative style suitable for recitation and singing. These are the texts of the Yongdam Yusa, "Songs Left from Yongdam," and include works such as the Gyohunga ("Song of Instruction"), the Anshimga ("Song of Peace of Mind"), and the Dodeokga ("Song of the Way and Virtue").

The choice of languages mattered. Literary Chinese addressed the educated, argued with Confucian opponents on their terms, and gave the teaching the dignity of the classical medium. Korean verse reached women, farmers, and artisans, and allowed the teaching to be memorized and performed, an advantage in a society where oral transmission was the main channel for most people. The combination is a reminder of the multilingual character of Joseon culture, discussed in Chapter 41, and of the role Hangeul had come to play in vernacular religious and literary expression since its creation in the fifteenth century.

The history of the texts requires a distinction between composition and publication. During Choe's lifetime the writings circulated in manuscript among followers. After his execution, the possession of these texts was dangerous, and Choe Si-hyeong's followers copied, hid, and transmitted them. The Donggyeong Daejeon was first printed in 1880 in Inje, in Gangwon Province, and the Yongdam Yusa was printed in 1881. Both dates fall after the main period of this chapter, and the printed texts reflect editorial decisions made by the community in the 1880s, including selection and arrangement. Later editions introduced further variations, and scholars compare versions to identify differences. Students should therefore treat the printed scriptures as reliable guides to Choe's teaching but not as untouched documents of 1860–1863.

Section 46.11

The First Believers and the Spread of Donghak

The first believers were largely people close to Choe: relatives, neighbors, and acquaintances in the Gyeongju area who had observed his transformation and who were drawn by his reported healing and teaching. From this local core the movement spread through personal networks. A believer who found relief from illness or comfort in the teaching brought a family member or a neighbor, and the new believer in turn spread the word through kinship and village connections. Because Donghak required no literacy and little equipment, it could travel easily along existing social routes.

Early expansion proceeded across northern and eastern Gyeongsang Province and, in 1861–1862, when Choe left Gyeongju for a period in Namwon in Jeolla Province, into the southwest. The surviving sources do not give reliable counts of believers. Official reports and later Donghak tradition both suggest rapid growth by 1862–1863, but the figures they give are not independent, and the safest statement is that the movement had grown beyond a local circle to a regional network of considerable size.

Why did people join? Several appeals reinforced one another. The religious message offered direct access to Heaven without the mediation of scholars or priests, and with a short practice anyone could learn. The promise of healing answered a pressing need. The ethical teaching offered dignity to those whom the status order treated as inferior, and the community offered mutual support in a period of economic insecurity. For some, the movement also spoke to anxiety about foreign threat and political disorder. Believers included farmers, artisans, petty traders, women, minor local functionaries, and impoverished literati; the evidence does not support a claim that any one stratum dominated. Different groups joined for different reasons, and the same person might be moved by several.

Everyday Life and Social History

Living as a Believer in Mid-Nineteenth-Century Gyeongsang

For a believer in a Gyeongsang village in the early 1860s, Donghak was lived in the rhythm of ordinary life. A farmer might rise to recite the incantation before work, observe the moral discipline of honest dealing within the household and neighborhood, and attend gatherings at which teaching was shared and the scriptures, often in the form of Korean verse, were chanted. A woman who could not read Chinese might learn the songs by hearing them and carry the teaching into the household. A sick family member might be treated with prayer and the talisman, perhaps alongside whatever other remedies the family could afford.

Belief carried risks. Households that joined could be denounced by neighbors, particularly by local scholars who regarded Donghak as heterodox, and officials could investigate, fine, or arrest believers. Because the community met in groups, secrecy became a practical concern. Most surviving sources about ordinary believers come from official records of investigation and from later devotional memory, so the voices of the believers themselves are heard only indirectly. Historians must read these sources for what they imply about daily life while keeping in mind that both were shaped by purposes other than documenting it.

Section 46.12

Religious Communities, Jeop Leaders, and Local Networks

As the number of believers grew, the movement needed a structure. The structure that emerged was the jeop (接), a congregation of believers gathered around a local leader, the jeopju (接主). According to Donghak tradition, in the twelfth lunar month of 1862 Choe formally appointed jeopju in a number of districts, with tradition giving the number as sixteen. The precise number and the details of appointment rest on later tradition, but the existence of the system by 1862–1863 is generally accepted.

The jeop performed several functions. It organized regular gatherings for recitation and teaching, it provided a framework for the discipline of members, it relayed instructions and texts between the founder and distant believers, and it offered mutual support. Because the jeopju was often a respected local person who had become a believer, the structure rested on existing local authority. Itinerant teachers, among them Choe himself, traveled to instruct and encourage congregations, binding them into a larger network.

The jeop differed from other Joseon associations. Village compacts, mutual-aid societies, and lineage organizations were based on residence, kinship, or shared economic interest, while Buddhist lay associations and shamanic circles had their own religious networks. The Donghak jeop was a religious community defined by shared belief and practice, recruiting across lines of kinship and, in principle, status, and linked beyond the village to a translocal leadership. That quality made it effective for diffusion and survival and also made it conspicuous to the state, which was suspicious of associations that crossed local boundaries and answered to a leader outside official channels.

Jeop (接) and Jeopju (接主)

Historical context. The system was instituted by Choe Je-u in 1862 according to tradition, and became the basic unit of Donghak organization. Later, under Choe Si-hyeong, larger units and more elaborate offices were introduced, but those developments lie beyond this chapter.

Why it matters. The jeop provided the framework through which the movement spread, held together under persecution, and, in 1894, mobilized in large numbers.

Potential misunderstanding. A jeop was not a military unit. It was a religious congregation, though its organization could later be used for other purposes.

Section 46.13

Official Suspicion and the Growing Conflict with Joseon Authorities

Opposition began locally. In 1861, as Donghak spread in the Gyeongju region, local Confucian scholars denounced it as heterodox. Some objected to its claim of new revelation, some to the mixing of statuses at its gatherings, some to what they saw as the superstitious character of the talisman and incantation, and some to its association, in their eyes, with Western teaching. These complaints reached the district and provincial authorities. In the autumn of 1861, facing mounting hostility, Choe left Gyeongju and spent several months in Namwon, in Jeolla Province, where he continued to write and teach. He returned to the Gyeongju region in 1862.

In 1862 the pressure intensified. That year saw a wave of popular uprisings across the southern provinces, among them the rising at Jinju in Gyeongsang, treated in Chapter 44. Officials were acutely sensitive to any gathering that might become a focus of unrest. In the autumn of 1862 Choe was arrested in Gyeongju. Several hundred believers, according to tradition, gathered to petition for his release, and he was freed. The episode shows both the strength of the community's loyalty and the risk it ran: a crowd demanding the release of a prisoner was itself the kind of assembly officials feared.

The central problem for the state was the combination of features that made Donghak distinctive. A religion with a new revelation, a talisman, and an incantation fell under the category of heterodox teaching. A network of congregations under a leader of unclear background looked like an unauthorized organization. Suggestions of association with Western teaching raised the specter of Catholic persecution cases. And in a year of uprisings, the speed of Donghak's growth suggested to some officials that it might be more than religious. Choe's response was to insist on the religious and loyal character of his teaching and to organize his followers more systematically, steps that strengthened the movement even as they made it more visible. By the autumn of 1863 the central government had become concerned enough to order an investigation, which led directly to the events described in the next section.

Section 46.14

The Arrest and Execution of Choe Je-u, 1863–1864

In 1863, before the second arrest, Choe took a step that would prove decisive for the movement's survival: he designated Choe Si-hyeong as his successor, entrusting him with leadership of the community. Late in the year, a royal envoy dispatched from the capital arrested Choe Je-u in the Gyeongju region, together with a number of followers, and began to take him toward Seoul. In the same weeks, King Cheoljong died and the young Gojong was enthroned under the regency of his father, the Heungseon Daewongun (Chapter 47). The prisoners were turned back toward Daegu, the provincial capital, where they were interrogated by the provincial authorities.

The proceedings centered on the charge of heterodox teaching, the offense of misleading the people with an unorthodox doctrine. Officials also pressed on the matters that most worried the state: whether Donghak had links with Western teaching, whether its gatherings and sword song indicated rebellion, and whether the talisman and incantation were sorcery. Official records sometimes refer to Choe by an earlier name, which reflects the way the proceedings treated him as an offender rather than as a teacher. The authorities concluded that the offense was established, and the sentence was death. Choe was executed at Daegu on the tenth day of the third lunar month of 1864, in the spring by the Gregorian calendar, and several followers were punished.

The distinction between official allegation and demonstrable fact is essential. Choe's surviving writings contain no call for armed rebellion and no plan to overthrow the dynasty; they express loyalty to the state in the conventional language of aiding the country and bringing peace to the people. The evidence of association with Catholicism is similarly weak, and Choe's own texts define his teaching against Western Learning. At the same time, the state did not act on mere fantasy. It faced a rapidly growing, translocal religious organization, led by a man who claimed direct revelation, in a period of widespread unrest. Within the legal and political framework of the time, regarding such a movement as dangerous was not irrational, though the response was severe and the evidence of actual sedition was thin. The execution was an act of a state defending orthodoxy and order, and it was also a calculated response to a perceived risk.

Cause and Effect

Why the Joseon State Suppressed Donghak

Long-term causes

A state ideology that policed the boundary of orthodoxy; a legal tradition against heterodox teaching and unauthorized assembly; the memory of earlier rebellions that had used prophecy and religion; and the long history of persecuting Catholicism.

Short-term causes

The rapid growth of Donghak after 1860; hostility from local scholars; the organization of congregations; and the widespread uprisings of 1862, which made officials unusually alert to gatherings.

Immediate trigger and event

The central government's decision in late 1863 to investigate, the arrest of Choe Je-u, and his execution at Daegu in 1864, in the first months of the Gojong regency.

Immediate and long-term effects

The loss of the founder and dispersal of believers; clandestine reorganization under Choe Si-hyeong; and, over the longer term, the formation of a community whose members regarded the founder as a martyr and sought to vindicate his name.

Historical complexity: Suppression was not a response to a single cause, such as an actual conspiracy, but to a convergence of legal categories, local complaints, political anxiety, and the visibility of an expanding organization. The execution did not end the movement and arguably left it with a founding martyr.

Section 46.15

Choe Si-hyeong and the Survival of Donghak

Choe Si-hyeong, known by the pen name Haewol, was born in 1827 in the Gyeongju region to a poor family. Orphaned young, he worked as a farm laborer and in paper making before coming to Donghak in 1861. He was a man of humble origin and, in contrast to Choe Je-u, had little formal education; his authority rested on devotion, steadiness, and the confidence of the founder, who designated him as his successor in 1863. After the founder's execution, he became the leader of a community that had lost its head and was exposed to persecution.

The years after 1864 were a period of survival. Believers in Gyeongju and its vicinity were vulnerable, and many dispersed. Choe Si-hyeong withdrew to mountain districts in northern Gyeongsang, Gangwon, and Chungcheong, moving frequently, living among the poor, and relying on trusted believers for shelter. From these places he maintained contact with congregations through messengers and itinerant visits, taught the incantation and the ethical discipline, and kept the scriptures in circulation by copying. The community's practices went underground: recitation was done quietly, gatherings were small, and the movement's visible profile was low.

The survival of Donghak in this period is explained by several factors. The jeop structure meant that the movement did not depend on a single site or leader. The religion's simplicity, a short incantation and a moral discipline, made it portable and easy to teach in secret. The mountains offered refuge, at a cost: they were poor, remote, and difficult for communication. And the memory of the founder's death gave believers a sense of shared purpose. In later decades the community would seek to have his name cleared, an aim called sinwon that moved from private aspiration to public petition in the 1890s, as discussed in Chapter 51.

Section 46.16

The Yeonghae Uprising of 1871

In the spring of 1871 an armed uprising took place at Yeonghae, a district town on the northeastern coast of Gyeongsang Province. It was led by Yi Pil-je, a restless and charismatic figure who, according to official interrogation records, had been involved in earlier unsuccessful conspiracies in the late 1860s and who had attached himself to the Donghak community. The rising was timed for the tenth day of the third lunar month, the anniversary by the lunar calendar of Choe Je-u's execution. Its declared motives included the vindication of the founder, whose name had been condemned, and the redress of grievances against local officialdom.

Participants included Donghak believers from several districts of northern Gyeongsang. According to the accounts, the rebels seized the district office, and the magistrate was killed, though the details differ among sources. The government responded with force, and the rising was crushed within a short time. Yi Pil-je was pursued, captured, and executed. A number of Donghak believers were killed or punished, and the community suffered another round of repression that drove it deeper into hiding.

The role of Choe Si-hyeong is contested, and this makes the episode a useful exercise in source analysis. Official interrogation records, which reflect the interests of the state and the pressures of interrogation, tend to depict Donghak's leadership as implicated. Donghak tradition, compiled later by a community that revered Choe Si-hyeong and had reason to distinguish its religion from armed rebellion, emphasizes his reluctance and his later efforts to avoid repetition. Historians generally accept that he was approached, that he had some contact with Yi Pil-je and the planners, and that the community was seriously affected. How far he endorsed the plan is uncertain, and the sources do not allow a definitive answer.

The significance of Yeonghae lies in what it was and was not. It was an armed action by Donghak believers under an outside leader, motivated by religious vindication and local grievance. It was not the Donghak Peasant Revolution of 1894, which was a far larger movement with different leadership, aims, and circumstances. It also demonstrates that by the early 1870s, elements within the Donghak community were willing to take up arms, and that the movement was not simply quietist. The wider history of Donghak cannot be understood either as an uninterrupted history of peaceful religion or as a history of rebellion from the start. The episode complicated both pictures.

Section 46.17

Rebuilding the Religious Community in the 1870s

After Yeonghae, Choe Si-hyeong faced the task of rebuilding a community that had been twice shattered. He sought refuge again in remote highland districts, notably in the Taebaek and Sobaek mountain areas, where he lived in poverty and mobility. In these conditions the community slowly regrouped. Believers who had survived gathered around him or around local leaders, and congregations were reformed in the mountain districts of Gangwon and Chungcheong and in the highlands of northern Gyeongsang. The movement became more concentrated in poor and remote areas, where official oversight was weak.

Consolidation involved the preservation and ordering of teaching. Choe Si-hyeong gathered and checked the founder's writings, taught the moral discipline, and gradually built his own authority as the guardian of the tradition. He emphasized ethical practice, hard work, and an inward religious life, and in later decades he developed teachings of his own, including the principle of serving others as one serves Heaven. The preparation of the scriptures for printing, completed in 1880 and 1881, belongs to the end of this period and followed from the work of preserving and organizing them in the 1870s.

The year 1876, when the Treaty of Ganghwa was signed (Chapter 48), is the endpoint of this chapter, but the surviving Donghak sources give little evidence of how the community reacted to the treaty or to the opening of Korea. What can be said is that the decades after 1876 brought new pressures, including foreign commercial penetration, fiscal strain, and the political upheavals described in Chapters 49 to 52, and that a community that had spent a decade reorganizing was positioned to grow when conditions changed. Its subsequent history includes the campaigns for vindication in the 1890s and the revolution of 1894, treated in Chapter 51, and its transformation in 1905 into Cheondogyo. These are outcomes that this chapter notes but does not narrate.

Section 46.18

Donghak, Korea's Religious Landscape, and the Meaning of Religious Innovation

Placing Donghak beside its neighbors clarifies what it was. Like Catholicism, it taught a Lord of Heaven and a practice of prayer, and both were persecuted as heterodox. But Catholicism arrived with an institutional church, foreign priests, sacraments, and a refusal of ancestral rites that the state regarded as an attack on the moral order, whereas Donghak was founded by a Korean, organized through local congregations, and generally retained Confucian family ritual. Against Confucian orthodoxy, Donghak shared a moral vocabulary but replaced scholarly study with an experience of Heaven open to all. Against Buddhism, it offered a this-worldly ethic and a short devotional practice in place of monastic discipline. Against popular religion, with which it shared healing practices and talismanic forms, it offered a coherent doctrine, a scriptural tradition, and a community with regular discipline. It drew on each of these and belonged to none.

Three descriptions of Donghak have been common, and none alone is sufficient. As a religion, it had a founder, a revelation, a theology, scriptures, rituals, and a community of believers, and its early history is intelligible primarily in those terms. As an ethical reform movement, it taught sincerity, honesty, and respect for persons, and sought to remake conduct as the basis of social renewal. As a source of social mobilization, it created networks that crossed local boundaries, appealed to the dispossessed, and expressed anxiety about the state of the country. Each description captures something true. Treated as exclusive, each distorts: the first can ignore the social meaning of the movement, the second can neglect its ritual and devotional core, and the third can make the religion into a mere vehicle for something else.

Religious innovation also needs a comparative perspective. Periods of social stress in many societies have produced new movements that reinterpret inherited materials and promise renewal, and Donghak is one of these. What marks it in Korean history is the combination of a vernacular scriptural tradition, a grassroots organizational form, and a claim to be an indigenous alternative to both established orthodoxy and foreign religion. That combination makes it important in the history of Korean religion, in the history of popular movements, and, later, in the way twentieth-century Koreans reflected on the origins of their own modernity.

  • The jeop structure of local congregations under local leaders
  • The Confucian ethical framework of family and social roles
  • The state's categories of heterodoxy and unauthorized assembly

Pace and experience of change: Change was abrupt in 1860–1864, when the founding, rapid growth, and execution occurred within four years, and slow thereafter. Those living in the Gyeongju region experienced the disruption most directly; believers in remote districts experienced greater continuity, with the founder's teaching reaching them through intermediaries. Why the pattern matters: It shows that a movement can be transformed by persecution without being destroyed.

Historical Debate

Was Early Donghak a Religion or a Movement of Social Reform?

The question: Historians have long differed on how to characterize Donghak in its first decades. Was it fundamentally a religion whose political and social significance was secondary, or was it from the beginning an expression of popular protest and social reform in religious language?

Interpretation A — A religion first: Scholars who stress the religious character of the movement point to Choe's revelation, his theology of Heaven and the person, his ritual practices, and his scriptures. They argue that his ethical and social teachings flow from his religious vision and that reading the early movement as primarily political misunderstands what believers experienced.
Interpretation B — A vehicle of social protest: Others, including historians influenced by Marxist and popular-history approaches, emphasize that the movement flourished among peasants and the dispossessed in a time of fiscal abuse, that its teaching of human dignity challenged the status order, and that its rhetoric of protecting the country and rescuing the people carried political weight. They read the religion as the form in which social grievance found expression.
Interpretation C — An integrated movement: Many scholars hold that the distinction is itself misleading in a society where religion and moral politics were not separate spheres. On this view, Donghak was a religious movement with social implications that different groups drew out in different ways at different times.

Evidence: Choe's writings and the movement's practices support the religious emphasis; the social composition of believers, the timing of growth, the official fear of unrest, and the Yeonghae uprising support the social emphasis. Both bodies of evidence are partial: the founder's texts are doctrinal, and the official and devotional sources have their own purposes.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns interpretation and emphasis more than the basic facts, and also involves terminology and the weight given to later events, especially 1894, in assessing the earlier movement.

Why it matters: If Donghak is read primarily as protest, the early decades appear as a preparation for revolution. If it is read primarily as a religion, the revolution of 1894 appears as one outcome among others, requiring explanation by the circumstances of the 1890s.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that Donghak should be understood first as a religion, with its own revelation, theology, ethics, scriptures, and community, and only afterward as a force in the political history of the 1890s. It arose in the mid-nineteenth century in a society whose official orthodoxy, popular practices, and prophetic traditions coexisted in tension, and which was strained by fiscal abuse, rural unrest, and anxiety about Western power.

The founder, Choe Je-u, was born in 1824 into a lineage on the margins of the yangban elite, educated in the Confucian classics, and probably excluded by his mother's status from the examinations. After years of hardship and search, he reported in 1860 an experience at Yongdam in which he was addressed by the Lord on High and given a talisman and an incantation. Historians cannot assess the supernatural claim but can read Choe's account as evidence of his understanding of his mission and of the setting in which he taught.

Choe named his teaching Donghak, Eastern Learning, in contrast to Seohak, Western Learning. The name did not make it simply anti-Christian or a Confucian revival. Donghak recombined Confucian, Daoist, Buddhist, popular, and perhaps other elements around a new center: the experience and service of Heaven.

Its central teaching, si cheonju, held that a divine presence dwells within each person and operates in the world, and that sincerity, reverence, and trust realize it. Choe's conception of Heaven developed during his lifetime, shifting from an external voice toward an indwelling presence, and was later reformulated by Choe Si-hyeong and Son Byeong-hui. Later doctrines such as the equation of humanity with Heaven should not be attributed to the founder in 1860.

Donghak was lived through practice: the recitation of the incantation, the use of the talisman, healing, and moral discipline. Healing was a significant factor in recruitment, though historians cannot judge its efficacy. The teaching of dignity before Heaven appealed across lines of status, but Choe did not call for the abolition of the status system and upheld Confucian family ethics.

Choe wrote in literary Chinese for educated readers and in Korean verse for a wider audience. His writings circulated in manuscript and were printed as the Donggyeong Daejeon and Yongdam Yusa only in 1880 and 1881. The movement spread through kinship and village networks across Gyeongsang and into Jeolla, and was organized from 1862 into jeop congregations led by jeopju.

Official suspicion arose from the movement's claim to new revelation, its talisman, its translocal organization, and its association in some minds with Western teaching, in a year of widespread uprisings. Choe was arrested in 1862 and released, arrested again in 1863, and executed at Daegu in 1864. His writings contain no call for rebellion, but the state acted within the legal categories of heterodoxy and unauthorized assembly.

Choe Si-hyeong, designated successor in 1863, led the community into remote highland districts and preserved its texts and practices. The decentralized jeop structure, the portability of the religion, and the memory of the founder's death sustained it. The 1871 Yeonghae uprising, led by Yi Pil-je with Donghak participation, was a violent episode with heavy consequences, and the extent of Choe Si-hyeong's involvement remains contested.

Historians disagree on whether early Donghak is best described as a religion, an ethical movement, or a vehicle of social protest, and many hold that the categories cannot be cleanly separated. Evidence is uneven, drawn from the founder's writings, hostile official records, and later devotional tradition, each with its own purposes.

The chapter's chronological limit, 1876, marks the opening of Korea, treated in Chapter 48. Donghak's later history, including the campaigns for vindication, the revolution of 1894, and the renaming as Cheondogyo in 1905, grew out of the foundation described here and did not define it from the start.

Chapter Reflection

The history of Donghak between 1860 and 1876 matters because it shows how a religion forms, spreads, and survives in a society under strain. The founder's experience, the theology of Heaven within the person, the practices open to the unlettered, and the congregational network that carried them together made a movement that outlasted its founder's execution. Understanding this history on its own terms guards against reducing Donghak to a prelude to 1894.

The chapter also reveals broader processes in nineteenth-century Korea. It shows the limits of an orthodoxy that policed boundaries but could not satisfy every spiritual need, the effects of fiscal and political strain on popular life, and the ways in which fear of foreign power shaped domestic responses. It shows, too, that religious innovation could draw on inherited materials to produce something new and distinctively Korean without being isolated from the East Asian traditions around it.

Several continuities extend beyond the chapter. The jeop organization, the memory of the founder's death, and the ethic of respect for persons all re-emerge in later decades, in the campaigns for vindication, the revolution of 1894, and the modern religion of Cheondogyo. Chapters 47 and 48 return to the court and to the opening of Korea, and Chapter 51 takes up the revolution. Students should read those chapters with the early history of Donghak in mind, and with the awareness that later importance does not exhaust earlier meaning.
