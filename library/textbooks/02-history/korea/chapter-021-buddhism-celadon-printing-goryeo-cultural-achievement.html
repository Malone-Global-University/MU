Buddhism, Celadon, Printing, and the Goryeo Cultural Achievement

Korean History: Ancient Kingdoms to a Divided Peninsula

Chapter Opening

In 1377, at a small mountain monastery south of the Goryeo capital, monks assembled metal type, inked it, pressed paper against it, and produced copies of a Buddhist teaching anthology intended to help students of Seon meditation grasp the essential sayings of past masters. The resulting book, later known by the short title Jikji, would eventually be recognized as the oldest surviving book printed anywhere in the world using movable metal type. No contemporary observer in 1377 could have anticipated that distinction, and the monks who produced it were not trying to make history in the sense modern readers might imagine; they were trying to preserve and transmit teachings they considered urgently important, using techniques their own religious and scholarly world had spent centuries developing.

The previous chapter examined the institutions that organized Goryeo society: a monarchy claiming political and cosmic legitimacy, a central bureaucracy staffed through examination and hereditary privilege, powerful aristocratic lineages known as munbeol, and a countryside whose agricultural surplus fed the court, the capital, and a network of religious institutions. This chapter asks what those institutions, together with the wealth, literacy, and specialized labor they concentrated, made it possible to create. The answer is a cultural world of considerable sophistication: elaborate Buddhist ritual and scholarship, monasteries that functioned simultaneously as sanctuaries, landholders, workshops, and libraries, ceramic workshops capable of producing some of the most technically accomplished glazed stoneware in the medieval world, and a printing culture that moved from woodblock reproduction of vast Buddhist canons to the earlier use, anywhere on earth for which a surviving book provides secure evidence, of cast metal type.

None of these achievements occurred in isolation, either from Goryeo's own social structure or from the wider world of continental East Asia. Buddhism arrived in Korea centuries earlier, examined in Chapters 12 and 15, but Goryeo Buddhism developed its own distinctive institutional shape, drawing simultaneously on Chinese Buddhist scholarship, Khitan Liao religious contacts, and a long indigenous tradition of royal patronage stretching back through Silla to the Three Kingdoms. Goryeo celadon drew on Chinese Yue ware and northern Song ceramic technology before Korean potters developed the distinctive inlay technique, called sanggam, that later observers would come to regard as a peculiarly Korean contribution to world ceramic art. Goryeo printing culture drew on a continental tradition of woodblock reproduction that had already produced enormous Buddhist canons in Song China, even as Goryeo craftsmen and officials pursued the alternative technology of movable type with a persistence not clearly matched, on the surviving evidence, anywhere else in the medieval world at so early a date.

This chapter therefore asks students to hold two ideas together. The first is that Goryeo's cultural production was remarkable, deserving serious and detailed attention rather than a brief mention squeezed between political chapters. The second is that this production was not the spontaneous genius of an isolated civilization; it was the predictable, if impressive, result of concentrating wealth, literacy, religious devotion, and skilled labor within particular institutions, and of maintaining sustained contact with religious and technical traditions across the wider East Asian world. Removing either half of this picture, treating Goryeo culture as either miraculously self-generated or as merely derivative of Chinese models, would misrepresent the historical record this chapter examines.

The chapter moves from the general to the specific. It begins by describing Buddhism's political and institutional role in the Goryeo state, then examines monasteries as economic and intellectual centers in their own right. It introduces two of Goryeo's most historically significant Buddhist thinkers, Uicheon and Jinul, whose careers illustrate different responses to the problem of reconciling scriptural study with meditative practice. It then turns to celadon ceramics, tracing the technology's origins, its Korean development, and its connection to both religious and aristocratic consumption. From there the chapter examines Goryeo art and architecture more broadly before turning to the culture of the book: woodblock printing, the vast project of reproducing the Buddhist canon, and finally the technically demanding development of movable metal type, culminating in the 1377 printing of the Jikji.

Two cautions guide the chapter throughout. First, students should resist the common tendency, sometimes encouraged by later nationalist historiography on multiple sides of modern East Asian borders, to reduce complicated technological histories to simple claims about who invented what first. Movable type had a long and only partially documented history in East Asia, and this chapter treats claims of priority with the caution the evidence requires, while still explaining clearly what the surviving evidence for the Jikji does and does not establish. Second, students should resist treating Goryeo's religious and artistic achievements as detached from the social order examined in Chapter 21's companion, Chapter 20. Royal patronage, aristocratic wealth, monastic landholding, and a literate bureaucratic elite were not incidental background to Goryeo's cultural production; they were its necessary condition.

By the chapter's end, students should understand Goryeo not simply as a dynasty that existed between Silla and Joseon, but as a distinctive cultural civilization in its own right, one whose religious institutions, artistic workshops, and printing culture connected the Korean peninsula to a wider medieval world stretching from Song and Liao China to the Buddhist monastic networks of continental Asia, while producing forms of religious thought, ceramic art, and printed text that Korean society would remember, reinterpret, and draw upon for centuries afterward.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain the political and institutional relationship between the Goryeo monarchy and Buddhist religious institutions.
  2. Describe the economic and intellectual functions of Goryeo monasteries beyond their religious role.
  3. Distinguish among major currents within Goryeo Buddhism, including doctrinal scholarship (Gyo) and meditative traditions (Seon).
  4. Analyze the careers of Uicheon and Jinul as different responses to the challenge of Buddhist scholarly and institutional reform.
  5. Trace the technical development of Goryeo celadon, including glaze technology and the inlay (sanggam) technique.
  6. Connect ceramic, artistic, and architectural production to systems of aristocratic and religious patronage.
  7. Explain why Goryeo institutions invested heavily in woodblock printing of Buddhist texts.
  8. Evaluate, with appropriate caution, the evidence for the development of movable metal type printing in Goryeo.
  9. Assess the historical significance of the Jikji and distinguish it from unsupported claims of an absolute technological "first."
  10. Identify connections between Goryeo cultural production and wider East Asian religious, artistic, and technological traditions.
  11. Explain why Goryeo's cultural achievements depended on the institutional and social order examined in the preceding chapter.
  12. Compare Goryeo Buddhism with the Buddhism of Silla examined earlier in this textbook, identifying continuity and change.

Historical Context

Chapter 19 traced Wang Geon's founding of Goryeo and the reunification of the peninsula's rival successor states after the fragmentation of Silla. Chapter 20 examined the institutions through which Goryeo's kings, aristocratic lineages, and bureaucratic officials organized political authority, landholding, and social status. This chapter builds directly on that foundation. The concentration of agricultural surplus in royal, aristocratic, and monastic hands; the existence of a literate examination-trained officialdom; and the survival of powerful hereditary families with resources to spend on religious and artistic patronage together created the conditions in which Goryeo's most celebrated cultural achievements became possible.

Buddhism had been established on the Korean peninsula since the Three Kingdoms period, examined in Chapter 12, and had flourished under state patronage during Unified Silla, examined in Chapter 15, producing monastic centers such as Bulguksa and the cave sanctuary of Seokguram. Goryeo's founders and early kings, including Wang Geon himself, presented their new dynasty as a continuation and intensification of this Buddhist royal tradition. Wang Geon's own Ten Injunctions, a set of instructions reportedly left for his successors, emphasized the protective power of Buddhism for the state and urged continued royal patronage of temples and Buddhist ceremonies, alongside instructions concerning geomantic principles, succession, and relations with border peoples.

At the same time, Goryeo maintained active diplomatic, religious, and commercial contact with Song China, with the Khitan Liao empire that controlled Manchuria and parts of the northern frontier, and, more indirectly, with Buddhist centers across continental Asia. These contacts brought scriptural texts, printed Buddhist canons, artistic techniques, and ceramic technologies into Goryeo, where court patrons, monastic scholars, and skilled artisans adapted and transformed them. Chapter 22 will examine how the same northern frontier contact eventually produced sustained warfare with Khitan Liao and, later, Jurchen Jin forces; this chapter examines the more constructive dimension of those same contacts, through which religious texts, artistic models, and technical knowledge moved into Goryeo alongside episodes of conflict.

Understanding Goryeo's cultural achievements therefore requires holding several threads together at once: a court and aristocracy wealthy and pious enough to fund elaborate religious and artistic projects, monasteries substantial enough to function as centers of scholarship and production, artisans skilled enough to master demanding technologies, and a wider East Asian world whose religious and technical traditions Goryeo both received and reshaped.

Section 21.1

The Cultural World of Goryeo

Chapter 20 described a Goryeo state organized around a monarchy that claimed both political authority and cosmic legitimacy, a central bureaucracy staffed partly through competitive examination and partly through hereditary privilege, a small number of powerful munbeol lineages who intermarried with the royal house and monopolized the highest offices, and an agricultural economy whose surplus, extracted through a land and taxation system examined in that chapter, ultimately supported the court, the capital, and the countryside's temples and monasteries. None of this institutional description would be complete without asking what all of that concentrated wealth, status, and literacy actually produced.

The answer, this chapter argues, is a cultural world of unusual richness for its time and place. Buddhist monasteries, generously endowed with land and labor, functioned simultaneously as centers of worship, scholarship, art production, and manuscript reproduction. Aristocratic and royal patrons commissioned ceramics, paintings, and ritual objects of considerable technical sophistication, both for religious use and for the display of refined taste. Literate officials trained in Chinese classical learning through the examination system, described in Chapter 20, brought scholarly habits into religious commentary, historical writing, and connoisseurship. And the same institutions that reproduced Confucian classical texts for the examination system also organized the immense labor required to carve, print, and preserve the Buddhist canon.

Understanding Goryeo cultural production as institutional, rather than as a series of isolated individual achievements, changes how students should read the rest of this chapter. Uicheon's scholarly synthesis was possible because he was a royal prince with access to court libraries, Song Chinese contacts, and monastic resources. Jinul's monastic reform movement succeeded because it attracted patronage and disciples sufficient to sustain a new religious community. Celadon workshops could refine glaze chemistry and inlay technique over generations because aristocratic and royal demand for fine ceramics remained steady and well-funded. Woodblock printing of the entire Buddhist canon required not inspiration alone but organized labor, financing, timber, skilled carvers, and administrative coordination on a scale only a well-resourced state and its monastic partners could sustain. This chapter's sections examine each of these achievements individually, but students should not lose sight of the institutional structure connecting them.

Section 21.2

Buddhism and the Goryeo State

Buddhism in Goryeo functioned as far more than a private system of personal belief. From the dynasty's founding, Buddhism was woven into the state's claims to legitimacy, its ritual calendar, and its relationship with the aristocracy. Wang Geon's Ten Injunctions, discussed in the Historical Context above, explicitly instructed his successors to maintain Buddhist temples and ceremonies, reflecting a conviction, inherited in part from Silla precedent, that Buddhist ritual protected the state and secured cosmic favor for the ruling house.

Goryeo kings sponsored elaborate state Buddhist ceremonies, most notably the Palgwanhoe (Festival of the Eight Vows) and the Yeondeunghoe (Lantern Festival), both of which combined Buddhist ritual with elements of indigenous religious practice and served as occasions for displaying royal authority, receiving tribute or homage from officials and, at times, foreign envoys, and reinforcing a sense of collective religious and political community centered on the throne. These ceremonies were not private devotional exercises; they were state occasions, organized and financed by the court, in which Buddhist ritual and royal political theater were inseparable.

The state also created formal positions for eminent monks within a quasi-official hierarchy, including the titles of wangsa (royal preceptor) and guksa (state preceptor), granted to senior monks who advised the king and lent religious authority to the throne. Monastic examinations, modeled in some respects on the civil service examination system examined in Chapter 20, tested monks' scriptural knowledge and could lead to advancement within an increasingly formalized monastic hierarchy. This formalization reflected the same broader Goryeo pattern of building durable institutions around a court-centered order: just as civil officials advanced through examination and hereditary privilege, monks could advance through a parallel structure that connected religious accomplishment to court recognition.

Royal and aristocratic patronage extended to temple construction, land grants, and the financing of major Buddhist ceremonies and printing projects. Wealthy families sometimes established or heavily endowed particular temples associated with their lineage, using Buddhist patronage as a means of displaying status, accumulating religious merit, and, in some cases, consolidating landholding under religious institutions that enjoyed certain tax and labor advantages, a pattern examined further in Section 21.3. The relationship between throne, aristocracy, and monastery was therefore not simply religious; it was woven into the political and economic fabric of the state examined in the previous chapter.

Section 21.3

Monasteries as Religious, Intellectual, and Economic Institutions

Goryeo monasteries should not be pictured merely as quiet retreats for meditation and worship, though they were certainly that as well. They were also substantial economic institutions, often controlling extensive landholdings, mills, and even commercial operations, supported by both direct royal and aristocratic grants and by donations from ordinary believers seeking religious merit. Chapter 20 introduced the general problem of landholding and tax-exempt or specially privileged land categories within the Goryeo land system; monastic land, often exempt from certain taxes and cultivated by dependent laborers or tenants, represented one of the more significant and, at times, politically sensitive categories within that system.

This economic substance supported monasteries' intellectual and artistic functions. Large monastic libraries held scriptural collections, commentarial literature, and historical texts. Monasteries maintained scriptoria and, increasingly, printing workshops capable of producing woodblock editions of Buddhist texts for internal use, for distribution to other monasteries, and for merit-generating donation by patrons. Skilled artisans, some of them monks and some lay craftsmen supported by monastic patronage, produced ritual implements, sculpture, and painted images. Major monasteries also functioned as sites of pilgrimage, community gathering, and, in some periods, informal centers of local economic activity, given their landholding, storage capacity, and connections to broader trade and transport networks.

This institutional strength, however, generated recurring tensions. Extensive monastic landholding reduced the tax base available to the state and could concentrate resources in institutions somewhat outside direct bureaucratic control. The recruitment of monks, sometimes from aristocratic families seeking to place younger sons advantageously or to accumulate religious merit for the lineage, connected monastic institutions closely to aristocratic politics rather than separating them cleanly from secular concerns. Confucian-trained officials, whose own worldview privileged secular governance, family obligation, and productive agricultural labor, sometimes criticized excessive Buddhist landholding, tax exemption, and the diversion of labor into monastic construction and support, tensions that would grow considerably by the late Goryeo period examined in Chapter 24. This chapter does not anticipate that later crisis in detail, but students should understand from the outset that Buddhist institutional wealth, examined here mainly for its cultural and artistic consequences, also carried genuine costs and provoked genuine criticism within Goryeo society.

Section 21.4

Buddhist Schools, Scholarship, and Religious Debate

Goryeo Buddhism was not a single uniform institution but a field of overlapping and sometimes competing traditions. Broadly speaking, historians distinguish between Gyo traditions, emphasizing scriptural study, doctrinal analysis, and textual scholarship, and Seon traditions, emphasizing meditative practice and the possibility of direct, non-conceptual insight into the nature of mind. These categories should not be understood as sharply bounded institutional sects in the modern sense; individual monks, monasteries, and periods combined doctrinal study and meditative practice in varying proportions, and the relationship between the two approaches was a recurring subject of debate and reform rather than a settled division.

Within Gyo traditions, Goryeo monks engaged deeply with scriptural commentary inherited from Chinese Buddhist scholarship, including traditions associated with the Hwaeom (Chinese: Huayan) school, which had already shaped Silla Buddhist thought, examined in Chapter 15, as well as newer engagement with Cheontae doctrine promoted especially by Uicheon, discussed in Section 21.5. Scriptural scholarship required access to an enormous and continually expanding body of Buddhist literature, much of it originally composed or translated in Chinese, which in turn helps explain Goryeo's massive investment in acquiring, copying, and eventually printing complete editions of the Buddhist canon, examined in Section 21.10.

Seon traditions, meanwhile, emphasized direct meditative cultivation, often under the guidance of an accomplished master, and expressed some suspicion toward purely textual or intellectual approaches to enlightenment. Seon monastic communities, often located in mountain settings somewhat removed from the capital, cultivated distinctive forms of communal discipline, meditation practice, and teacher-student transmission. The tension and occasional rivalry between more textually oriented Gyo approaches and more meditation-centered Seon approaches formed one of the central intellectual problems Goryeo Buddhist thinkers confronted, and it is this problem that both Uicheon and Jinul, examined in the following two sections, attempted to address, though from different starting points and with different proposed solutions.

Buddhist scholarship in Goryeo was also shaped by sustained contact with Chinese and, more indirectly, Khitan Liao Buddhist institutions. Goryeo monks traveled to Song China to study, acquire texts, and consult with Chinese teachers; Chinese and Khitan Buddhist texts circulated within Goryeo monastic libraries; and Goryeo's own scholarly compilations, most notably Uicheon's catalog discussed below, were themselves later valued and consulted by scholars beyond Korea. This pattern of exchange should be understood as genuinely reciprocal rather than a one-directional transfer of learning from a more advanced China to a receiving Korea; Goryeo Buddhist scholarship made its own distinctive contributions to a broader East Asian and, in some respects, transregional Buddhist intellectual world.

Section 21.5

Uicheon and the Search for Buddhist Synthesis

Uicheon, born around 1055 as a son of King Munjong, entered monastic life while still young, a path not unusual for royal or aristocratic sons in a society where Buddhist ordination could combine genuine religious vocation with family strategy and status considerations. Uicheon's position as a royal prince gave him access to resources, court connections, and an education in Buddhist and secular scholarship that few ordinary monks could have commanded, and his career illustrates how Goryeo's concentration of wealth and status at the top of society could, in particular cases, be channeled into significant scholarly achievement. His plan to study abroad in Song China initially met resistance at court, and in 1085 Uicheon left for Song without royal authorization, a departure historians generally describe as effectively clandestine rather than one undertaken with prior royal permission.

In Song China, Uicheon studied with prominent Buddhist teachers, collected texts, and engaged with contemporary Chinese Buddhist scholarship before returning to Goryeo. Once home, he undertook an ambitious project: compiling a comprehensive catalog of East Asian Buddhist commentarial literature, works of scriptural interpretation and doctrinal explanation produced across China, Korea, and, to some extent, Japan. Uicheon completed this catalog, the Sinpyeon Jejong Gyojang Chongnok, in 1090, and the associated project of collecting, copying, and printing many of the commentarial texts it identified, known as the Gyojang, proceeded across the following decade, roughly from 1091 to 1102. This catalog and printing project represented a major undertaking of textual scholarship, requiring not only Uicheon's own learning but the institutional resources, scribes, and printing capacity that royal and monastic patronage made available.

The Gyojang should be distinguished carefully from the Tripitaka proper, discussed further in Section 21.10. Where a Tripitaka, in the strict sense, comprises the Buddha's discourses (sutras), monastic disciplinary codes (Vinaya), and systematic scholastic treatises (Abhidharma), Uicheon's Gyojang was a supplementary collection specifically of later commentarial and exegetical writings, the interpretive literature that had grown up around canonical scripture but that Uicheon judged to be incompletely represented, and at risk of being scattered or lost, within the canon collections then available in Goryeo. His catalog and printing project addressed this specific gap rather than duplicating or replacing the canon itself.

Intellectually, Uicheon promoted the Cheontae tradition, associated in China with efforts to systematize and harmonize the Buddha's teachings into a coherent hierarchy of doctrines suited to learners of different capacities. Uicheon saw in Cheontae doctrine a framework capable of reconciling the scriptural emphasis of Gyo traditions with the meditative emphasis of Seon traditions, arguing that genuine practice required both doctrinal understanding and meditative cultivation rather than treating them as opposed alternatives. This synthetic ambition reflects a recurring theme in Goryeo Buddhist thought: the conviction, shared in different forms by both Uicheon and, a century later, Jinul, that the perceived opposition between scriptural study and meditation represented a problem to be solved rather than a permanent division to be maintained.

Uicheon's project should be understood within the wider pattern of East Asian Buddhist textual exchange discussed in the preceding section. His catalog drew on Chinese, Korean, and, to a lesser extent, Japanese sources, and it was in turn valued by later scholars across the region as a major bibliographic resource. Uicheon's career therefore illustrates concretely how royal patronage, international study, and monastic institutional resources combined to produce a work of lasting scholarly significance, connecting Goryeo intellectual life directly to the wider currents of East Asian Buddhism examined throughout this chapter.

Section 21.6

Jinul and the Reform of Korean Seon Buddhism

Roughly a century after Uicheon's death, Jinul, born in 1158, confronted a related but distinct problem within Goryeo Buddhism. By Jinul's lifetime, many historians argue, Goryeo Buddhist institutions, including some prominent monasteries, had become deeply entangled with aristocratic patronage, landed wealth, and, at times, worldly political concerns, at some cost to the rigor of meditative discipline that Seon traditions in particular were meant to cultivate. Jinul's response took the form of an organized reform movement rather than purely scholarly synthesis.

Jinul founded what became known as the Concentration and Wisdom Community (Jeonghye Gyeolsa), a monastic association dedicated to renewed rigor in meditative practice combined with continued doctrinal study, explicitly rejecting a purely worldly or status-oriented approach to monastic life. This community eventually became associated with the monastery later known as Songgwangsa, which developed into one of Korea's most historically significant Seon monastic centers. Jinul's reform movement attracted both monastic and lay support, suggesting that his critique of institutional laxity and his call for renewed practice resonated with concerns shared beyond a narrow circle of specialist monks.

Intellectually, Jinul is particularly associated with the formulation, developed from earlier Chinese Chan sources but given influential expression in his own writing, of "sudden awakening, gradual cultivation." This formulation held that an initial breakthrough of insight into the nature of mind could occur suddenly, but that fully realizing and stabilizing that insight in practice required continued, gradual cultivation through meditation, ethical discipline, and, importantly, continued engagement with doctrinal study rather than its rejection. Jinul thereby offered his own synthesis of the Seon and Gyo tension examined in Section 21.4, one that, like Uicheon's a century earlier, refused to treat meditation and scriptural learning as mutually exclusive, even though Jinul approached the problem primarily as a meditation teacher and reformer rather than as a comprehensive bibliographic scholar.

Comparing Uicheon and Jinul illustrates a broader point about Goryeo Buddhist history: the tension between doctrinal study and meditative practice was not resolved once and for all by either figure, but recurred as a persistent problem to which successive generations of Buddhist thinkers proposed their own solutions, shaped by the particular institutional and social pressures of their own moment. Uicheon worked from a position of royal privilege and international scholarly exchange to build doctrinal synthesis; Jinul worked from a position of monastic reform, responding to concerns about institutional laxity, to reassert meditative discipline while still preserving a role for doctrinal learning. Both responses depended on the same underlying Goryeo social order, examined in Chapter 20, that could produce a learned royal-prince monk on one hand and a religious reform movement capable of attracting sustained lay and monastic support on the other.

Section 21.7

Goryeo Celadon

Among Goryeo's most celebrated material achievements is celadon, a type of high-fired stoneware covered with a glaze that, through careful control of iron content and firing atmosphere, produces the distinctive blue-green or gray-green color for which the ware is named in English. Celadon technology originated in China, where Yue ware and, later, various Song dynasty kilns had already achieved considerable technical sophistication before Goryeo potters adopted and adapted the underlying methods. Goryeo potters, working within kilns established primarily in the southwestern peninsula, gradually developed their own distinctive forms, glaze qualities, and, eventually, decorative techniques, producing ceramics that Song Chinese observers themselves are recorded as praising for their color and refinement.

Producing fine celadon required substantial technical knowledge and control over several interacting variables: the composition of the clay body, the chemical composition and thickness of the glaze, and the temperature and, crucially, the oxygen level within the kiln during firing, since a reducing (oxygen-poor) atmosphere at high temperature was necessary to produce celadon's characteristic color rather than a duller or browner tone. This knowledge was accumulated and transmitted within specialized workshops over generations, representing exactly the kind of concentrated skilled labor and long-term institutional investment this chapter has emphasized as a precondition for Goryeo's major cultural achievements.

Goryeo's most historically distinctive contribution to celadon technology was the development, by the twelfth century, of the inlay technique known as sanggam. In this method, artisans carved decorative designs into the surface of leather-hard, unfired clay, filled the resulting recessed lines with white and black slip (liquid clay), and then, after the slip dried and the excess was removed, applied the celadon glaze and fired the piece. The result was a ceramic surface displaying crisp, contrasting decorative motifs, often floral, geometric, or figural, set beneath a translucent glaze, achieving a decorative effect distinct from either painted or purely carved ceramic decoration. Sanggam celadon required considerable additional labor and technical control beyond producing plain celadon, and its development and refinement represent a genuinely significant Korean innovation within the broader East Asian celadon tradition, even though the underlying glaze technology itself originated in China.

Celadon served both religious and secular purposes within Goryeo elite consumption. Buddhist monasteries used celadon vessels for ritual purposes, including incense burners, ritual ewers, and reliquary containers, connecting ceramic production directly to the religious institutions examined earlier in this chapter. Aristocratic households used celadon tableware, vases, and decorative objects as markers of refined taste and status, consistent with the broader pattern, examined in Chapter 20, by which Goryeo's munbeol elite expressed social distinction through consumption as well as through office-holding and landholding. Celadon production therefore illustrates concretely how technical skill, religious demand, and aristocratic patronage combined within Goryeo's institutional order to produce lasting artistic achievement.

Section 21.8

Art, Architecture, and Material Culture

Goryeo's cultural achievement extended well beyond ceramics into painting, sculpture, architecture, and other forms of material culture, much of it likewise connected to Buddhist religious patronage. Goryeo Buddhist painting, particularly imagery of bodhisattvas such as Avalokiteshvara, produced for temple use and aristocratic devotion, employed refined mineral pigments and gold applied with considerable technical skill, reflecting both religious devotion and the material resources available to elite patrons who commissioned such works as acts of merit-making or memorialization. Surviving examples, along with textual references to lost works, indicate a sophisticated painting tradition supported by monastic and aristocratic patronage in ways parallel to the ceramic workshops discussed above.

Buddhist sculpture in Goryeo, produced in stone, wood, and metal, populated temple halls with images of buddhas, bodhisattvas, and guardian figures, serving devotional, ritual, and didactic purposes. Metalwork extended to ritual implements such as bells, incense burners, and reliquaries, some displaying considerable technical and decorative sophistication, again produced by specialized artisans supported by monastic or aristocratic commissions. Architecturally, Goryeo temple construction, though later reconstruction and the destruction of many original structures during subsequent invasions limit what survives directly from the period, is known through surviving structures, archaeological remains, and textual description to have employed sophisticated timber-frame construction techniques within a broader East Asian architectural tradition, adapted to Korean materials, climate, and aesthetic preference.

Manuscript culture, distinct from but related to the printing culture examined in the following sections, also flourished within Goryeo's monastic and aristocratic circles. Hand-copied Buddhist scriptures, sometimes written in gold or silver ink on indigo-dyed paper, represented particularly prestigious acts of religious patronage, requiring specialized calligraphic skill and costly materials. Such manuscripts illustrate that even as woodblock and, eventually, movable-type printing expanded the reproduction of Buddhist texts, older traditions of prestigious manuscript production continued alongside the newer printing technologies, meeting different needs, religious, aesthetic, and devotional, within the same broader culture of textual reproduction.

Taken together, Goryeo's painting, sculpture, metalwork, and manuscript traditions confirm the chapter's central argument: cultural production of this scale and sophistication required sustained investment by royal, aristocratic, and monastic patrons, specialized artisan labor developed and transmitted over generations, and continued engagement with artistic and technical models drawn from the wider East Asian world, all operating within the institutional order examined in Chapter 20.

Section 21.9

Scholarship, Literacy, and Literati Culture

Goryeo's cultural production was not confined to Buddhist religious contexts. The civil service examination system examined in Chapter 20 trained a substantial body of officials in Classical Chinese composition, historical learning, and Confucian ethical and political thought, and this literate elite produced a body of secular scholarship, historical writing, and literary composition that ran alongside, and at many points intersected with, the Buddhist scholarship examined elsewhere in this chapter.

Goryeo officials and scholars compiled historical works documenting earlier Korean states across the twelfth and thirteenth centuries. Kim Bu-sik, a leading official and historian, completed the court-sponsored Samguk Sagi, an official history of the Three Kingdoms, in 1145 under King Injong. More than a century later, the Buddhist monk Iryeon compiled the less official Samguk Yusa, already discussed in Chapter 1's Primary Source Window for its preservation of the Dangun tradition. These historical projects required access to earlier written and oral sources, sustained scholarly labor, and, often, royal or court sponsorship, again illustrating this chapter's recurring theme that sophisticated intellectual production depended on institutional support rather than emerging spontaneously.

Poetry and literary composition in Classical Chinese formed an expected accomplishment for educated officials, who exchanged poems, composed commemorative and occasional verse, and cultivated literary reputations that could enhance standing at court. Some Goryeo literary figures, including Yi Gyu-bo, whose writings also provide the important textual evidence for early movable metal type printing discussed in Section 21.11, left behind substantial bodies of poetry, prose, and miscellaneous writing that historians use today to reconstruct aspects of Goryeo intellectual and social life extending well beyond their most famous individual contributions.

This broader literate culture connected to Buddhist scholarship at many points: officials often held personal Buddhist devotional practices even while operating within a Confucian-trained bureaucratic career, monastic scholars sometimes came from the same aristocratic families that produced civil officials, and skills in Classical Chinese composition and textual scholarship transferred readily between secular and religious contexts. Rather than picturing Goryeo intellectual life as divided into separate Buddhist and Confucian spheres, students should understand it as a single literate culture in which individuals moved between religious and secular scholarship according to career, family strategy, and personal inclination, all supported by the same underlying system of literacy, patronage, and institutional resources examined throughout this chapter and the one preceding it.

Section 21.10

Woodblock Printing and the Culture of Books

Woodblock printing, in which text and, at times, images are carved in relief on wooden blocks, inked, and pressed against paper, had already developed a long history in East Asia by the time Goryeo undertook its most ambitious printing projects. The technique allowed a single carved block to produce many identical printed sheets, making it far more efficient than manuscript copying for reproducing texts in significant quantity, provided the initial investment of carving could be justified by the number of copies eventually produced or the importance of preserving a text reliably.

Goryeo's monastic and court institutions invested heavily in woodblock printing primarily to reproduce Buddhist scripture. The most ambitious early such project was the First Goryeo Tripitaka (Chojo Daejanggyeong), begun in 1011 under King Hyeonjong, during a period of Khitan invasion examined further in Chapter 22, and continued in stages across several decades before its completion around 1087. In the strict sense a Tripitaka, meaning "three baskets," comprises three categories of canonical text: the Buddha's own discourses (sutras), the codes of monastic discipline (Vinaya), and systematic scholastic treatises (Abhidharma), all drawn from across the Buddhist textual tradition, much of it originally composed or translated into Chinese. Producing a complete Tripitaka required carving tens of thousands of individual wooden printing blocks, each corresponding to a page of text, a task demanding enormous quantities of suitable timber, skilled carvers capable of reproducing text accurately and legibly in reverse relief, careful editorial oversight to ensure textual accuracy across such a vast project, and sustained financing over decades.

This first Goryeo Tripitaka should be understood as motivated by several overlapping purposes: the straightforward practical need to make canonical texts available for study and ritual use across Goryeo's monasteries, considerable religious merit believed to accrue from sponsoring or participating in the reproduction of sacred texts, and, given its origin during a period of foreign invasion, an explicitly protective or apotropaic purpose, in which the act of reproducing the canon was believed to invoke Buddhist protection for the state during times of danger. This same protective motivation would resurface with even greater urgency during the Mongol invasions examined in Chapter 23, when Goryeo undertook a second, later recarving of the canon, known to history as the Tripitaka Koreana, partly in the hope of securing Buddhist protection against the invading Mongol forces. This chapter introduces the underlying printing tradition and the eleventh-century First Tripitaka; the specific narrative of the Tripitaka Koreana's creation amid thirteenth-century invasion belongs to Chapter 23, where the wartime context that shaped it receives full treatment.

Distinct from the Tripitaka proper was the Gyojang project associated with Uicheon and discussed in Section 21.5: a supplementary collection specifically of commentarial and exegetical literature, cataloged by Uicheon in 1090 and printed in the following decade, roughly from 1091 to 1102. Because the Gyojang addressed a different category of text, later interpretive commentary rather than scripture, discipline, or scholastic treatise, it should not be conflated with the Tripitaka itself, even though both projects drew on the same underlying woodblock printing infrastructure and institutional support.

Beyond canonical and commentarial Buddhist printing, Goryeo woodblock printing extended to other religious, historical, and literary texts, and printing capacity existed at multiple monastic and, eventually, some private or local levels, not only within a single centralized state project. This broader printing culture created both an increasing supply of accessible texts for study and a base of technical printing expertise, including skilled carvers, editors, and papermakers, upon which the later development of movable metal type, examined in the following section, could draw. Woodblock printing therefore functioned not merely as a technology used for a few celebrated projects but as an ongoing institutional practice that shaped Goryeo's wider culture of scholarship, religious practice, and textual transmission.

Section 21.11

Movable Metal Type

Woodblock printing, however effective for producing many copies of a single fixed text, had a significant limitation: each block could reproduce only the specific page it had been carved for, so producing a different text required carving an entirely new set of blocks, and a block used only rarely represented a substantial investment for limited return. Movable type, in which individual characters are cast or carved separately and then arranged, inked, and reused in different combinations to print different texts, offered a potential solution to this limitation, particularly valuable for texts printed in relatively small quantities, since the same set of type characters could be reused for many different works rather than requiring a new woodblock carving for each.

The historical record indicates that movable type using various materials, including ceramic type, had been experimented with in China before the technique spread or was independently developed elsewhere in East Asia, though the surviving evidence for very early Chinese movable type is fragmentary and its practical use appears to have remained limited for various technical reasons, including the difficulty of producing durable, precisely uniform type characters at scale. Goryeo craftsmen and officials pursued a related but technically distinct approach: casting individual type characters in metal, typically bronze, a method requiring specialized metallurgical knowledge including mold-making, casting technique, and the subsequent finishing of cast characters to a precise and uniform size suitable for arrangement into a printing frame.

Textual evidence indicates that movable metal type printing was practiced in Goryeo earlier than the surviving 1377 Jikji, examined in the following section. A well-known example is a passage, preserved in the collected writings of the Goryeo official and literary figure Yi Gyu-bo, describing the printing of a ritual text, the Sangjeong Gogeum Yemun, using cast metal type around 1234. The printed book itself does not survive, and historians must rely on Yi Gyu-bo's description rather than on any physical printed page from that particular edition. This distinction matters a great deal for how the evidence should be handled: the 1234 reference provides credible textual evidence that movable metal type printing existed in Goryeo by that date, but it does not provide surviving physical proof in the way an actual surviving printed book would.

Students should therefore distinguish carefully between two related but different claims. The first, better-supported claim is that Goryeo developed and used movable metal type printing by at least the 1230s, based on credible textual description, predating any confirmed surviving movable-metal-type printed book from elsewhere in the world. The second, more restricted claim, discussed fully in the next section, is that the 1377 Jikji is the oldest surviving book anywhere in the world actually printed using movable metal type, a claim that rests on the physical survival of the object itself rather than on textual description alone. This textbook, consistent with the broader outline's instruction to treat disputed technological priority claims cautiously, presents both claims with their appropriate evidentiary basis rather than collapsing them into a single undifferentiated assertion of Korean priority in the invention of movable type as a general technology.

Section 21.12

The Jikji

The Jikji Simche Yojeol, commonly known by the short title Jikji, is an anthology of essential sayings and teachings attributed to notable Seon Buddhist masters, compiled by the monk Baegun and intended as a teaching aid for students seeking to grasp the core of Seon practice through concise, memorable statements rather than lengthy doctrinal exposition. The surviving edition was printed in 1377 at Heungdeoksa monastery near present-day Cheongju, using individually cast movable metal type characters arranged, inked, and pressed to produce the printed pages. The original type used to print the surviving Jikji has not itself survived, so its precise metal composition cannot be directly analyzed; while Goryeo metal-type casting is generally understood to have used bronze or related copper alloys, the surviving book itself is described, by the institutions that curate and study it, as printed with cast metal type rather than as verified bronze type.

The Jikji's historical significance rests on a specific and well-documented fact: it is the oldest known surviving book anywhere in the world printed using movable metal type. This claim should be stated with precision. It does not claim that Goryeo was necessarily the first place in the world where movable metal type printing was ever attempted, since, as the previous section discussed, textual evidence suggests earlier Goryeo use of the technology, and independent experimentation with movable type using other materials had occurred elsewhere in East Asia. Rather, the claim concerns physical survival: among all books printed using movable metal type anywhere in the world for which a physical copy has survived to the present day, the Jikji is, on current evidence, the oldest.

Only a portion of the original 1377 printing of the Jikji survives, held today in the Bibliothèque nationale de France in Paris, having reached Europe through a complex history of acquisition during the era of Western contact with Korea in the late nineteenth century, examined further in later chapters of this textbook. Its presence in a French national library, alongside French-language and international scholarly attention to the volume in the twentieth century, contributed significantly to the book's recognition and its eventual designation, by the United Nations Educational, Scientific and Cultural Organization, within its Memory of the World program, honoring its significance to the history of printing.

The Jikji's religious content should not be overshadowed by its technological significance. As an anthology of Seon teachings intended for practical instruction, it reflects the same broader currents in Goryeo Buddhism examined earlier in this chapter, including the continued vitality of Seon meditative tradition roughly two centuries after Jinul's reform movement, and the persistent Goryeo institutional investment in reproducing and disseminating Buddhist teaching through print. That a Seon teaching anthology, rather than a court document or commercial text, should be the specific work whose movable-metal-type printing survives to the present day is itself a reminder of how closely Goryeo's printing technology remained tied to Buddhist religious institutions and purposes throughout the period this chapter examines.

Section 21.13

Patronage, Knowledge, and Goryeo's Cultural Legacy

This chapter has examined Buddhist institutional life, doctrinal scholarship and monastic reform, ceramic art, painting and sculpture, and the successive technologies of woodblock and movable-type printing. Considered individually, each of these subjects might appear as a separate specialist topic. Considered together, and in relation to the institutional order examined in Chapter 20, they reveal a single underlying pattern: Goryeo's cultural achievements were the product of sustained, coordinated investment by royal, aristocratic, and monastic patrons in specialized human skill, whether the skill of a royal-prince scholar-monk compiling a bibliographic catalog, a meditation teacher building a reform community, a potter mastering glaze chemistry and inlay technique, a woodblock carver reproducing scripture with painstaking accuracy, or a metal caster producing uniform type characters for a printing frame.

None of these achievements developed in isolation from the wider East Asian world. Buddhist doctrine, ceramic technology, and printing technique all arrived in Goryeo through contact with China and, to a lesser degree, the Khitan Liao state, and Goryeo scholars, artisans, and religious institutions engaged actively with those continental traditions rather than passively receiving them. At the same time, Goryeo developed distinctive forms within each tradition, including sanggam celadon inlay, a Korean line of Seon reform associated with Jinul's community, and a documented, and in the case of the Jikji physically surviving, tradition of movable metal type printing predating comparable surviving evidence elsewhere in the world. This combination of continental connection and Korean adaptation exemplifies the wider historical pattern this textbook has emphasized since Chapter 12: Korean civilization developed through active engagement with, rather than isolation from or simple imitation of, its neighbors.

The chapters that follow shift attention away from cultural production toward political and military crisis. Chapter 22 examines the wars with Khitan Liao and Jurchen Jin along Goryeo's northern frontier, the military grievances that produced the 1170 coup, and the warrior government and social rebellion that followed. Chapter 23 returns to some of this chapter's themes, particularly Buddhist canon printing, in the specific and dramatic context of the Mongol invasions, when the recarving of the Buddhist canon as the Tripitaka Koreana became bound up with Goryeo's struggle for survival under Mongol pressure. Readers should carry forward from this chapter a clear sense of the institutional and technical foundation, established well before the crises of Chapters 22 and 23, upon which those later, more dramatic episodes of Goryeo's Buddhist printing culture were built.

Historical Vocabulary
Daejanggyeong and the Buddhist Canon

Daejanggyeong, often translated "Buddhist canon" or referred to using the Sanskrit-derived term "Tripitaka" ("three baskets"), refers strictly to three categories of canonical Buddhist text: the Buddha's discourses (sutras), the codes of monastic discipline (Vinaya), and systematic scholastic treatises (Abhidharma), accumulated across centuries of Buddhist textual history, much of it composed in India and Central Asia and later translated into Chinese, from which most Goryeo Buddhist textual study drew.

Later interpretive and commentarial writing on these canonical texts is a distinct category, sometimes called exegetical or commentarial literature, and is not part of the Tripitaka in the strict sense. Uicheon's Gyojang project, examined in Section 21.5, specifically addressed this commentarial category, which he judged to be incompletely represented in existing Goryeo canon collections; students should therefore avoid treating "Buddhist canon" and "commentarial literature" as interchangeable terms.

Reproducing a complete Tripitaka through woodblock printing was an undertaking of enormous scale, since a complete canon could run to many thousands of individual texts and tens of thousands of printed pages, each requiring its own carved woodblock. Goryeo's investment in the First Tripitaka, begun in 1011 and examined in Section 21.10, reflected both practical needs for accessible scripture and a belief in the religious merit and protective power associated with reproducing sacred text.

Why it matters: Understanding the scale and purpose of canon-printing projects, and the distinction between the canon proper and later commentarial supplements such as the Gyojang, helps explain why Goryeo institutions developed such extensive printing expertise, expertise that later supported the development of movable metal type examined in Sections 21.11 and 21.12.

Historical Vocabulary
Seon and Gyo

Seon (the Korean pronunciation corresponding to Chinese Chan and, later, Japanese Zen) refers to a Buddhist tradition emphasizing meditative practice, often under the guidance of an accomplished teacher, and the possibility of direct, non-conceptual insight into the nature of mind.

Gyo is a general term referring to doctrinal or scriptural approaches to Buddhist practice, emphasizing textual study, commentary, and systematic understanding of Buddhist teaching.

Potential misunderstanding: Students should avoid treating Seon and Gyo as two rigidly separated, mutually exclusive sects in the modern institutional sense. Individual monks, monasteries, and historical figures, including both Uicheon and Jinul, examined in Sections 21.5 and 21.6, engaged with elements of both approaches, and much of Goryeo Buddhist intellectual history concerns attempts to define the proper relationship between them rather than a simple contest between two fixed camps.

Technology & Evidence

Woodblock Printing versus Movable Type: Weighing the Evidence Carefully

Woodblock printing carves an entire page of text into a single wooden block, ideal for producing many copies of a fixed text such as a scripture intended for repeated reprinting, but inefficient for texts needed only in small numbers, since each new text requires an entirely new set of carved blocks.

Movable type casts or carves individual characters separately, allowing the same set of type to be rearranged and reused for many different texts, at the cost of requiring precise, durable, uniformly sized characters and a more complex process of composing, inking, and printing a page.

What the evidence supports: A textual reference describes Goryeo movable metal type printing around 1234, though the printed book itself does not survive. A physical book, the Jikji, survives from 1377, printed using cast movable metal type, and is recognized as the oldest surviving movable-metal-type book known anywhere in the world.

What the evidence does not support: A claim that Goryeo was necessarily the very first place on earth where movable type of any kind was ever conceived or attempted, since earlier and independent experimentation with movable type using other materials is documented elsewhere in East Asia, with evidence that is fragmentary and does not permit confident, precise priority claims.

Why the distinction matters: Historians distinguish between the invention of a general technological idea, which may have occurred more than once, independently, in different places, and the survival of physical evidence, which is often a matter of chance preservation as much as of original historical frequency. Precise, appropriately qualified claims about Goryeo's printing achievement are more historically responsible, and ultimately more impressive, than vague or overstated claims of absolute world priority.

Uicheon's Preface to the Sinpyeon Jejong Gyojang Chongnok, 1090

Creator: Uicheon, Goryeo royal prince and Buddhist monk · Date: 1090 CE · Type: Preface to a surviving catalog of East Asian Buddhist commentarial literature

Context: Uicheon compiled the Sinpyeon Jejong Gyojang Chongnok after years of study in Song China and extensive correspondence with monks and scholars across the region. He completed the catalog in 1090, and its preface survives as a genuine record of the concerns that motivated the project, which in turn guided the Gyojang printing effort carried out over the following decade.

Paraphrase: The following is a faithful paraphrase, not a verbatim translation, of the preface's stated concerns.

Uicheon explains that while the sutras, monastic disciplinary codes, and treatises of the canon proper had already been widely reproduced and circulated, the vast body of later commentarial and exegetical writing produced by generations of teachers across China, Korea, and Japan remained scattered, incomplete, and at real risk of being lost. He describes his own effort, through years of searching, correspondence, and collection, to gather this commentarial literature together, organize it into a single catalog, and thereby preserve and make available to future students an interpretive tradition that the canon's core texts alone could not supply.

Analysis: This preface illustrates Uicheon's self-understanding as a preservationist and bibliographic scholar working across national and institutional boundaries, and it directly confirms the distinction, discussed in Section 21.5, between the Tripitaka proper and the supplementary Gyojang collection of commentarial literature that his catalog and subsequent printing project were specifically designed to address.

Limitations: The preface reveals Uicheon's own stated purposes and self-presentation; it cannot by itself establish how completely his catalog succeeded in recovering the commentarial literature it targeted, nor how contemporaries outside his own circle of correspondents assessed the project's value.

Perspective: This source represents the perspective of a royal-born monastic scholar with access to court resources and international travel; it does not represent the perspective of ordinary monks, lay believers, or Confucian-trained officials who may have viewed such extensive Buddhist textual projects differently.

Historical Debate

Did Buddhist Institutional Wealth Strengthen or Weaken the Goryeo State?

The question: Monasteries in Goryeo controlled substantial land, labor, and resources, closely connected to royal and aristocratic patronage. Historians differ on how to weigh the overall effect of this institutional wealth on the Goryeo state and society.

Interpretation A — Buddhist institutional wealth strengthened the state: Some historians emphasize that Buddhist patronage reinforced royal legitimacy, funded significant cultural and artistic achievement, provided social services and community functions in the countryside, and gave the throne a religious partner whose ritual and ideological support helped stabilize dynastic authority, particularly in a period without a single unifying state ideology as fully developed as later Joseon Neo-Confucianism.
Interpretation B — Buddhist institutional wealth created structural problems: Other historians point to the tax exemptions and land accumulation associated with major monasteries, the diversion of labor and resources into temple construction and monastic support, and the entanglement of monastic recruitment and leadership with aristocratic politics, arguing that these factors placed long-term strain on state finances and contributed to tensions that would become more visible in the late Goryeo crisis examined in Chapter 24.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on similar evidence, including land records, monastic construction projects, court patronage records, and later Confucian officials' criticisms of Buddhist institutional wealth, but weigh the balance of benefit and cost differently.

Why it matters: How historians answer this question shapes broader interpretations of Goryeo's later political crisis and of the eventual Joseon dynasty's turn toward Neo-Confucianism and, at times, restriction of Buddhist institutional privilege, examined in Volume II.

Student task: Using the evidence about monastic landholding, royal patronage, and cultural production presented in this chapter, construct a brief argument for each interpretation, then explain which considerations you find most persuasive and why.

Multiple Perspectives

Religious Life in Goryeo: Different Vantage Points

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns historians have identified across many sources. They are not quotations from any single named historical actor.

From the perspective of a Goryeo king: Sponsoring Buddhist ceremonies, temple construction, and canon printing reinforces the dynasty's claim to cosmic favor and protection, while also providing occasions to display royal authority before officials, aristocrats, and, at times, foreign envoys.
From the perspective of a monastic scholar such as Uicheon: Access to royal resources and international study makes possible ambitious projects of textual preservation and doctrinal synthesis that few ordinary monks could otherwise undertake, though such privilege also connects scholarly religious life closely to court politics and aristocratic family strategy.
From the perspective of a Confucian-trained government official: Extensive monastic landholding and tax exemption divert resources from state revenue and productive agricultural labor, even though Buddhist ritual and patronage remain, for now, an accepted and even expected part of court life.
From the perspective of a lay believer or tenant farmer working monastery-controlled land: Local monasteries provide religious ritual, occasions for merit-making donation, and, in some cases, access to the accumulated agricultural and craft resources monasteries can offer to surrounding communities, even as monastic landholding shapes and constrains the terms of everyday agricultural labor.
Continuity and Change Over Time

From Silla to Goryeo Buddhism

What Changed

  • Goryeo formalized monastic hierarchy further, including titles such as wangsa and guksa, and monastic examinations, building on but extending Silla-era royal patronage examined in Chapter 15
  • Goryeo undertook printing on a scale, and eventually with a technology (movable metal type), not achieved during Silla, reflecting both accumulated technical knowledge and greater institutional investment in textual reproduction
  • The Seon-Gyo tension, addressed by figures such as Uicheon and Jinul, became a more explicitly debated and institutionally significant problem in Goryeo than in Silla Buddhist life
  • Ceramic technology advanced considerably, with Goryeo celadon and its sanggam inlay technique representing a level of technical achievement beyond Silla-era ceramics

What Stayed the Same

  • Buddhism remained closely tied to royal legitimacy and state ritual, continuing the pattern established under Silla
  • Monasteries continued to function as economic as well as religious institutions, controlling land and labor
  • Continued contact with Chinese Buddhist scholarship and artistic technique remained a constant source of new ideas and models for adaptation

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both continuity and change helps students avoid treating Goryeo Buddhism as either a simple continuation of Silla precedent or a wholly new development unrelated to earlier Korean Buddhist history.

Cause and Effect

Why Was the Jikji Printed Using Movable Metal Type?

Long-term causes

Centuries of accumulated woodblock printing expertise, sustained institutional investment in Buddhist textual reproduction, and earlier, textually documented Goryeo experimentation with cast metal type by the 1230s.

Short-term causes

A specific monastic need at Heungdeoksa to produce a teaching anthology of Seon masters' sayings for practical instructional use, likely in a print run too small to justify the labor of carving an entirely new set of woodblocks.

Event

The 1377 casting, composing, inking, and printing of the Jikji Simche Yojeol using cast movable metal type.

Effect (immediate and long-term)

Immediately, a usable teaching text for Seon students; in the long term, the survival of this particular copy made it, centuries later, the oldest known surviving movable-metal-type printed book in the world, and a significant object of later historical, technological, and diplomatic interest.

Historical complexity: The book's later fame as a world "first" is a matter of preservation and later historical recognition, not something the monks who produced it in 1377 could have anticipated or intended.

Everyday Life and Social History

Religion, Craft, and Ordinary Experience in Goryeo

Behind the celebrated names of Uicheon and Jinul, and behind the surviving objects of celadon and printed text examined in this chapter, stood a much larger population whose everyday religious and working lives receive less direct documentation but were nonetheless essential to Goryeo's cultural production. Anonymous woodblock carvers spent years mastering the exacting skill of carving legible text in reverse relief. Potters and kiln workers, often organized in family or workshop groups near suitable clay and fuel sources, developed and guarded technical knowledge about glaze mixtures and firing conditions passed down through hands-on apprenticeship rather than written instruction. Tenant farmers and laborers on monastery-controlled land supported, through their agricultural labor, the surplus that financed temple construction, ceremonies, and printing projects, without necessarily sharing directly in the religious prestige or artistic recognition those projects generated.

Ordinary lay believers across Goryeo society, meanwhile, participated in Buddhist practice through pilgrimage to temples, donations that funded merit-making religious activity, participation in state ceremonies such as the Palgwanhoe and Yeondeunghoe discussed in Section 21.2, and personal devotional practice that historians can reconstruct only imperfectly, given that most surviving textual sources reflect elite, literate, and often specifically monastic perspectives. Because women, farmers, and unfree or dependent laborers left few written records of their own, historians studying Goryeo religious and cultural life must read elite sources carefully for indirect evidence of these broader populations' experience, while remaining honest about the limits such source bias imposes on any complete social history of Goryeo religion and craft.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined Goryeo's religious, artistic, and technological achievements as products of the institutional order described in Chapter 20 rather than as isolated cultural triumphs disconnected from Goryeo's political and economic structure. Royal, aristocratic, and monastic wealth, combined with specialized skilled labor and sustained contact with the wider East Asian world, together made possible the developments this chapter has traced.

Buddhism functioned in Goryeo as a central pillar of state legitimacy and ritual life, formalized through royal ceremonies such as the Palgwanhoe and Yeondeunghoe, official monastic titles such as wangsa and guksa, and monastic examinations paralleling the civil service system examined in the preceding chapter. Monasteries operated simultaneously as religious sanctuaries, substantial economic institutions controlling land and labor, and centers of scholarship, art production, and, increasingly, printing.

Within Goryeo Buddhism, the relationship between doctrinal scholarship (Gyo) and meditative practice (Seon) formed a recurring intellectual problem. Uicheon, a royal prince turned monk, pursued doctrinal synthesis through the Cheontae tradition and an ambitious international bibliographic project cataloging East Asian Buddhist commentarial literature. A century later, Jinul pursued monastic reform through his Concentration and Wisdom Community, articulating an influential formulation of sudden awakening combined with gradual cultivation that likewise sought to reconcile meditation and doctrinal learning rather than treating them as opposed.

Goryeo celadon developed from Chinese ceramic technology into a distinctive Korean artistic tradition, reaching a particular high point with the development of sanggam inlay decoration, a technically demanding process requiring considerable skilled labor and reflecting sustained aristocratic and religious demand for refined ceramic objects. Painting, sculpture, metalwork, and manuscript production extended this pattern of skilled artisan labor supported by religious and aristocratic patronage across multiple artistic media.

Goryeo's printing culture began with large-scale woodblock reproduction of the Buddhist canon, an undertaking of enormous labor and organizational complexity motivated by practical, devotional, and, at times, protective religious purposes. This tradition of printing expertise contributed to the later development of movable metal type, documented textually by the 1230s and confirmed physically by the surviving 1377 Jikji, recognized as the oldest surviving movable-metal-type printed book anywhere in the world. The chapter distinguished carefully between textual evidence for earlier movable type use and the specific, physically verifiable claim represented by the surviving Jikji, cautioning against overstated or imprecise claims of absolute world priority.

Throughout, the chapter emphasized that these achievements depended on sustained institutional investment, specialized and often anonymous skilled labor, and continued engagement with Chinese and, to a lesser extent, Khitan Liao religious, artistic, and technical traditions, while also producing distinctively Korean forms and, in the case of the surviving Jikji, an object of genuine and precisely stated world historical significance.

These conclusions prepare students for the next two chapters, which turn from cultural achievement to military crisis. Chapter 22 examines the wars with Khitan Liao and Jurchen Jin, the 1170 coup, and subsequent military rule, while Chapter 23 examines the Mongol invasions and the wartime recarving of the Buddhist canon as the Tripitaka Koreana, a project directly continuous with the woodblock printing tradition examined in this chapter but undertaken under drastically different and far more desperate circumstances.

A general term for doctrinal or scriptural approaches to Buddhist study and practice.

A Korean celadon decorative technique using carved and slip-inlaid designs beneath a translucent glaze.

High-fired glazed stoneware characterized by a blue-green or gray-green color achieved through controlled firing conditions.

A glassy coating applied to ceramics before firing, whose composition and firing atmosphere determine its color and finish.

A printing method using text carved in relief on wooden blocks, effective for producing many copies of a fixed text.

Individually cast metal characters arranged and reused to print different texts, offering flexibility unavailable with woodblock printing.

A 1377 Buddhist teaching anthology, the oldest surviving book anywhere in the world printed using movable metal type.

Powerful hereditary aristocratic lineages within Goryeo society, examined fully in Chapter 20, whose wealth supported religious and artistic patronage.

A container, often finely crafted, used to hold and venerate sacred relics or remains.

A note, often at the end of a printed or manuscript text, recording information about its production, sponsorship, or date.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has asked students to see Goryeo's religious, artistic, and technological achievements not as isolated marvels but as the natural, if still remarkable, product of the institutional order examined in Chapter 20. A monarchy invested in Buddhist legitimacy, an aristocracy wealthy enough to patronize art and scholarship, monasteries substantial enough to fund printing projects and support scholarly and meditative communities, and continued contact with the wider East Asian Buddhist and artistic world together made possible everything from Uicheon's bibliographic scholarship to Jinul's monastic reform, from finely inlaid celadon to the printed pages of the Jikji.

These achievements also reveal important continuities and tensions that would shape later Korean history. The close relationship between Buddhism and the state, so productive of cultural achievement in this chapter, would become a source of significant criticism and eventual reform under the Neo-Confucian Joseon dynasty examined in Volume II. The printing expertise developed here through canon reproduction and movable type would resurface, transformed by new purposes and new crises, in the recarving of the Tripitaka Koreana during the Mongol invasions examined in Chapter 23, and would remain part of a much longer Korean printing tradition extending eventually to the promulgation of Hangeul itself, examined in Chapter 27.

Students should carry forward from this chapter an appreciation for how cultural achievement of this kind depends on institutional structure as much as individual talent, and a habit of evaluating technological and artistic claims with the same evidentiary care this chapter has modeled regarding the Jikji and movable type. Precision about what evidence does and does not establish is not a diminishment of Goryeo's genuine achievement; it is what allows that achievement to be understood, and appreciated, accurately.

The chapters that follow will test this same Goryeo society, and its Buddhist institutions in particular, against military crisis, political rupture, and foreign invasion. Understanding the cultural world examined in this chapter, one built through patronage, skilled labor, and international exchange, will help students appreciate both what was placed at risk during the wars and invasions of Chapters 22 and 23, and why Goryeo's religious and cultural institutions responded to those crises in the specific ways they did.
