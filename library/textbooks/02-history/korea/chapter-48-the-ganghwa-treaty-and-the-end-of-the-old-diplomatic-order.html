The Ganghwa Treaty and the End of the Old Diplomatic Order

Korean History: Ancient Kingdoms to a Divided Peninsula

Chapter Opening

In September 1875 a Japanese gunboat approached Ganghwa Island, the same gateway to the Han River that French and American forces had attacked in 1866 and 1871. Within days, Korean shore batteries and a Japanese landing party had exchanged fire. Less than six months later, on February 26, 1876, Joseon's commissioners signed a treaty that described Joseon as an independent state, provided for new ports, authorized Japanese coastal surveys, and removed Japanese residents from Korean jurisdiction in specified cases. How did a regulated diplomatic order that had endured for centuries come to an end so quickly, and what did the new agreement actually change?

Ganghwa Island lies at the mouth of the Han River, close enough to Seoul that any hostile force controlling its waters could threaten the capital's river traffic. Its fortifications, garrison, and walled town had been part of Joseon's defensive thinking since the seventeenth-century wars with the Manchus (Chapter 36), and the campaigns of 1866 and 1871 (Chapter 45) had made it a symbol of resistance to foreign pressure. It was therefore fitting, and not accidental, that the confrontation between Joseon and Meiji Japan unfolded in these waters and that the treaty talks took place in the island's town. The agreement concluded there is known as the Treaty of Ganghwa, or more formally the Japan–Korea Treaty of Amity (in Korean, Joil suho jogyu). It contained twelve articles.

This chapter asks how Joseon moved from a regulated diplomatic order, centered on ritual deference to Qing China and on carefully supervised relations with Japan, to an unequal treaty relationship with a modernizing Japanese state. The answer cannot be reduced to a single warship. It involves a dispute over the wording of letters that lasted from 1868 to 1873, a Japanese government that had abolished the domain through which it once dealt with Joseon, a political crisis in Tokyo over whether to coerce Korea, a transfer of power inside the Joseon court in 1873, the armed incident at Ganghwa in 1875, the expedition led by Kuroda Kiyotaka, and the choices of Korean officials who tried to avoid war without surrendering what they understood as Joseon's sovereignty.

The chapter has two responsibilities. First, it treats the treaty itself in legal, diplomatic, military, and commercial detail, because the document's language, its omissions, and the supplementary agreements that followed it determined how the new relationship worked in practice. Second, it completes Volume II. The Ganghwa Treaty closes the Joseon dynasty's long period of regulated foreign relations and opens the era of treaty-based diplomacy, but it does not by itself produce the reforms, rivalries, and crises examined in Volume III, beginning with Chapter 49. Joseon in 1876 retained its monarchy, its bureaucracy, its legal system, and meaningful choices. Treating colonization in 1910 as the foreordained result of this treaty would erase those choices.

The evidence for these events is rich but uneven in perspective. The treaty exists in Japanese and Chinese-language versions, and Japanese diplomatic papers record instructions, reports, and internal debates in detail. Korean evidence includes court records, correspondence, and the memorials of officials and scholars, though the official annals for Gojong's reign were compiled in the 1930s under Japanese colonial authority and must be read with that context in mind. Qing archives and the papers of officials such as Li Hongzhang show how Beijing and Tianjin understood events. Foreign observers, especially diplomats stationed in East Asia, supplied outside commentary. Each source group has its own purposes and blind spots, and historians have repeatedly found that the same events look very different depending on which they privilege.

The chapter proceeds in five divisions. Division I explains the diplomatic system that existed before 1876 and its breakdown after the Meiji Restoration. Division II traces Japanese pressure, failed negotiations, and the Un'yō incident. Division III examines Kuroda's expedition and the negotiations at Ganghwa. Division IV analyzes the treaty article by article. Division V follows the August 1876 supplementary agreements, the Korean response, and the end of the old diplomatic order. Throughout, students should keep in view two questions that historians still debate: how much of the outcome was dictated by Japanese military power, and how much reflected Korean calculation under severe constraints.

Understanding the Ganghwa Treaty matters beyond its own year. It was the first modern treaty Joseon signed, and it supplied precedents, both in vocabulary and in legal structure, for the agreements with Western powers examined in later chapters. It also illustrates a pattern recurring across nineteenth-century Asia: a state that had itself been compelled to accept unequal treaties, Japan, used similar instruments against a neighbor. Recognizing both the coercion and the Korean agency in this story is the necessary starting point for the remaining chapters of the textbook.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Describe Joseon's diplomatic system before 1876, including tributary and investiture relations with Qing China and regulated relations with Japan through Tsushima.
  2. Explain why the diplomatic letter dispute of 1868–1873 involved competing assumptions about title, hierarchy, and legitimate interstate relations rather than mere ceremony.
  3. Analyze the Japanese debate over Korea in 1873 and explain why Meiji policy was not a single, unchanging plan.
  4. Reconstruct the Un'yō incident of September 1875 and evaluate the competing accounts of how the fighting began.
  5. Trace the negotiations of January–February 1876, identifying the principal negotiators and the constraints each side faced.
  6. Interpret the treaty's articles, distinguishing the original treaty from the supplementary agreement and trade regulations of August 1876.
  7. Distinguish the meanings of jaju (self-governing) and "independence" in Qing, Korean, and Japanese usage, and assess Article I's significance.
  8. Explain how extraterritoriality, coastal surveys, and unrestricted trade created practical inequality despite language of reciprocity.
  9. Compare the responses of Gojong's court, Confucian opponents, merchants, and coastal communities to the treaty.
  10. Assess the argument that the treaty resulted from gunboat diplomacy against the argument that Korean decisions materially shaped it.
  11. Evaluate the treaty as a turning point that closes Volume II without making later colonization inevitable.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did Joseon's older system of sadae and gyorin diplomacy organize relations with Qing China and Japan, and what assumptions did it rest upon?
  2. Why did a dispute over letters, seals, and titles become a diplomatic crisis between 1868 and 1873?
  3. Was Japanese pressure on Korea the product of a coherent national plan, or of competing factions and opportunities?
  4. What does the Un'yō incident reveal about the problems of reconstructing military confrontations from sources produced by the winning side?
  5. What alternatives did Korean officials have in early 1876, and what considerations led the court to negotiate?
  6. What did the word "independent" mean to the Japanese, Qing, and Korean officials who encountered it in Article I?
  7. How can a treaty phrased in reciprocal language produce unequal practical outcomes?
  8. Why should the original treaty be distinguished from the August 1876 agreements when assessing its commercial effects?
  9. In what sense did 1876 end an "old diplomatic order," and in what respects did earlier patterns continue?

Historical Context

By the 1870s Joseon had passed through a decade of acute foreign and domestic pressure. The wars with France in 1866 and the United States in 1871 (Chapter 45) had ended with foreign withdrawals that the Heungseon Daewongun's government presented as victories and commemorated with a policy of refusing treaties (Chapter 47). Within the kingdom, fiscal strain, local unrest, and the legacy of in-law rule (Chapters 43–44) shaped how officials weighed risks. Catholic persecution (Chapter 45) and the emergence of Donghak (Chapter 46) had also heightened anxieties about heterodoxy and foreign influence.

The wider regional setting was changing even faster. The Opium Wars had forced the Qing into treaty relations with Western powers, and Russia had acquired territory adjoining the Tumen River in 1860. Japan, after the Meiji Restoration of 1868, was constructing a centralized state, abolishing its domains in 1871, and sending missions abroad to revise the unequal treaties it had itself been forced to sign in the 1850s. In 1871 it concluded an equal treaty of friendship with the Qing, and in 1874 it sent an expedition to Taiwan. Joseon officials watched these developments through reports carried by the annual embassies to Beijing, through contacts at Busan, and through Qing communications. Their information was incomplete but not nonexistent.

Students should keep three features of the Joseon position in mind. First, the dynasty's foreign relations were not improvised; they were embedded in long-standing institutions with their own vocabulary, rituals, and legal assumptions. Second, Joseon officials distinguished among foreign states: relations with the Qing, with Tsushima and Japan, and with "Western barbarians" belonged to different categories, and the question in 1875–1876 was which category Meiji Japan occupied. Third, the court was divided. Some officials favored resisting any new arrangement; others sought to restore traditional relations on acceptable terms; a few believed Joseon needed to learn from the wider world. These divisions, not a single will, shaped the decisions this chapter examines.

Division I — The Existing Diplomatic Order and Its Breakdown · Section 48.1

Joseon's Diplomatic World on the Eve of 1876

Joseon's foreign relations before 1876 were organized around two principles that Korean officials rarely regarded as contradictory. The first, sadae ("serving the great"), governed relations with the Chinese dynasty: after the Manchu conquest and the submission of 1637 (Chapter 36), Joseon sent regular embassies to Beijing, received Qing investiture of each new king, used the Qing calendar, and observed the rituals of a tributary state. The second, gyorin ("neighborly relations"), governed dealings with Japan, where Joseon dealt with the Tokugawa shogunate as a peer of broadly comparable standing, though the formal channel ran through the Sō family of Tsushima. Together these principles are often called the sadae-gyorin framework.

Historical Vocabulary
Sadae and Gyorin

Definition: Sadae was the practice of showing deference to the larger Chinese state through tribute missions and investiture; gyorin was the practice of maintaining regulated, ritually correct relations with a neighboring state of comparable standing, especially Japan.

Historical context: Both terms appear in Joseon sources from the early dynasty (Chapter 30) and were maintained as organizing ideas for centuries. They described conduct as much as hierarchy: they specified who wrote to whom, in what form, with what titles, and through which officials.

Why it matters: The 1868–1876 crisis arose when Japan abandoned the forms of gyorin without Joseon's consent. Korean officials who rejected Japanese letters were defending a system they understood as legitimate and orderly.

Potential misunderstanding: Sadae did not mean that the Qing governed Joseon's domestic affairs. In practice, Joseon administered its own government, law, and taxation, and the Qing usually refrained from interfering. Nor did it mean that Joseon officials lacked their own interests in dealing with Beijing; the system was also a source of security and prestige.

The Qing relationship combined ritual hierarchy with practical autonomy. Annual embassies, most notably the winter-solstice mission, brought Joseon officials into regular contact with Beijing's political and intellectual life, and the embassies were also channels of trade and information. Investiture confirmed the legitimacy of each king. Because Qing officials had on several earlier occasions told Western diplomats that Korea managed its own internal and external affairs, Joseon's relationship with Beijing seemed compatible with considerable freedom of action. That ambiguity, a ritual subordination coexisting with practical self-direction, would become central in 1876.

Maritime controls reinforced the system. Joseon restricted private overseas voyages and treated unauthorized foreign ships as threats or as problems to be referred upward. Foreign castaways were normally repatriated, in the case of non-Japanese castaways often through Beijing. This apparatus protected the court's monopoly on external relations but also meant that Joseon had no standing offices for dealing with unfamiliar states. When France, the United States, and later Japan appeared in new roles, officials had to extend existing categories to unprecedented situations.

Section 48.2

Japan, Tsushima, and the Established Channels of Exchange

After the devastation of the Imjin War (Chapters 32–35), Joseon and the Tokugawa government restored relations gradually. The Giyu Treaty of 1609 between Joseon and the Sō domain of Tsushima regulated the annual ships, the allowances, and the conduct of trade. Tsushima, a poor island domain dependent on trade with the peninsula, played a dual role: it acted for the Tokugawa in Korean matters and, in Joseon's eyes, remained a subordinate frontier house that received Joseon recognition through seals and titles. The domain's officials, at times, shaded information in order to protect their own interests, as scholars have shown from both Korean and Japanese records.

The Japanese residence in Busan, the Waegwan, was moved to Choryang in 1678 and remained there until 1876. It was a supervised compound where Japanese merchants and officials of the Sō lived and conducted trade under Joseon regulation. Interpreters and officials known as hundo mediated, and licensed Korean merchants from Dongnae and elsewhere handled exchanges. Japanese were restricted to the compound, and Korean officials controlled provisioning and the framework of commerce. Commodities flowing to Japan included ginseng, rice, cotton, hides, and re-exported Chinese goods; Japan supplied copper, sulfur, and southeastern Asian goods such as pepper and sappanwood, among others. The scale of trade fluctuated and was never a mass market.

Formal missions complemented this commerce. Between 1607 and 1811 Joseon sent twelve large embassies, called tongsinsa (communication envoys), to Japan, which were important cultural events, carrying scholars, artists, and physicians whose encounters with Japanese counterparts were widely recorded. The final mission, in 1811, was received only at Tsushima, reflecting the Tokugawa government's financial constraints and the declining intensity of formal exchange. By the nineteenth century, therefore, the old channel was narrow and depended heavily on Tsushima and the routine at the Waegwan. When the Meiji government set about reorganizing Japan's external relations, it would be dismantling the very machinery through which Joseon understood Japan.

Section 48.3

The Meiji Restoration and the Diplomatic Letter Dispute, 1868–1873

The Meiji Restoration of 1868 replaced the Tokugawa shogunate with a government acting in the emperor's name. The new government wanted to announce its existence to its neighbors and to assume direct control of foreign policy. In the closing weeks of 1868 and early 1869 it used the Sō family's envoys to deliver a letter to Joseon's local officials at Dongnae announcing the change. The letter differed from established practice in two ways that mattered to Korean readers: its language described the Japanese ruler with terms associated with an emperor (tennō) and referred to imperial authority, and it bore a new seal in place of the one that Joseon had long recognized for the Sō family.

For Joseon officials these differences were substantive. In the East Asian hierarchy, only the Chinese ruler was an emperor, and the Joseon king stood in a tributary relationship to him. A Japanese ruler who styled himself emperor and wrote to the Joseon king as though to an inferior claimed a rank that Joseon had never conceded. The replacement of the seal severed the documentary chain through which gyorin had worked. Local officials, including the interpreter-official Ahn Dong-jun, declined to accept the letter, and their position was upheld in Seoul at a time when the Daewongun's government was resisting any alteration of the arrangements.

Primary Source Window I

The Disputed Diplomatic Correspondence between Meiji Japan and Joseon, 1868–1873

Creators: Japanese government officials and Tsushima envoys; Joseon officials at Dongnae and in Seoul · Dates: 1868–1873 · Type: Official diplomatic correspondence and reports preserved in Japanese diplomatic archives and Korean court and local records

Context: The letters announced Japan's political transformation and sought to continue relations on new terms. Their rejection is documented in Korean local reports and in Japanese accounts of the stalled negotiations.

Paraphrase: The following is a paraphrase, not a direct quotation, of the substance of the exchange.

Japanese letters announced that authority had been restored to an imperial court and used titles and a seal that differed from earlier practice. Korean officials responded that the wording and seal departed from established precedent and could not be received, and they asked that the earlier forms be restored. Later Japanese communications objected that Joseon was refusing a government's legitimate announcement of itself.

Analysis: The exchange shows that both governments invoked legitimacy: Japan appealed to its new constitutional order, Joseon to established precedent and hierarchy. The documents also show that each side read the other's conduct as an insult.

Limitations and perspective: Surviving documents largely come from officials on both sides; they do not reveal private deliberations. Japanese accounts often present Joseon's refusal as arrogance, while Korean sources present Japan's change as a violation of agreed forms. The views of ordinary merchants at the Waegwan, who had practical interests in continued trade, are largely missing.

Questions for students:

  1. What do the sources say was objectionable about the Japanese letters?
  2. Who created the sources, and under what circumstances?
  3. What purposes may have shaped how each side described the dispute?
  4. What can historians learn about competing understandings of diplomacy from this exchange?
  5. What cannot be established from these documents alone?

Other developments compounded the dispute. Japan abolished its domains in 1871, ending the Tsushima domain's separate existence, and in 1872 the Foreign Ministry took over the Busan establishment directly. From Joseon's perspective the intermediary with which it had dealt for centuries had been removed unilaterally. In 1873 Dongnae authorities reportedly posted a notice at the Waegwan criticizing Japan's adoption of Western dress and practices, language that provoked indignation when reported in Tokyo. The notice, and the broader refusal, fed arguments in Japan that Korea had insulted the new government.

Section 48.4

Gojong's Personal Rule and the Reconsideration of Foreign Policy

The political transition of 1873, treated more fully in Chapter 47, altered the environment for Joseon's foreign policy. In late 1873 a memorial from the Confucian scholar Choe Ik-hyeon criticized the Daewongun's conduct of government, and in its aftermath the Daewongun withdrew from power and the young King Gojong, then in his early twenties, began to rule personally. Officials associated with the Min family, relatives of Gojong's consort, rose in influence. The change did not itself determine foreign policy, but it removed the figure most closely identified with the refusal of foreign contact.

Within the court there was no consensus. Some officials, reflecting the anti-foreign legacy of the 1860s and 1870s, held that any concession to Japan would invite further demands. Others thought Joseon should avoid a conflict it could not win and suggested restoring routine relations with Japan under acceptable forms. Elder statesmen such as Park Gyu-su, grandson of the eighteenth-century scholar Park Ji-won (Chapter 38), are usually associated with a cautious willingness to negotiate. These officials were not proposing to abandon sovereignty; they sought to distinguish between Japan, an old neighbor with whom customary relations might be restored, and the Western powers whose demands Joseon had resisted.

That distinction proved both reasonable and risky. It was reasonable because Japan had been a recognized partner for centuries and because military conflict seemed dangerous. It was risky because the Meiji government regarded the old arrangement as obsolete and wanted a new treaty, not a restoration. Gojong's government thus entered the negotiations of 1874–1876 with a goal, preserving gyorin, that its counterpart did not share.

Division II — Japanese Pressure and the Un'yō Incident · Section 48.5

Seikanron and Japan's Debate over Korea

In Japan the question of Korea had been debated since the Restoration. Some officials in 1868–1870, among them Kido Takayoshi, had suggested coercive measures, and reports from officials sent to Busan, such as Sada Hakubō, urged intervention. The rejection of Meiji letters angered those who regarded it as an affront to national honor. Samurai who had lost privileges in the new order also saw Korea as an arena in which military service might regain meaning.

The crisis came in 1873. Saigō Takamori proposed to go to Korea himself as an envoy, and the cabinet agreed in principle in the summer. Historians disagree about Saigō's intentions: some argue that he expected to provoke a war, others that he sought to negotiate and, if killed, to provide a justification for war, and still others that he mainly wanted to resolve the status of samurai. The Iwakura mission, returning from its tour of Western states in September 1873, opposed the plan. Ōkubo Toshimichi and Iwakura Tomomi argued that Japan should prioritize domestic development and treaty revision. The emperor sided with them in October, and Saigō, Itagaki Taisuke, and others resigned from the government.

The result was not a rejection of coercion but a disagreement about timing and means. The government that remained, led by Ōkubo, still wanted a treaty with Korea and favored methods modeled on Western powers' experience with Japan. In 1874 it sent an expedition to Taiwan, and in 1875 it concluded an agreement with Russia over Sakhalin and the Kuril Islands, freeing attention for Korea. Meiji policy was thus neither a single plan nor a simple response to Korean provocation; it was the product of factional struggle, strategic priorities, and the idea that Japan's recent experience of unequal treaties provided a model.

Section 48.6

Failed Diplomacy, Intelligence Gathering, and the Qing Connection, 1873–1875

Negotiations resumed after Gojong began to rule. In 1874 Japanese officials, including Moriyama Shigeru, met Korean counterparts at Busan, and Joseon's local officials, notably the interpreter Hyeon Seok-un, explored how the dispute might be resolved. Progress was inconsistent. Korean officials remained reluctant to accept the new seal and imperial titles, and a dispute over whether Japanese representatives could appear in Western-style dress at formal receptions became a symbolic obstacle. In May 1875 Japanese warships exercised near Busan in a display of strength that alarmed local residents and officials.

Korean officials were not passive. Embassies to Beijing brought news of Japan's Taiwan expedition and of Qing discussions. Scholars with Chinese connections, such as Kang Wi, who traveled in the Qing orbit and argued for a more active understanding of foreign affairs, helped bring information and ideas to circles near the court. Historians have recently emphasized this informal diplomacy as evidence that Joseon officials were gathering intelligence and considering alternatives, though the precise extent of Kang Wi's influence on court decisions is difficult to establish and should not be exaggerated.

The Qing were also involved. Chinese officials, who had told Western diplomats in 1866 and 1871 that Korea ran its own affairs, now advised Joseon to avoid a rupture with Japan and, in late 1875, warned that Japan might resort to force. Qing leaders had limited military capacity in the region and many commitments elsewhere, and their counsel emphasized prudence. For Joseon this guidance reinforced the case for negotiating but did not provide a security guarantee.

Section 48.7

The Un'yō Incident of September 1875

The Un'yō was a small Japanese gunboat. In late summer 1875 it was dispatched to survey and explore Korean waters; it reached the waters near Ganghwa Island around September 20. The Ganghwa estuary was a restricted defensive zone containing forts reinforced after 1871. According to Japanese accounts, particularly the report of the ship's commander, Inoue Yoshika, a boat was sent ashore near Ganghwa's Chojijin fort to look for fresh water and was fired upon from the fortifications. The Un'yō returned fire, and on the following days its crew attacked the fort. On about September 22 a landing party went ashore on neighboring Yeongjong Island, attacked the garrison, and burned and looted the area, withdrawing with captured weapons. Japanese reports claimed few Japanese casualties and heavy Korean losses; accounts commonly mention around three dozen Korean dead, though figures vary by source.

The story of how the shooting began is contested. Japanese reports portray the Un'yō as a ship searching for water that was attacked without warning. Korean officials considered the ship an intruder in a restricted area that ignored warnings. Foreign observers and later historians have noted that the Un'yō's mission included survey work and that its approach to a defended estuary was probably intended at least to test Korean reactions. Whether Inoue acted on explicit instructions to provoke is not firmly established; what is clear is that the Japanese government subsequently exploited the incident. Recent military-historical analyses reconstructing the engagement from ship capabilities, weaponry, tides, and the sequence of bombardment help clarify the course of the fighting but do not by themselves settle questions of intent.

Section 48.8

The Aftermath of the Incident: Military Pressure and Diplomatic Demands

Tokyo presented the incident as an unprovoked attack on a Japanese vessel flying its national flag, an insult requiring redress, and used that framing to justify armed diplomacy. The government did not need to resolve the factual disputes about the shooting to use it: the incident supplied a vocabulary of grievance consistent with the language of international law that Japan had adopted. Japanese officials also compared their intended course with the experiences of Western powers in Asia, including the opening of Japan by the United States in the 1850s.

For Joseon the incident was a military and diplomatic predicament. Korean forces at Ganghwa and Yeongjong had been defeated by a single small vessel with modern weapons, and the court had to consider what a larger expedition could do. The defenders and their families suffered casualties and destruction, and the incident demonstrated that the defensive measures built after 1871 were inadequate against Japanese naval gunnery. The court's officials also had to explain the event to Beijing and to weigh Qing advice. Some conservatives argued that it demonstrated the need for firmness; others concluded that resistance could not succeed and that negotiation was necessary.

Cause and Effect

How a Naval Confrontation Became the Basis for an Unequal Agreement

Long-term causes

The sadae-gyorin system's reliance on forms and intermediaries; Japan's reorganization after 1868; the changing balance of power in East Asia; Japan's own experience of unequal treaties.

Short-term causes

The unresolved letter dispute of 1868–1873; the takeover of the Busan establishment in 1872; the Seikanron controversy; stalled talks in 1874–1875; Japanese naval demonstrations.

Immediate trigger

The Un'yō's approach to Ganghwa and the exchange of fire of September 1875, followed by the landing on Yeongjong.

Immediate effects

Korean casualties and the loss of weapons; Japanese demands for redress and a treaty; Qing advice to avoid war; a decision in Tokyo to send an armed mission.

Long-term consequences

A treaty with unequal provisions; a new framework for relations with Japan; precedents for later treaties with other powers.

Historical complexity

The incident did not by itself cause the treaty. It acted within a context of deliberate Japanese policy, Korean uncertainty, and Qing counsel; some historians stress Japanese intent, others Korean miscalculation.
Division III — Coercive Diplomacy and the Ganghwa Negotiations · Section 48.9

Kuroda Kiyotaka's Expedition and Japan's Armed Diplomacy

In December 1875 the Meiji government appointed Kuroda Kiyotaka, a Satsuma-born general and a leading figure in the government, as chief plenipotentiary, with Inoue Kaoru, a statesman from Chōshū with experience in foreign affairs, as his deputy. Their instructions combined two aims: to obtain redress for the Un'yō incident and to conclude a treaty of friendship and commerce modeled on the agreements Western powers had imposed on Japan and others. The mission was accompanied by a naval squadron and troops. Its composition reflected a choice: Japan would negotiate, but with armed force visibly present.

Armed diplomacy of this kind had a recognizable pattern in nineteenth-century Asia. Naval force served less to fight a war than to define the alternatives the other side faced. Japanese leaders had firsthand knowledge of this method, for Commodore Perry's squadron had pressed the Tokugawa government in 1853–1854, and they knew how the combination of visible power and willingness to talk could produce concessions. Kuroda's squadron anchored in Korean waters in January 1876, moved to the Ganghwa area, and conveyed clearly that failure to negotiate could lead to military escalation, including pressure on the river approach to Seoul.

Japan's objectives were commercial, strategic, and political. A treaty would open Korean ports and markets to Japanese merchants. It would give Japan information about the coast and a legal position on the peninsula. It would also clarify Korea's status in relation to the Qing. Not all Japanese leaders held these goals equally, and Japan's navy and army had limited resources, which is one reason the government preferred a negotiated outcome backed by threat to a war of conquest.

Section 48.10

Shin Heon, the Joseon Court, and the Decision to Negotiate

Gojong's government appointed Shin Heon, an elderly general who had served in the military establishment, as chief Korean commissioner (in the language of the time, the senior official responsible for receiving the Japanese delegation), with Yun Ja-seung as his deputy. The choice of a senior military figure signaled seriousness and also reflected the preference for treating the talks as a matter of receiving an envoy rather than concluding a modern treaty. The commissioners worked under instructions from a court that was still deliberating.

Deliberations in Seoul show the range of views. Those favoring resistance argued that Japan had forsaken its obligations and that Joseon's defenses, though imperfect, had repelled Western forces in the past. Those favoring accommodation emphasized the defeat at Ganghwa, Qing advice, the danger of an attack on the river approach to the capital, and the possibility that old relations could be restored in new form. Some officials argued that the Japanese were an old neighbor with whom relations could be regulated, whereas treaties with Western powers would be unacceptable; this distinction shaped the court's willingness to proceed. The king's own role was important: he was a young monarch exercising authority for the first time, and the decisions reflected his calculation as well as the advice of senior officials.

The constraints on Korean officials were real. They lacked reliable intelligence about Japanese capabilities and intentions, had no experience of treaty negotiation in the modern sense, and faced a counterpart who controlled the military initiative. Yet they were not without options: they could delay, bargain over wording, seek Qing support, and attempt to shape the terms. Whether they used these options effectively is a question taken up in the Historical Debate below.

Section 48.11

Qing China, Korean Sovereignty, and the Changing East Asian Order

The Qing position was ambiguous. Beijing regarded Joseon as a tributary and claimed a protective responsibility, but it had often stated that Korea conducted its own internal and external affairs, a formulation that preserved Chinese prestige while avoiding obligations. In January 1876, as Kuroda's mission prepared to negotiate, the Japanese minister Mori Arinori discussed Korea with Li Hongzhang at Tianjin. Li acknowledged Joseon's tributary status while stating that Korea managed its own affairs and that China would not interfere in them. Japanese officials took this as support for their position that Korea was free to conclude its own treaty.

Japan's interest was to ensure that Korea's treaty-making capacity was recognized without reference to Qing authority, and Japanese negotiators sought a formula that declared Korea an independent state. The Qing, for their part, had limited military options and were more concerned with threats elsewhere, including Central Asia and the coast. They advised Joseon to settle, hoping to avert conflict and preserve the relationship, but they did not intend to surrender suzerainty. The result was a gap in understanding: Japan used "independence" as a legal and political term, while Beijing and Seoul could understand Korea's autonomy as consistent with continued tribute.

The conflict between these meanings did not end in 1876. It became central to the diplomatic maneuvering examined in Chapters 49 through 51, when the Qing would try to convert its ritual authority into practical control. What matters here is that the treaty's language arose in a context where each government had reasons to accept ambiguity.

Section 48.12

The Signing of the Treaty of Ganghwa, February 26, 1876

Formal talks opened in mid-February 1876 at Ganghwa town, in the island's government offices. Kuroda and Inoue presented a draft treaty, and the Korean commissioners objected to some provisions and sought clarification of others. The Japanese delegation made clear that the unresolved Un'yō affair and the rejection of earlier letters were not to be treated as a reason to prolong discussion. The Korean negotiators treated the sessions as a means of restoring friendly relations; the Japanese treated them as the occasion for a new treaty.

The treaty was signed on February 26, 1876, by Kuroda and Inoue for Japan and by Shin Heon and Yun Ja-seung for Joseon. It had twelve articles. Its text was prepared in Japanese and Chinese, and Korean officials worked from the Chinese version. Several features distinguished it from the Western treaties with Japan: it was bilateral and formally reciprocal, it did not contain a most-favored-nation clause, and it did not specify tariff rates, leaving commercial rules to later negotiation. Yet it was unequal in effect, as the following division shows, because its reciprocal language concealed asymmetries in power and presence. Korean officials understood much but not all of the document's implications; the question of what they understood remains a subject of research.

The treaty also differs from what Korean officials thought they were negotiating. Many in Seoul regarded it as a renewal of friendship (suho) with an old neighbor, comparable to earlier gyorin arrangements. The Meiji government regarded it as an instrument of the modern international system. That difference in conception is key to understanding both the speed of the negotiation and the subsequent disputes over interpretation.

Historical Debate

Gunboat Diplomacy or Korean Choice?

The question: Was the Ganghwa Treaty primarily the outcome of Japanese military coercion, or did Korean diplomatic calculations materially shape the agreement?

Interpretation A — Coercion was decisive: Historians emphasizing coercion point to the Un'yō's actions, the squadron that accompanied Kuroda, the explicit threats, and the model of Western unequal treaties. On this view, Korean officials faced a choice between signing and war, and the treaty's provisions reflect Japanese priorities.
Interpretation B — Korean calculation shaped the outcome: Other scholars emphasize that Joseon's court deliberated, gathered information through Qing channels and informal contacts, sought to restore gyorin, and secured features such as the reciprocal wording and the absence of some demands. On this view, Korean officials made choices under constraint rather than simply yielding.
Interpretation C — Both processes interacted: A third approach holds that coercion defined the range of options while Korean choices determined which option was taken and how it was framed, so that the treaty reflects both pressures.

Evidence: Japanese diplomatic papers document intent and instructions; Korean records show deliberation; Qing correspondence reveals advice; the treaty text records outcomes. No single source group settles the question.

Points of disagreement: The debate concerns interpretation and weight, and in part terminology: how much latitude Korean officials had, and how much their understanding of the treaty differed from Japan's.

Why it matters: The answer affects whether the treaty is read as an imposition or a negotiated, if unequal, settlement, and thus how later Korean policy choices are understood.

Student task: Identify one piece of evidence that supports each interpretation and explain what additional evidence would help distinguish them.

Division IV — The Treaty and Its Unequal Provisions · Section 48.13

Article I: Independence, Equality, and the Qing Relationship

Article I is the treaty's most politically significant provision. In the standard English translation it declares that Chosen, "being an independent state, enjoys the same sovereign rights as does Japan." The article continues by calling for intercourse on terms of equality and courtesy and by stating that rules and precedents obstructing friendly relations were to be abrogated and replaced with more liberal and comprehensive arrangements. The second clause was the legal instrument by which the old Tsushima-based framework was set aside.

Historical Vocabulary
Jaju (自主) and "Independence"

Definition: The Chinese-character phrase rendered in English as "independent state" in Article I used a term (jaju, Chinese zizhu) meaning self-directing or self-governing.

Historical context: In Qing and Korean usage, a state could be jaju while remaining a tributary: Joseon governed itself and conducted its own policies yet performed rituals of deference to Beijing. Japanese officials, schooled in Western international law, intended the word to convey independence in the sense of freedom from any suzerain.

Why it matters: Both readings were available, and each government could claim that the treaty confirmed its own understanding. The ambiguity allowed signature in 1876 and fueled disputes later.

Potential misunderstanding: The word did not mean that the Qing relationship ended in 1876. Investiture and tribute continued, and the Qing later sought to convert them into tighter supervision.

The article's political significance lay in how it altered Joseon's position without formally abolishing the Qing relationship. For Japan it created a precedent: if Korea was an equal sovereign, then Japan could deal with it directly and the Qing had no standing in the matter. For Joseon's court, the article could be read as confirming longstanding autonomy and therefore as compatible with sadae. For the Qing, it was acceptable as long as it did not alter investiture and tribute. The article thus reduced Beijing's room to claim a role in Korea's treaty relations while leaving its ritual position intact, a situation that persisted until the Sino-Japanese War of 1894–1895 (Chapter 51).

Section 48.14

Diplomatic Representation and the New Rules of Interstate Relations

Articles II and III set out the mechanics of the new relationship. Article II provided for the exchange of envoys: Japan could send an envoy to Seoul to discuss diplomatic matters, and Joseon could send one to Tokyo, with each envoy either residing in the other capital or returning after completing his business. The provision replaced the older pattern in which communication moved through Tsushima and Dongnae with direct contact between capitals. Joseon responded promptly by sending an envoy to Japan in the spring of 1876, but the question of a permanent Japanese envoy in Seoul remained sensitive and was resolved only later.

Article III concerned language. Official communications from Japan to Joseon were to be in Japanese, accompanied for a limited period by a Chinese version, while Joseon would use Chinese. The article reveals the practical reality that Chinese remained the shared diplomatic language of East Asia, and it also shows that Joseon's official use of Hangeul (Chapter 27) had no place in international correspondence. The ten-year Chinese translation period was a concession to practicality that also hinted at Japan's intention to establish its own language of diplomacy.

The new rules of interstate relations replaced ritual hierarchy with the formal equality of sovereign states. The exchange of titles, seals, and letters that had caused the dispute of 1868–1873 was, in principle, settled by creating a framework in which each side used its own forms. In practice Joseon had accepted the Meiji government's terms of address, the very point it had resisted for seven years, and the treaty marked the end of the Sō-mediated system. For Korean officials who had thought of the talks as restoring friendship, that result was a larger change than they may have appreciated.

Section 48.15

Treaty Ports, Land Rights, and the Opening of Commerce

Articles IV and V addressed trade. Busan already contained a regulated Japanese establishment, so Article IV continued that place as a site of commerce under treaty terms rather than Sō-era regulations. Article V provided for two additional ports to be selected on the coasts of specified provinces, with a timetable for opening, and permitted Japanese subjects at the ports to lease land and erect buildings or rent Korean buildings. The treaty did not name Wonsan and Incheon; those were chosen later, and their development as treaty ports belongs principally to Chapter 49.

The change from the Waegwan system was significant even where continuity was apparent. Under the old arrangement, Japanese were confined to a supervised compound, trade passed through licensed intermediaries, and Joseon officials controlled supplies and procedures. Under the treaty, Japanese could reside and hold property in designated places, and the framework for commerce shifted from Korean regulation toward negotiated rules. The term "open port" thus described not simply an additional harbor but a legal zone in which Japanese had rights that Korean subjects did not enjoy in Japan.

Everyday Life and Social History

Ports, Coasts, and the Anticipated Effects of Expanded Trade

For residents of Busan and Dongnae, the Waegwan had been a familiar presence, a source of livelihood for licensed merchants, interpreters, porters, and suppliers, and a source of tension when regulations were strained. The treaty promised to enlarge that world. Commercial brokers and merchant houses of the kind that operated in Joseon's trade towns, and merchants from places such as Dongnae and Gaeseong, could expect new opportunities and new competition, while peasants who sold grain and cotton into markets linked to the ports might see prices change in ways that were not easy to foresee.

For coastal communities near Ganghwa and Yeongjong, the immediate experience of 1875 had been violence: soldiers killed, a fort and nearby buildings burned, and fear that further attacks would follow. Fishing and farming communities lived under the anxieties of a fortified estuary, and families of the dead had to cope with loss. Surviving sources, largely produced by officials, say little about their feelings, so historians reconstruct their experience indirectly from administrative reports and later accounts.

The record is thin on the effects of Japanese residence in these first months, and historians should not project later developments, such as the large-scale rice trade and imports of cotton textiles that many scholars associate with the following decade, back onto 1876. What can be said is that expectations varied: officials worried about security, merchants about competition and opportunity, and farmers about the cost of grain.

Section 48.16

Coastal Surveys, Maritime Access, and Japanese Representatives

Articles VI through VIII dealt with the sea and with representation. Article VI provided for assistance to vessels wrecked or in distress on the other's coast, a humanitarian provision with a long history in Korean practice that nonetheless acquired new weight when it was extended to vessels of an expanding maritime power. Article VII allowed Japanese vessels to survey the Korean coast and to make charts of its waters, on the ground that the coast was dangerous and the information would improve safety.

The survey provision had strategic implications. Accurate hydrographic information is useful for commerce but also for military operations, and Joseon had never permitted foreign cartography of its coasts. The treaty therefore transferred knowledge that had been a defensive asset, and the Un'yō's earlier conduct showed the interest of Japanese navy officers in precisely this information. The provision was reciprocal in form, but only Japan possessed the means and the intention to use it extensively.

Article VIII allowed the Japanese government to station officials at the open ports to protect Japanese merchants and their interests. These representatives would handle relations with Korean authorities and exercise the powers granted by the treaty over Japanese subjects. They were also, in effect, the first permanent foreign officials in Joseon's treaty ports. Their presence anticipated the consular systems that expanded after 1876 and linked the ports directly to the Japanese state.

Section 48.17

Free Trade, Extraterritoriality, and Unequal Legal Authority

Article IX provided that trade at the ports would be conducted freely, without interference or restriction by officials of either government. Under the Waegwan system, Korean officials had regulated trade closely; under the treaty, they were barred from the kind of supervision that had defined it. The article did not specify tariffs or customs duties, leaving them to later negotiation, and the trade regulations of August 1876 (Section 48.18) then resolved the matter in Japan's favor.

Historical Vocabulary
Extraterritoriality (Consular Jurisdiction)

Definition: A legal arrangement under which foreign nationals residing in a state are subject to their own country's officials and law rather than to the local courts.

Historical context: Western powers had obtained such rights in China and Japan in the mid-nineteenth century; Japan then sought to end them in its own case and, in 1876, obtained a version of them in Korea.

Why it matters: Extraterritoriality limited the reach of Korean law over foreign residents and made enforcement dependent on foreign officials' decisions.

Potential misunderstanding: Article X was written reciprocally, with Korean offenders tried by Korean authorities and Japanese offenders by Japanese. Because only Japanese were present in the ports in numbers, the reciprocity did not produce equal outcomes.

Article X is the clearest example of formal reciprocity masking practical inequality. By providing that each side's nationals would be tried by their own officials, it established a legal regime in which Korean authorities could not try Japanese defendants. Because Koreans were not resident in Japanese ports in comparable numbers, the symmetrical language operated asymmetrically. The provision also had a historical irony: Japan had itself struggled to remove such arrangements from the treaties imposed on it in the 1850s, and it now applied a similar device to Korea.

Articles XI and XII completed the document. Article XI provided for further negotiations to settle commercial and other regulations, which produced the August agreements; Article XII provided for the treaty's effect from the date of signing. Taken together, the articles did more than regulate commerce. They changed the forms of diplomacy, widened Japanese access to ports, land, coasts, and information, and limited Korean legal authority over Japanese subjects, while leaving Joseon's government, monarchy, and domestic administration intact.

Division V — Implementation, Consequences, and Historical Synthesis · Section 48.18

The Supplementary Agreement and Trade Regulations of August 1876

Article XI of the treaty required further negotiation, and in the summer of 1876 Korean and Japanese representatives met again at Ganghwa. Joseon had meanwhile sent its first mission to Meiji Japan, led by Kim Gi-su, in the spring and early summer. Such missions, called susinsa (envoys of amity), allowed Korean officials to observe Japanese government offices, schools, factories, and military institutions at first hand, and Kim's reports presented a mixture of curiosity, caution, and unease. On August 24, 1876, the two sides concluded a supplementary agreement and a set of trade regulations that supplied the working rules the treaty had left open.

The supplementary agreement addressed the conduct of Japanese residents. It limited the area within which Japanese could travel around the ports, specified the use of property and the hiring of local labor, and arranged communication between officials. Joseon's negotiators, among them Jo In-hui, sought to limit the geographic reach of Japanese activity, and the agreement's travel restrictions reflect that aim. The trade regulations addressed commercial procedures and currency: Japanese currency was allowed to circulate in the open ports, and trade was not subjected to the customs duties that most states of the period imposed. Japanese merchants also gained the freedom to export grain, a provision whose effects on the Korean rice market became a source of grievance in later years.

Section 48.19

The Immediate Korean Response to the Treaty

Reaction in Korea was divided. Before the signing, Choe Ik-hyeon, the scholar who had criticized the Daewongun in 1873, presented a memorial to the king in January 1876, reportedly kneeling in public with an axe to signify willingness to be punished for his words. Such protest drew on the tradition of wijeong cheoksa, defending orthodoxy and rejecting heterodoxy. Choe argued that Japan was no different from the Western powers, that trade would exchange useful Korean goods for luxury items, and that opening relations would endanger Confucian morality and invite foreign influence. He was exiled for his action, and his arguments circulated among scholars.

Multiple Perspectives

How Different Groups Understood the Treaty of Ganghwa

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns historians have identified across many sources. They are not quotations from any single named historical actor, and the groups did not possess equal power.

Gojong's court and officials favoring accommodation: The treaty could be understood as a means of avoiding war and restoring relations with an old neighbor on terms that left Joseon's sovereignty and Qing relationship intact. Its risks were acknowledged but weighed against the danger of attack.
Confucian scholars such as Choe Ik-hyeon: The treaty was a surrender to a power indistinguishable from the Western threat, endangering moral order and economic self-sufficiency, and the proper response was resistance despite the military risk.
Japanese officials: The treaty was the instrument by which Japan obtained recognition of Korea's treaty-making capacity, commercial access, and a legal position, and it conformed to the international law Japan had adopted. Many regarded it as only a first step.
Korean merchants and port communities: The treaty promised both opportunity and competition. Those with links to the Waegwan trade could hope for expanded business; others feared dependence on Japanese suppliers, and farmers worried about the grain trade. Their views are recorded mostly indirectly.
Qing officials: The treaty could be tolerated if tribute and investiture continued, and it might restrain Japan peacefully. Over time, however, it also loosened Beijing's room to define Korea's status, which would prompt a more assertive Qing policy.

Among officials, the dominant view was that the treaty was acceptable as a means of preserving peace, and the court proceeded to implement it. The reception of Kim Gi-su's mission and the instructions given for the new arrangements show an effort to manage change cautiously. Opposition did not disappear, however. Scholars' protest, rural unease, and fears about economic dependence were part of the political background to the crises of the 1880s examined in Volume III.

Section 48.20

The End of the Old Diplomatic Order and the Beginning of a New Era

The Ganghwa Treaty ended the diplomatic order that had governed Joseon's relations with Japan, and it opened a period in which Joseon's relations with all foreign states were conducted through treaties. It did not end the Qing relationship, the monarchy, or the domestic institutions of the Joseon state. The year 1876 therefore marks a transition within continuing structures rather than a clean break, and it is the proper endpoint for Volume II.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Traditional Diplomacy and Treaty-Based Diplomacy

What Changed

  • Relations with Japan moved from a Tsushima-mediated, ritual framework to direct state-to-state treaty relations
  • Japanese residents gained property rights, consular officials, and legal immunity from Korean courts
  • Commerce moved from supervised exchange toward freer trade without customs duties
  • Foreign access to Korean coastal information widened through surveys

What Remained Continuous

  • The monarchy, bureaucracy, laws, and tax system of Joseon continued
  • Tribute and investiture with the Qing continued
  • Chinese remained the language of diplomacy
  • Many Korean officials continued to see Japan through the lens of gyorin

Timing and experience: The change was abrupt in diplomacy, gradual in commerce, and uneven in society. Port merchants and officials in Seoul experienced it more directly than most peasants. Why it matters: Recognizing both change and continuity prevents reading 1876 either as a total collapse or as a minor adjustment.

As the closing of Volume II, the treaty belongs within a long arc. Joseon had been founded in 1392 as a Neo-Confucian dynasty (Chapters 25–26) that built institutions of civil government, produced Hangeul and a body of scientific and technical work (Chapters 27–28), and organized society through a status system that gradually changed (Chapters 29 and 42). It survived the Imjin War and the Manchu invasions (Chapters 31–36), recovered through agricultural and commercial growth and intellectual change (Chapters 37–41), and then faced the nineteenth-century combination of weak monarchy, fiscal strain, rebellion, and foreign pressure (Chapters 43–47). The treaty answered a specific question, how to deal with Meiji Japan, but it also exposed the dynasty to a wider international system for which its institutions had not been designed.

It would be a mistake to read this as the first step toward annexation in 1910. In 1876 Joseon retained its government and its capacity for choice. Later chapters show that outcomes depended on the Imo Mutiny of 1882, Qing and Japanese rivalry, domestic reform movements, the Donghak uprising, war between Qing and Japan, and Russian involvement, none of which was foreordained. Chapter 49 turns to the opening of ports and the new international order; the volume closes here with a treaty that changed the rules of Joseon's diplomacy and opened a period of far greater uncertainty.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has explained how Joseon moved from a regulated diplomatic order to treaty relations with Meiji Japan. The old order combined sadae toward the Qing with gyorin toward Japan, and relations with Japan passed through the Sō domain of Tsushima and the supervised Waegwan at Busan. It was a stable system with its own vocabulary of titles, seals, and precedents.

The Meiji Restoration disrupted that system. The Japanese government's letters of 1868–1869 used imperial terminology and a new seal, and Joseon officials, defending established forms, declined to accept them. The abolition of the Tsushima domain and the takeover of the Busan establishment in 1871–1872 removed the traditional intermediary. The dispute was therefore not simply ceremonial; it concerned legitimacy and hierarchy.

In Japan, the Seikanron crisis of 1873 revealed disagreement about whether and how to coerce Korea, and the government that emerged still sought a treaty. In Joseon, the transfer of power from the Daewongun to Gojong in late 1873 opened space for reconsideration, but the court's aim of restoring gyorin differed from Japan's aim of a modern treaty. Negotiations in 1874–1875, informed by Qing advice and informal Korean information-gathering, failed to resolve the dispute.

The Un'yō incident of September 1875 supplied Japan with a justification for armed diplomacy. The circumstances of the shooting remain contested, but the Japanese government used the incident to send Kuroda Kiyotaka and Inoue Kaoru with a naval squadron. Their mission combined negotiation with the visible threat of force, in a manner familiar from Western pressure on Japan itself.

The Korean court, after deliberation, chose to negotiate under Shin Heon and Yun Ja-seung. The Qing, whose position ambiguously acknowledged Korean tributary status while describing Korea as self-governing, advised settlement. The treaty signed on February 26, 1876 contained twelve articles. Article I called Joseon an independent state with equal sovereign rights, using wording that Japan, Joseon, and the Qing could read differently.

The remaining articles arranged envoys and language, continued Busan and provided for two additional ports with land rights, allowed coastal surveys and Japanese officials at the ports, guaranteed free trade, and established consular jurisdiction over Japanese subjects. Their formal reciprocity masked practical asymmetry because Japanese, not Koreans, were present in Korean ports and waters.

The supplementary agreement and trade regulations of August 24, 1876 gave working content to the treaty, limiting Japanese travel but also providing for Japanese currency in the ports and trade without customs duties. The distinction between the February treaty and the August rules is essential to assessing the treaty's commercial effects.

Korean reactions were divided. Officials mostly accepted the treaty as a means of avoiding war, Confucian scholars such as Choe Ik-hyeon denounced it, and merchants and coastal communities faced uncertain prospects. Historians debate how far the outcome reflected coercion and how far Korean calculation, with many emphasizing that both operated together.

The treaty ended the old diplomatic order without ending the Joseon state or the Qing relationship. It closed Volume II by changing the rules under which Joseon met the world, while leaving open the alternatives that later chapters examine. Joseon's colonization in 1910 was not its necessary consequence.

Sō domain of Tsushima
The island domain that mediated Japanese–Joseon relations until its abolition in 1871.
Meiji Restoration
The 1868 change of government in Japan that replaced the shogunate with a centralizing state acting in the emperor's name.
Tennō
The Japanese term for the emperor, whose use in 1868 letters Joseon officials considered an unacceptable claim of rank.
Seikanron
The 1873 Japanese debate over sending an envoy to, or attacking, Korea.
Gunboat diplomacy
The use of naval force or its threat to obtain diplomatic or commercial concessions.
Un'yō incident
The September 1875 clash between a Japanese gunboat and Korean forces near Ganghwa and Yeongjong.
Plenipotentiary
An envoy authorized to negotiate and sign a treaty on behalf of his government.
Jaju
A term meaning self-directing, used in Article I and readable as compatible with tributary status or as independence.
Suzerainty
A relationship in which a larger state holds ritual or political superiority over a nominally self-governing one.
Treaty port
A port opened by treaty to foreign residence and trade under special legal conditions.
Hydrographic survey
The measurement and charting of coastal and sea waters, permitted by Article VII.
Extraterritoriality
The exemption of foreign nationals from local courts in favor of their own officials, established by Article X.
Unequal treaty
A treaty whose terms, whatever their wording, impose greater burdens or fewer rights on one party in practice.
Supplementary agreement
The August 24, 1876 agreement that supplied rules for travel, property, labor, and communication.
Trade regulations
The August 1876 commercial rules covering currency, duties, and procedures.
Susinsa
Envoys of amity, the Korean missions to Japan after 1876, beginning with Kim Gi-su's.
Wijeong cheoksa
The Confucian principle of defending orthodoxy and rejecting heterodoxy, invoked against the treaty.
Ri
A Korean unit of distance used to define the zone of Japanese travel around the ports.

MGU Library Research Project

Building a Source Dossier on the Treaty of Ganghwa

This project asks you to assemble and analyze a small dossier of sources on the 1876 treaty, comparing how Japanese, Korean, and Qing materials describe the same events.

  1. Obtain the treaty. Locate a reliable English translation of the twelve articles and, if possible, the August 1876 agreements.
  2. Gather perspectives. Identify at least one Japanese, one Korean, and one Qing or foreign source or reliable scholarly study discussing the negotiations.
  3. Compare accounts. Note where the sources agree and disagree on the Un'yō incident, the aims of the negotiators, and the meaning of Article I.
  4. Assess limitations. For each source, identify author, date, audience, and purpose, and note what is missing.
  5. Write your findings. In a 3–5 page essay, present what the dossier establishes, what remains uncertain, and how the evidence bears on the debate between coercion and Korean agency, using cautious language where evidence is limited.

Library resources to use: Treaty collections, translated diplomatic documents, scholarship on late Joseon foreign relations, and the MGU History Library's reference materials on East Asian diplomacy.

Chapter Reflection

The Ganghwa Treaty matters because it changed the rules by which Joseon met the world. For centuries, Korean officials had understood diplomacy as a matter of appropriate forms: who wrote to whom, with what titles, through which intermediaries. In 1876 that world gave way to a legal order of treaties, equal sovereigns, consuls, and open ports, one in which formal equality coexisted with real inequality of power.

The chapter also shows how much depended on interpretation. The same word could signal independence to Japanese officials and self-direction within a tributary relationship to Qing and Korean readers. The same treaty could be a renewal of friendship to one court and an instrument of expansion to another. Historians' attention to these differences guards against reading either government's intentions as transparent.

Looking forward, the treaty's precedents shaped the agreements, rivalries, and reforms of Volume III, beginning with the treaty-port world of Chapter 49. Looking back, it concludes the Joseon story told in Volume II, in which a long-lived dynasty adapted to wars, social change, and crises, and then faced an international environment that its institutions had not anticipated.

Finally, the chapter invites humility about outcomes. Korean officials in 1876 did not know what the next decades would bring, and neither did their Japanese counterparts. Understanding the choices open to them, and the constraints that narrowed those choices, is the first step in understanding the events that follow.
