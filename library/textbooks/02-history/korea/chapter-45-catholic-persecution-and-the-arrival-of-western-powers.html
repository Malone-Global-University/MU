Catholic Persecution and the Arrival of Western Powers

Korean History: Ancient Kingdoms to a Divided Peninsula

Chapter Opening

In 1784 a Korean scholar was baptized in Beijing and returned to Seoul with books. Within a few years a community of believers existed in the capital, led by Koreans and without a resident European priest. Eighty-two years later, in 1866, French warships anchored off Ganghwa Island, within reach of the Han River and the capital, citing the execution of French missionaries. The distance between these two moments is the subject of this chapter.

Historians cannot reconstruct this story from a single kind of source. Court chronicles and interrogation records show how officials understood Catholicism and foreign ships. Church histories, letters and martyrologies show how believers and missionaries understood their experience. French and American naval and diplomatic reports show Western purposes and assumptions. Each is partial, and none records the thoughts of most villagers, women and soldiers, whose lives were affected by both persecution and war.

The chapter argues that Korea’s confrontation with the West was not simply a conflict between modernization and isolation. It involved disputes over religious authority and the ritual basis of Confucian family order, over what sovereignty meant when foreign ships entered Korean waters, and over how a state should respond when Western navies were coercing its neighbors. Korean Catholics were participants in Korean history, not foreign agents, and Western powers were not interchangeable: French intervention followed the executions of missionaries, while American action followed the General Sherman and commercial and diplomatic aims.

The chapter follows two intersecting strands, Catholic history from 1784 and Western encounters from 1832, and ends with the consequences of 1871, noting that persecution continued to 1873. The decisive crisis with Japan is the subject of Chapter 48.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how Catholicism entered Joseon and why the church was Korean-led.
  2. Distinguish Western Learning from religious conversion.
  3. Compare the persecutions of 1791, 1801, 1839, 1846 and 1866.
  4. Describe how Catholic communities survived, including the roles of women and lay leaders.
  5. Trace the General Sherman affair, the French expedition and the American expedition.
  6. Distinguish French and American motives.
  7. Evaluate interpretations of Joseon policy as isolation or defense of sovereignty.
  8. Analyze sources from Korean, church and foreign perspectives.

Historical Context

By the nineteenth century Joseon was a Neo-Confucian dynasty more than four hundred years old. Its legitimacy rested on a hierarchy of ruler and subject, parent and child, expressed in education, law and above all in ancestral ritual. Its foreign relations were organized around tributary relations with Qing China and regulated exchange with Japan, with periodic embassies to Beijing that were also the main channel by which Joseon learned of the outside world.

The late eighteenth century also saw intellectual change: scholars interested in practical reform and in Western science, factional struggle at court between groups out of and in power, and in the nineteenth century the dominance of in-law families and increasing rural distress (Chapters 43 and 44). These circumstances shaped both the appeal of Catholicism, which offered a new moral community, and the way the state reacted, since officials saw challenges to ritual as challenges to the political order.

Beyond Korea, the Qing was defeated by Britain and France in the Opium Wars, Russia reached the Tumen River in 1860, and Japan would soon be forced open. Joseon’s decisions in the 1860s were made in this context of uncertainty.

Section 45.1

Joseon and the Western Challenge

This chapter follows two histories that intersected for a few years in the 1860s. The first is the history of Catholicism in Joseon, from its adoption by scholars in the 1780s to the great persecution of 1866 and after. The second is the history of Western ships and governments approaching Korea, which moved from commercial requests and surveys to the French and American operations of 1866 and 1871. The connection between them was real, since French missionaries were the stated reason for the French expedition, but the two were not identical. American actions arose from the loss of the General Sherman and from commercial and diplomatic interests, and Catholic Koreans were not agents of any Western state.

The sources reflect the positions of their authors. Joseon court records, interrogation protocols and official edicts present Catholicism as heterodoxy and foreign vessels as intruders. Church histories, written by missionaries and Korean believers, emphasize martyrdom and faith. French and American naval and diplomatic reports explain Western aims in the language of their own governments. Modern historians in Korea, Europe and the United States have used these materials to ask different questions, from the history of religion and gender to the study of imperialism and sovereignty, and the chapter notes where their conclusions differ.

The chapter has a defined place in the textbook. The general politics of in-law rule belong to Chapter 43, rural crisis to Chapter 44, the Donghak movement to Chapter 46, the Daewongun’s rule as a whole to Chapter 47, and the Japanese treaty crisis to Chapter 48. Here the Daewongun appears only as far as he shaped the persecutions and the confrontations of 1866–1871.

Section 45.2

Western Learning and the Introduction of Catholic Ideas

Before Catholicism was a community in Joseon, it was a body of texts. Books produced in China by Jesuit missionaries, among them Matteo Ricci’s dialogue on the Lord of Heaven and works on astronomy, mathematics and geography, reached Seoul with the annual embassies to Beijing from the seventeenth century. Joseon scholars valued the scientific writings, and the calendar reform that adopted Western methods in the Qing made such knowledge difficult to ignore. This body of material, grouped under Seohak, was not a single doctrine, and the court did not initially treat reading it as a crime.

In the later eighteenth century, small circles of scholars, many of them associated with the Namin, a faction out of power at court, studied the religious books with intense seriousness. Later church tradition describes study retreats around 1779 in which such scholars debated the texts, although the details come from later sources and should be treated with caution. For some, Catholic teaching offered a moral seriousness and an ethic of equality before God that appealed amid dissatisfaction with factional politics. For others, it remained an object of scholarly curiosity. Yi Ik’s school and its successors contained both attitudes.

The distinction between studying Western ideas and entering a religious community is central to this chapter. Reading Ricci was compatible with Confucian scholarship; baptism, a new moral discipline and above all refusal of ancestral ritual were not. The conflict that followed was therefore not simply between knowledge and ignorance, but between a tolerated intellectual interest and a form of belonging that competed with family, lineage and the state for loyalty. Historians rely on the writings of the scholars, later church histories and court records, each of which was written with different purposes.

Historical Vocabulary

Seohak, Cheonjugyo, SahakSeohak means Western Learning. Cheonjugyo (Lord of Heaven teaching) was the Korean name for Catholicism. Officials called it sahak, heterodox learning, which expressed their view that it threatened orthodoxy. Seohak and Catholicism were not identical: many scholars read Western books without conversion.

Section 45.3

The Korean-Led Founding of the Catholic Church, 1784–1790

The decisive step came in 1783–1784. Yi Seung-hun, whose father was attached to the embassy to Beijing, traveled with it, met Catholic clergy in the Qing capital and was baptized early in 1784 with the name Peter. He returned to Seoul with books and religious objects and baptized Yi Byeok, who had already studied the texts. Yi Byeok in turn taught relatives and fellow scholars, among them members of the Jeong and Gwon families, including Jeong Yak-jong and Gwon Il-sin. The result was a community formed on Korean initiative, without a resident European priest, a feature distinctive in the history of Catholic missions.

The early church met in private houses in Seoul, including that of the interpreter Kim Beom-u, and drew on the ranks of scholars, middle people (jungin) and, increasingly, commoners and women. In the mid-1780s the believers organized an informal hierarchy, with leaders conducting rites and performing priestly functions, including celebrating Mass and conferring sacraments, that they were not authorized to perform (lay baptism itself was valid). In 1785 authorities discovered a gathering of believers in Seoul. The government treated it chiefly as an assembly of questionable teaching and dispersed it, punishing Kim Beom-u with exile, while elite participants were warned.

When the Korean leaders later sought clarification from the Beijing church, the answer was stern. In about 1790 the bishop in Beijing told them that the priestly functions laymen had assumed were not valid, though lay baptism was and that Catholics could not perform ancestral rites. This reply, which Korean believers had not anticipated, set up the conflict of 1791. It also shows that the Korean community and the institutional church were separate actors with sometimes conflicting expectations, a tension that would continue after missionaries arrived (Section 45.7).

Section 45.4

Ancestor Rites, Confucian Orthodoxy, and the Jinsan Incident of 1791

Ancestral rites sat at the heart of Joseon society. Family members prepared food, bowed before a spirit tablet and maintained lineage records; the practice expressed filial duty, defined inheritance and supported status. Neo-Confucian orthodoxy had made it a legal and moral norm, and a person who abandoned it was open to the charge of unfilial conduct, a serious offense because filial piety underlay the legitimacy of government as well. For Catholic leaders in Beijing, the rites appeared to be a form of worship incompatible with the faith; for Joseon officials they were a civil duty. The disagreement could not be bridged by translation alone.

In 1791 Yun Ji-chung, a Catholic from Jinsan in Jeolla Province, learned that Catholics must not hold ancestral rites. After his mother died he did not erect a spirit tablet or hold the rites, and he burned his family’s tablets, with his cousin Gwon Sang-yeon. When this came to light, local authorities investigated and the case reached the court. Both men were executed in Jeonju, reportedly late that year. They are generally counted as the first Korean Catholic martyrs, though the label reflects the later church’s view.

King Jeongjo, who valued Confucian order but also relied on Namin officials, preferred limited action. The court punished those who had abandoned the rites while avoiding a broad campaign, and it sought to counter Catholic teaching by promoting orthodox Confucian learning. Officials such as Chae Je-gong, a Namin leader, urged restraint. The result was that the church survived and grew. The incident also shows that the state saw the issue first as one of family and ritual order. The political fears associated with later persecutions were not yet dominant.

Historical Debate

The question: Was suppression of Catholicism chiefly a defense of Confucian social and political order, or did factional struggle and fear of foreign intervention carry equal weight?

Confucian order: The ancestor-rite dispute of 1791, the language of heterodoxy in edicts and the questions put to prisoners show concern for family and ritual order as the basis of the state.
Politics and security: The timing of 1801 after Jeongjo’s death, the purge of Namin figures, the Silk Letter and the Russian approach in 1866 suggest that factional and external fears were also central.
Combined view: Many historians treat these as interacting. Ritual order supplied the language and legitimacy; politics and security determined timing and intensity.

Evidence and points of disagreement: The debate concerns causation and weighting, not whether persecution happened. Court records stress orthodoxy; church sources stress faith; neither records private motives.

Why it matters: It determines whether the persecutions are read as ideological, political, or both.

Student task: Use §§45.4, 45.5 and 45.13 to find evidence for each view.

Section 45.5

The Sinyu Persecution of 1801

Jeongjo died in 1800, and the new king, Sunjo, was a child. Queen Dowager Jeongsun, the widow of Yeongjo, took the regency, and her circle, associated with the Byeokpa faction, had long been hostile to the Namin and to the Catholic movement. In early 1801 she issued an edict condemning Catholicism as a teaching that denied the ruler and father, and ordered local officials to organize five-household mutual-responsibility groups to detect believers. The edict was religious in language but was also a means to remove political rivals.

The persecution that followed, the Sinyu persecution, was the first nationwide campaign. Yi Seung-hun was executed, as were leading figures in the early church. Zhou Wenmo, the Chinese priest who had entered Korea in 1795 and ministered in secret for several years, surrendered or was captured and was executed in the spring. Jeong Yak-jong was executed; his brother Jeong Yak-yong and others were exiled, which is why historians read the episode as both a religious and a factional purge. Hundreds died or were punished, although precise totals are not established.

Late in the year officials intercepted a letter that Hwang Sa-yeong, a believer hiding in a mountain area, had written on silk to the bishop in Beijing. It described the persecution and proposed that foreign powers apply pressure on Joseon to protect the faith. The authorities used the letter as evidence that Catholics were disloyal and prepared to invite foreign intervention, and Hwang was executed. The letter reflected the desperation of one man, and not a program endorsed by all believers, but it strengthened the association of Catholicism with foreign threat for decades.

Section 45.6

Catholic Communities after 1801: Survival, Women, and Refuge

After 1801 the church lost most of its educated leaders but did not disappear. Believers scattered to mountainous and remote districts, especially in the southeastern and central interior, where some formed small villages and supported themselves by farming, charcoal-burning or pottery. Worship was held in homes, and the church depended on lay catechists, who taught prayers and doctrine, led communal worship and preserved the community without a resident priest. Because many believers could read Hangeul but not Chinese characters, vernacular religious texts, copied by hand, became the principal vehicle of instruction.

Family networks were the structure that held these communities together. Marriage, kinship and shared hiding places allowed believers to pass books and information along paths of trust, and women played central roles as teachers, hosts and messengers. Kang Wan-suk, a noblewoman who sheltered the priest Zhou Wenmo, is among the best-documented examples, and her execution in 1801 shows the risks involved. Women’s religious participation was also controversial, since gatherings that included both sexes conflicted with norms of seclusion, and officials used this as an additional charge against believers.

Lay leaders such as Jeong Ha-sang, son of the executed Jeong Yak-jong, spent decades maintaining contact with the Beijing church, and in 1811 believers sent an appeal to the Pope through Beijing seeking priests. Regional persecutions flared in 1815 and 1827, mainly in the south, where local officials denounced villages and confiscated property. Evidence for these years comes mostly from interrogation records and later church histories, which together give a view of persecution but only a fragmentary view of ordinary religious life.

Section 45.7

The Korean Apostolic Vicariate and French Missions

In 1831 the Vatican created a separate Korean vicariate, taking Korea out of the jurisdiction of the Beijing diocese, and entrusted it to the Paris Foreign Missions Society. The decision followed years of Korean petitions and reflected the Vatican’s reorganization of its missions. The first vicar apostolic, Barthélemy Bruguière, died in 1835 before entering the country, but Pierre Maubant crossed the border in 1836, followed by Jacques Chastan in 1837 and by Bishop Laurent Imbert in the same year.

Entry was clandestine. Missionaries traveled from Manchuria, joined the envoy caravans near the frontier at Uiju and were guided to Seoul by Korean believers such as Jeong Ha-sang and Yu Jin-gil, who organized the routes. Missionaries wore Korean mourning dress, which allowed a foreigner to hide his face, and lived in the homes of believers. The missionaries also selected young Koreans for training, and in 1836 three students, including Kim Dae-geon, were sent to Macao to prepare for the priesthood.

The arrival of priests altered the church. The Korean laity, who had managed the church for fifty years, now had resident clergy with sacramental authority, and relations between lay leaders and missionaries were generally cooperative but not free of tension. The foreign presence also gave officials a stronger basis to connect the church with outside powers, a link that became especially significant in the persecution of 1839 and again in 1866.

Section 45.8

The Gihae Persecution of 1839

The Gihae persecution began in 1839 under the regency of Queen Dowager Sunwon, whose Andong Kim relatives dominated the court. An edict early in the year ordered officials to suppress the teaching and revived the five-household system for detecting believers. Historians connect the campaign to rivalries among in-law families, since factional opponents of the Andong Kim used Catholic ties against rivals, but the language of the persecution was Confucian: Catholics were accused of rejecting parents and ruler.

Arrests spread in Seoul and the provinces. Jeong Ha-sang, who had written a defense of the faith addressed to officials, was arrested and executed in the autumn, as was Yu Jin-gil. Bishop Imbert, who surrendered and instructed Maubant and Chastan to do likewise in order to protect the believers, was executed with them at Saenamteo outside Seoul in September. Many Korean believers were killed by execution, torture or imprisonment. Precise totals are uncertain; the church later canonized 103 martyrs from across several persecutions in 1984.

The persecution ended the first generation of foreign missionaries. Officials justified it as the defense of moral order, and interrogation records show that they pressed believers to renounce the faith as an act of filial and political loyalty. Some did so; others refused. Historians of the church stress the courage of the refusers, while historians of the state emphasize that officials also saw an alien organization with foreign connections.

Section 45.9

Kim Dae-geon and the Byeong-o Persecution of 1846

Kim Dae-geon was born in 1821 into a Catholic family; his father Kim Je-jun was executed in the Gihae persecution. Selected as a youth by Maubant, he studied in Macao, where he also learned Latin and some French, and he was ordained in Shanghai in 1845, becoming the first Korean-born Catholic priest. He returned to Korea in 1845 with Bishop Jean Ferréol and Father Marie-Antoine Daveluy, entering partly by sea.

In 1846 Kim tried to open a sea route along the western coast so that French missionaries could enter more safely. He was arrested near Hwanghae coast, and officials interrogated him over weeks. His surviving letters and the records of his questioning show an educated man who explained his faith and knowledge of geography to officials. He was executed outside Seoul in September 1846, the Byeong-o persecution. Several lay leaders, including Hyeon Seok-mun, also died.

The episode drew foreign attention because French Admiral Cécille left a letter on the coast in 1846 protesting the killing of missionaries in 1839, an early sign of the link between missionary and naval questions. Kim’s story, however, should not stand for all Korean Catholics. He was unusual in training abroad and in priestly status, while the great majority of believers were farmers, artisans and women whose experiences were recorded only fragmentarily.

Everyday Life and Social History

Catholic communities in the countryside depended on routine as much as belief. Families gathered on Sundays and feast days for prayer led by a catechist or elder, copied devotional texts in Hangeul, and used coded language and trusted relatives to avoid detection. Mountain villages gave protection but also poverty, and a household that fled lost land and standing. Women taught prayers to children, hosted visitors and carried messages, and their role was both essential and a target of official criticism.

The general pattern is documented in trial records and church histories; individual details above are illustrative of the pattern and do not describe one specific village.

Section 45.10

Catholicism in Joseon Society, 1846–1865

From the late 1840s the Catholic community recovered, helped by a period in which officials were distracted by court politics and rural disorder (Chapters 43 and 44). Bishop Ferréol died in 1853, and Siméon Berneux succeeded him as vicar apostolic, entering Korea in 1856, joined by Daveluy and other priests. The missionaries expanded the training of Korean assistants, printed Hangeul religious works and traveled among communities in a rotating cycle to administer the sacraments.

Communities now existed in many provinces, centered on mountain villages and market towns, and included farmers, merchants, artisans, a few former yangban families and many women. Estimates of membership in the 1860s are usually around twenty thousand, but they derive from church sources and should be read as approximate. Lay catechists and local elders still performed much of the organizational work, since the number of foreign priests was small.

Opposition remained. Local gentry and village leaders resented the refusal of ancestral rites, and rumors about Catholic secrecy persisted. At court, a few officials and royal relatives showed tolerance or even sympathy, which allowed the church to grow, but the arrangement depended on the goodwill of individuals and not on any legal protection.

Section 45.11

Western Imperial Expansion and the Changing East Asian Order

The Opium War of 1839–1842 ended in the Treaty of Nanjing, by which the Qing ceded Hong Kong, opened ports and accepted tariff provisions, with extraterritoriality following in supplementary agreements of 1843–1844. A second conflict in 1856–1860 ended with the Convention of Beijing, after Anglo-French forces entered Beijing and destroyed the Summer Palace, and with further concessions to Britain, France and Russia. Russia also obtained the Maritime Province, which gave it a border with Joseon on the Tumen River.

Joseon learned of these events from embassies to Beijing and from the Chinese writings that circulated in Seoul. Officials drew varied conclusions. Some believed that the Qing defeat showed the danger of Western naval power; others regarded the West as a maritime threat to be repelled. Many linked the missionary presence to the same expansion, a connection fostered by the fact that France, whose priests were the main Catholic presence in Korea, was among the powers coercing China.

The treaties that followed widened the missionary question. The Tianjin treaties of 1858 and the Beijing Convention of 1860 allowed foreign missionaries to travel in the Chinese interior and recognized Christian missions, and France claimed a special role in protecting Catholic missions. This link between missionary protection and French diplomacy mattered later for Korea, because it gave French officials a ready justification for acting when French priests were killed in 1866. It was not inevitable that they would do so, but the precedent existed.

News of these events reached Joseon in several ways. The annual embassies to Beijing brought back reports, and in 1860–1861 the flight of the Qing court from Beijing to Rehe prompted Joseon to send envoys, so the court had direct and alarming information. Chinese writings on world geography and Western powers, among them Wei Yuan’s widely read compilation on the maritime countries, reached scholars in Seoul in the mid-nineteenth century and shaped discussion of the West. At the same time the Taiping rebellion, which shook the Qing from 1850, made Beijing seem less able to protect its tributaries.

Joseon officials did not agree about what this meant. A few scholars argued that Western technology deserved study, while many officials, drawing on long-standing Confucian suspicion of commerce and distrust of heterodoxy, saw Western expansion as a combination of force and doctrine. Their term yang-i, Western barbarians, expressed that assessment. The experience of the Qing strengthened the view that concessions to Western powers led to further demands, not to security.

Section 45.12

Foreign Ships, Commerce, and Early Diplomatic Encounters

Western ships appeared periodically along the Korean coast. In 1832 the British ship Lord Amherst, carrying the East India Company agent Hugh Hamilton Lindsay and the Protestant missionary Karl Gützlaff, sought trade on the west coast. Officials accepted gifts and supplies but refused a commercial arrangement and sent the ship away. Later visits included surveying vessels in the 1840s, the French naval letter of 1846 and Russian approaches to the northern and eastern coasts in the 1850s and 1860s.

Joseon had procedures for foreign vessels: officials questioned crews through interpreters or written Chinese, supplied food and water where needed and referred matters of principle to the court and, if necessary, to Beijing. Trade and diplomacy were conducted through established channels with Qing China and Japan, and the court regarded direct commercial treaty relations with Western states as outside that system. These procedures were not simple isolation, but they allowed little room for negotiation with parties that arrived without invitation.

Western ships were identified in Joseon records as iyangseon, strange-shaped ships, and the term shows how common their appearance had become by the 1840s and 1850s. The visit of the Lord Amherst in 1832 was the first large encounter. Hugh Hamilton Lindsay sought trade and Karl Gützlaff, a Protestant missionary and linguist, distributed Chinese-language religious tracts and medicines and reportedly gave out seed potatoes. Officials on the west coast reported the visit to the court, accepted limited exchanges and rejected any commercial arrangement. They had no authority to do otherwise, and the court treated the request as one that Joseon had no mechanism to grant.

Later visits followed. In 1845 the British survey ship Samarang charted parts of the southern coast and Jeju, and French naval vessels appeared in 1846 and in the following decades. Russian ships called on the northeast coast in the 1850s, and in the 1860s Russian officials on the new Tumen border asked for trade, an approach that alarmed Joseon because it came by land. In each case Joseon officials followed their procedures: they questioned the crews, wrote reports to the court, provided food and water to those in distress, and referred political requests to the capital. Where they could, they directed foreign requests to Beijing.

These encounters had two effects. They taught the court what to expect, namely requests for trade and treaties that it would refuse. They also made officials increasingly suspicious that explorers and traders, missionaries and navies were parts of the same phenomenon. Catholic priests who entered secretly appeared to confirm that view, and the 1866 crisis brought the strands together.

Section 45.13

The Byeongin Persecution of 1866

The Byeongin persecution of 1866 began under the regency of the Daewongun, whose wider rule is treated in Chapter 47. In early 1866 Russian ships appeared in the north seeking trade. Some Catholic leaders, including members of the court circle, proposed that an alliance with France and Britain could counter Russia, and the Daewongun was said to have considered it. The plan came to nothing, and opponents of Catholicism at court, among them officials of the dowager queen’s circle, argued that the Russian approach showed the danger of the foreign religion.

The Daewongun then authorized suppression. Bishop Berneux was arrested in February and executed in March 1866, followed by Daveluy and other priests. Nine of the twelve French missionaries in Korea were killed; three, including Félix-Clair Ridel, escaped. Thousands of Korean believers were arrested in many provinces, and a large number were killed, though estimates vary and are partly drawn from church tradition. The campaign continued in waves until 1873.

The persecution is best understood as a process whose intensity changed with the political situation. Its immediate cause, the Russian approach and the court’s fear of foreign alliance, was layered on Confucian objections to the faith and on factional politics. Its consequence was dramatic: Ridel’s escape and report led directly to the French expedition (Section 45.15), which in turn intensified the repression.

The political circumstances of early 1866 were unusual. Russian ships appeared on the northeastern coast in January and asked for trade, a request the court refused, but one that created alarm about a northern threat. A few Korean Catholics with court connections, among them Nam Jong-sam, reportedly proposed that Joseon seek an understanding with France and Britain to counter Russia, and the Daewongun, whose wife and household had Catholic contacts, is said to have considered a meeting with Bishop Berneux. The meeting did not happen. Court conservatives and the regent dowager’s circle argued that the Russian approach showed the danger of the faith, and the Daewongun, whose position depended on conservative support, decided on suppression.

Implementation followed the earlier model. Edicts ordered local magistrates to find and arrest believers, using the five-household system and informers. Berneux was arrested in late February and executed in March, and other French priests, including Daveluy, were executed later that month; execution sites varied by victim, with Saenamteo among the best known. Korean believers were beheaded, strangled or died of torture and imprisonment, and Nam Jong-sam, for example, was executed outside Seosomun on 7 March. Property was confiscated, families fled, and the campaign reached provinces from Gyeonggi to the south. The traditional church estimate of about eight thousand deaths is widely repeated but not firmly substantiated; a 2016 scholarly study treats a contemporary report of more than two thousand deaths by September 1868 as more credible, and no reliable total for the whole persecution has been established.

The consequences for the church were severe. Its priests were almost all dead and its communities scattered, and the surviving believers could not hold public worship. Three priests escaped, Ridel, Calais and Féron, and Ridel crossed the Yellow Sea by boat to China and reported the killings to the French authorities. The persecution thus ended one phase of Catholic history and triggered the Western military response that followed.

Section 45.14

The General Sherman Incident, 1866

The General Sherman was an armed merchant schooner, American-flagged but with a mixed Anglo-American commercial background, chartered by a British trading firm operating in China. It carried goods for sale and the Welsh Protestant missionary Robert Jermain Thomas as interpreter. It left Chinese waters in the summer of 1866 and entered the Taedong River in late August, heading toward Pyongyang, a city outside any port Joseon had opened to foreign trade.

Provincial officials repeatedly told the crew to leave and supplied some provisions, following the procedure used for unauthorized vessels. The ship nevertheless continued upriver. Korean accounts describe tension, including the detention of a Joseon officer aboard the vessel, and hostilities followed in which local soldiers and residents took part. As the river level fell, the ship grounded, and Korean forces attacked it with fire. It burned in early September and the crew did not survive.

Accounts differ sharply. Joseon sources emphasize an armed vessel violating restrictions and attacking residents; later American and missionary sources emphasize a trading mission destroyed without cause. Neither side’s record is neutral, and the sequence of who fired first cannot be settled from the surviving evidence. The affair mattered because it later became the stated reason for American inquiry and, eventually, the expedition of 1871 (Section 45.17).

Section 45.15

The French Expedition to Ganghwa, 1866

The French operation grew from the executions of the French missionaries in the Byeongin persecution. A missionary who escaped, Félix-Clair Ridel, reached China and reported the killings. French officials in Beijing, notably the chargé d’affaires, pressed for action, and Rear Admiral Pierre-Gustave Roze, commanding French naval forces in East Asia, assembled a squadron. French aims combined retribution, the safety of missionaries, and an attempt to force Joseon to negotiate; historians debate how far the expedition was also meant to open trade.

In September 1866 French ships made a reconnaissance up the Han River toward Seoul. In October a larger force occupied Ganghwa town, the island that guarded the river approach to the capital and held a royal library and archive. The French sent a demand for punishment of those responsible for the executions, while the Joseon court, under the Daewongun, rejected negotiation and prepared resistance. Troops were assembled on the mainland and the court treated the occupation as an invasion of the capital’s defenses.

In November a French detachment attacked the fortress at Jeongjoksanseong and was repulsed by Joseon forces under Yang Heon-su, including many tiger hunters, according to later accounts. The French then withdrew from the island. They carried away silver and a large quantity of manuscripts and books from the royal archive, and they destroyed buildings. Korean authorities counted this a victory; French officials concluded that their force was too small to compel negotiation. In 2011 France sent 297 volumes to South Korea under a renewable-loan agreement, not a transfer of ownership. For Korean Catholics the aftermath was grim: the failure of the expedition intensified the persecution, and the Daewongun’s government used the foreign threat to justify further repression.

The French force was modest in size by the standards of the Opium Wars: several warships, including the frigate Primauguet, and a landing party of some hundreds of sailors and marines. In September 1866 three vessels explored the Han River as far as the approaches to Seoul, and Roze returned in October with the main force. French officers wanted to demonstrate that the river road to the capital was open and to press the demand for punishment, so they occupied Ganghwa town in mid-October. Its magistrate and garrison withdrew, and the French took silver from the treasury and removed manuscripts and books from the Oegyujanggak archive, including volumes of royal ceremonial records, while setting fire to some buildings.

The Daewongun responded by closing the river approach to Seoul, assembling troops on the mainland and appointing commanders including Yi Gyeong-ha and Yang Heon-su. A French attempt to land on the mainland at Munsusanseong in late October was repulsed. On 9 November a French detachment advanced on Jeongjoksanseong, a fortress on the south of the island, and met strong resistance from Korean defenders. French reports record a few killed and several dozen wounded, and the force withdrew, abandoning the occupation within days. Korean accounts stress the defenders’ skill and the French retreat, while French accounts stress the destruction they had done and their lack of resources for deeper operations.

Civilians on Ganghwa experienced the occupation as requisition, fear and loss, and evidence for their perspective is thin because court records emphasize official and military matters. The expedition’s results were limited: no negotiation, no change of policy and a court that judged resistance successful. For the French government it was an inconclusive episode, and it did not lead to a larger campaign.

Section 45.16

After the French Expedition: The Oppert Incident of 1868

Western commercial efforts continued after the French withdrawal. The most notorious was that of Ernst Oppert, a German merchant based in Shanghai who had already made unsuccessful attempts to open trade on the Korean coast. In 1868 he organized an expedition with an American associate, Frederick Jenkins, and a French priest, Stanislas Féron, who had escaped the persecution of 1866 and provided local knowledge. They landed in Chungcheong Province and tried to dig up the tomb of Prince Namyeon, the father of the Daewongun, reportedly intending to use the remains as leverage in negotiations.

The attempt failed. The tomb was solidly built, the tide was falling and local villagers and soldiers gathered, so the party withdrew. Its consequences were out of proportion to its success. Korean officials and the Daewongun regarded it as proof that Westerners were prepared to violate sacred obligations, and the presence of a priest in the party strengthened the association of Catholicism with foreign aggression. The incident reinforced the policy of refusing negotiation, and it was cited in later official statements justifying the persecutions.

Oppert was not a newcomer. He had made two unsuccessful commercial voyages to the Korean coast in 1866, and he knew Korean officials would not agree. By 1868 he decided on an approach that avoided official channels. He chartered a steamship, recruited an armed crew of Europeans and Asians, and took with him Jenkins, who had experience of the region, and Féron, who had escaped the persecution and could advise on the language and the layout of the country. The expedition landed in Chungcheong and proceeded to the tomb of the Daewongun’s father in Deoksan, a site identified with the help of Féron and Korean Catholic intermediaries.

The party dug at the tomb but found the covering of lime and stone too hard to remove quickly. When the tide began to fall and local residents and officials gathered, the party withdrew without the remains. Joseon authorities reacted with outrage, since desecrating a tomb violated the most basic filial obligations, and the Daewongun, whose own father’s tomb was concerned, took the affair as personal. It confirmed what many in the court believed: that Western traders and Catholic priests were allies in aggression, and it contributed to the refusal of the American approach.

Section 45.17

American Diplomacy and the Road to the Expedition of 1871

American interest in Korea grew out of the loss of the General Sherman and from concern for shipwrecked sailors. In 1866 an American schooner, the Surprise, had been wrecked on the Korean coast, and its crew were treated with courtesy and sent overland to China, an experience that suggested that a treaty might be possible. In 1867 and 1868 American warships, including the Wachusett and the Shenandoah, visited to investigate the fate of the General Sherman, with limited results.

By 1870 Frederick Low, the American minister in Beijing, and Secretary of State Hamilton Fish had agreed on an approach: a naval force would accompany a diplomatic mission to seek a treaty on shipwrecks and commerce. The Qing government, when asked, said that Korea was a tributary but managed its own affairs, so China would not mediate. This statement, later important in debates about the legal status of Korea, left the Americans to make their own approach.

Low sent letters to Korean officials, and the Joseon response was courteous but firm: the court refused to negotiate and defended its handling of the General Sherman. Its reasons combined belief that the ship had provoked its destruction, the recent French experience, the Oppert incident and the conviction that treaty relations would endanger both the social order and the dynasty’s authority. The Americans thus sailed in 1871 expecting resistance but hoping for talks.

American policy rested on a different set of motives from the French. The United States had concluded a treaty with Japan in 1854 after Commodore Perry’s expedition, and by the 1860s American merchants and shipowners wanted guarantees for sailors wrecked on East Asian coasts and for access to ports. The Grant administration was also working with other powers in China and wished to avoid acting alone. The loss of the General Sherman, with its crew killed, gave the government a specific grievance, though it was unclear how far the owners and crew had acted lawfully.

Low’s instructions called for a treaty and for an explanation of the General Sherman affair, not for conquest. He and Rodgers were authorized to use force to defend the expedition if attacked. In 1871 Low wrote to the Korean government through Chinese channels announcing the visit. The Qing foreign office forwarded the correspondence as an exception but declined responsibility for Joseon’s independent decisions. Joseon replied politely that it did not wish to hold relations with the United States and that the Sherman had been destroyed because of its own aggression.

The court’s refusal drew on several experiences: the French occupation, the Oppert attempt, the Qing example and the argument that a treaty would open the way to missionaries and commerce that would undermine ritual order. The result was that the Americans came with limited aims and the Joseon court, which regarded any foreign ship in the Ganghwa channel as a threat, was prepared to fight.

Continuity and Change Over Time

What changed? The challenge shifted from a domestic dispute over ritual in 1791, to nationwide campaigns in 1801 and 1839, to the combination of persecution and foreign warships in 1866.

What continued? Officials consistently defended ritual order and refused unauthorized foreign contact. The family network remained the church’s base.

When and how? Change was gradual but accelerated in 1866. Who experienced it? Believers and Ganghwa residents experienced violence directly; court officials experienced continuity in policy.

Why it matters: The military outcomes of 1866 and 1871 reinforced a policy that rested on earlier assumptions.

Section 45.18

The American Expedition and the Battles of Ganghwa, 1871

The American expedition followed Minister Frederick Low’s effort to negotiate through China. Low accompanied Rear Admiral John Rodgers and the U.S. Asiatic Squadron, which included the warships Colorado and Monocacy, to Korean waters in 1871. American objectives, as stated in contemporary government papers, were a treaty protecting shipwrecked sailors and arranging commercial access, with explanation of the General Sherman affair. The force was not sent to conquer territory.

Americans were looking for talks with an authorized Joseon representative, but the Joseon court was unwilling to treat. In early June, American survey vessels moved up the Ganghwa channel and were fired on by Korean forts. The Americans considered this an unprovoked attack on a peaceful survey; Joseon officials considered it a response to a foreign intrusion into restricted waters. After no apology arrived, American sailors and marines landed on June 10 and attacked a series of forts, ending with the citadel at Gwangseongbo on June 11.

Korean defenders under Eo Jae-yeon were outgunned and fought at close quarters. American reports record very small losses on their side, only a few killed; reports of Korean dead run to a couple of hundred, although the figures vary and come mainly from American sources. Rodgers did not obtain negotiations and withdrew in July without a treaty. The court regarded the outcome as a success, and the Daewongun had stone markers erected in Seoul and other places rejecting foreign relations. The episode shows the limits of coercive demonstration: it killed Koreans and destroyed forts but did not produce the diplomacy it sought, and the contrast with Japan’s later approach is examined in Chapter 48.

The expedition was larger than the French one. Rodgers commanded a squadron including the flagship Colorado, the Alaska, Benicia, Monocacy and Palos, with well over a thousand officers and men, and it assembled off the west coast near Incheon in late May 1871. Low had sent a letter to the court announcing the mission, and Joseon officials replied with courteous refusals and instructions to leave. Low and Rodgers intended to explore the Ganghwa channel, the route to Seoul, and to await a Korean envoy with authority to negotiate.

On 1 June two ships, the Monocacy and Palos, accompanied by launches, moved up the channel and came under fire from Korean forts near Gapgot. The Americans withdrew and demanded an apology within ten days. Joseon officials offered none and maintained that the vessels had entered closed waters. On 10 June about 650 sailors and marines landed, with naval guns in support, and occupied Chojijin that day. On 11 June they reached Deokjinjin and assaulted the citadel of Gwangseongbo after a bombardment, and the defenders under Eo Jae-yeon fought until the fortress fell. Three Americans were killed, including Lieutenant Hugh McKee, and American sources recorded 243 Korean bodies, with some estimates higher.

The Americans captured a number of prisoners, whom they later released, and the commander’s banner. After the fighting Rodgers waited for a Korean response for several weeks. None came that opened negotiation, and he withdrew in early July, since he did not think his force could advance further on Seoul. The outcome was a military American victory and a diplomatic failure, with Joseon treating it as a successful defense. The court erected stone tablets in Seoul and other places rejecting foreign relations, and the regime’s confidence was reinforced for several years.

Cause and Effect

Long-term causes

Confucian ritual order, the tributary diplomatic system, Western expansion in East Asia, and a church formed outside state control.

Short-term causes

The Russian approach of 1866, the executions of French priests, the loss of the General Sherman and the Oppert attempt.

Effects

Two repelled expeditions, thousands of Catholic deaths, and a hardened policy of refusal.

Complexity

Religious, commercial and diplomatic motives interacted but did not share one cause.

Section 45.19

Religion, Sovereignty, and the Western Challenge by 1871

By 1871 Joseon’s leaders had resisted two Western military expeditions and suppressed the Catholic community. The court’s reasoning joined three elements. First, Confucian political authority rested on a hierarchy of family and ruler, and the Catholic refusal of ancestral rites seemed to challenge that structure. Second, the court believed that foreign trade and diplomacy would draw Joseon into a system, visible in the fate of the Qing, in which sovereignty was lost under pressure. Third, the experiences of 1866 and 1871 seemed to confirm that firm refusal worked.

Historians dispute how to characterize this policy. Some Korean and Western scholars have described it as isolationism, a term later applied by critics of the regime. Others emphasize that Joseon maintained regular diplomacy with Qing China and Japan and that the Daewongun’s government acted against particular threats, not against all contact. The chapter’s evidence suggests that Joseon’s refusal was selective and rested on a reasoned, if fallible, assessment of the dangers of Western coercion.

The costs were also real. Thousands of Korean Catholics died, Ganghwa’s defenders and residents suffered in the fighting, and the court gained little understanding of how Western powers and Japan were reshaping the region. The next foreign approach would come from Japan, which had itself been opened by Western pressure and had experience of Western diplomacy. That crisis is the subject of Chapter 48.

Chapter Summary

Catholicism entered Joseon through books from Qing China and was adopted by a small group of scholars in the 1780s. Yi Seung-hun’s baptism in Beijing in 1784 began a community led by Koreans without resident priests.

The conflict over ancestral rites, after the Beijing church’s prohibition, led to the executions of Jinsan in 1791. The court treated the case as a violation of ritual order and acted with relative restraint.

After Jeongjo’s death, the Sinyu persecution of 1801 combined condemnation of heterodoxy with factional purge. The Silk Letter linked Catholicism with foreign intervention in official eyes.

The church survived through lay catechists, family networks and women. In 1831 a vicariate was created and French priests entered from 1836, bringing both sacraments and a stronger foreign association. The persecutions of 1839 and 1846 killed missionaries and lay leaders, including Jeong Ha-sang and Kim Dae-geon.

Western expansion in China altered how Joseon officials understood foreign threats. Occasional ships were handled by established procedures, which did not include negotiating treaties.

The Byeongin persecution of 1866 followed a Russian approach and court politics. It killed most French missionaries and many Korean believers and led directly to the French expedition to Ganghwa.

The General Sherman was destroyed on the Taedong River in 1866 in a conflict whose responsibility remains contested. The French expedition occupied Ganghwa and withdrew after the fight at Jeongjoksanseong, taking archival materials with it.

The Oppert incident of 1868 hardened attitudes, and American diplomacy led to the 1871 expedition, in which American forces attacked the Ganghwa forts and withdrew without a treaty.

By 1871 the court regarded refusal as vindicated. The policy was a reasoned if costly response, and it left Joseon unprepared for the approach from Japan examined in Chapter 48.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows that Joseon’s encounter with the West began before the treaty era and involved Koreans as agents: scholars who adopted a new faith, believers who sustained it, officials who defended an order and soldiers and residents who resisted invasion.

It also shows that religion, trade and force became entangled, and that the court’s response cannot be reduced to isolation. Joseon maintained regional diplomacy while refusing relations that it believed would endanger sovereignty and ritual order.

The chapter prepares for the later crises. The Catholic community survived the persecutions and continued in the treaty era, and the memory of 1866 and 1871 shaped political attitudes. Chapter 46 turns to Donghak, another religious response to Joseon’s circumstances.
