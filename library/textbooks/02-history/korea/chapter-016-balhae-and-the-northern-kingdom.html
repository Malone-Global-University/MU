Balhae and the Northern Kingdom

Korean History: Ancient Kingdoms to a Divided Peninsula

Chapter Opening

In 698, in the rugged uplands east of the Liao River basin, a coalition of displaced Goguryeo people and Mohe communities defeated a pursuing Tang army at a place their descendants would remember as Tianmenling. Out of that victory, a new state took shape on the wreckage of Goguryeo, one that would govern a vast stretch of Manchuria, the northern Korean peninsula, and parts of the Russian Far East for more than two centuries. Its name was Jin at first; by the early eighth century, and formally in Tang diplomacy from 713, it was known as Balhae.

Chapter 15 examined Silla's court, its monasteries, and its cultural achievements in the century after 668. Read alone, that chapter could leave the impression that Silla's victory over Baekje and Goguryeo settled the political future of the entire peninsula and its northern hinterland. It did not. Tang China's occupying forces controlled the former Goguryeo capital region for barely a decade before withdrawing under pressure from both Silla in the south and renewed resistance in the north. Into the resulting vacuum, over roughly thirty turbulent years, a new political order emerged that historians studying this period increasingly describe using a phrase first argued for at length by the eighteenth-century Korean scholar Yu Deuk-gong: the "North and South States" era. Silla was the southern state. Balhae was the northern one.

Balhae has often been treated unevenly by the very national historiographies that might be expected to give it sustained attention. Korean historical writing, both traditional and modern, has periodically rediscovered and re-emphasized Balhae as an essential chapter of Korean history, precisely because it preserved a Goguryeo-descended political and cultural presence in the north after 668. Chinese historical writing has more often situated Balhae within the history of Chinese frontier administration and the Tang tributary order, emphasizing its formal titles from the Tang court and its place within a regional system centered on the Tang capital. Russian archaeology has approached Balhae mainly through the material remains scattered across the Primorsky region of the Russian Far East, where Balhae's northeastern frontier once extended. Japanese scholarship has examined Balhae largely through the lens of its many diplomatic missions to the Japanese court, missions that produced some of the richest surviving documentary evidence for the kingdom. None of these traditions is simply wrong, and none by itself is sufficient. Balhae was a state whose very existence resists being folded neatly into any one of the national historiographies that later grew up around its former territory.

This is, in part, a problem of evidence. Balhae itself left behind no surviving dynastic history of its own composition. What survives comes mostly at second hand: brief notices in the Chinese dynastic histories of the Tang and later periods, records of diplomatic exchanges preserved in Japanese court chronicles, scattered later Korean references, and, increasingly, the archaeological record recovered from sites in northeastern China, North Korea, and the Russian Far East. Piecing together Balhae's political institutions, its social order, and the everyday lives of its population requires historians to read across these fragmentary and sometimes contradictory sources with considerable care, always asking whose interests and whose vantage point produced the record that survives.

Balhae's population was not homogeneous. Its ruling elite descended substantially, though not exclusively, from Goguryeo's former aristocracy and military class. Alongside them lived various Mohe communities, tribal peoples of the Sungari and Amur river basins whose own political organization, prior to Balhae's founding, had rarely extended beyond loosely federated groupings. Balhae's kings governed both populations, along with others living within its shifting frontiers, through institutions that borrowed heavily from Tang China's administrative vocabulary while adapting that vocabulary to a very different geographic and demographic reality. Understanding Balhae, then, means resisting two temptations at once: the temptation to see it simply as Goguryeo continuing under a new name, and the temptation to see it as an undifferentiated "Tungusic" or "Manchurian" state with only accidental Korean connections.

Balhae also matters because of where it stood. Its territory bridged the Korean peninsula, the Manchurian interior, and the Inner Asian steppe world, placing it in direct and continuous contact with Tang China to its west and southwest, Silla to its south, Japan across the East Sea, and various Khitan, Mohe, and other frontier peoples along its northern and western borders. Balhae occupied an unusually diverse range of ecological and political zones simultaneously: temperate agricultural lowlands, cold northern forests, major river systems draining toward both the Pacific and the interior, and mountainous borderlands where settled and mobile ways of life met and sometimes merged.

This chapter examines Balhae on its own historical terms: its founding amid the chaos following Goguryeo's destruction, the mixed origins of its population, the territory it governed, the institutions it built, and the diplomatic world it inhabited. It does not yet narrate Balhae's ninth-century difficulties or its sudden collapse before the Khitan-led Liao state in 926, a story reserved for Chapter 18, where it belongs alongside the broader unraveling of the Silla-Balhae order. Nor does this chapter attempt the fuller account of Balhae's maritime and commercial connections that belongs to Chapter 17. What follows here is Balhae at its formation and its height: a durable northern kingdom that made the peninsula's post-668 political landscape one of two states, not one.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain the political circumstances following Goguryeo's fall in 668 that made Balhae's emergence possible.
  2. Trace the career of Dae Joyeong, the Battle of Tianmenling in 698, and the establishment of the state first called Jin and later Balhae.
  3. Analyze the historiographical problem of Balhae's mixed Goguryeo and Mohe foundations without reducing the kingdom to either element alone.
  4. Describe Balhae's territorial expansion under Kings Mu and Mun and the environmental and geographic diversity of the territory it governed.
  5. Explain Balhae's governmental institutions, including its adaptation of Tang administrative models to frontier conditions.
  6. Distinguish Tang China's formal diplomatic titles for Balhae's rulers from actual Tang political sovereignty over Balhae.
  7. Compare Balhae's relationship with Silla to a simple narrative of constant hostility, identifying periods of rivalry, distance, and limited exchange.
  8. Assess the significance of Balhae's diplomatic missions to Japan and its position relative to the Khitan and other frontier peoples.
  9. Evaluate archaeological evidence from Balhae's capitals, including Sanggyeong, for what it reveals about urban planning, elite culture, and Tang influence.
  10. Explain the character of Buddhism and elite cultural life in Balhae in comparison with, but not simply as a copy of, contemporary Silla.
  11. Identify the range of populations governed by Balhae and explain why the kingdom should not be understood as a single uniform society.
  12. Construct an interpretive argument about why modern national categories are inadequate to Balhae and why the kingdom nonetheless belongs centrally within Korean history.

Historical Context

Chapter 13 traced the wars of 598 to 676 that destroyed Baekje and Goguryeo and left Silla in control of much of the peninsula south of the Daedong River region. Chapter 14 examined Unified Silla's government, aristocracy, and administration, and Chapter 15 explored its Buddhist culture. None of those chapters, however, followed the fate of Goguryeo's former northern territories in any detail, because that story belongs here.

When Tang forces and their Silla allies destroyed Goguryeo's capital, Pyongyang, in 668, Tang China attempted to absorb the conquered territory directly, establishing the Andong Protectorate to govern the region. This project proved unsustainable. Goguryeo's former population, numbering in the hundreds of thousands according to Chinese sources, did not simply accept Tang rule. Tang administrators forcibly relocated substantial numbers of Goguryeo people westward into Chinese territory, a policy intended to break up potential centers of resistance but one that also scattered experienced Goguryeo military and administrative elites across a wide arc of northern China, including the region around Yingzhou (near modern Chaoyang in Liaoning), where many were resettled alongside various Khitan and Mohe communities already living under loose Tang oversight.

Tang control over the former Goguryeo region weakened steadily through the 670s and 680s, pressured from the south by Silla's own expansion and from within by the difficulty of governing a resistant, ethnically mixed, and geographically vast frontier with limited garrison forces. By the 690s, Tang authority over the northeastern frontier had become fragile enough that a coordinated uprising, beginning among Khitan groups near Yingzhou in 696, triggered a wider collapse of Tang frontier control. It was in the aftermath of this uprising that Goguryeo and Mohe communities under Dae Joyeong's leadership broke away from Tang jurisdiction and moved east toward the mountainous region around the upper Sungari and Mudan river systems, beyond the reach of an immediate Tang military response.

This is the specific, contingent sequence from which Balhae emerged. It was not the automatic or inevitable restoration of Goguryeo, nor a spontaneous Mohe uprising alone, but the product of a particular frontier crisis, a specific leadership, and a defensible geography that together allowed a new political order to take root where Tang power had briefly faltered.

Section 16.1

After Goguryeo: The Displaced North

The destruction of Goguryeo's royal government in 668 did not destroy Goguryeo society. Chinese sources report the forced relocation of large numbers of Goguryeo people to interior regions of Tang territory, a policy consistent with earlier Tang practices for managing conquered frontier populations. These relocations were substantial but not total: they scattered many of Goguryeo's military and administrative elites away from their former homeland, while leaving other portions of the population in place in the former Goguryeo core territory, now nominally administered through the Andong Protectorate.

Tang's protectorate arrangement proved difficult to sustain. Silla's continued expansion in the south absorbed portions of former Goguryeo territory during the 670s, discussed in Chapter 13, while resistance movements and administrative strain elsewhere forced Tang to relocate the protectorate's headquarters multiple times, each move signaling weakening rather than strengthening control. By the 680s, Tang authority over the northeastern frontier rested less on direct administration than on a loose supervisory relationship with various local leaders, including Goguryeo remnant communities and Mohe tribal groups who had been drawn into the Tang frontier system, often through resettlement near garrison towns such as Yingzhou.

This arrangement held only as long as Tang could project sufficient military force to the frontier. When a Khitan chieftain's rebellion against harsh local Tang administration broke out near Yingzhou in 696, the resulting disorder spread rapidly and exposed how thin Tang's actual control over the region had become. Goguryeo and Mohe communities resettled in the area, led by figures including Dae Joyeong (in some accounts working alongside or in tension with a Mohe leader named Qisi Bi Yu), seized the opportunity to break away eastward, out of the immediate reach of Tang garrisons, toward the more defensible mountainous terrain around the upper Sungari River.

It is important not to romanticize this movement as a unified national uprising. The surviving evidence suggests a coalition assembled under pressure, drawing together groups with different backgrounds and, quite possibly, different immediate goals, united chiefly by the shared need to escape Tang punitive forces and to establish a secure base beyond the frontier's reach. That the coalition succeeded, and that it produced a durable state rather than a short-lived breakaway movement, owed as much to Tang's limited capacity to project power so far from its heartland as to any particular unity among the founders themselves.

Section 16.2

Dae Joyeong and the Founding of Balhae

Tang responded to the eastward flight of Dae Joyeong's coalition by dispatching a punitive force under a Khitan-descended Tang general. In 698, this pursuing army was defeated at Tianmenling, a mountain pass east of the frontier zone, a victory that allowed Dae Joyeong's coalition to establish itself securely beyond immediate Tang reach. Later that year, Dae Joyeong proclaimed a new state, initially called Jin (振, sometimes transliterated Jin or Zhen), with its base near Dongmo Mountain, in the vicinity of modern Dunhua in present-day Jilin province, China.

For roughly fifteen years, this new state operated without formal Tang recognition, a period during which it consolidated territory, absorbed additional Mohe communities, and built the administrative and military foundations of a lasting kingdom. Tang's own priorities during these years, including conflict with the Turkic and Tibetan frontiers to the west, left limited capacity to contest the new state's existence by force, and by 705 the Tang court had begun sending diplomatic missions to Dae Joyeong's court rather than military expeditions.

Formal recognition came in 713, when the Tang emperor granted Dae Joyeong the title "Commissioner with Special Powers, Grand Commandant, Commandery Prince of Balhae" (Balhae gunwang, 渤海郡王), along with the honorary title Area Commander of Hummhan Prefecture. From this point the state's name in Chinese and Korean sources becomes Balhae, and the ruling house is generally known by the Chinese-derived surname Dae (Chinese: Da), used in Tang investiture documents and later Korean and Chinese histories. It was not until 762, under a later Balhae king, that Tang elevated this recognition further, formally investing Balhae's ruler as King of Balhae Kingdom (Balhae gukwang, 渤海國王), a higher grade of the same investiture relationship. This act of investiture, at both stages, is significant precisely because of what it did and did not establish. Tang's grant of a royal title indicated formal Tang recognition of Balhae's ruler as a legitimate frontier authority within a hierarchical diplomatic order, but it did not restore Tang administrative control over Balhae's actual territory or government, a distinction examined further in Section 16.6.

Dae Joyeong died around 719 and was succeeded by his son, later known by his temple name King Mu, whose reign would see Balhae expand its territory substantially and, for a time, openly confront Tang military power.

Section 16.3

Goguryeo and Mohe Foundations

No question about Balhae has generated more sustained disagreement among historians than the relative weight of its Goguryeo and Mohe foundations. The problem begins with the sources themselves. The Jiu Tangshu (Old Book of Tang), compiled in the tenth century, describes Dae Joyeong as "a separate branch of Goguryeo" (高麗別種). The later Xin Tangshu (New Book of Tang), compiled in the eleventh century, instead describes Balhae's founders as Sumo Mohe who had attached themselves to Goguryeo (粟末靺鞨附高麗者), using different and not entirely reconcilable language. Korean sources compiled centuries afterward, including sections of the thirteenth-century Samguk Yusa discussed in earlier chapters' treatment of historiographical method, tend to emphasize Balhae's Goguryeo character, consistent with a broader medieval and later Korean historiographical interest in claiming Balhae as part of a continuous Korean political inheritance.

Modern historians have approached this discrepancy in different ways. Some emphasize that "Mohe" in Tang usage was often applied loosely to frontier populations without necessarily implying a fixed ethnic category distinct from Goguryeo's own diverse population, since Goguryeo itself, as Chapter 8 explained, had long incorporated Mohe-speaking and other frontier communities within its own borders and armies. On this reading, describing Dae Joyeong as "Mohe" and as "Goguryeo" need not be strictly contradictory, since Goguryeo's population was never ethnically uniform, and a person of Mohe background could have risen within Goguryeo's own military and administrative structures well before 668.

Other historians place more weight on the structural composition of Balhae's early population and leadership. They point out that Balhae's ruling elite, its titles, its written culture (conducted in Classical Chinese, as was standard practice across the region), and much of its ceremonial and religious life show continuity with the administrative and cultural patterns of the former Goguryeo elite. At the same time, the greater part of Balhae's overall population, particularly outside the court and major administrative centers, appears from archaeological and textual evidence to have consisted of Mohe communities who had not necessarily been closely integrated into Goguryeo before 668 and who retained distinct material cultures, visible in ceramic traditions, burial practices, and settlement patterns that differ across Balhae's territory.

This textbook's position is that Balhae is best understood neither as "Goguryeo restored" nor as "a Mohe state with a Korean veneer," but as a genuinely composite frontier kingdom: a ruling structure substantially descended from and culturally continuous with Goguryeo's elite, governing a population in which Mohe communities, organized into several distinct tribal and regional groupings, made up a very large, probably a majority, share of the people actually living under Balhae's rule. Both elements were essential to the kingdom's formation, its endurance, and its ultimate character. Reducing Balhae to either element alone flattens a genuinely mixed historical reality for the sake of a cleaner national narrative, whether that narrative privileges a Korean, a Chinese, or another modern national framework.

Historical Vocabulary
The "North and South States" (Namgukguk Sidae)

Definition: "North and South States" (남북국시대, Namgukguk Sidae) is a historiographical term describing the period from Balhae's founding around 698 to its fall in 926 as an era in which two major states, Silla in the south and Balhae in the north, coexisted on and around the Korean peninsula, rather than a single unified "Unified Silla" era.

Historical context: The term and the interpretive framework behind it were argued for at length by the eighteenth-century Joseon scholar Yu Deuk-gong in his 1784 work Balhaego ("Study of Balhae"), which criticized earlier Korean historiography for neglecting Balhae and proposed treating Silla and Balhae together as parallel Korean states of the same era, comparable in structure to China's own "Northern and Southern Dynasties" terminology for an earlier period of division.

Why it matters: This framework directly shapes how this textbook organizes Part III, treating Balhae as a full counterpart to Silla rather than a peripheral appendix, consistent with the outline's insistence that Balhae "must remain part of the narrative."

Potential misunderstanding: Students should not assume that Yu Deuk-gong's eighteenth-century framing straightforwardly reflects how Silla's and Balhae's own contemporaries understood their relationship in the eighth and ninth centuries. As Section 16.7 explains, the direct evidence for close Silla-Balhae interaction during the period itself is comparatively thin, even though the "North and South States" framework remains a valuable and influential later historiographical tool for organizing this era.

Historical Vocabulary
Mohe

Definition: "Mohe" (靺鞨) is a Chinese-language term used in Tang-era sources to refer to a number of related but distinct tribal peoples inhabiting the Sungari, Mudan, and Amur river basins in what is now northeastern China and the Russian Far East. The term was not necessarily a self-designation used uniformly by these peoples themselves; it functioned largely as an outside administrative and ethnographic category applied by Chinese dynastic officials and chroniclers.

Historical context: Chinese sources distinguish several Mohe groupings, most importantly the Heishui ("Black River") Mohe of the lower Amur and the Sumo ("Sumo River," referring to the upper Sungari/Songhua) Mohe, whose territory lay closest to Goguryeo's northern frontier and whose communities contributed substantially to Balhae's founding population. Some Mohe groups had long-standing contact, sometimes cooperative and sometimes hostile, with Goguryeo before 668; others remained largely independent of Goguryeo authority and only later came under Balhae's rule, sometimes through conquest.

Why it matters: The Mohe peoples are ancestral, in the loose historical sense historians use for such deep continuities, to later Tungusic-speaking peoples of the region, including groups that would eventually be organized as the Jurchen and, much later, the Manchu. Understanding Mohe history therefore connects Balhae not only to Korean history but to the much longer history of Manchuria as a distinct historical region, a connection this textbook does not pursue in full but which students should recognize.

Potential misunderstanding: Students should avoid treating "Mohe" as naming a single unified people with one language, one political structure, and one relationship to Balhae. The sources describe multiple Mohe groupings with different histories, different levels of integration into Balhae's state, and, in some cases, ongoing autonomy or conflict with Balhae's central authority even after the kingdom's founding.

Section 16.4

Territorial Expansion and Frontier Geography

Balhae's territory expanded considerably during the eighth century, particularly under King Mu and King Mun, until the kingdom controlled, at its greatest extent, a vast area including much of present-day Jilin and parts of Heilongjiang province in China, the northern portion of the Korean peninsula, and portions of the Russian Far East along the Ussuri and lower Amur river systems. Later Chinese sources, describing this expanded and increasingly sophisticated kingdom, sometimes referred to Balhae with the admiring phrase "haidong shengguo," often rendered "the flourishing state east of the sea," a description reflecting Tang perceptions of Balhae's administrative order and cultural attainment rather than a neutral geographic label.

This description, examined further below, reflects the kingdom's later territorial and administrative maturity rather than its condition at founding. Balhae's territory encompassed remarkable environmental diversity. The southern and western portions, closer to the former Goguryeo heartland and the routes toward Tang China, supported settled agriculture, including millet and other grain cultivation adapted to the region's relatively short growing season. The northern and eastern portions, extending into the Amur and Ussuri basins, were dominated by forest and river-valley environments where hunting, fishing, and forms of shifting cultivation remained central to many Mohe communities' livelihoods. Balhae's rulers therefore governed a kingdom that combined a settled agricultural and administrative core, organized along lines recognizably similar to contemporary Tang and Silla practice, with an extensive frontier zone where the reach of central authority was necessarily lighter and where local Mohe leaders likely retained considerable autonomy in day-to-day governance.

Major river systems structured both settlement and administration. The Sungari (Songhua) and its tributary the Mudan provided the core corridor connecting Balhae's successive capitals, while the Amur and Ussuri basins to the northeast marked the kingdom's most distant and loosely controlled frontier. Mountain ranges separating these river systems from the Korean peninsula's interior, along with the Tang frontier to the west, gave Balhae's core territory a degree of natural defensibility that helps explain both the kingdom's initial survival after 698 and its multi-century endurance thereafter.

This same geographic breadth, however, created lasting administrative challenges. Communication and troop movement across such distances, especially in a climate with harsh winters, required sustained infrastructure and could not always keep pace with challenges arising simultaneously on different frontiers, a vulnerability that would matter considerably in the kingdom's final decades, examined in Chapter 18.

Balhae's territorial expansion and administrative maturity reached their high point somewhat later than King Mun's reign, under King Seon (Dae Insu), who ruled from roughly 818 to 830. Under King Seon, Balhae extended its authority further into the lower Amur (Heilongjiang) region, pressed into parts of the Liaodong frontier, and pushed its southern reach toward Silla's northern border near the Daedong River and Wonsan Bay area. It was also during this period that Balhae's mature administrative geography, conventionally described as a system of five capitals, fifteen prefectures, and sixty-two subordinate provinces, took its fullest form, distributing governance across the kingdom's diverse regions more systematically than in earlier reigns. It is this expanded, administratively consolidated kingdom under King Seon, rather than the kingdom at its founding, that later Chinese sources had most fully in mind when they applied the admiring phrase "haidong shengguo," "the flourishing state east of the sea."

Section 16.5

Government and Administration

Balhae's kings governed through an administrative structure that drew heavily, though not slavishly, on Tang institutional models, a pattern common across much of East Asia during this period, as Chapter 12 noted regarding the Three Kingdoms more broadly. Balhae's central government, as reconstructed from later Chinese descriptions and the more limited direct archaeological and epigraphic evidence, included a set of central chancelleries and ministries whose names and functions echoed Tang's Three Departments and Six Ministries system, adapted with some distinctive terminology of Balhae's own devising rather than reproduced in every detail.

Below the central government, Balhae organized its territory into a hierarchy of administrative units, including a number of major regional capitals or "circuits" (府, generally rendered "prefectures" or "superior prefectures" in translation) and subordinate prefectures and counties, again broadly modeled on Tang provincial administration but adapted to Balhae's own geographic and demographic circumstances. The precise number and boundaries of these units shifted over the kingdom's history and remain only partially reconstructable from surviving evidence, but later Chinese sources describe a substantial and evidently functioning multi-tiered administrative system, sufficient to govern a large and environmentally diverse territory.

Kingship in Balhae combined elements familiar from Tang and from earlier Goguryeo practice: hereditary succession within the Dae royal house, court ritual conducted substantially in the vocabulary of Chinese statecraft, the use of era names for dating royal reigns (itself a marker of considerable administrative sophistication and, implicitly, of a degree of independent sovereignty, since era names were normally the prerogative of "emperors" rather than merely "kings" in the Chinese-centered diplomatic order), and a military establishment capable of sustained campaigns, as King Mu's reign would demonstrate.

Direct evidence for taxation, land tenure, and labor obligation within Balhae is thinner than for contemporary Tang or Silla, since no Balhae legal or administrative code survives. Historians infer, from the kingdom's evident capacity to support a substantial court, army, and network of planned capital cities, that Balhae's government commanded significant agricultural surplus and labor from at least its settled core territories, likely through mechanisms broadly comparable to, if not identical with, Tang-style land allocation and tax-in-kind systems, while frontier Mohe communities probably contributed tribute in kind, including furs, ginseng, and other regional products, alongside a greater degree of local self-governance than existed in the agricultural core.

Section 16.6

Balhae and Tang China

Balhae's relationship with Tang China moved through distinct phases that students should not collapse into a single undifferentiated story of either hostility or subordination. The kingdom's origins lay in open flight from Tang authority and a military victory over a Tang army at Tianmenling. Yet within roughly fifteen years, Tang had shifted from pursuit to diplomatic recognition, granting Dae Joyeong the title King of Balhae Commandery in 713 and thereby formally incorporating Balhae into the broader Tang-centered diplomatic order that governed relations among East Asian states during this period.

This diplomatic order operated through a system of investiture, in which Tang emperors granted titles, seals, and calendars to neighboring rulers, symbolically recognizing them as legitimate authorities occupying a subordinate but respected position relative to the Tang emperor. Silla and various steppe polities participated in comparable investiture arrangements during this same period. Japan, by contrast, maintained extensive diplomatic and cultural exchange with Tang without accepting the same formal investiture relationship that bound Silla and Balhae; Japan's Tang-era court engaged the wider Tang diplomatic world on different terms, a distinction students should keep in mind when comparing Balhae's diplomacy across its several neighbors. It is essential for students to distinguish this symbolic and diplomatic subordination from actual political control. Tang's investiture of Balhae's kings did not mean that Tang officials administered Balhae's territory, collected its taxes, commanded its armies, or dictated its domestic policy. Balhae's kings governed independently, maintained their own administrative institutions, conducted their own foreign relations with Japan and others, and, as King Mu's reign demonstrated, were fully capable of waging open war against Tang when their interests required it.

That war came in the 730s, when King Mu, alarmed by Tang diplomatic overtures to a Mohe grouping (the Heishui Mohe of the lower Amur) that Balhae itself regarded as within its own sphere of influence, launched a series of military actions, including a notable naval raid against the Tang coastal prefecture of Dengzhou on the Shandong peninsula in 732, an episode demonstrating both Balhae's naval capability and the seriousness with which its rulers guarded their frontier interests against Tang encroachment. Tang responded with a coordinated but only partially successful campaign, and, at Tang's request, Silla also mounted an attack on Balhae's southern frontier in 733, an expedition that failed amid severe winter conditions and difficult terrain, examined further in Section 16.7. The Balhae-Tang conflict itself eventually subsided into renewed diplomatic relations rather than continued warfare, a pattern of oscillation between conflict and accommodation not unlike relationships between Tang and several of its other frontier neighbors during this period; by 762 Tang had elevated Balhae's ruler to the higher investiture title King of Balhae Kingdom, discussed in Section 16.2.

Following this episode, Balhae-Tang relations settled into a long period of largely peaceful diplomatic and cultural exchange lasting through much of the eighth and ninth centuries. Balhae sent frequent tribute and diplomatic missions to the Tang court, adopted Tang administrative vocabulary and court ritual extensively, and, according to later Chinese sources, sent students to study in Tang China, including at the Tang capital's imperial academies, a pattern of educational exchange comparable to Silla's own well-documented practice of sending students to Tang institutions.

Section 16.7

Balhae and Silla

The relationship between Balhae and Silla is central to this Part's organizing concept of the "North and South States," and it deserves more careful treatment than a simple assumption of permanent hostility allows. The two states shared a long, if not always clearly demarcated, border in the northern Korean peninsula, and both, in different ways, claimed a connection to the political inheritance of the Three Kingdoms period: Silla through its role in the wars of 660 to 668 and its continued rule over most of the peninsula, Balhae through its Goguryeo-descended leadership and its control of much of Goguryeo's former northern territory.

Sustained warfare between Balhae and Silla is not documented across the kingdom's history, though one important direct confrontation is clearly attested. Following Balhae's 732 naval raid on the Tang prefecture of Dengzhou, discussed in Section 16.6, Tang requested that Silla attack Balhae's southern frontier in support of the Tang response. Silla did launch the requested expedition in 733, but severe winter weather and difficult mountain terrain caused the campaign to fail before it achieved any significant result. This episode indicates that Balhae and Silla were capable of direct military confrontation when drawn into it by their shared relationship with Tang, even though such confrontation did not become a recurring pattern. Silla did construct northern frontier defenses, including a long wall project in the border region undertaken in the early ninth century, suggesting continued wariness about the northern frontier even in the absence of further attested warfare.

Diplomatic contact between the two states, while less frequent than each state's diplomacy with Tang, was not entirely absent. A formal overland route known as the Silla-do ("Silla road") connected the two kingdoms, and Silla sent recorded diplomatic missions to Balhae in 790 and again in 812. This evidence suggests that Balhae and Silla maintained a functioning, if modest, channel of direct communication alongside their more extensive and competitive relationships with Tang, rather than existing in complete diplomatic isolation from one another.

That underlying rivalry surfaced clearly in 897, when the Balhae prince Dae Bong-ye petitioned the Tang court to seat Balhae's delegation ahead of Silla's in the order of diplomatic precedence. Tang rejected the request, upholding Silla's customary position. This datable episode, sometimes called the "dispute over precedence," reflects each state's claim to primary status within the Tang-centered diplomatic order and, implicitly, each state's sense of rivalry with the other for regional standing. It also confirms that Balhae and Silla related to one another substantially through parallel and competitive relationships with Tang China, each state seeking recognition and status from the same continental power, even as direct contact through channels such as the Silla-do continued alongside this competition.

This pattern is itself historically significant. It indicates that the "North and South States" of this period functioned less as a single interconnected Korean political system and more as two separate states, linked by a shared border, an intermittent overland route, and important elements of historical and cultural inheritance, while conducting their most consequential diplomacy with China and, in Balhae's case, with Japan as well. Later Korean historiography, most explicitly the scholar Yu Deuk-gong's 1784 work Balhaego ("Study of Balhae"), would argue for treating Silla and Balhae together as a single "North and South States" era of Korean history, a framing that became influential in later Korean historical writing even though it was formulated many centuries after Balhae's fall and remains itself a subject of ongoing scholarly discussion. The eighth- and ninth-century evidence itself suggests a relationship more distant and formal, and more heavily mediated through Tang, than a simple narrative of close North-South Korean partnership would imply.

Section 16.8

Japan, the Khitan, and the Frontier World

Balhae conducted an active and remarkably well-documented diplomatic relationship with Japan, recorded in Japanese court chronicles including sections of the Shoku Nihongi and later compilations. Balhae sent more than thirty diplomatic missions to Japan over the course of its history, beginning during King Mu's reign in the 720s, missions that involved substantial delegations, formal court ceremony, and, on the Japanese side, corresponding return missions, though at a somewhat less frequent rate. These missions carried official correspondence between the two courts, exchanged gifts including furs, ginseng, and other Balhae regional products for Japanese textiles and other goods, and served clear diplomatic purposes beyond simple commerce.

Historians have offered several, not mutually exclusive, explanations for why Balhae invested so heavily in this distant relationship. Some emphasize strategic calculation: a diplomatic partnership with Japan gave Balhae a counterweight of sorts to its more immediate and occasionally tense relationships with Tang and with Silla, and Balhae's early missions to Japan, sent not long after King Mu's confrontation with Tang, may have been intended partly to signal Balhae's independent standing and to seek potential support against Tang pressure. Others point to more straightforward commercial and cultural motives, noting that the exchange of prestige goods and the cultivation of formal diplomatic ties carried genuine value independent of any specific military alliance. This textbook does not treat these explanations as mutually exclusive; strategic, commercial, and prestige motives likely operated together, as they commonly did in premodern East Asian diplomacy. A fuller account of the maritime routes, seasonal timing, and the practical experience of these missions belongs to Chapter 17's broader treatment of trade, travel, and the maritime world.

Along its northern and western frontiers, Balhae's relationship with the Khitan and other steppe and forest peoples remained more variable and less fully documented. Khitan groups, organized loosely during most of Balhae's history into a confederation without the centralized state structure they would later develop, alternated between trade, raiding, and more distant coexistence with Balhae's frontier territories. This relationship would prove decisive in the kingdom's final decades, when the Khitan confederation, unified and transformed into the Liao state under Abaoji in the early tenth century, turned its growing military power directly against Balhae, a story reserved for Chapter 18. During the eighth and much of the ninth century, however, the Khitan represented one frontier concern among several rather than an immediate existential threat, and Balhae's rulers appear to have managed this frontier through a combination of military readiness, diplomatic engagement, and the same kind of variable local authority that characterized Balhae's relationship with its own more distant Mohe communities.

Section 16.9

Capitals, Cities, and Material Culture

Balhae's kings relocated their capital city on several occasions over the kingdom's history, a pattern historians attribute variously to strategic considerations, administrative reorganization, and the practical challenges of governing such an extensive and environmentally varied territory. The kingdom's most important and longest-serving capital was Sanggyeong (Shangjing), established under King Mun in the mid-eighth century near the site of modern Ning'an in Heilongjiang province, China, along the Mudan River.

Archaeological excavation at the Sanggyeong site, conducted over the twentieth and twenty-first centuries by Chinese, and in earlier periods Japanese and Russian, archaeological teams, has revealed a substantial planned city, laid out on a rectilinear grid with a walled outer city, an inner palace-city, and a formal north-south axial avenue, a design vocabulary directly comparable to, and very likely modeled on, the Tang capital at Chang'an, itself the template for planned capital cities across much of contemporary East Asia, including Japan's Heijō-kyō (Nara) and Heian-kyō (Kyoto). This shared urban design language does not indicate that Balhae simply copied Tang planning without adaptation; excavated palace foundations, roof-tile patterns, and ondol (underfloor heating) systems at Sanggyeong show distinctive local features, including ondol heating technology closely associated with the Korean peninsula's architectural tradition and previously attested in Goguryeo contexts, integrated into an otherwise Tang-influenced urban framework.

Excavated material culture from Sanggyeong and other Balhae sites includes distinctive roof tiles bearing lotus and other decorative motifs, high-quality glazed ceramics sometimes compared favorably by later Chinese observers to Tang wares, Buddhist stone and bronze sculpture, and stone lantern and pagoda remains associated with Buddhist temple complexes within and near the capital. Tomb architecture at Balhae sites provides some of the chapter's richest direct evidence: the tomb of Princess Jeonghye, a daughter of King Mun excavated near Dunhua in present-day Jilin province, and the tomb of her sister Princess Jeonghyo, excavated near the Sanggyeong capital region in present-day Heilongjiang province, both combine Tang-style construction techniques with tomb epitaph inscriptions in Chinese that provide valuable direct textual evidence, otherwise extremely scarce, for Balhae court titles, genealogy, and Confucian-inflected commemorative language used within the Balhae elite.

Together, this archaeological evidence supports a picture of a sophisticated, cosmopolitan court culture at Balhae's capitals, one that drew extensively on continental Tang models while integrating distinctive elements connected to the Goguryeo architectural and cultural tradition, a material confirmation of the composite character examined throughout this chapter.

Section 16.10

Religion and Cultural Life

Buddhism occupied an important place in Balhae's elite religious and cultural life, as the archaeological evidence of temple foundations, stone pagodas, and Buddhist statuary at Sanggyeong and other sites indicates. This should not, however, simply be read as a repetition of Chapter 15's account of Silla Buddhism. Balhae's Buddhist institutions developed within a different political and cultural framework, more directly and continuously connected to Tang Buddhist practice through Balhae's extensive diplomatic and educational exchange with the Tang court, and simultaneously carrying forward elements of Goguryeo's own earlier Buddhist tradition, examined in Chapter 12.

Confucian court culture is also evident in the surviving epigraphic record, most clearly in the tomb epitaphs of Princess Jeonghye and her sister Princess Jeonghyo, whose inscriptions employ formal Confucian commemorative language, classical literary allusion, and genealogical formulas closely comparable to contemporary Tang funerary practice. This suggests that Balhae's court elite pursued Classical Chinese literary and philosophical education with some seriousness, consistent with the kingdom's practice of sending students to study in Tang China.

Beyond the court, direct evidence for religious and cultural practice among Balhae's wider population, and particularly among its Mohe communities, is much thinner. Archaeological evidence from more peripheral sites suggests continuity of earlier Mohe material culture, including distinctive ceramic traditions and burial customs differing from those of the Tang-influenced capital elite, indicating that Balhae's cultural life, like its population, was not uniform across the kingdom's territory. Historians should resist projecting the well-documented Buddhist and Confucian court culture of Sanggyeong onto the whole of Balhae's diverse population without qualification.

Section 16.11

Population and Regional Diversity

One of the more useful surviving descriptions of Balhae's population structure comes not from a Chinese source but from the Japanese historical compilation Ruijū Kokushi, which reports that Mohe people were numerous within Balhae while a smaller group described as "toin" were fewer in number but served as village and local leaders. Historians commonly interpret "toin" in this context as referring to Goguryeo-descended inhabitants, though the identification involves some interpretive uncertainty given the brevity of the source. Read cautiously, this evidence supports a population structure in which Goguryeo-descended communities were prominent within local and administrative leadership while Mohe communities, organized into various tribal and regional groupings, made up a substantially larger share of the overall population, some closely integrated into Balhae's administrative system and others retaining considerably more local autonomy, particularly in the kingdom's more distant northern and eastern territories. Students should treat this description as a valuable but limited data point rather than a precisely measured demographic record.

This division should not be treated as a simple, fixed two-tier hierarchy uniform across the kingdom's history and territory. Some Mohe communities, particularly those in closer proximity to the capital region and longer integrated into the state, likely achieved significant status within Balhae's administrative and even military elite over time; Chinese sources note Mohe commanders serving in Balhae's forces and, on occasion, in embassies to Tang and Japan. Other Mohe groups, especially the more distant Heishui Mohe of the lower Amur, maintained a more independent and sometimes contested relationship with Balhae's central authority throughout the kingdom's history, occasionally aligning with Tang against Balhae's interests, as the conflict examined in Section 16.6 illustrates.

The northern Korean peninsula portion of Balhae's territory, meanwhile, likely retained a population more directly continuous with Goguryeo's former subjects, distinct in some respects from both the Manchurian Mohe territories and the Manchurian Goguryeo-descended elite concentrated at court. Regional administrative centers across this diverse territory probably varied considerably in their ethnic composition, economic basis, and relationship to central authority, a variation historians can only partially reconstruct given the limits of the surviving evidence.

Recognizing this internal diversity matters for the same reason it mattered in earlier chapters' treatment of Goguryeo, Silla, and the Three Kingdoms more broadly: Balhae should not be imagined as a single, uniform national population simply relocated northward, but as a genuinely composite frontier state whose cohesion depended on political and administrative structures binding together communities with different backgrounds, languages, and material cultures.

Section 16.12

What Kind of State Was Balhae?

Balhae resists easy classification within modern national historical categories, and this chapter has tried to show why. It was not simply "Korea in the north," if that phrase is taken to imply a population and culture identical to Silla's. It was not simply a Tang tributary state, if that phrase is taken to imply that Tang controlled its internal government. It was not simply a Mohe tribal confederation, if that phrase is taken to imply the absence of a sophisticated, literate, Tang-influenced court culture and a durable multi-generational royal house. And it was not simply a temporary successor state destined to collapse quickly, given that it endured for more than two centuries, considerably longer than several of the Three Kingdoms period polities examined in Part II.

A more accurate description situates Balhae as a genuinely composite frontier kingdom, built by a Goguryeo-descended leadership in coalition with Mohe communities, governing a large and environmentally diverse territory through institutions adapted from Tang models, and maintaining an independent, multidirectional foreign policy that engaged Tang China, Silla, Japan, and various frontier peoples according to its own calculated interests rather than as a simple client of any single power.

This composite character is precisely why Balhae belongs centrally, rather than marginally, within a history of Korea organized around continuous interaction with continental and maritime East Asia, the organizing principle established in Chapter 1. Balhae's history cannot be told as an isolated national story, nor should it be absorbed entirely into the history of Tang China, of Manchuria considered apart from Korea, or of a later, retrospectively unified "Korean" narrative. Its history is genuinely shared among several regional historiographical traditions, and understanding it responsibly requires holding all of these threads together rather than resolving the tension in favor of any single modern national claim.

The Chinese Dynastic Histories on Balhae's Origins

Creator: Tang and Song dynasty court historians · Date: the Jiu Tangshu compiled mid-tenth century; the Xin Tangshu compiled mid-eleventh century, both describing earlier eighth-century events · Type: Official Chinese dynastic history

Context: Because Balhae left no surviving dynastic history of its own, historians depend heavily on these later Chinese compilations, produced by court historians working from earlier Tang records, embassy reports, and administrative archives, for basic information about Balhae's founding and its relationship with Tang.

Paraphrase: The following is a paraphrase, not a direct quotation, of the relevant content.

The Jiu Tangshu describes Dae Joyeong's origin as a separate branch of Goguryeo, while the later Xin Tangshu instead identifies Balhae's founders as Sumo Mohe who had attached themselves to Goguryeo. Both texts describe his flight eastward following the disturbances near Yingzhou, his coalition's victory over a pursuing Tang force, and the eventual establishment of a state that eventually received Tang investiture under the name Balhae.

Analysis: The discrepancy between the two histories regarding Dae Joyeong's and his coalition's origin is itself valuable evidence, illustrating how a single tradition of Chinese official historical writing could produce differing accounts across different eras, likely reflecting different source materials, different assumptions about ethnic categorization, or shifting later Chinese perspectives on the Goguryeo-Mohe relationship.

Limitations: These accounts were produced by an outside power, generations or centuries after the events described, working from Tang administrative records that were themselves shaped by Tang diplomatic and frontier-management interests rather than by any Balhae perspective. No surviving Balhae source allows historians to check these accounts against an internal record.

Perspective: Both histories represent an external, continental Chinese administrative viewpoint. The perspective of Balhae's own founders, and of the Mohe communities who joined the founding coalition, does not survive in comparable detail.

Historical Debate

Whose History Is Balhae?

The question: Should Balhae be understood primarily as a chapter of Korean history, a chapter of Chinese frontier and Manchurian history, or a distinct regional history not fully owned by either modern national tradition?

Interpretation A — Balhae belongs centrally to Korean history: Historians emphasizing this view point to Balhae's Goguryeo-descended ruling house, its preservation of Goguryeo cultural and architectural elements such as ondol heating, its later inclusion in Korean historiography including the concept of the "North and South States," and its role, through refugee migration after 926, in shaping early Goryeo, examined in Chapter 18.
Interpretation B — Balhae belongs to the history of Chinese Manchuria and its frontier administration: Historians emphasizing this view point out that much of Balhae's core territory and many of its major archaeological sites, including Sanggyeong, lie in present-day northeastern China, alongside its formal investiture and tributary relationship with the Tang court, its heavy adoption of Tang administrative and urban models, and its participation in the Tang-centered investiture and tributary diplomatic order alongside several other frontier states.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the same core evidence: the Chinese dynastic histories, Japanese court chronicles, and the archaeological record from Balhae's capitals and outlying sites, located across present-day northeastern China, North Korea, and the Russian Far East.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns primarily interpretation and emphasis rather than a dispute over basic facts: how much weight to give Balhae's Goguryeo-descended elite versus its majority Mohe population, how to read the ambiguous ethnic terminology of the Chinese dynastic histories, and how much significance to attach to formal Tang investiture titles.

Why it matters: Because Balhae's former territory now lies across three modern states, China, North Korea, and Russia, its historical interpretation carries ongoing political sensitivity, particularly in relation to modern territorial and heritage claims connected to the broader modern dispute over the historical status of Goguryeo, sometimes described in the context of the early twenty-first-century "Northeast Project" debate between Chinese and Korean historians and governments.

Student task: Compare Interpretation A and Interpretation B using the specific evidence presented in this chapter, and defend your own evidence-based position on how Balhae's history should be framed. Your position might align closely with one interpretation, combine elements of both, or propose a different framing entirely, but it should identify which pieces of evidence you find most persuasive and why.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From Goguryeo to Balhae

What Changed

  • Political center of gravity shifted from Goguryeo's Pyongyang-centered core to a new capital region along the Sungari and Mudan rivers, further north and west
  • The state's population incorporated a much larger proportional share of Mohe communities than Goguryeo's population had included
  • Formal international status shifted from Goguryeo's long-standing rivalry with Sui and Tang to Balhae's investiture as a recognized, if independent, Tang tributary kingdom
  • Urban planning at Balhae's capitals adopted Tang-style grid layouts more systematically than is documented for Goguryeo's own capitals

What Stayed the Same

  • A ruling elite substantially descended from and culturally continuous with Goguryeo's aristocracy
  • Architectural continuity, including ondol underfloor heating, connecting Balhae's palace and residential construction to earlier Goguryeo practice
  • Buddhism as a major elite religious tradition, carried forward from Goguryeo's own Buddhist institutions
  • A geopolitical position bridging the Korean peninsula, Manchuria, and the steppe, comparable to Goguryeo's own long-standing frontier position

Why the pattern matters: Balhae's history demonstrates that a defeated state's political and cultural inheritance can persist and transform into a new kingdom under changed demographic and geographic conditions, rather than simply disappearing with the fall of its royal government.

Cause and Effect

The Formation of a Northern Successor State

Long-term causes

Goguryeo's long history as a large, Manchuria-spanning frontier power with a substantial military and administrative elite, and its extended prior contact, sometimes cooperative and sometimes hostile, with neighboring Mohe communities.

Short-term causes

Tang's forced relocation policy after 668, which concentrated displaced Goguryeo elites alongside Mohe and Khitan communities near the Tang frontier garrison of Yingzhou, and the weakening of Tang frontier administration through the 680s and 690s.

Triggering event

The 696 Khitan uprising near Yingzhou, which disrupted Tang frontier control and created the immediate opportunity for Dae Joyeong's coalition to break away and move east.

Effect

Victory at Tianmenling in 698, the founding of the state of Jin, its 713 recognition as Balhae, and the establishment of a durable northern kingdom that endured for more than two centuries.

Historical complexity: No single cause, whether Goguryeo resentment, Mohe ambition, or Tang weakness alone, adequately explains Balhae's formation; the kingdom emerged from the intersection of long-term political and social conditions with a specific, short-lived frontier crisis.

Everyday Life and Social History

Ordinary Life in a Composite Frontier Kingdom

Most of Balhae's population did not live at Sanggyeong and left no epitaphs comparable to those of Princess Jeonghyo. Agricultural communities in the kingdom's southern and western core likely cultivated millet and other grains suited to the region's climate, paid taxes or tribute to local and central authorities, and organized village and kin-based social life in patterns broadly comparable to contemporary Tang and Silla rural society, though direct textual evidence for Balhae peasant life is extremely limited.

In the forested and river-valley regions further north and east, Mohe communities combined hunting, fishing, and limited cultivation, producing furs, ginseng, and other regional products that entered both Balhae's internal tribute system and, ultimately, the kingdom's diplomatic gift exchanges with Tang and Japan. Archaeological evidence from Mohe settlement sites indicates distinctive house forms, storage pits, and ceramic traditions differing from those found at Sanggyeong, underscoring that everyday material life across Balhae's territory varied considerably by region and by community, even as the kingdom's court projected a unified and cosmopolitan image outward through its diplomatic missions and monumental architecture.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced the formation, growth, and character of Balhae, the northern kingdom whose existence makes clear that Silla's victory in 668 did not produce a single unified Korean state. Following Goguryeo's destruction, Tang China's attempt to administer the former Goguryeo territory directly through the Andong Protectorate proved unsustainable, weakened by continued resistance, Silla's own southern expansion, and the practical difficulty of governing a vast and distant frontier.

Out of the disorder created by the 696 Khitan uprising near Yingzhou, Dae Joyeong led a coalition of displaced Goguryeo people and Mohe communities eastward, defeating a pursuing Tang army at Tianmenling in 698 and founding a new state, initially called Jin. Tang recognition followed in 713, when Dae Joyeong received the title King of Balhae Commandery, giving the kingdom the name by which it is now known.

The chapter examined at length the genuinely disputed question of Balhae's Goguryeo and Mohe foundations, concluding that the kingdom is best understood as a composite frontier state: a Goguryeo-descended ruling elite governing a population in which various Mohe communities made up a very large share of the total population, with both elements essential to the kingdom's character.

Balhae expanded significantly under Kings Mu and Mun, governing an extensive and environmentally diverse territory spanning parts of the modern Korean peninsula, northeastern China, and the Russian Far East. Its government adapted Tang institutional models, including a central ministry structure and provincial administrative hierarchy, to frontier conditions, while its kings maintained independent sovereignty, using era names and conducting their own foreign policy.

Balhae's relationship with Tang moved from military conflict through formal investiture to extensive peaceful exchange, interrupted by King Mu's confrontation with Tang in the 730s, including a notable naval raid on the Shandong coast. Its relationship with Silla appears to have been distant and formally correct rather than either close or consistently hostile, with both states directing most of their diplomatic energy toward Tang rather than each other. Balhae's extensive diplomatic relationship with Japan, involving more than thirty missions, demonstrated the kingdom's active, multidirectional foreign policy.

Archaeological evidence from Balhae's capitals, particularly Sanggyeong, reveals a sophisticated urban culture combining Tang-style planned city design with distinctively Korean architectural elements such as ondol heating, alongside Buddhist temple remains and elite tomb inscriptions that provide rare direct textual evidence for the kingdom's court culture. This evidence, together with the more fragmentary record of Mohe material culture at outlying sites, confirms that Balhae's population and culture varied considerably across its territory.

The chapter closed by arguing that Balhae resists easy classification within any single modern national historical tradition, and that this composite, genuinely shared character is precisely what makes the kingdom essential to a Korean history organized around continuous interaction with continental and maritime East Asia, rather than a marginal note appended to the history of Silla, Tang, or Manchuria alone.

This chapter has deliberately stopped short of Balhae's later difficulties and its sudden conquest by the Khitan-led Liao state in 926, along with the resulting refugee movements into early Goryeo. That story, along with the broader unraveling of the Silla-Balhae order, belongs to Chapter 18. Chapter 17 will take up the maritime and commercial world in which Balhae, alongside Silla, Tang, and Japan, participated, examining the merchants, pilgrims, and travelers who connected these states across the seas.

The Tang practice of granting titles, seals, and calendars to neighboring rulers, symbolically recognizing their authority within a hierarchical diplomatic order without necessarily controlling their internal government.

Early Balhae ruler associated with territorial expansion and open military conflict with Tang, including the 732 naval raid on Dengzhou.

Long-reigning Balhae ruler associated with administrative consolidation, capital relocation to Sanggyeong, and expanded diplomacy with Japan.

Early ninth-century Balhae ruler under whom the kingdom's territorial extent and five-capital administrative system reached their fullest development.
Sanggyeong (Shangjing)
Balhae's principal capital from the mid-eighth century onward, located near modern Ning'an, Heilongjiang province, and the site of extensive archaeological excavation.
North and South States
A term, primarily developed in later Korean and Japanese scholarship, describing the coexistence of Silla in the south and Balhae in the north after 698.
Ondol
An underfloor heating system associated with the Korean peninsula's architectural tradition and attested at both Goguryeo and Balhae sites.
Haidong shengguo
A later Chinese honorific phrase, often translated "the flourishing state east of the sea," applied to Balhae at its height.
Princess Jeonghye and Princess Jeonghyo
Two daughters of King Mun whose excavated tombs, near Dunhua and near Sanggyeong respectively, and inscribed epitaphs provide rare direct textual evidence for Balhae court culture and titles.
Heishui Mohe
The Mohe grouping of the lower Amur River basin, more distant from Balhae's core and, at times, in conflict with or allied against Balhae's central authority.
Sumo Mohe
The Mohe grouping of the upper Sungari River region, closer to Balhae's core territory and more closely integrated into the kingdom's founding population.
Khitan
A frontier steppe people whose loose eighth- and ninth-century confederation would later unify into the Liao state and conquer Balhae in 926.
Era name
A distinct dating system used by a ruler to number the years of his reign, whose independent use by Balhae's kings signaled a degree of sovereign standing within the Tang-centered diplomatic order.
Jiu Tangshu / Xin Tangshu
The "Old" and "New" Books of Tang, official Chinese dynastic histories compiled in the tenth and eleventh centuries respectively, providing differing accounts of Dae Joyeong's origins.
Composite state
A political entity governing multiple distinct regions or populations under one authority, a concept central to understanding Balhae's structure.
Tributary order
The hierarchical diplomatic system centered on the Tang court through which neighboring rulers received investiture titles while retaining independent internal governance.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has argued that the political history of the Korean peninsula and its northern hinterland after 668 cannot be told through Silla alone. Balhae's more than two centuries of independent existence, its sophisticated adaptation of continental institutions, its wide-ranging diplomacy, and its composite Goguryeo and Mohe population together demonstrate that the era following the Three Kingdoms wars produced not a single unified Korean state but a genuine dual order, the "North and South States" that give this Part its name.

Understanding Balhae also requires a particular kind of historical humility. Because so little of Balhae's own voice survives, historians must reconstruct the kingdom through the eyes of its neighbors, Tang China and Japan chiefly, supplemented by an archaeological record still being uncovered and interpreted across three modern countries. This should make students cautious about any confident, single-sentence answer to the question "what was Balhae," while also making clear why the effort to understand it responsibly matters, both for Korean history and for the wider history of Northeast Asia.

Balhae's story does not end with this chapter. Its final decades, its sudden collapse before the Khitan-led Liao state, and the migration of Balhae refugees, including a crown prince, into the newly forming Goryeo state will be examined in Chapter 18, alongside the parallel unraveling of Silla in the south. Its participation in the wider maritime and commercial world of Tang-era East Asia, alongside Silla's own merchants and pilgrims, will be examined in Chapter 17. Readers should carry forward from this chapter a clear sense of Balhae as a substantial, independent, culturally distinctive kingdom in its own right, not merely a name to be mentioned in passing before Silla's story resumes.

The larger lesson of this chapter, consistent with the methodological commitments established in Chapter 1, is that Korean history has never been a story of one people in one place under one government. Balhae's Goguryeo-descended court, its Mohe subjects, its Tang-modeled institutions, and its diplomatic reach across Northeast Asia together show a Korean history whose northern chapter was, for more than two hundred years, written from a capital far from the peninsula's center, among peoples whose full historical inheritance still resists being claimed entirely by any single modern nation.
