Manchuria, Militarization, and the Expansion of the Japanese Empire

Korean History: Ancient Kingdoms to a Divided Peninsula

Chapter Opening

On the night of September 18, 1931, an explosion damaged a stretch of railway track outside Mukden, in southern Manchuria. Within hours, Japanese troops were attacking Chinese garrisons. Within three days, units stationed in colonial Korea crossed the Amnok (Yalu) River without waiting for orders from Tokyo. A railway incident far from the peninsula had changed what Korea was for.

This chapter asks how Japan's expansion into Manchuria transformed colonial Korea into an increasingly militarized component of the Japanese empire, and how that expansion reshaped the political, economic, and human geography of Northeast Asia. Its chronology runs principally from 1931 to 1945, with essential background from the Russo-Japanese War of 1904–1905. Its subject remains Korea. It is not a chapter on Japanese or Chinese military history, though it must describe events in Manchuria and China because those events changed the conditions under which Koreans lived, worked, farmed, migrated, resisted, and served.

Three developments organize the chapter. The first is Japan's seizure and occupation of Manchuria beginning in September 1931, and the creation of the nominally independent state of Manchukuo in 1932. The second is the growing strategic importance of the Korean peninsula as a military base, industrial region, and transport corridor between the Japanese home islands and the continent. The third is the escalation from regional expansion to full-scale war in China in 1937, armed confrontations with the Soviet Union in 1938 and 1939, a wider Pacific War from December 1941, and the abrupt collapse of Japanese imperial power in August 1945.

Korea's position in this story was unusual. Since the annexation of 1910, examined in Chapter 54, the peninsula had been governed as a colony. Its people had no voice in Japanese decisions about war and peace. Yet Korea was not a bystander. Japanese army units based on the peninsula took part in the 1931 invasion. Korean farmers had been settling in Manchuria for decades and became entangled in conflicts among Chinese authorities, Japanese officials, and local communities. Korean railways, ports, power plants, and mines were folded into a continental supply system. Some Koreans served the empire as officials, businessmen, police, and later soldiers, while others fought it, fled it, or endured it. The chapter therefore treats Koreans as historical actors operating under severe constraint, not as either passive objects or equal participants in imperial decision-making.

The evidence for these developments is uneven. Japanese army, diplomatic, and colonial records survive in large quantity, though they were shaped by censorship, bureaucratic self-justification, and, after 1945, deliberate destruction. League of Nations documents, American diplomatic papers, and the findings of the Lytton Commission provide international perspectives. Chinese and Korean newspapers, memoirs, and later oral histories record how the events were perceived by those who lived through them, though colonial censorship severely limited what Korean newspapers could print in the 1930s. Statistical series produced by the Government-General and the South Manchuria Railway Company are valuable but are the products of the institutions they measure. Each source type receives attention in the chapter, and four are examined in Primary Source Windows.

The chapter also takes care with boundaries. Industrial investment and factory construction in colonial Korea belong to Chapter 58. Korean political and armed resistance belongs to Chapter 59. Assimilation policy belongs to Chapter 60. The lived experience of forced labor, military service, and civilian mobilization belongs to Chapter 62. The overseas independence movements of the war years belong to Chapter 63, and the politics of liberation to Chapters 64 and 65. Chapter 61 supplies the strategic and military frame within which those developments occurred.

Finally, the chapter ends where Chapter 1 insisted the story of division must begin: with contingency. The line at the 38th parallel was not a long-planned partition. It emerged from the military arrangements surrounding Japan's surrender in August 1945, when the Soviet Union's attack on Manchuria and the American decision to define an occupation boundary produced two zones on the peninsula. Understanding how Korea came to be the place where Soviet and American armies met requires understanding how Japan's continental empire was built, stretched, and broken.

Historical Context

The Korean peninsula and Manchuria share a land frontier along the Amnok and Duman (Tumen) rivers and a long history of political and cultural connection, discussed in Chapters 1, 8, and 16. Goguryeo and Balhae had governed territory on both sides of that frontier. In the modern period the frontier became an international boundary between Korea and the Qing empire, and after 1912 between Korea and the Republic of China. Its mountain and river landscape, its sparse state presence, and its mixed population made it a zone where migrants, smugglers, armed bands, and political exiles moved with relative ease, and where successive governments struggled to impose control.

Japan's military and economic presence in southern Manchuria began with the Russo-Japanese War, examined in Chapter 54. Under the 1905 Treaty of Portsmouth, Japan took over Russia's lease on the Liaodong peninsula and the southern branch of the Russian-built railway, which became the South Manchuria Railway. At the same time Japan established a protectorate over Korea, and in 1910 annexed it. From these years onward Japanese planners tended to see the two territories as parts of one strategic problem: a colony on one side of the frontier, a zone of Japanese commercial and military rights on the other, and a Chinese government of uncertain strength in between.

By the late 1920s that arrangement was under pressure. Chinese nationalism, fueled by the Northern Expedition and the nominal reunification of China under the Nationalist government in 1928, challenged foreign privileges. Chinese authorities in Manchuria built railways and a port that competed with Japanese-controlled lines. The world economic depression after 1929 hurt Japan's economy and strengthened arguments within the military and the press that Manchuria was a vital resource base and a "lifeline" for the empire. Within the Japanese army, mid-ranking officers increasingly doubted that civilian governments would defend what they regarded as Japan's continental rights.

Colonial Korea in 1931 was governed by the Government-General under a Japanese governor-general, a post that until then had been held by both military officers and civilians. Korea's population, over twenty million, was overwhelmingly rural and colonized. Its economy, whose development is traced in Chapter 58, had been oriented around rice exports to Japan and was beginning to shift toward electricity, chemicals, and mining. The March First Movement of 1919 and the more recent resistance movements covered in Chapters 56 and 59 had pushed Japanese officials to combine repression with limited concession. A substantial Korean population, probably numbering in the hundreds of thousands, had moved to Manchuria, driven by land shortage and tenancy on the peninsula, by the lure of rice cultivation on frontier land, and by political exile. These Korean settlers were the immediate human link between the two territories.

Section 61.1

Korea and Manchuria in Japan's Imperial Strategy, 1905–1931

Japan's position in Manchuria before 1931 rested on treaty rights, not territorial sovereignty. The Treaty of Portsmouth transferred to Japan the lease of the southern tip of the Liaodong peninsula, known as the Kwantung Leased Territory with its ports at Dalian and Port Arthur, and the southern branch of the former Russian railway, which in 1906 became the South Manchuria Railway Company with headquarters at Dalian. The treaty also allowed Japan to station guards along the line. These guards, together with the garrison of the leased territory, became the Kwantung Army, so named by the end of the First World War. In 1915 Japan pressed the Twenty-One Demands on China and obtained agreements extending the leases to ninety-nine years. Chinese officials accepted these concessions under heavy pressure, and Chinese nationalists continued to challenge their legitimacy.

Japanese influence in southern Manchuria in this period was substantial but partial. Japan controlled the railway zone, had a leased territory, kept troops and consular police in the region, and invested in mining, shipping, and agriculture. Chinese authorities nevertheless governed the surrounding provinces. From the 1910s the dominant figure in Manchuria was the warlord Zhang Zuolin, whom the Kwantung Army alternately supported and distrusted. In June 1928 a bomb destroyed his train near Mukden. Kwantung Army officers were responsible, though the Japanese government did not punish the conspirators severely. His son Zhang Xueliang then aligned his territory with the Nationalist government at Nanjing. This distinction between influence before 1931 and occupation afterward is essential: the Manchurian Incident replaced a system of privileges with direct military domination.

Korea's position within this system was structural. The Government-General controlled a peninsula whose northern rivers faced Manchuria and whose railways joined the Japanese-controlled line at the frontier. Two permanent Japanese divisions, the 19th at Nanam in the northeast and the 20th at Yongsan in Seoul, were stationed in Korea under the Korea Army, and they were the nearest large Japanese formations to Manchuria. Strategists regarded Korea as a flank defense against the Soviet Union and as a reserve for operations in the north. Scholars in Japan developed a historiography of "Manchuria–Korea" (Man-Sen) connections that cited the ancient territorial links of Goguryeo and Balhae to argue that the two regions belonged together under Japanese leadership, a political use of the past that this textbook treats as a historical phenomenon in its own right, not as neutral scholarship.

The strategic logic was thus interlinked. A hostile or unstable Manchuria could threaten the northern Korean frontier. Korean independence activists, as Chapter 59 shows, used the Gando region and other parts of Manchuria as bases. Japanese leaders therefore tied the security of the colony to the security of Japan's rights across the Yalu and Tumen. This was an argument about interests, not an inevitable path. Civilian governments in the 1920s, particularly under Foreign Minister Shidehara Kijūrō, pursued conciliation with China and did not seek territorial conquest. What changed in 1931 was less the strategic doctrine than the willingness of the army to impose it on its own initiative.

Section 61.2

Korean Migration, Chinese Communities, and the Wanbaoshan Incident of 1931

Korean movement into Manchuria predates Japanese rule. From the nineteenth century, farmers from the northern provinces crossed the Tumen and Amnok rivers to cultivate frontier land, especially in the Gando (Jiandao) region, and the movement accelerated after annexation as colonial land policies, rising rents, and tenancy pushed many peasants off the land. Others were political exiles. By 1930 the Korean population of Manchuria numbered in the hundreds of thousands, concentrated in eastern Jilin province and spread along the railways into the south and center. Many were tenants or small cultivators who introduced or expanded wet-rice farming in a region where Chinese farmers had grown mainly dry-field crops.

Their legal position was ambiguous and was manipulated by both governments. Under the Gando Convention of 1909, Japan acknowledged Chinese sovereignty over the region in return for recognition of Korean residence rights and a Japanese consular presence. After annexation Japan regarded Koreans as Japanese subjects entitled to consular protection, and claimed the right to protect them. Chinese authorities regarded them as foreign residents or, in some cases, as people required to adopt Chinese nationality in order to own land, and viewed Japanese protection as a pretext for intervention. Korean settlers were thus caught between two states, and many suffered pressure from Chinese officials and landlords while also being treated by Japanese police and diplomats as an instrument of influence.

The Wanbaoshan dispute of 1931 exposed this entanglement. In spring 1931 a group of Korean farmers near Wanbaoshan, a village near Changchun, began digging an irrigation canal across land leased from a Chinese intermediary for rice cultivation. Local Chinese farmers, whose land the channel crossed or threatened to flood, protested and attempted to stop the work. Japanese consular police intervened to protect the Koreans, and in early July there was an armed confrontation with some shooting. Historians generally agree that Japanese officials used the incident to reinforce their claim to protect Koreans, and that the dispute was more about land and water than about national hostility.

The consequences in Korea were worse than the dispute itself. On the evening of July 2 and again early on July 3, the Korean-language Chosun Ilbo published sensational extra editions reporting, falsely and in inflammatory terms, that Korean farmers had been killed. The reporting drew on information transmitted through Japanese consular channels. In the following days anti-Chinese riots broke out in Incheon, Seoul, Pyongyang, and other cities. Chinese residents, many of them merchants and laborers, were attacked and killed, and shops and homes were destroyed. Counts of the dead differ greatly by source, ranging from dozens to well over a hundred, with Pyongyang the most severely affected city, and the casualty figures cannot be stated with confidence. Colonial police were slow to restrain the violence. The episode is evidence that Korean frustration with colonial rule could be redirected against another minority population, that the press was able to inflame public opinion, and that the Japanese authorities could use ethnic conflict among those under their control. It also damaged relations between Korean and Chinese communities and was cited by Japanese propaganda as evidence of Chinese hostility toward Koreans.

Section 61.3

The Kwantung Army and the Manchurian Incident of September 18, 1931

The Kwantung Army in 1931 was a modest force, with something on the order of ten thousand men, in a region where the Northeastern Army under Zhang Xueliang was several times larger, though much of that army was stationed south of the Great Wall. Its significance lay in its officer corps. Staff officers such as Colonel Itagaki Seishirō and Lieutenant Colonel Ishiwara Kanji believed that Japan's survival required control of Manchuria, that civilian diplomacy had failed, and that a pretext should be created for action. Their attitude reflected a wider pattern in the Japanese army usually described by the term gekokujō, "the lower overturning the upper," in which subordinate officers acted on their own authority and expected superiors and the government to ratify accomplished facts.

On the night of September 18, a small charge detonated beside the South Manchuria Railway at Liutiaohu, north of Mukden. The damage to the track was slight, and a train reportedly passed over the section shortly afterward. The Kwantung Army blamed Chinese soldiers and began attacking the barracks at Beidaying and the city of Mukden. Within days its forces held Mukden, Changchun, and Jilin, helped by the fact that Zhang Xueliang, wary of a war with Japan and focused on political rivalries within China, ordered his troops not to resist. Postwar investigation, including the findings of the International Military Tribunal for the Far East, concluded that the explosion was staged by Japanese officers. This is the view held by the great majority of historians today, while the exact roles of individual officers remain a subject of research.

Tokyo's initial response shows that expansion was not the unified policy of the Japanese state. The government of Prime Minister Wakatsuki Reijirō and Foreign Minister Shidehara announced a policy of non-expansion and sought to restrain the army. The Army Ministry and General Staff were divided, and some senior officers privately sympathized with the plotters. Over the following weeks the army continued its advance, the cabinet failed to compel obedience, and public opinion, largely supported by the press, rallied behind the army. The Wakatsuki cabinet resigned in December 1931. Its successor under Inukai Tsuyoshi was weaker in its control of the army, and Inukai was assassinated by naval officers in May 1932. The pattern was one in which military initiative in the field, supported by popular enthusiasm and by terror against dissent, defeated civilian restraint.

Official Accounts of the Manchurian Incident, 1931–1932

Creator: Kwantung Army and Japanese government statements of September 1931; findings of the League's Lytton Commission (1932) · Type: Official communiqués, diplomatic statements, and an international investigative report · Note: the material below is paraphrase, not quotation.

Context: Within hours of the explosion the Kwantung Army issued accounts stating that Chinese troops had damaged the railway and attacked Japanese guards, and that Japan acted in self-defense. Korean newspapers, operating under colonial censorship, largely reproduced Japanese official versions. The Lytton Commission, appointed by the League in late 1931, visited Manchuria in spring 1932 and heard evidence from Japanese, Chinese, and Manchukuo authorities.

Paraphrase of the contrasting claims:

The army's account held that Chinese soldiers blew up the track and fired on Japanese patrols, and that the subsequent attacks were defensive measures. The Lytton report, by contrast, concluded that the army's night operations could not be regarded as legitimate self-defense, while declining to say that the explosion itself had been proved to be a Japanese plot, and it also acknowledged that Japan had genuine grievances and interests in Manchuria.

Analysis: The two accounts show how a report prepared for a political purpose can differ from one produced by an outside body with limited powers. The Lytton Commission's careful wording reflected the limits of its evidence and its diplomatic role. It is not evidence that the incident was ambiguous. Later testimony and postwar investigation, including that of the Tokyo tribunal, provided the more complete account of Japanese involvement.

Limitations: The army statements were self-serving and issued by an interested party. The Lytton report was written in a diplomatic setting and could not compel testimony. Neither source records the perspective of ordinary residents of Mukden or of Koreans in the region.

Perspective: One source represents the Japanese military, the other an international commission largely drawn from Western powers. Chinese, Korean, and local civilian voices are largely absent.

Section 61.4

Japanese Forces in Korea and the Expansion of the Manchurian Campaign

The Kwantung Army began the campaign with too few troops to hold the territory it had struck, and its commander, Lieutenant General Honjō Shigeru, asked for reinforcement. The nearest large force was the Korea Army, commanded by General Hayashi Senjūrō. The Korea Army and the Kwantung Army were separate commands answering to the Army Ministry and General Staff in Tokyo, and the two should not be conflated. Hayashi sympathized with the Kwantung Army's aims, and on September 21 he ordered a mixed brigade from the 20th Division to cross the Yalu from Sinuiju into Manchuria. He did so without the authorization of the emperor, who held formal command, and without the prior approval of the cabinet.

The act was a clear breach of command procedure, and the army's response is a good illustration of how the system functioned. The cabinet, after debate, chose to accept the accomplished fact and approved the financing of the troops' operations, and the General Staff did not discipline Hayashi. Hayashi went on to a political career that included a term as prime minister in 1937. Historians generally treat the episode as a landmark in the erosion of civilian and central control, because it demonstrated that a field commander could commit the empire to military operations and be rewarded for it.

Korea's stationed forces thereafter took part in further operations. Units from the Korea Army served in northern Manchuria and in the pacification of the territory through 1932, and the peninsula became the staging area for reinforcements moving by rail across the Yalu bridge from Sinuiju to Andong. This had immediate consequences for Korean society. The army requisitioned rail capacity, increased its presence in the border provinces, and tightened its relationship with the colonial police. The colony's role in imperial planning shifted from that of a rear garrison to that of a platform for continental operations.

The strategic consequences for Korea were therefore profound even though no fighting took place on the peninsula. First, Korea's border no longer faced a Chinese-governed frontier but a region controlled by the same empire, which altered the problem of border control, as Section 61.11 shows. Second, Korea became more valuable to Japan as a transport and industrial hinterland. Third, Korea's colonial administration came increasingly under the influence of military priorities, a shift symbolized by the appointment of army generals as governors-general in the following years. These changes are examined in Division III.

Section 61.5

The Conquest of Manchuria and the Establishment of Manchukuo, 1931–1932

After Mukden, Japanese forces extended their control along the railways and toward the northern and western provinces. The bombing of Jinzhou in October 1931 and the occupation of Qiqihar in November opened the way toward the Chinese heartland, and Harbin fell in February 1932. By then Zhang Xueliang's forces had withdrawn from Manchuria, and some local Chinese commanders had switched sides or retreated. Other forces continued to fight, and the occupation was followed by years of guerrilla resistance, discussed in Section 61.8. In January 1932 fighting broke out in Shanghai, a separate incident that drew international attention and, historians generally agree, was partly designed to divert it.

The army had decided against annexation. Annexing Manchuria would provoke the Western powers and violate Japan's treaty commitments, and Ishiwara and others believed that a nominally independent state, led by Chinese or Manchu figures and advised by Japanese, would give Japan control with less international cost. Local collaborators were recruited, among them former officials of the old Qing and warlord governments. Provincial "independence" committees were organized in the winter of 1931–1932, and the new state was proclaimed on March 1, 1932, under the name Manchukuo ("Manchu state").

Puyi, the last Qing emperor, who had been deposed in 1912 and had lived in Tianjin, was brought to Manchuria and installed as chief executive. In March 1934 he was enthroned as emperor under the reign name Kangde. The capital was placed at Changchun, renamed Hsinking ("new capital"). The choice of Puyi reflected the army's wish for a figure with symbolic legitimacy among Manchu and some Chinese conservatives, and Puyi's own hope of restoring Qing rule, hopes that Japanese officials used and did not intend to fulfill.

Manchukuo must be distinguished from the territories that Japan formally incorporated. Korea and Taiwan were colonies governed directly by Japan. Karafuto (southern Sakhalin) was administered as part of the Japanese state. The Kwantung Leased Territory was a lease. Manchukuo was neither colony nor lease but a formally separate state, with its own flag, government ministries, and, after 1934, imperial title, though it relied on Japan for defense, diplomacy, and a large proportion of its senior administrators. In September 1932 Japan formally recognized it through a protocol that also confirmed the stationing of Japanese troops. Only a small number of other states, mainly Japan's allies and clients, did so, and the Soviet Union and the Western powers withheld recognition until later.

Section 61.6

Government, Military Control, and Imperial Ideology in Manchukuo

Manchukuo's formal institutions resembled those of a modern state. It had a State Council, ministries headed by Chinese and Manchu officials, a legislative council with limited powers, courts, and a police system. Real power lay elsewhere. In each ministry a Japanese vice-minister, and in the State Council a Japanese director-general of general affairs, controlled the budget, appointments, and policy. The commander of the Kwantung Army, who from 1934 also served as ambassador to Manchukuo, had the final voice on matters of importance, including the emperor's own conduct. The system was often described as naimen shidō, "guidance from behind," and an influential group of officials, often called the "Manchurian clique" or "new bureaucrats," used it to carry out planned economic development on a scale not possible in Japan.

The state's ideology celebrated harmony. Official language spoke of the "kingly way" (wangdao), a Confucian-inspired ideal of benevolent rule, and of "five races in harmony" (gozoku kyōwa), meaning Han Chinese, Manchus, Mongols, Japanese, and Koreans. A mass organization, the Concordia Association (Kyōwakai), was established in 1932 to promote the ideal and mobilize support. In practice the categories were hierarchical. Japanese held the senior posts and received the highest pay. Koreans, who as Japanese subjects were treated in some respects as part of the ruling nation, in others as a subordinate group, were prominent as policemen, interpreters, clerks, and traders, as well as farmers, and became a visible and resented intermediary population in the eyes of many Chinese. Chinese residents formed the overwhelming majority and were largely excluded from decision-making.

The experiences of the different groups varied by place, occupation, and time. Japanese settlers, promoted by an agricultural emigration program from the later 1930s, took over land that had been purchased or confiscated, often at prices well below its value, from Chinese farmers. Koreans in the settlement program were also moved onto Manchurian land, sometimes alongside Japanese colonists and sometimes in separate villages, and in many cases occupied land that Chinese farmers had formerly held. Mongols in the western districts were governed through a separate system that gave a limited degree of autonomy to their leaders while subordinating them to the Kwantung Army. The rhetoric of racial harmony therefore coexisted with economic and political domination, and the gap between the two is one of the main subjects of historical analysis of the regime.

Historical Debate

Was Manchukuo an Independent State or a Japanese-Controlled Imperial Institution?

The question: Manchukuo had a flag, a constitution-like organic law, a head of state, ministries, and diplomatic recognition from some states. It was also created by the Kwantung Army, depended on Japanese officials, and hosted Japanese troops. How should historians classify it?

Interpretation A — A puppet state: Most historians, and the League of Nations investigation, describe Manchukuo as a state in form only. They point to the army's role in its creation, the presence of Japanese officials in every ministry, the confidential arrangements by which Puyi accepted Japanese control of defense and appointments, and the fact that Manchukuo's policies tracked Japanese military and economic priorities.
Interpretation B — A hybrid imperial experiment with its own dynamics: Others emphasize that Manchukuo was neither a colony nor a simple puppet. It developed its own bureaucracy and planning institutions, was sometimes used by officials to pursue policies that differed from those of Tokyo, and involved real collaboration by Chinese, Manchu, Mongol, and Korean participants whose motives ranged from opportunism to conviction. This view does not deny Japanese domination but stresses the complexity of the institutions it created.

Evidence: The principal evidence includes the organic law of 1932, the Japan–Manchukuo protocol of September 1932, Puyi's own later account, the personnel records of Manchukuo's ministries, and the Lytton Commission's findings.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns terminology and emphasis, more than the underlying facts. Both sides accept that the Kwantung Army held ultimate power. They differ over whether "puppet" adequately captures the range of local actors and institutions.

Why it matters: The answer affects how historians treat the responsibility of Chinese, Manchu, and Korean participants and how they understand the broader relationship between nominal sovereignty and real power in modern empires.

Student task: Identify which pieces of evidence best support each interpretation, and explain which additional evidence would help resolve the disagreement.

Section 61.7

Railways, Industry, Natural Resources, and the Development of Manchukuo

Manchukuo was valuable to Japan because of what lay under and above the ground. The Fushun coal field and the iron ore and steelworks at Anshan supplied raw materials for Japanese industry. Soybeans, the region's major export, provided income, and large areas of fertile plain could support grain and settlement. Ports at Dalian and, later, Korean ports on the Sea of Japan provided outlets. The railway network, much of it built by the South Manchuria Railway Company and extended after 1932, tied the whole together.

The South Manchuria Railway Company was the central institution of this development. It operated the railway, owned mines, ports, hotels, steelworks, and warehouses, and ran a large research department that conducted surveys of the region's economy and society. After 1932 the army and the Manchukuo government took over parts of its role, and the Chinese Eastern Railway, formerly Soviet-managed, was sold to Manchukuo in 1935. In 1937 Manchukuo adopted a Five-Year Plan for industrial development, and a new holding company, Manchurian Heavy Industries Development, was established under the Japanese industrialist Ayukawa Yoshisuke to organize heavy industry. The combination of state planning and large corporations made Manchukuo a model for the controlled war economy that Japan later tried to build at home.

The development was real and also unequal. Investment flowed to the railway zone, to cities such as Mukden, Hsinking, and Harbin, and to mining and heavy industry that served Japanese needs. Chinese farmers lost land to agricultural settlement programs, and Chinese workers in mines and factories worked for lower wages and in worse conditions than their Japanese counterparts. Labor in the mines was often coerced, and the conditions at some sites, such as Fushun and the Manchukuo coal fields, were notorious for high mortality. Historians therefore distinguish between the construction of infrastructure and industry, which was substantial, and its distribution of costs and benefits, which was heavily unequal. Chapters 58 and 61 treat these developments as parts of one imperial system, with this chapter concerned mainly with the strategic dimension.

Section 61.8

Chinese Resistance, the League of Nations, and Japan's International Isolation

China's response to the invasion combined diplomacy and armed resistance. The Nationalist government, which was divided and engaged in a civil war against the Communists, appealed to the League of Nations and the United States, relying on the hope that international pressure would compel Japan to withdraw. Within Manchuria, volunteer armies and former soldiers resisted the occupation, and Chinese guerrilla forces, including units later linked to the Communist Party, continued to operate in the northeast through the 1930s. The Japanese army and Manchukuo police responded with large-scale counterinsurgency campaigns. Chinese resistance in these years must be distinguished from the Korean armed independence organizations treated in Chapter 59, though in practice they sometimes cooperated and sometimes clashed.

The international response was slower and more limited than China hoped. On September 30, 1931, the League Council adopted a resolution noting Japan's statement that it had no territorial designs and would withdraw its troops to the railway zone as security permitted. The troops did not withdraw. In January 1932 U.S. Secretary of State Henry Stimson announced that the United States would not recognize any situation brought about by means contrary to the Pact of Paris, the policy known as nonrecognition or the Stimson Doctrine. The League then appointed a commission under the Earl of Lytton, which reported in October 1932. The report found that Japan's military operations were not legitimate self-defense and that Manchukuo had not arisen from a genuine independence movement, but it also recognized Japanese interests and recommended an autonomous regime under Chinese sovereignty, with international advisers.

The League's Assembly adopted a report based on the Lytton findings in February 1933, with only Japan voting against. The Japanese delegation under Matsuoka Yōsuke walked out, and Japan gave notice of withdrawal on March 27, 1933; its withdrawal became legally effective on March 27, 1935. The affair demonstrated the limits of collective security: the League had no military force, its leading powers were preoccupied with the depression, and none was willing to impose sanctions. Japan's isolation was real, since few states recognized Manchukuo, but it did not reverse the occupation. In May 1933 the Tanggu Truce created a demilitarized zone south of the Great Wall and ended the main fighting in North China, in effect accepting Japan's gains. The episode encouraged both Japanese hardliners and those who would later challenge the international order elsewhere.

Division III · Sections 61.9–61.12Korea's Militarization and Imperial Integration
Section 61.9

Korea as a Military Base, Transport Corridor, and Continental Supply Route

Chapter 58 traced how Korean railways, roads, and ports were built. This section asks what they were used for after 1931. The main axis was the line running from Busan through Seoul and Pyongyang to Sinuiju, where a bridge crossed the Yalu to Andong and connected with the South Manchuria Railway. Troops, ammunition, and supplies moving between Japan and Manchuria could travel by ferry from Shimonoseki to Busan and then by rail across the whole peninsula. The route was one of the main arteries of the continental empire, and its capacity was a matter of military concern; the army pressed for double-tracking, improved signaling, and priority for military traffic.

A second axis lay in the northeast. Railways in Hamgyong province connected the Tumen frontier with ports on the Sea of Japan, notably Chongjin, Unggi, and Najin. After 1932 Japanese planners developed these ports as an alternative route to northern and eastern Manchuria, shortening the distance from Japanese ports such as Tsuruga and Niigata to Hsinking and Harbin. Najin was developed as a maritime outlet for the connected rail network. From 1933, the South Manchuria Railway Company operated associated northern Korean rail services through a dedicated railway administration; this rail-management role should not be confused with ownership or direct management of every port facility. The route bypassed the longer passage through Dalian, put Korean territory at the center of Manchurian trade, and also had a strategic function, since it brought Japanese forces closer to the Soviet Maritime Province.

Military installations multiplied along these routes. The Korea Army expanded its garrisons in the north, built airfields and fortifications near the frontier, and held exercises in the border provinces. Communications networks, including telegraph and, increasingly, radio, were organized to serve military as well as civil needs. The army and the Government-General coordinated the evacuation and billeting of troops and the use of civilian labor for construction. The peninsula thus became what contemporary Japanese called a heitan kichi, a logistics or supply base, a phrase that became prominent in official language by the later 1930s.

Korea's strategic position cut in two directions. It gave the peninsula a significance to Japan it had not had before, which meant greater investment in some regions and greater military control. It also made it more vulnerable if the Japanese position on the continent were to fail, since the same railways that carried troops northward would later carry Soviet forces southward in 1945. The military function of these networks, in short, tied the Korean population's daily environment to events far from the peninsula.

Section 61.10

Strategic Industry, Energy, and the Economic Foundations of Militarization

The conquest of Manchuria coincided with a change in colonial economic policy. Governor-General Ugaki Kazushige, an army general who held office from 1931 to 1936, promoted the industrialization of the north as part of a plan to make Korea a complement to Manchukuo and Japan, and the Government-General and the army increasingly regarded heavy industry and mining as strategic priorities. Colonial industrial growth in these years is the subject of Chapter 58; this section examines the military and strategic dimensions of that growth.

Energy was the foundation. Large hydroelectric projects in the northern mountains, on the Pujon, Jangjin, and Heochon rivers and later on the Yalu, supplied cheap electricity to the industrial zone around Hungnam on the east coast. The Hungnam complex, developed by the Nippon Chisso company under Noguchi Shitagau, produced chemical fertilizer and, increasingly, explosives and other chemicals for military use. Mining expanded, particularly of gold, iron ore, tungsten, magnesite, and coal, while steel, light metals, and munitions were produced in northern towns such as Chongjin. The Supung dam on the Yalu, built jointly by Japanese and Manchukuo authorities in the late 1930s and early 1940s, was among the largest hydroelectric projects in Asia of its time and provided power on both sides of the river.

The integration of northern Korea with Manchukuo's industrial development was deliberate. Raw materials, power, and labor moved across the frontier, and planners drew up regional programs that treated the two as a single economic unit. The Government-General and the Kwantung Army coordinated plans, and large Japanese firms invested in both territories. Korean businessmen and officials participated in this system, in some cases as managers, contractors, or suppliers, and the growth of a Korean industrial workforce in the north, many of them migrants from the agricultural south, was one of the social consequences.

These developments did not benefit Koreans equally. Ownership of the large industrial enterprises remained overwhelmingly Japanese, and the geographic pattern of industrial investment, concentrated in the north, left the south largely agricultural. Wartime controls over materials and labor later turned the industrial economy fully toward military production. Historians continue to debate the long-term significance of this industrial base for postwar Korea, a subject treated in Volume IV. For this chapter the central point is that the strategic needs of the Japanese army shaped the location and character of Korean industry.

Section 61.11

Border Security, Military Policing, and the Suppression of Korean Independence Organizations

Before 1931 the Korean–Manchurian frontier was an international boundary across which Japanese authorities had limited reach. Independence organizations, as Chapter 59 describes, used bases in Chinese territory, and Japanese police could pursue them only through consular channels, cooperation with Chinese authorities, or occasional military expeditions such as that into the Gando region in 1920. The occupation of Manchuria removed that obstacle. With Japanese-controlled administration on both sides of the border, the Government-General, the army, and the Manchukuo authorities could build a unified system of control.

That system had several elements. The Korea Army and the Kwantung Army conducted military operations against armed groups. The Government-General police, together with Japanese consular police in Manchuria, expanded surveillance and intelligence work, maintaining files on suspected activists and informants in communities on both sides of the river. Manchukuo police and the Japanese-organized Concordia Association assisted. The Japanese authorities also created special units, including Korean policemen and auxiliaries, for operations in the border region, and offered rewards and amnesty to induce surrender or informing. Border crossing was restricted, and travel documents and household registration were tightened.

The effect on Korean resistance was major. Armed organizations that had operated from Manchurian bases found their sanctuaries narrowed, and many groups were destroyed, withdrew into the Chinese interior, or merged with Chinese resistance forces, including Communist-led guerrilla units. The wider consequences are examined in Chapters 59 and 63. Within Korean communities in Manchuria, the situation became dangerous from several directions. Japanese and Manchukuo police suspected settlers of aiding the guerrillas, while Chinese Communist organizations during the early 1930s suspected Korean members of working for Japan. This suspicion culminated in the Minsaengdan affair of 1932–1935, in which hundreds of Koreans were accused and many killed by Communist-led organizations, a subject still debated by historians and one that illustrates how the war and occupation trapped Korean civilians between competing authorities.

The main historical point is the transformation of the environment of resistance. Chapter 59 shows what Koreans did to resist. This section shows that after 1931 the conditions under which they could do it had shifted decisively against them, since the Japanese state now controlled the territory in which most Korean armed activity had formerly been possible.

Section 61.12

Koreans and Chinese under the New Imperial Borderland Order

The occupation altered the lives of the civilians of the borderland as profoundly as it altered military geography. For Korean settlers the change was double-edged. On one hand, the removal of Chinese authorities eased some pressures that Koreans had faced, such as restrictions on land ownership and pressure to adopt Chinese nationality, and the number of Korean migrants grew substantially during the 1930s as the Japanese authorities encouraged emigration. On the other hand, Korean settlers became subject to a new level of surveillance, to colonial police and military control, and, in many places, to the resentment of Chinese neighbors who saw them as beneficiaries and instruments of the occupying power.

The Government-General and Manchukuo authorities organized Korean emigration in the later 1930s through settlement programs, including those run by a colonization company created for the purpose. Settlers were placed in designated villages, often in areas of the north and east that officials considered strategically significant, and some were organized in self-defense groups for protection against guerrillas. Such "collective settlements" were supervised and often located in the same regions as Japanese colonists, though their conditions generally differed. Land acquisition for these projects frequently involved the purchase of Chinese-owned land on unfavorable terms, and the displacement of Chinese farmers was a recurring source of grievance and unrest.

The experiences of Chinese villagers varied widely. Many lost land, whether to Japanese or to Korean settlers, and those suspected of aiding guerrillas faced punitive measures, including forced relocation into guarded "collective hamlets" established to cut off support for armed bands. Border trade, which had linked Korean and Manchurian markets, was reorganized under official control, and local economies were reshaped by the demands of the new state. Legal status and wages differed according to ethnicity and nationality, a structure that placed Japanese at the top, Koreans in an intermediate and contested position, and Chinese at the bottom of the formal hierarchy.

The borderland therefore cannot be described as a single population under a single regime. Korean tenant farmers, Korean merchants and policemen, Chinese smallholders, Japanese colonists, and officials of the colonial and Manchukuo governments each occupied different positions and had different interests. Imperial expansion affected civilian communities as much as armies and factories, and it created tensions among the colonized peoples that persisted beyond 1945.

Division IV · Sections 61.13–61.16Expanding War, Border Conflict, and Strategic Escalation
Section 61.13

The Marco Polo Bridge Incident and Full-Scale War in China, 1937

The Japanese position in North China had grown since 1933. Japanese forces held a garrison under the 1901 protocol that followed the Boxer uprising, and the army pursued policies to detach the northern provinces from Nationalist control. Chinese anti-Japanese feeling intensified, and after the Xi'an Incident of December 1936 the Nationalists and Communists moved toward a united front. On the night of July 7, 1937, Japanese and Chinese troops exchanged fire near the Marco Polo Bridge (Lugouqiao) outside Beijing. The origins of the first shots remain uncertain, and historians generally regard the incident as an accident that was escalated by decisions on both sides.

Local negotiations might have limited the dispute, but neither government was willing to retreat. Japan sent reinforcements, including troops from Korea and Manchuria, and the army's leadership expected a short campaign that would force Chinese concessions. Beijing and Tianjin fell in late July. In August fighting spread to Shanghai, where Chinese forces fought hard, and in December Japanese troops captured Nanjing and committed mass killings of civilians and prisoners, whose extent is debated, with estimates ranging from tens of thousands to over three hundred thousand depending on definitions of the area and period. The Chinese government withdrew inland and continued to resist.

Japan's expectations proved wrong. Chinese resistance did not collapse, and in January 1938 Prime Minister Konoe Fumimaro announced that Japan would no longer deal with the Nationalist government. The result was an open-ended war in which Japan occupied the major coastal cities and rail lines but could not impose a settlement. In 1940 Japan sponsored a collaborationist government under Wang Jingwei at Nanjing, which gained little genuine legitimacy. The war in China became the central military commitment of the Japanese empire and consumed an increasing share of its manpower and resources.

For Korea the war meant a new level of involvement. The peninsula supplied troop movements, supplies, and facilities, and formations of the Korea Army’s 20th Division were sent to North China in July 1937; a contemporary U.S. military attaché report estimated that approximately two-thirds of the division had arrived by July 22. The Government-General responded with intensified political control and, within months, with the policies of mobilization examined in Chapters 60 and 62 and in the next section. The 1937 war was a different stage of expansion from the 1931 occupation of Manchuria, and it should not be confused with the Pacific War of 1941, which widened a conflict already four years old.

Section 61.14

The National Mobilization Law of 1938 and Korea's Changing Strategic Role

As the war continued, the Japanese government enlarged its powers to direct the economy. The National General Mobilization Law, promulgated on April 1, 1938, authorized the government to control labor, materials, finance, transport, and information in time of war, largely by imperial ordinance and without the need to pass new laws through the Diet. It was extended to Korea, Taiwan, and Karafuto in May. The law's passage marked a shift in the relationship between state and economy in Japan, and it applied to the colonies as part of the empire's war effort.

In Korea the law provided the legal basis for a series of measures. The Government-General set priorities for the allocation of materials, introduced controls on prices and distribution, and directed investment toward industries deemed essential to war. Government agencies and industrial associations were reorganized to carry out planning, and transport was subordinated to military needs. The slogan of Korea as a logistics base appeared in official statements, and the Governor-General from 1936, General Minami Jirō, emphasized that Korea must contribute to the war effort. The colonial administration, police, and army coordinated more closely than before.

The law's strategic meaning for Korea lay in its subordination of the colonial economy to the needs of a war whose objectives Koreans had no part in setting. Resources such as rice, metals, and labor were increasingly directed toward military use. The regulations applied to Korean and Japanese enterprises alike, though in practice Japanese firms held the dominant positions. The detailed experience of Korean workers, conscripts, and civilians under these measures is the subject of Chapter 62, and the cultural policies that accompanied them are treated in Chapter 60. This section concerns the framework: a legal and administrative structure that integrated Korea more fully into the imperial war economy.

Section 61.15

The Soviet Frontier: Lake Khasan in 1938 and Nomonhan in 1939

Japan's military leadership had long regarded the Soviet Union as a threat and a potential enemy, and the occupation of Manchuria gave Japan a long frontier with Soviet territory, including the Soviet Far East and, through its influence in the region, the frontier of Soviet-allied Mongolia. In the late 1930s the Soviet Union strengthened its forces in the Far East, and border incidents became frequent. Two major clashes occurred, which were geographically distinct and should not be treated as a single episode.

The first was at Lake Khasan, in July and August 1938, near the point where the borders of Korea, Manchukuo, and the Soviet Union meet along the lower Tumen River. The dispute concerned a hill ridge known to the Japanese as Changkufeng, and the two sides disagreed on the exact line of the frontier. Soviet troops occupied the heights, and Japanese forces, drawn from the Korea Army's 19th Division stationed at Nanam, counterattacked. The fighting lasted about two weeks and ended with a ceasefire arranged in Moscow in August. The Soviet forces retained the ground, and casualties were heavy on both sides, though the figures vary by source. The role of the Korea Army in the fighting shows how the peninsula's garrison had become part of the northern defense.

The second clash, at Nomonhan (Khalkhin Gol in Russian), took place from May to September 1939 on the frontier between Manchukuo and Mongolia, far to the west. The Kwantung Army, acting with the same independence it had shown in 1931, committed forces to a dispute over the border, and Soviet and Mongolian forces under Georgy Zhukov responded with armor, aircraft, and artillery in large numbers. The Japanese suffered a serious defeat, with casualties numbering in the tens of thousands on both sides, and a ceasefire was agreed in September. The circumstances of the first skirmishes remain disputed, with each side blaming the other, but the military outcomes are well documented.

The consequences were considerable. The defeats weakened the faction in the army that favored a strike against the Soviet Union and strengthened the argument for expansion southward toward the resources of Southeast Asia, an option strengthened by the Soviet–Japanese Neutrality Pact of April 1941. They also exposed the Kwantung Army's weaknesses in equipment and led to some tightening of central control over field commanders. For Korea, the clashes confirmed the peninsula's role on the northern front and the continuing military importance of its border region, without leading to any change in colonial status.

Section 61.16

The Pacific War of 1941 and Korea within Japan's Greater East Asian Empire

In December 1941 Japan attacked American and British targets across the Pacific and Southeast Asia, beginning the Pacific War; the assault on the Dutch East Indies followed in January 1942. The decision followed the failure to end the war in China, the American embargo on oil and other materials, and the army's and navy's determination to secure resources in Southeast Asia. Within months Japan occupied Malaya, the Philippines, the Dutch East Indies, and Burma. The war thus became a conflict across much of the Asia-Pacific region, and the continental commitments in China and Manchuria remained.

Japanese leaders described the new order as the Greater East Asia Co-Prosperity Sphere, a term first prominently used in 1940, which proclaimed a bloc of Asian peoples liberated from Western colonialism under Japanese leadership. The ideology had little to offer Koreans, who had been Japanese colonial subjects for decades, and who were told to regard themselves as part of the leading nation. Korea's position was therefore different from that of the newly occupied territories. It was a long-established colony, administered under the Government-General, with a more developed industrial base and with an existing apparatus of control. The propaganda of assimilation that accompanied this position is examined in Chapter 60.

Korea's strategic significance to Japan grew. It supplied industrial products such as chemicals, metals, and electric power, large quantities of rice and other agricultural goods, and a network of railways and ports that linked Japan with the continent. It was also a source of labor and, from 1944, of conscripted soldiers, matters treated in Chapter 62. The peninsula's importance to the empire in these years was as a resource base and a link in a chain, and decisions about it were made in Tokyo and by the Government-General, not by Koreans.

Section 61.17

Japan's Overextended Continental Empire, 1942–1945

By 1942 Japan was fighting simultaneously in China, in Southeast Asia, and across the Pacific, while keeping a large army in Manchuria against the possibility of Soviet attack. The commitments exceeded its means. Industrial output could not match that of the United States, and shortages of steel, oil, and other materials constrained production. Strategic planners disagreed about priorities, with the army's attention divided between the continental war and the defense of the home islands and the navy's between the Pacific and the protection of sea lanes.

Shipping became the decisive vulnerability. American submarines and aircraft sank a large proportion of the Japanese merchant fleet from 1943, and by 1944–1945 the routes that carried raw materials from Southeast Asia and goods between Japan and the continent were severely disrupted. In 1945 American aircraft mined the waters around Japan, including the straits between Korea and Japan, and attacked ports, with the result that the sea routes linking the home islands to Korea and Manchuria became hazardous. The continental land route through Korea, which had been a secure artery, was increasingly the sole link on which the empire relied for supplies from the mainland.

The war in China remained unfinished. Japan held major cities and railways but could not suppress Chinese Nationalist and Communist forces, and in 1944 a large offensive in southern China brought territorial gains without ending the war. The Kwantung Army, once the empire's elite force, was progressively stripped of its best units, which were transferred to the Pacific, and replaced with newly raised and poorly equipped formations, so that by 1945 it was far weaker than its numbers suggested. In Korea, the Korea Army was expanded and on February 6, 1945 reorganized into the Seventeenth Area Army, responsible chiefly for operational defense preparations in southern Korea, alongside a separate Korea administrative defense command. Responsibility for some northern defense preparations was placed with the Kwantung Army, creating overlapping command arrangements. The effect on Korea's strategic position was that the peninsula, once a rear base, became a potential front line, and the military presence on it grew heavier while the means to equip it declined. The household-level consequences of this period are the subject of Chapter 62.

Section 61.18

The Soviet Invasion of Manchuria and the Collapse of Japanese Rule, August 1945

At the Yalta Conference in February 1945, Soviet leader Joseph Stalin agreed to enter the war against Japan within a few months of Germany's defeat. In April the Soviet Union announced that it would not renew the 1941 Neutrality Pact. Japanese leaders, meanwhile, hoped that Soviet mediation could secure better terms of peace, a hope that proved illusory. The Allied powers had also addressed Korea's future. The Cairo Declaration of November 1943, issued by the United States, Britain, and China, stated that Korea would become free and independent in due course, a formula that did not specify a timetable or a form of government.

On August 6 and 9, 1945, the United States dropped atomic bombs on Hiroshima and Nagasaki. On August 8 the Soviet Union declared war on Japan, and on August 9 Soviet armies attacked across the frontier of Manchukuo from the north, east, and west in a coordinated offensive under Marshal Aleksandr Vasilevsky. The Kwantung Army, depleted and poorly equipped, could not hold. Soviet forces advanced rapidly, and within days they entered northern Korea, landing at ports such as Unggi, Najin, and Chongjin and moving south by rail. The emperor's broadcast of August 15 announced Japan's acceptance of surrender, and the Kwantung Army ceased organized resistance over the following days, though fighting continued in some places. Puyi abdicated and was captured by Soviet forces, and Manchukuo ceased to exist.

The collapse brought great hardship. Well over a million Japanese civilians in Manchuria faced flight, internment, hunger, and disease, and many died, while Koreans in Manchuria, perhaps around two million, were caught in the same upheaval, some returning to the peninsula and many remaining. Chinese residents, long subjected to Japanese rule, suddenly confronted a different set of armed authorities, and the region was soon contested between Nationalist and Communist forces.

The division of Korea emerged from these circumstances. In mid-August American planners, anticipating that Soviet troops might occupy much of the peninsula before American forces could arrive, proposed in General Order No. 1 that Japanese forces north of the 38th parallel surrender to the Soviet Union and those south to the United States, and the Soviet government accepted. The line was a military arrangement for receiving the Japanese surrender, devised in haste, and not a plan for permanent partition. American forces landed at Incheon in early September. Koreans had no role in these decisions. The consequences of the line for Korean politics, examined from Chapter 64 onward, were not foreseen by those who drew it.

Section 61.19

Korea and the Historical Consequences of Japanese Continental Militarization

The occupation of Manchuria changed Korea's strategic position in the Japanese empire. Before 1931 Korea had been a colony and a flank defense. Afterward it became a base for continental operations, a corridor for supplies, an industrial region integrated with Manchukuo, and a frontier zone of intensive policing. Military priorities increasingly shaped colonial administration, and the governors-general in this period included army generals. The militarization of Korean society, which Chapter 62 describes in human terms, rested on this strategic transformation.

Expansion also changed the lives of civilian communities. Korean settlers in Manchuria, Chinese farmers, and the residents of northern Korea experienced new forms of surveillance, displacement, and opportunity, while Korean resistance lost the sanctuaries it had once enjoyed. In the long run the imperial overextension that followed contributed to Japan's defeat. The wars in China, the border conflicts with the Soviet Union, and the Pacific War exhausted the empire's resources until it could not resist the Allied and Soviet offensives of 1945.

Students should recognize both the limits and the complexity of Korean agency. Korea was subjected to Japanese imperial decision-making and did not direct it. At the same time, Korean officials, businessmen, policemen, soldiers, settlers, and workers took part in the imperial system in varied ways and for varied reasons, while others resisted or withdrew. These distinctions matter for historical understanding and for the memory of the period in both Korean states. The chapters of this part form a sequence: Chapter 58 traces the colonial economy, Chapter 59 resistance, Chapter 60 assimilation, and this chapter the strategic frame that bound them together. Chapter 62 turns to the human experience of total war.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how Japan's occupation of Manchuria and its subsequent wars transformed colonial Korea into a militarized component of a continental empire. Beginning with the Russo-Japanese War, Japanese strategists treated Korea and southern Manchuria as linked territories. Japan's presence in Manchuria before 1931, based on leases, railway rights, and troops, was substantial but different from the occupation that followed.

Korean migration to Manchuria created a population that connected the two territories and became entangled in competing claims. The Wanbaoshan dispute and the anti-Chinese riots in Korea in July 1931 showed how a local conflict, inflammatory reporting, and colonial politics could produce ethnic violence, and how Japanese authorities used such incidents to support a claim to protect Koreans abroad.

The Mukden Incident of September 18, 1931, was staged by officers of the Kwantung Army acting on their own initiative, and the civilian government's attempt to restrain them failed. The Japanese Korea Army, a separate command, crossed the Yalu on September 21 without proper authorization, an act the government accepted after the fact. These events demonstrated the erosion of central control over field commanders.

Japan created Manchukuo in 1932 as a formally independent state under Puyi. Its institutions, ideology of racial harmony, and economic development masked a structure of Japanese domination, with the Kwantung Army holding final authority. Historians disagree about how best to describe it, but not about where power lay. The League of Nations and the Lytton Commission condemned Japan's actions without any means of reversing them, and Japan notified the League of its withdrawal in 1933, which took effect in 1935.

Korea's strategic role grew. Its railways, ports, and bases supported continental operations, and its energy, mining, and heavy industry were integrated with Manchukuo's. The Government-General, the army, and Manchukuo authorities built a system of border security that undermined the sanctuaries of Korean armed resistance, while civilians in the borderland, Korean, Chinese, and Japanese, experienced displacement, surveillance, and unequal treatment in different ways.

The war in China from 1937, the National General Mobilization Law of 1938, and the border conflicts with the Soviet Union at Lake Khasan and Nomonhan marked successive stages of escalation. Nomonhan and Lake Khasan were separate events with different consequences, which helped steer Japan's strategy toward the south. The Pacific War beginning in December 1941 extended commitments the empire could not sustain, and Korea became a supply base and, by 1945, a potential front.

The Soviet invasion of August 1945 destroyed Manchukuo, brought Soviet forces into northern Korea, and, together with the American proposal for surrender zones, produced the occupation line at the 38th parallel. The line was an arrangement for receiving Japan's surrender, not a plan for permanent division. Its later consequences belong to subsequent chapters.

Throughout, Korea was subject to decisions made by others, while Koreans acted within the imperial system in many ways, as officials, businesspeople, settlers, and soldiers, and as resisters. The evidence for these developments is uneven and shaped by the institutions that produced it, which is why the chapter has stressed the comparison of sources. Chapter 62 turns to the human experience of the total war that this strategic transformation made possible.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter shows how the history of Korea in the 1930s and 1940s was bound to events beyond the peninsula. A railway explosion in Manchuria, an unauthorized march across the Yalu, and a war in China altered what Korea was for the empire that ruled it. The chapter's lesson is that colonial subjects can be shaped profoundly by decisions in which they have no voice, and that careful history must keep both the constraint and the variety of responses in view.

It also shows how institutions can hold a formal shape while power lies elsewhere. Manchukuo's flag, ministries, and ideology differed from the reality of military control. Historians, and students, must learn to ask who actually decided, and what evidence shows it.

The chapter's developments shaped what followed. The industrial regions of northern Korea, the transport networks, the experience of Korean communities in Manchuria, and the circumstances of Japan's collapse all fed into the postwar world examined in later chapters. The occupation line at the 38th parallel, drawn in haste in August 1945, was the product of a particular military moment, and it was not foreordained by any earlier plan.

Chapter 62 will turn from strategy to experience, asking what total war meant for Koreans who were drafted, mobilized, uprooted, or compelled to labor. The strategic frame of this chapter is the necessary background, but it is only a frame. The human history lies in the lives it shaped.
