Liberation, August 1945

Korean History: Ancient Kingdoms to a Divided Peninsula

Chapter Opening

In the second week of August 1945, the great military machinery of the Pacific War was reaching its climax in Hiroshima, Nagasaki, and Manchuria. For most Koreans, the same weeks were measured in smaller units: the grain left in the household, the whereabouts of a son or husband sent to a mine or a battlefront, the rumors passed between neighbors, and the question of whether the war would end before the next collection of rice or the next summons for labor. Liberation arrived at the point where these two scales of history met.

By the summer of 1945, Korea had been a Japanese colony for thirty-five years, and for the last several of those years it had been a mobilized rear area of a failing empire. Rice was collected under official quotas, food and goods were rationed, and young men and women had been drawn into factories, mines, construction sites, military service, and military-linked labor inside and outside the peninsula. The colonial police and the Government-General remained in place, and the possibility of a last-ditch Japanese defense of the home islands and the empire was a source of dread as well as hope. Korean households did not experience the end of the war as a diplomatic abstraction. They experienced it as the sudden loosening of an apparatus that had shaped nearly every aspect of daily life.

This chapter examines the weeks around that loosening. It begins with the interlocking military and diplomatic developments that ended Japanese resistance: the Potsdam Declaration, the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki, and the Soviet Union's entry into the war, which began operations in northern Korea on August 9, six days before Japan's surrender announcement. It then follows the events of August 15, when the Emperor's recorded broadcast announced Japan's acceptance of the Allied terms. That date has become the central day of commemoration in both Korean states, although the legal surrender took place later, on September 2, and American forces did not arrive in southern Korea until September 8 and 9.

The chapter's central argument is that August 1945 was simultaneously a moment of national liberation, popular political mobilization, institutional breakdown, and international uncertainty. Koreans did not wait passively for decisions made in Washington, Moscow, or Tokyo. Political prisoners walked out of prisons; crowds gathered in Seoul and provincial towns; Yeo Un-hyeong and other leaders negotiated with colonial officials and organized the Committee for the Preparation of Korean Independence; and, within days, local people's committees began to assume tasks that colonial institutions had performed or abandoned. Yet this self-organization took place inside an Allied military framework that had no mechanism for the immediate transfer of sovereignty to a unified Korean government. No Allied government had recognized a Korean administration. The great overseas leaders of the independence movement, discussed in Chapter 63, had not yet returned. And, within days of the surrender announcement, American planners proposed a line at the 38th parallel to allocate Japanese surrender responsibilities between Soviet and American forces.

The chapter therefore insists on a set of distinctions that later memory tends to compress. Liberation from colonial rule, military occupation, and national division were related but distinct developments. Soviet troops were fighting in northern Korea before August 15; American troops were not in southern Korea until September. The Committee for the Preparation of Korean Independence was not the same organization as the Korean People's Republic proclaimed on September 6. The people's committees varied greatly in membership and purpose, and should not automatically be described as Communist organizations. The 38th parallel was, at first, a military surrender boundary, not the frontier between two Korean states. And August 1945 should not be read through the lens of 1948. The creation of two states was a later outcome of events narrated in Chapters 65 and 66; in August, it was only one of several possible futures.

The evidence for these weeks is unusually rich but uneven. Contemporary newspapers, including the former Government-General daily Maeil Sinbo, preserve a record of speeches and announcements, but they were produced under unstable and partly colonial conditions. The Committee for the Preparation of Korean Independence issued a declaration and program that survive as documents. Allied diplomatic and military records, notably the order assigning Japanese surrender responsibilities, are available in American archives and published diplomatic series, but they reflect the concerns of the occupying powers. Memoirs and oral histories, written years later, preserve vivid experiences while also compressing and reshaping them in light of what happened after. Photographs of celebrations and prisoner releases are among the best-known images of the period, yet many are frequently misdated, and some associated with August 15 depict August 16 or later. Part of the task of this chapter is to teach students to read all of these sources with care.

Within the larger textbook, Chapter 64 marks the transition between Part XI's account of total war and the occupation-era chapters that follow. Chapter 62 treated wartime mobilization and Chapter 63 the independence movements and their rival claims to legitimacy; this chapter treats the experience and immediate consequences of liberation. Chapter 65 will examine how Soviet administration in the north and American military government in the south began to build two political systems, and Chapter 66 will follow the violence, elections, and state-founding of 1948. Chapter 64 stops at the threshold. Its closing question is the one that Koreans themselves faced in the last days of August: Korea had been freed from Japanese rule, but who would govern a liberated Korea?

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why Japan's surrender resulted from interacting military and diplomatic pressures, including the Potsdam Declaration, the atomic bombings, and Soviet entry into the war, rather than from a single uncontested cause.
  2. Distinguish the four dates August 9, August 15, September 2, and September 8–9, 1945, and explain what each marks in the history of liberation and occupation.
  3. Describe the condition of Korean society on the eve of liberation, and explain why liberation did not equal the immediate establishment of an independent government.
  4. Analyze the negotiations between Yeo Un-hyeong and Japanese colonial officials, distinguishing what the participants claimed from what was implemented.
  5. Trace the formation, composition, and program of the Committee for the Preparation of Korean Independence, and distinguish it from the Korean People's Republic.
  6. Assess the emergence and variety of local people's committees, and explain why they cannot be treated as uniform or automatically Communist organizations.
  7. Evaluate social expectations and tensions at liberation, including questions of land, labor, gender, collaboration, and justice.
  8. Trace Soviet military operations in northern Korea and explain why Soviet forces fought there before Japan's August 15 announcement.
  9. Explain the origin and meaning of the 38th parallel as a military surrender line, and distinguish it from the later political division.
  10. Compare the situations of northern and southern Korea between the surrender announcement and the arrival of American forces.
  11. Interpret primary and contemporary sources for August 1945, including an imperial rescript, a speech, a political declaration, and a military order, with attention to authorship, purpose, and limitation.
  12. Contextualize the return of Koreans from the former Japanese Empire and the staggered return of overseas independence leaders within the unfinished process of liberation.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did the end of Japanese colonial rule not produce an immediate, internationally recognized Korean government?
  2. How should historians weigh the atomic bombings, Soviet entry, and internal Japanese political calculation in explaining Japan's decision to surrender?
  3. What did Yeo Un-hyeong and the Japanese officials each want from their negotiations, and how can historians tell what was actually agreed and implemented?
  4. Did Korean organizations receive authority from the colonial administration, or did they create their own authority during its collapse?
  5. Why did people's committees spread so quickly, and why do they resist simple political labels?
  6. How did liberation differ for former prisoners, workers, farmers, women, colonial employees, and people accused of collaboration?
  7. Why did Soviet forces enter Korea before the surrender announcement, while American forces arrived weeks afterward?
  8. Why is the 38th parallel best understood in August 1945 as a military arrangement, and why did it nonetheless have far-reaching political consequences?
  9. What does the staggered return of Koreans and overseas leaders reveal about the difference between liberation and the achievement of a settled political order?

Historical Context

Students approaching Chapter 64 should recall the structure of Japanese rule established after the annexation of 1910 (Chapter 55). The Government-General of Korea, headed by a Governor-General appointed from among Japan's senior military and political figures, governed the peninsula through a large bureaucracy and a heavily armed police system. Koreans staffed much of the lower and middle administration, including many police positions, while the highest offices remained in Japanese hands. After the March First Movement of 1919 (Chapter 56), colonial policy shifted toward limited cultural concessions and then, during the 1930s and the Pacific War, toward intensified assimilation, economic mobilization, and coercion (Chapters 60–62).

The war years placed extraordinary burdens on Korean society. Chapter 62 treated the mobilization of labor and soldiers, the exploitation of agriculture and industry, the system of sexual slavery, and the pressures of cultural assimilation. By 1945, rice requisition, rationing, and shortages of fuel and goods were part of daily life, while many Koreans were working or fighting far from home. Inside Korea, independence organizing was fragmentary and dangerous. Chapter 63 examined the larger and better-organized independence movements outside the peninsula: the Provisional Government in China, Communist and nationalist guerrilla networks in Manchuria and the Soviet Far East, and Korean activists in the United States. These overseas organizations had competing claims to represent the nation, but none of them governed Korean territory in 1945.

The international setting was equally important. The Cairo Declaration of 1943 had stated that Korea would become free and independent "in due course," a phrase whose indeterminacy would matter greatly. The Allied powers had not agreed on how or when sovereignty would pass to Koreans, and the military planners who prepared the final campaigns against Japan were primarily concerned with defeating Japanese forces, not with building Korean institutions. The Soviet Union's declaration of war on Japan in August 1945 added a second major power with troops on the Asian mainland and an interest in Korea's northern border region.

Geography shaped these developments. The peninsula's northern industrial and mining districts, its hydroelectric facilities and ports, and its southern agricultural plains were linked by railways and administrative networks that ran across what would soon become a military dividing line. The northeast, near the Soviet frontier, was exposed to the earliest Soviet operations; Seoul, the colonial capital, was the seat of the Government-General and the headquarters of the Japanese forces in the south. Chapter 64 follows these geographic differences without treating them as destiny. The peninsula's economic regions were different, but they had been integrated for decades within a single colonial system.

Finally, students should keep in mind a methodological caution. The Korean states established in 1948 each later developed official narratives of liberation that emphasized different heroes and different stages of the story. This chapter treats those later narratives as part of the historiography of liberation, not as a substitute for evidence about August 1945.

Section 64.1

Korea on the Eve of Liberation, Summer 1945

To understand what liberation meant in August 1945, it is necessary to picture the society that received it. Chapter 62 examined wartime mobilization in detail; this section summarizes only the conditions that bore most directly on the first days of freedom. By the summer of 1945, Korean society had been under war conditions for years. The colonial state's demands on agriculture, labor, and daily consumption had grown more intrusive as Japan's military position deteriorated, and the rural majority of the population felt these pressures most acutely through the collection of grain.

Food was the most widespread concern. Under the wartime system, farm households were required to deliver quotas of rice and other grain to the state at fixed prices, and urban populations depended on rationing that became thinner and more reliant on substitute foods as the war continued. Shortages of fertilizer, fuel, and labor reduced agricultural output, and the unevenness of rationing, the black market, and wartime inflation made the difference between households with access to food and those without a matter of daily anxiety. Poor harvest conditions in the final war years are often cited, though the severity varied by region and the statistics of a wartime colonial state must be used with caution. What is clear from the testimony of contemporaries and later recollection is that hunger and the fear of hunger were pervasive background conditions of liberation.

Labor mobilization had reached into nearly every community. Men had been drafted into the Japanese military and into labor service in Korea, Japan, Manchuria, and other parts of the empire, while women and students had been assigned to factory, farm, and service work. Many families did not know whether absent sons and husbands were alive, and families of those sent overseas could not know when, or whether, they would return. Within Korea, industrial production, especially in the northern mining and heavy-industry districts and in port and rail facilities, had been pushed to serve military needs. American submarine and air operations in the last months of the war, including mine-laying that disrupted coastal and cross-strait shipping, contributed to the sense that the empire was being cut off from its sources of supply.

Japanese authorities in Korea were also preparing for the possibility of an invasion of the home islands and for defense of the peninsula itself. Large numbers of Japanese troops were stationed in Korea, and the colonial government established local civil defense and volunteer corps that drew Koreans into last-ditch preparations. Estimates of the size of Japanese forces on the peninsula at the war's end vary, but they amounted to several hundred thousand personnel, in addition to a large Japanese civilian population. This meant that when the surrender came, the peninsula contained not only a Korean population hungry for change but also a heavily armed colonial establishment whose members were uncertain of their own futures.

Surveillance and repression were part of the same picture. The colonial police, including the thought police and special higher police, maintained extensive monitoring of political and religious life, and prisons held independence activists, Communists, religious dissenters, and others convicted or detained under security laws. Preventive detention provisions allowed the authorities to hold some political offenders beyond the end of their sentences. Under these conditions, organized independence activity inside Korea was fragmentary and dangerous. Yeo Un-hyeong's underground Alliance for National Foundation, established in 1944, and scattered Communist and nationalist networks, student circles, and religious groups existed, but they were small, often isolated from each other, and unable to challenge the police state openly. Larger and better-organized movements were located abroad, as Chapter 63 explained.

Public uncertainty is perhaps the best summary of the mood on the eve of liberation. Many Koreans suspected from rumor, reduced Japanese confidence, and the audible signs of war that Japan was losing, but the timing and manner of its end were unknown to nearly everyone. Foreign broadcasts were officially forbidden, and information circulated unevenly by word of mouth. Some Koreans feared a final Japanese stand, others feared reprisals against Koreans if Japanese power collapsed violently, and still others, particularly those with ties to the colonial administration, feared accountability. This uncertainty would shape the first days of freedom: many Koreans hesitated to believe the news, and the colonial authorities, who knew of Japan's decision slightly earlier than the public, had to decide how to protect themselves and their civilians in a society that had long been governed by coercion.

Section 64.2

The Final Crisis of Japan's Empire, August 1945

Japan's surrender was not the result of a single event. It emerged from the interaction of military defeat, diplomatic pressure, and deep divisions within the Japanese leadership over whether and on what terms to end the war. For Korea, the sequence matters because it determined when the colonial order lost its military support and which armies would be on the ground when it did.

On July 26, 1945, the United States, Britain, and China issued the Potsdam Declaration, demanding the unconditional surrender of Japan's armed forces and warning of destruction if Japan continued resistance. That demand was not identical to an express requirement that every Japanese civil institution be abolished. The declaration reaffirmed that the terms of the Cairo Declaration would be carried out, and therefore, by implication, that Korea would be separated from Japan. It did not mention Korea by name in the operative demands for surrender, and it did not describe any process of Korean self-government. The Japanese government's initial public reaction, delivered by the prime minister, used a word that has been debated ever since; it was interpreted by Allied governments as a rejection. Whether it was intended as a rejection or as a holding response while Japanese leaders hoped for Soviet mediation remains a topic of historical discussion, but the practical result was that the war continued.

On August 6, an American aircraft dropped an atomic bomb on Hiroshima. Hiroshima held a substantial population of Koreans, among them forced laborers, conscripted workers, and long-term residents of the Japanese empire, and a significant number of Koreans were among the dead and injured. Estimates of Korean casualties in Hiroshima and Nagasaki vary and have been the subject of dispute and later activism, and Korean survivors faced particular difficulties of recognition and medical care after the war. This is an important reminder that liberation, for some Koreans, was inseparable from catastrophe. On August 8, the Soviet Union, which had remained neutral in the Pacific War under a 1941 pact with Japan, declared war, effective in Manchuria and Korea on August 9. On that day Soviet forces began a large offensive against Japanese forces in Manchuria, and Soviet air and naval units struck targets in northeastern Korea. A second atomic bomb was dropped on Nagasaki the same day.

The Japanese leadership then entered a crisis of decision. In the early hours of August 10, at an imperial conference, the Emperor intervened in the deadlock between those who wished to accept the Allied terms with the condition that the imperial institution be preserved and those who wished to continue fighting or demand further guarantees. Japan communicated a conditional acceptance through neutral governments. The American reply, which described the Emperor's authority as subject to the Supreme Commander of the Allied Powers, did not resolve the deadlock, and a further imperial decision on August 14 settled the matter. The Emperor's statement was recorded that night, and a group of officers attempted unsuccessfully to prevent its broadcast.

Historians continue to debate the relative weight of the atomic bombings and Soviet entry in this decision. Some emphasize the shock of the bombings; others emphasize that Soviet entry destroyed the hope of Soviet mediation and ended the strategic plan of a decisive defensive battle on the home islands and the continent; and many stress that both pressures operated within an already desperate military situation marked by blockade, firebombing, and exhaustion, and within a leadership divided over the preservation of the imperial system. This textbook does not attempt to resolve that debate. Its point for Korean history is that Japan's defeat arrived through the combined action of several forces, and that one of them, Soviet military operations, placed a foreign army on Korean soil before Japan had announced its surrender.

The Soviet offensive in northern Korea followed from the larger Manchurian campaign. Soviet forces attacked the northeastern ports and rail links from August 9 and, as Section 64.13 describes, fought Japanese units in the northeast for several days, in some places beyond the surrender announcement. The war's end in Korea thus did not look the same everywhere. In Seoul and much of the south, the war ended without combat on the ground; in the northeast, it ended in bombardment, landings, and fighting. The colonial regime lost its capacity to resist at different speeds in different places, and Korean households experienced the end of Japanese rule through very different circumstances depending on where they lived.

Section 64.3

The Allied Powers and the Problem of Korea's Future

When Japanese power collapsed, no mechanism existed to transfer authority in Korea to a Korean government. That fact, more than any later development, defines the political problem of August 1945. Understanding it requires looking at what the Allied powers had said about Korea and what they had not decided.

The Cairo Declaration, issued by the United States, Britain, and China after the conference of late November 1943 and published on December 1, stated that the three powers, mindful of the enslavement of the Korean people, were determined that Korea would become free and independent "in due course." For Korean nationalists, this was the first formal Allied commitment to independence, and its text became a central reference point in later Korean politics. Yet the phrase contained a deliberate ambiguity. It set no date, and the Allies had not agreed on whether independence would follow immediately upon Japan's defeat or after a period of external supervision. Within Korea, the declaration's content was known only through rumor and censored reports, and many Koreans in August 1945 expected independence to follow immediately upon Japanese surrender.

American planners had been thinking along different lines. President Roosevelt had discussed with Soviet leaders the idea of a multilateral trusteeship for Korea, a period of international tutelage that he envisaged as lasting a generation or less, and American officials generally assumed that Koreans, deprived of self-government for decades, would need a transitional arrangement. The discussions were informal, no agreement on details was reached, and the Korean public had no part in them. When the war ended suddenly, the concept of trusteeship existed as a loose assumption and a source of later controversy (examined in Chapter 65), not as an operational plan. In August, trusteeship remained a possibility rather than a policy, which is exactly why it created uncertainty.

The Allied governments also had no recognized Korean government with which to deal. The Provisional Government, based in China, had claimed legitimacy since 1919 and had Chinese support in various forms, but neither the United States nor the Soviet Union had recognized it, and its attempts to obtain Allied acknowledgment, including efforts by its representatives to participate in the international conference in San Francisco earlier in 1945, had been unsuccessful. Other organizations, including Communist and nationalist groups with ties to the Soviet Union, China, and the United States, held competing claims, as Chapter 63 explained. This fragmentation gave the Allies a practical argument, and in some cases a convenient excuse, for treating Korea as territory to be occupied rather than as a state to be handed over.

Military objectives were the immediate drivers of Allied planning. American forces had been preparing for an invasion of Japan, and the occupation of Korea had not been a central priority until Soviet entry into the war made the peninsula's future more urgent. Soviet planners, for their part, had a strategic interest in Korea's northern frontier and ports. At the Potsdam conference, American and Soviet military staffs discussed coordination of operations, including air and naval boundaries, but no occupation zones for Korea were agreed. The result, as Section 64.16 shows, was that the allocation of surrender responsibilities was decided in haste in the days after Soviet forces were already fighting in Korea.

The effect of these conditions was an absence of any agreed mechanism for the transfer of sovereignty. Japan would surrender to the Allied powers; Allied commanders would accept the surrender of Japanese forces in the areas assigned to them; and Korea's political future would be settled later by the Allied powers. Korean organizations, including those that formed in the first days of freedom, were not parties to any of these arrangements. This did not mean that Korean political action was irrelevant: as the following sections show, Koreans created facts on the ground that Allied commanders could not ignore. But it did mean that liberation unfolded without a clear legal route from colonial rule to Korean statehood, a gap that every Korean political group would try to fill in its own way.

Section 64.4

August 15, 1945: Japan's Surrender and Korea's Liberation

On the morning of August 15, Japanese radio announced that an important broadcast would be made at noon. Japan and Korea shared the same standard time, and at noon the Emperor's recorded voice was transmitted across the empire. The speech, delivered in a formal and archaic register that was difficult even for many Japanese listeners to follow, announced that the Japanese government had accepted the terms of the Allied declaration. It did not use the word "surrender," and its explanation of the war's end referred to the changing course of the war and the use of a new and terrible weapon, while casting Japan's wartime aims in terms of self-preservation and the stability of East Asia.

How and when the news reached Koreans varied greatly. Radio ownership in Korea was unevenly distributed, and many of the sets were in Japanese households, government offices, and the homes of well-off Koreans. A person in a rural village might have learned of the broadcast from a neighbor who had been in town, from a police or village official, or from rumor that followed in the hours and days after the announcement, and some villages learned of Japan's acceptance of the surrender terms only days later. In Seoul and other cities, many Koreans heard the news from radios or from others who had; in the northeast, where fighting continued, the circumstances were different again. Many people at first doubted what they heard, a doubt rooted in the long habit of treating official announcements with suspicion and in the possibility that the news was a prelude to some new demand.

The colonial administration, for its part, was not taken entirely by surprise. Senior officials in Seoul had received indications of Japan's likely surrender in the preceding days, and Government-General officials, including Governor-General Abe Nobuyuki and Administrative Vice-Governor-General Endō Ryūsaku, had already begun to consider how to protect Japanese lives, maintain order, and manage the transition. Their fears were concrete. Several hundred thousand Japanese civilians lived in Korea, many in cities and colonial settlements, and Japanese officials feared that decades of resentment might erupt into violence. They also feared the consequences of a sudden collapse of the police and food-distribution systems. Some documents were reportedly destroyed in these days, and there were also signs of rapid financial and property movements by officials and civilians preparing for the future, though the details of these practices are still being reconstructed by historians.

On August 15, authority was weakened but not extinguished. Japanese military and police units remained armed and in place, and the Government-General continued to exist as the formal governing body, awaiting the arrival of Allied forces and instructions from Tokyo. The Allied powers had not yet received the formal surrender, which would be signed on September 2 and whose terms would be executed over the following weeks by army commanders on the ground. In law, therefore, Japan still held Korea on the evening of August 15. In practice, the confidence and coherence of the colonial system were gone, and in the days that followed, Koreans acted accordingly.

The reactions of Koreans were not uniform. Accounts and memoirs describe tears, shouting, singing, the sudden appearance of hidden flags, and gatherings in public spaces, but also caution, silence, and hesitation. People who had worked in colonial institutions, particularly the police and the administration, were fearful. Households with absent family members felt hope and anxiety in equal measure. Some of the most vivid descriptions of celebration come from August 16 and the days afterward, not from the day of the broadcast itself, a fact consistent with the delayed spread of the news and with the care taken by many people to confirm it. This chapter treats August 15 as the central date of liberation without insisting that all of its famous images belong to that single day.

Why did August 15 become the central commemorative date? The reason lies partly in the symbolism of the Emperor's broadcast, which gave the day a clear and unmistakable public meaning, and partly in what later political actors made of it. In the south, the day became known as Gwangbok ("restoration of light"), and later, in 1948, the Republic of Korea's formal establishment was proclaimed on the same date, linking the two commemorations. In the north, the day is called haebang ("liberation"), a term that emphasizes freedom from foreign rule and has its own place in official historical narrative. The date thus carries different political overtones in the two Koreas while commemorating the same moment. Historians should treat it both as the day when Japan announced its surrender and as the symbolic starting point of a process that unfolded over several weeks and in some respects over several years.

Historical Vocabulary
Gwangbok (光復) and Haebang (解放)

Definition: Gwangbok means, literally, the "restoration of light," with the sense of recovering what was lost. Haebang means "liberation" or "release." Both are commonly used in Korean for the end of Japanese rule in 1945.

Historical context: The language of restoration had earlier uses in the independence movement, including in the name of the Provisional Government's Restoration Army, and expresses the idea that Korea was recovering sovereignty that Japan had taken away. In South Korea, August 15 is observed as Gwangbokjeol; in North Korea, the same date is commemorated as liberation day. Neither term is a neutral label for the political events of August 1945, because each implies an interpretation of what was recovered and by whom.

Why it matters: The choice of vocabulary reflects later historical memory. "Restoration" emphasizes continuity with a pre-colonial national state and the independence movement; "liberation" can emphasize the role of external forces, revolutionary struggle, or both, depending on the speaker.

Potential misunderstanding: Neither term means that independent statehood was achieved on August 15. Liberation from colonial rule and the founding of independent states were separated by three years of occupation and political struggle.

Section 64.5

Yeo Un-hyeong, Japanese Officials, and the Negotiations over Public Order

Of the Korean figures who acted in the first hours of liberation, none was more consequential than Yeo Un-hyeong (also romanized Lyuh Woon-hyung), and none has been more debated. Born in 1886 into a Christian-influenced yangban-background family in Gyeonggi Province, Yeo had been involved in independence politics since the 1910s, including in the Korean community in Shanghai, and had been imprisoned by the colonial authorities. In the 1930s he worked as a journalist and newspaper executive in Seoul. In August 1944, under conditions of severe police surveillance, he helped establish a clandestine Alliance for National Foundation (Geonguk Dongmaeng), a small network intended to prepare for the possibility of Japan's defeat. The organization was modest in size, but it gave Yeo and his associates a framework for rapid action when the moment came.

The sequence of contacts around August 14–16 is reconstructed from participants' later accounts and, more precisely, from the contemporary newspaper report of Yeo's public speech. In that August 16 speech, Yeo said he had been summoned by Administrative Vice-Governor-General Endō Ryūsaku at 8 a.m. on August 15. The specific hour belongs to Yeo's reported account, not to a surviving verbatim transcript of their private conversation. Their general agreement is widely accepted, though the preliminary approaches and the subsequent scope of authority differ among sources. In the days before the broadcast, Japanese officials in Seoul, anticipating the collapse of their authority, sought Korean intermediaries who could help keep order. Accounts indicate that Endō Ryūsaku approached several moderate Korean figures, and sources differ over the sequence of these contacts and over who was approached first. Endō was reportedly concerned about violence against Japanese civilians and about the breakdown of food distribution and police authority, and he wanted a Korean figure with enough public standing to restrain unrest. Yeo agreed to talk, but on conditions.

The five conditions require careful attention because they show what Yeo thought most urgent. First came the release of people held for political and economic offenses. Second came delivery of sufficient food for the three months from August through October, an immediate concern in a city and countryside exhausted by wartime collection. Third was noninterference with Korean maintenance of order and nation-building work. Fourth was freedom to train and organize students and young people. Fifth was noninterference with the participation of workers in reconstruction. The surviving Maeil Sinbo report summarized Yeo's own presentation of those demands; it does not prove that every local Japanese commander obeyed them. Their importance lies in the attempt to convert public safety negotiations into space for Korean political organization. Documentary reference.

Accounts of the conditions, as they are commonly reported, include the immediate release of political prisoners throughout Korea, provision of food supplies for the Korean population for August, September, and October, noninterference with Korean efforts to maintain public order, noninterference with the organization of students and youth, and noninterference with the organization of workers and peasants. The wording and the exact number of conditions vary slightly among sources, and students should treat the list as a reconstruction from sources of different dates, not as a verified text of a signed agreement. No signed treaty or comparable written instrument is generally cited as the agreement's basis, and the arrangement appears to have been an understanding reached verbally under extraordinary pressure.

It is important to distinguish what the participants claimed from what was implemented. Yeo and his associates later presented the conditions as a set of demands that Japanese officials accepted, and they used this acceptance to justify the Committee for the Preparation of Korean Independence's activities. Japanese-side recollections, including those associated with colonial officials, tend to describe the request as narrower, a matter of preserving order and protecting Japanese lives and property until the Allies arrived, not as a transfer of governing authority. The record of implementation is also mixed. Political prisoners were released in Seoul on August 16 and in other places over the following days, a concrete outcome of the agreement or at least consistent with it. Food guarantees and noninterference were less reliably honored: Japanese military and police authorities, who did not all share the civilian officials' views, resumed restrictions on Korean organizing within days, and the Government-General's posture hardened once it received indications of Allied intentions. The negotiation therefore produced real effects, especially in its first days, but cannot be read as a clean or durable delegation of power.

Yeo's decision to negotiate with colonial officials was controversial then and remains so. For critics, dealing with the Government-General risked conferring legitimacy on a regime that had no right to bargain over Korea's future, and they questioned whether Korean leaders should have accepted any Japanese request. For supporters, the talks were a pragmatic way to reduce bloodshed and secure the release of prisoners at a moment when Korean leaders had no army and the colonial state still had guns. Yeo, whose political outlook combined nationalism with a broad leftist and reformist orientation, argued that unity and discipline were essential. Both views can be held in the light of the evidence: the negotiations were neither a betrayal nor a transfer of sovereignty, but an improvised arrangement whose consequences depended on how each side used it.

Historical Debate

Did Japanese Authorities Transfer Meaningful Authority, or Did Koreans Create Their Own?

The question: Historians ask whether the understanding reached between Yeo Un-hyeong and Japanese colonial officials amounted to a meaningful transfer of administrative authority to Korean leaders, or whether Korean organizations established their own authority in the vacuum created by the colonial administration's collapse.

Interpretation A, delegated authority: Some accounts emphasize that Japanese officials explicitly asked Yeo to maintain order and accepted conditions about prisoners, food, and organizing, giving the CPKI a degree of recognition that helped it begin work and persuaded the police and local officials to cooperate with it in the first days.
Interpretation B, self-created authority: Others argue that the colonial government had no authority left to transfer, since sovereignty over Korea would be settled by the Allied powers, and that the CPKI and local committees drew their legitimacy from popular support, the release of prisoners, and the willingness of Korean communities to follow them, not from Japanese permission.
Interpretation C, a limited and unstable arrangement: A third view treats the understanding as a narrow security arrangement useful to both sides: Japanese officials sought safety for their civilians, and Yeo and his colleagues secured immediate gains. On this view, authority was neither transferred nor entirely self-created but contested and soon curtailed when Japanese military and police leaders reasserted control.

Evidence: The evidence includes Yeo's contemporary public statements, later memoirs by Korean participants, Japanese officials' recollections, contemporary reports of prisoner releases, and records of how Japanese police and military authorities behaved over the following days.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns interpretation and terminology: what counts as a "transfer" of authority, and how much weight to give to Japanese agreement as opposed to Korean mobilization. There is also uncertainty about evidence, because the main accounts were written by interested parties after the fact.

Why it matters: The question bears on how the CPKI's legitimacy is understood. If it depended on Japanese delegation, critics could charge it with compromised origins; if it rested on popular mobilization, it could claim to represent the Korean people. It also shapes how historians describe Korean agency in a moment often told as the story of foreign decisions.

Section 64.6

Political Prisoners, Prisons, Police Stations, and the End of Colonial Repression

Few images of liberation are more enduring than that of political prisoners emerging from prison gates into crowds of well-wishers. The release of prisoners was both a humane event and a political one. It returned to public life people who had spent years or decades in confinement for resisting colonial rule, and it demonstrated, more concretely than any speech, that the machinery of colonial repression was no longer functioning as it had.

Seodaemun Prison in Seoul, which the colonial authorities had expanded and which had held numerous independence activists, became the best-known site of release. On August 16, political prisoners were freed in Seoul, and crowds gathered outside to welcome them. Similar releases occurred in other cities over the following days. The numbers of political prisoners released are difficult to establish with precision. Colonial prisons held people convicted under security and thought-control laws alongside those convicted of ordinary crimes, and records from the final days of the regime were incomplete and in some cases destroyed. Estimates for the whole peninsula run to several thousand political detainees, and any figure should be treated as approximate.

The released prisoners included Communists, nationalists, Christians and other religious dissenters, labor activists, and members of cultural organizations. Among them were people detained in connection with the Korean Language Society case of 1942, a prosecution of scholars working on a Korean dictionary and orthography; accounts indicate that the surviving detainees held in Hamhung were released around August 17, though two of those arrested had died in custody. Christians imprisoned for refusing to participate in Shinto shrine worship were also reported to have been freed in places such as Pyongyang. These releases carried symbolic weight, because they returned to public view people whose resistance had been defined by language, faith, and conscience as well as by political organization.

Families played an important role in these days. Relatives who had not known whether imprisoned family members were alive went to prisons and police stations to look for them. Some found them; others learned that they had died in custody or been transferred to unknown locations. The sudden appearance of former prisoners in streets and villages also affected local politics. Many were treated as heroes and quickly drawn into organizing committees, while others, weakened by long detention, needed care and rest. The political return of Communists who had been in prison or in hiding contributed to the rapid reorganization of the left in Seoul and other cities, a development that would shape the committees discussed in later sections.

The weakening of the colonial police posed a different kind of problem. The police system had been the day-to-day instrument of colonial control, and Koreans served in it in large numbers, particularly in lower ranks. When the surrender was announced, some police officers remained at their posts, some left or hid, and some tried to maintain order or adapt to the new situation. In places, crowds confronted police stations and officers, and Koreans who had served the colonial regime feared reprisals. The Japanese authorities, in turn, wanted to prevent disorder but also did not wish to disarm or dissolve their own police. As a result, the first days of freedom featured a patchwork: in some districts Korean committees or youth groups took over patrolling; in others, existing police continued under the old structure with greater or lesser local cooperation; in still others, the police simply disappeared from public view.

The problem of replacing coercive institutions without creating disorder was acute and unresolved. A society cannot function without some means of settling disputes, protecting property, and keeping roads and markets safe, and the Korean leaders who emerged in August had to improvise. The Committee for the Preparation of Korean Independence, discussed in Section 64.8, created security corps to fill part of the gap. But the legitimacy of these bodies, their relationship to the surviving police, and the question of how to treat former police officers would remain contested, a theme that returns in Section 64.12 and in Chapter 65.

Section 64.7

Celebration, National Flags, Speeches, and the Public Reclamation of Korea

The days after the broadcast saw the first mass public expression of Korean national feeling that had been possible in decades. In Seoul, crowds gathered in streets and open spaces on August 16, and in other cities and towns, similar gatherings followed as news spread. People brought out flags, sang, spoke, and walked through public places that had long been associated with colonial authority. The scenes were widely described in memoirs and photographed, and they have become part of the national memory of both Korean states.

The reappearance of the Taegeukgi, the Korean flag, was one of the most visible signs. Under colonial rule, display of the Korean flag had been forbidden, and the Japanese flag had been required at official occasions. In August 1945, people produced Taegeukgi from hiding places, made them quickly from available cloth and paint, or modified Japanese flags. The flag's design was not entirely standardized, and variants were common, a reminder that it had been used mainly in exile and clandestine contexts for decades. The flag's presence on buildings, in processions, and in the hands of crowds gave public expression to the feeling that Korea was again visible as a nation.

Speeches and demonstrations offered political content as well as emotion. Yeo Un-hyeong's address, discussed in Section 64.5, was one among many. Local figures, former prisoners, and organizers spoke in towns and villages, and some of these gatherings were the first steps toward the committees described later. Cultural expression also surfaced: songs, including a patriotic anthem sung to a borrowed melody before an official national anthem was adopted, were performed in public, and Korean-language speech and writing, so long constrained in official life, took a more open place in signage, announcements, and everyday address. Schools were in a transitional state. Many students had been mobilized for labor service rather than ordinary classes, and the reorganization of schooling, including the teaching of the Korean language, began in the weeks that followed (Section 64.10).

The mood in the streets was not uniform. Celebration coexisted with grief for those who had died or had not returned, with anxiety about food and work, and with fear among those who had been associated with the colonial regime. Reports also describe attacks on symbols of Japanese authority, including Shinto shrines, some of which were burned or desecrated, while Japanese authorities held ceremonies to close others. Violence against Japanese civilians appears to have been limited in the first days in Seoul compared with what many had feared, although local incidents did occur and fear persisted. Historians should be cautious about generalizing from a few well-known accounts.

Regional differences were substantial. In Seoul and other large cities, news spread quickly and crowds assembled on the 16th. In rural districts, the timing varied: some villages learned within a day, others later, and the form of response depended on local leaders, the presence of police or Japanese officials, and the community's own organization. In the northeast, where Soviet forces were fighting at the ports, the transition was entangled with combat and with the arrival of a foreign army (Section 64.13). On the southern coast and on islands such as Jeju, communities with strong maritime and migration ties to Japan had their own experiences of the news and of the sudden uncertainty about family members abroad. This variety is a reminder that "the first day of liberation" was not a single event but a set of local experiences spread across a peninsula with uneven communications.

The public reclamation of Korea involved more than celebration. It was the first act of political life after colonial rule: the reappropriation of streets, symbols, and languages that had been controlled by an alien state. In this sense, the crowds of August 16 were already engaged in the same project as the committees that formed alongside them: reasserting Korean ownership of public space. What was undecided was who would organize that project and under what authority.

Section 64.8

The Committee for the Preparation of Korean Independence

The Committee for the Preparation of Korean Independence (Joseon Geonguk Junbi Wiwonhoe, abbreviated here as CPKI) began to take shape in Seoul on the afternoon of August 15, as Yeo and his associates converted their existing networks into a public organization. A declaration of its presence and aims followed on August 16, and historians have identified August 17 as the point by which its leadership, with Yeo as chairman and Ahn Jae-hong as vice chairman, was formally organized. These dates describe successive stages of formation rather than three mutually exclusive founding claims. Its chairman was Yeo Un-hyeong, and its vice chairman was Ahn Jae-hong, a veteran nationalist and journalist whose background was more moderate and conservative than Yeo's. The committee drew on Yeo's Alliance for National Foundation and on a wide circle of nationalists, socialists, Communists, and others who were in Seoul or able to reach it in these days.

The CPKI's early organization reflected its dual purpose: to maintain order in the immediate term and to prepare for self-government. It created departments for matters such as general affairs, organization, propaganda, finance, and food supply, and it set up security and youth organizations to patrol the streets and protect property. Reports of the size and effectiveness of these security forces vary, and the committee's actual capacity to supply food or enforce order was limited. Nevertheless, in the first days, the CPKI served as a visible Korean authority in Seoul, one whose announcements were read, whose orders were followed in places, and whose organization provided a model for local groups elsewhere.

Establishing the stages of the committee's creation matters for more than dating a founding ceremony. An informal planning circle that met after Yeo's negotiations could act quickly through preexisting personal connections, but it could not instantly operate as a nationwide organization. The public announcements of August 16 invited others to treat the committee as an actual center of authority. The identification of Yeo and Ahn in formal leadership positions by August 17 made possible more regular departmental work and cooperation with local bodies. This gradual transition from network to committee also explains why contemporaries might give different answers when asked when the organization began. A report dated to the day of the announcement and a later institutional history are describing different moments in a single process.

Its program also carried an unresolved tension between provisional administration and political transformation. Maintaining food and order could be defended as a service to everyone, but asking what kind of democratic government should replace colonial rule required judgments about class, collaboration, representation, and the rights of workers and farmers. The declaration's appeal to progressive democratic groups should be read as an effort at coalition-building with boundaries: it expressed opposition to groups associated with imperialism and antidemocratic repression. It therefore cannot be summarized as a politically neutral statement that all Koreans agreed upon. More conservative nationalists could favor independence and public safety while opposing the committee's wider political implications. These disagreements arose while the government being imagined had neither recognized sovereignty nor effective control over the whole peninsula.

The committee's composition was broad but not all-inclusive. Moderate nationalists and leftists both took part, and its leaders sought to present it as a united front. But several prominent conservative nationalists, including figures who would soon form the Korean Democratic Party, declined to join or stayed aloof. Their reasons are debated, but accounts cite suspicion of the committee's links with the Japanese authorities, distrust of its leftist elements, and a preference to wait for the Provisional Government and for Allied direction. Meanwhile, Communists who emerged from prison or hiding, among them Pak Hon-yong, who resurfaced in Seoul in August, began to reorganize the left and to seek influence within the committee and its local branches. The CPKI thus became a site of competition among groups, not a settled coalition.

The relationship between the CPKI and the Japanese authorities remained ambiguous. In the first days, Japanese officials tolerated and in some respects cooperated with the committee, in keeping with the understanding reached with Yeo. As described in Section 64.5, Japanese military and police authorities took a harder line within about a week, restricting the committee's activities as Allied intentions became clearer and as the Japanese military asserted its responsibility for order until the Allies arrived. The CPKI's strength therefore lay less in any formal power than in its ability to attract popular support and to coordinate local action.

On August 28, the CPKI circulated a declaration and program (some historical accounts discuss a related announcement on August 26; the surviving declaration reproduced by the National Institute of Korean History is dated August 28). These documents are among the best primary evidence for the committee's aims, and are reproduced and discussed by the National Institute of Korean History. Their general content, as summarized in the source window below, stressed the goal of an independent Korean state, the construction of democratic government, the maintenance of order in the transitional period, and the protection of ordinary people's livelihoods.

It is crucial not to confuse the CPKI with the Korean People's Republic, proclaimed in Seoul on September 6. The People's Republic was announced at a national assembly convened by the CPKI leadership just before the American landing, and it claimed to be a provisional government. It was a different organization, with a different structure and a more openly governmental claim, and its relationship to the CPKI, to the American occupation, and to Korean political groups is treated in Chapter 65. In August, the CPKI described itself as preparing for an independent government, not as the government itself.

Section 64.9

People's Committees and the Emergence of Local Korean Government

While attention in later memory often falls on Seoul, much of the immediate work of liberation took place in towns, counties, and villages. Within days of August 15, local groups across the peninsula organized committees to maintain order, protect property, distribute food, and assert Korean authority. Some called themselves branches of the Committee for the Preparation of Korean Independence; others used names such as peace-preservation committees, self-government associations, or, increasingly, people's committees (inmin wiwonhoe). The National Institute of Korean History records approximately 145 organizations associated with the CPKI by the end of August, a figure that captures only those linked to the central committee and almost certainly understates the number of locally initiated bodies.

The speed of this organization has several explanations. Colonial administration had left a vacuum at the local level, where police and officials were uncertain or absent. Local communities had existing social structures, including village elders, religious congregations, schools, youth groups, and the networks of former prisoners and activists, that could be quickly turned to new purposes. The Seoul committee's example and its calls for local organization provided a template, and travelers and messengers carried news and instructions. And the sense of opportunity, coupled with fear of disorder, motivated communities to act without waiting for external direction.

The practical test of a committee was not its name but its ability to do something when a problem reached the village or district office. Someone had to keep watch over a police station when its officers no longer commanded public trust, decide which grain stocks were available for distribution, investigate reports of theft, and prevent disputes over Japanese property from turning violent. Local schoolteachers could write notices and keep lists; railway and postal workers could sustain contact between towns; former activists could provide organizers. At the same time, none of these capacities automatically conferred legal sovereignty. A committee might patrol a market while a Japanese official continued to hold the keys to its warehouse, or negotiate with a police officer while lacking the armed force to replace him. Actual power therefore existed in degrees and had to be negotiated daily.

Regional variation is best understood through concrete administrative questions. A northern mining town needed to secure machinery and wages when Japanese managers vanished or lost authority; a southern rice-growing district faced arguments over collections, tenants' obligations, and access to stored grain; a port community had to handle passengers, military traffic, and returning workers. Where Christian congregations or established nationalist associations supplied trusted leaders, a local committee might initially be broadly accepted. Where underground labor or communist networks were stronger, organizers could press more directly for changes in property and management. Neither situation was uniform, and the political affiliation of a leader cannot by itself establish what an organization actually did in its first week. Historians need county records, surviving committee statements, and local testimony rather than assumptions projected backward from 1948.

These committees varied widely. Their leaders might be local notables, teachers, Christian pastors, former prisoners, Communist organizers, youth leaders, landowners, or some combination, depending on the community. Some were dominated by moderate nationalists who had held local influence under colonial rule; others included or were led by leftists, especially in places where Communist or labor organizers had been active. Their priorities likewise differed. Many focused on public order, the safe departure of Japanese residents, and the restoration of food distribution; others pressed for changes in land tenure, the punishment of collaborators, or the takeover of Japanese-owned property and enterprises. It is therefore misleading to describe the people's committees as a single movement or to label them automatically as Communist organizations. Their initial membership and local priorities varied considerably, and the political polarization that later made the label meaningful developed over subsequent months.

It is also important to distinguish among kinds of committees. Some were formally affiliated with the central CPKI and followed its instructions; others arose spontaneously and only later connected themselves to Seoul; and still others remained independent of any national organization. Such distinctions matter because later political actors, including occupation authorities and rival Korean groups, tended to treat the committees as a coordinated system, which they were not in August. The relationship between local committees and the central committee would become a major issue in the weeks after the CPKI's transformation into the Korean People's Republic and the arrival of American forces.

Regional contrasts help illustrate the range. In Seoul, the CPKI itself was the dominant body, and its activities were bound up with the presence of the Government-General and the Japanese army headquarters. In provincial towns, the balance of forces depended on whether Japanese police and officials remained, and on how local Koreans responded. In rural communities, committees often emerged from existing village leadership, with the task of keeping order and dealing with food and the return of mobilized men and women. In northern industrial regions, where workers' organizations and underground networks had existed, committees took a more active role in the takeover of factories and mines, a tendency amplified after Soviet forces arrived. In the agricultural south, particularly in some southwestern and southeastern districts, committees were closely linked to tenant farmers' grievances, a link that would intensify political conflict in the months to come. These contrasts should be treated as tendencies, not rigid regional rules, and local histories frequently complicate them.

The people's committees thus represent a significant form of Korean political self-organization in August 1945. They did not constitute a government in the legal sense, and their authority depended on local consent, local force, and the forbearance of Japanese and, later, Allied authorities. But they showed that Koreans were able to improvise institutions rapidly, and they ensured that the American and Soviet forces would arrive in a society already engaged in organizing itself.

Section 64.10

Government Offices, Schools, Factories, and the Reclamation of Public Institutions

Self-government required more than patrols and proclamations. It required the operation of the institutions that make modern life function: offices, railways, post and telegraph, schools, newspapers, hospitals, and factories. In August 1945, nearly all of these institutions were still structured around a Japanese-dominated hierarchy, and Koreans faced the challenge of removing Japanese authority while keeping essential services running.

Koreans were already present in these institutions in large numbers, but typically in subordinate positions. Colonial offices employed Korean clerks, translators, police, and lower-ranking officials; railways and communications relied on Korean workers; schools employed Korean teachers; and factories and mines drew on a Korean workforce. At the top of most institutions stood Japanese officials, managers, engineers, and school principals. The colonial system had also limited opportunities for Koreans to gain advanced technical and managerial experience, a legacy that shaped the capacity of the new committees. When Japanese managers and officials departed, were dismissed, or stopped coming to work, Korean employees sometimes took over, but they often lacked training in the top functions, access to records and finances, or the authority to direct the larger systems they now inhabited.

The gap between an office's official head and the people who knew how to operate it was especially consequential. A railway dispatcher, Korean clerk, or hospital attendant might have knowledge indispensable to daily continuity without possessing authority to sign an order or authorize expenditure. When Japanese supervisors withdrew, fled, or awaited instructions, Korean employees could keep a service operating while still struggling to obtain supplies and legal control. Conversely, removing every experienced colonial-era employee at once could interrupt essential functions, even though their continued service raised painful questions about participation in the old system. This was not simply a contest of patriotism against collaboration; it was an immediate administrative problem involving expertise, trust, payrolls, inventories, and public safety. Its eventual resolution would become a major controversy during military government.

Education illustrates the difficulty of separating symbolic change from institutional capacity. The removal of Japanese patriotic rituals and the restoration of Korean speech could happen quickly, but schools still needed teachers, textbooks, budgets, buildings, and decisions about how to teach recent history. For students whose lessons had been interrupted by wartime labor details, liberation promised renewed study; for teachers trained in Japanese materials, curriculum reform required more than changing the language of an announcement. An American military historical account written later reports both the rejection of Japanese textbooks and a shortage of Korean-language replacement materials. That account reflects an occupation administration's retrospective perspective, yet the underlying material problem is clear: cultural independence could be declared before the infrastructure needed to sustain it was rebuilt. Railways and transportation were of special importance because they linked regions, moved food, and carried people returning home. Railway workers in some districts organized to keep trains running, though disruption was widespread. In factories and mines, workers formed committees to protect equipment and maintain production, a movement often called self-management. These committees varied in effectiveness; some kept plants operating, while others struggled with shortages of raw materials, fuel, and managerial expertise. In some places, workers sought to prevent departing Japanese owners and managers from removing machinery or stock, while questions arose about ownership and the status of Japanese-owned property, questions that would become pressing under the occupations (Chapter 65).

Schools and the press illustrate another dimension of institutional change. Teachers and school administrators faced the task of reorienting education from a curriculum designed for colonial assimilation to one that could be conducted in Korean, with textbooks and materials that did not yet exist in sufficient numbers. Efforts to teach Hangeul and to restore Korean-language instruction began in the first weeks, building on the work of language scholars and teachers. In the press, Korean staff who had worked in colonial newspapers and printing houses began to produce new publications, while political organizations issued bulletins and handbills. The freedom to publish in Korean, to organize meetings, and to address public questions was one of the most tangible changes ordinary people experienced.

Currency and finance were additional sources of strain. The late-colonial period had seen heavy expansion of money in circulation to meet wartime and transitional costs, and historians have pointed to a sharp increase in the issue of currency in the last weeks as a contributor to inflation. Korean committees had no control over this system, and the practical result was that wages, prices, and savings were unstable during the very weeks when people were trying to restore normal economic life.

The uneven nature of this reclamation should be emphasized. Some institutions came under Korean control relatively smoothly, others were disputed, and others remained in Japanese hands until the Allies arrived. In most places, the process combined removal of Japanese authority with the continuation of existing routines, staff, and, in many cases, personnel who had worked within the colonial system. Section 64.11

Ordinary Koreans at Liberation: Workers, Farmers, Women, Students, and Families

Behind the political organizations and public speeches were millions of people whose expectations of liberation were shaped by their daily circumstances. Because most surviving documents from August 1945 were produced by officials, newspapers, and political leaders, historians reconstruct these expectations from memoirs, oral histories, later petitions, and the demands that committees and organizations made in the following months. The picture that emerges is of widespread hope for material and social change, combined with uncertainty about whether and how it would be achieved.

For farmers, the central issue was land. A large share of the rural population consisted of tenant farmers or farmers who cultivated part of their land as tenants, and rents were commonly a large fraction of the crop. Tenants expected that the end of colonial rule would bring changes in landholding or at least in rents, and many hoped that land owned by Japanese individuals and companies would be redistributed. Landowners, including Korean landowners who had prospered under colonial rule, had equally strong interests in preserving their property and feared that committees might support tenants. These competing expectations were not resolved in August; the first actions on rents and land belong to the occupation period and are treated in Chapter 65.

For workers, liberation raised hopes of better wages, shorter hours, and recognition of their organizations. Industrial and mining workers, many of whom had worked under harsh wartime discipline, formed committees and, in some cases, sought to take part in running workplaces. The conditions of workers varied greatly: wartime laborers who had been moved from villages to distant sites faced different problems from established factory workers, and both differed from the workers of small workshops and the urban poor. For many, the first priority was simply to be paid, to obtain food, and to find a way home.

Women's experiences of liberation were varied. Women had been mobilized for factory, farm, and service work, and many had borne the burdens of households whose men were absent. Some women had been subjected to the system of military sexual slavery described in Chapter 62, and their situation at the war's end was precarious, since many were stranded far from home and returned late or not at all, and those who did return often faced silence and stigma. At the same time, women took part in the organizing of August: women's associations were reported to have formed in Seoul within days of liberation, and women participated in demonstrations, committees, and relief work. Demands for educational opportunity, legal equality, and political participation were voiced early, though they had to be pursued against entrenched social expectations. It is accurate to say that hopes for gender equality were articulated in August 1945, but not that substantial changes had been achieved by the end of the month.

Students and young people also played active roles. Many had been mobilized for labor service or military training, and their release from these obligations coincided with a surge of youth organizing. Student groups and youth corps helped in patrolling, in distributing information, and in the political demonstrations of the first days. For some, liberation meant the chance to resume or begin education; for others, it meant joining political organizations that offered a vision of the new society.

Families bore the weight of the unresolved questions of return and loss. Households waited for sons and husbands who had been drafted, for laborers sent abroad, and for relatives in Japan and Manchuria. Others discovered that family members had died. These private experiences are part of the history of liberation, even though they rarely appear in the political documents of the period. They also help explain why many Koreans measured the new freedom partly by whether it brought their people home.

The key distinction for historians is between hope for reform and reforms actually achieved. In August, the expectations were abundant; the achievements were limited to the opening of political space, the release of prisoners, the formation of local institutions, and the beginning of Korean-language public life. Rents, land ownership, wages, schooling, and the legal status of women were largely unchanged by the end of the month. Much of the political conflict of the following years would turn on how, by whom, and how fast these expectations would be addressed.

Section 64.12

Collaboration, Retribution, Social Tensions, and the Problem of Justice

Liberation brought immediate questions about how to treat those who had cooperated with or benefited from colonial rule. The term most commonly used in Korean for those accused of collaboration was chinilpa, "pro-Japanese faction," and the related language of "traitors to the nation" quickly entered public speech. These terms covered a very wide range of conduct, from the leaders who had publicly supported Japanese war policy and assimilation, to police officers and officials who had carried out colonial orders, to businesspeople who profited from colonial contracts, to the countless ordinary people who had complied with colonial demands to survive. Chapter 60 examined the complexity of this spectrum; here the point is that August 1945 forced a public reckoning with it.

Resentment was strongest toward institutions and individuals most directly associated with coercion. Police officers, especially those who had worked in political and thought-control units, and informants and officials who had enforced requisition and labor mobilization, were frequent targets of anger. So were those who had grown wealthy on colonial connections or who had publicly advocated mobilizing Koreans for the war effort. At the same time, many Koreans who had worked in colonial institutions, particularly in the lower ranks of the administration and police, regarded themselves as having had little choice, and feared that popular anger would not distinguish among degrees of responsibility.

The reaction of communities varied. In a number of places, crowds confronted police stations, and some police officers and officials were threatened or assaulted; there were also reprisals against property and individuals associated with the colonial order. Historians should be cautious about the numbers and the scale of these incidents. Contemporary Japanese and American reports, local Korean accounts, and later memoirs differ, and each has its own institutional bias; Japanese reports tended to emphasize threats to Japanese residents, while Korean accounts often stress restraint and organized discipline. The available evidence suggests that, in August, large-scale vengeance against Japanese civilians did not occur in most places, though local violence and fear were real. The deeper conflict was among Koreans, over who had cooperated, who had resisted, and who had the standing to judge.

Disagreement about method was as important as disagreement about who was guilty. Some leaders and activists favored popular action, including public denunciations and the removal of officials by committees, as the legitimate expression of a long-suppressed people. Others, including many committee leaders who emphasized discipline, urged restraint and argued that punishment should wait for a proper legal process under an independent government. Still others, particularly among those who had served in the colonial administration or who supported order above all, worried that attacks on police and officials would undermine public safety and the functioning of institutions on which people depended. Questions about what legal bodies could try such cases, under what law, and with what safeguards, could not be answered in August, because no sovereign Korean government existed to create them.

Nor was there agreement about the scope of accountability. A narrow view would target only the most notorious individuals; a broad view would include entire categories of officials and professionals. The problem was made more difficult by the fact that expertise in administration, policing, finance, and technology was scarce, and a purge of everyone with colonial experience would have required the new institutions to start without trained personnel. These issues, which would shape the politics of the occupations and later republics, are taken up in Chapters 65 and 66 and in Volume IV. The point for this chapter is that the debate over justice began immediately, and that its terms, popular action versus legal process, broad versus narrow accountability, and order versus retribution, were set within days of liberation.

Section 64.13

The Soviet Army Enters Northern Korea

The Soviet entry into Korea needs precise dating because it is often described as having followed the Japanese surrender. In fact, Soviet military operations in Korea began on August 9, 1945, six days before Japan's announcement, as part of the Soviet Union's declaration of war and its massive offensive against Japanese forces in Manchuria. Soviet leaders had committed at the Yalta conference to enter the war against Japan within a few months of Germany's defeat, and the timing of the attack reflected that commitment and the Soviet desire to secure territorial and strategic objectives in Northeast Asia before the war ended.

The first Soviet actions in Korea were directed at the northeastern ports and rail connections close to the Soviet frontier. Soviet aircraft and naval units attacked military facilities in the northeastern coastal zone beginning August 9. Soviet landings took Unggi on August 11; operations at Rajin followed on August 12–13, while fighting for Chongjin began on August 13 and lasted through August 16. These dates refer to different operations—air attack, amphibious landing, and control of the ports—rather than one uniform southward march. Japanese garrisons, supported by coastal defenses and by units positioned to defend the northeastern approaches, resisted, and the fighting, especially at Chongjin, one of the peninsula's major industrial ports, continued for several days and in some accounts extended past August 15. Precise dates and casualty figures differ in the sources, and students should treat the sequence as approximate. The point to retain is that the northeast experienced armed combat, bombardment, and the movement of troops and refugees before and after the surrender announcement.

The operation was a coordinated air, naval, and land campaign, not one infantry column crossing the border. The Pacific Fleet's landing forces sought to secure the coastal ports and interrupt communications with Japan, while the 25th Army became responsible for advancing into and administering northern Korea. Unggi was taken on August 11 with comparatively limited opposition; Rajin required fighting on August 12–13; and the larger battle for Chongjin involved landings, artillery, and stubborn resistance through August 16. A chronology that simply says “the Soviets arrived on August 9” misses the distinction between the first raids, possession of a port, and the arrival of an occupation command. It also misses the civilians whose experience of liberation involved shelling and flight rather than public celebrations. Military-historical study.

Military arrival and civilian political transition did not proceed on a single schedule. A northern county might hear the surrender broadcast and form a Korean committee while Soviet troops were still days away; an eastern port might already have seen combat and Soviet occupation. The 25th Army's broader assignment to occupy the northern zone followed an order issued on August 17, whereas the first coastal engagements had begun on August 9. These differences help explain why the Soviet command later encountered Korean political bodies it had not created, alongside Japanese installations it had to disarm or secure. The first tasks were transport control, surrender processing, weapons collection, and essential infrastructure, even as every military decision affected which Koreans could exercise political authority.

From the northeastern ports, Soviet forces, including units of the 25th Army moving from the Soviet Maritime region, advanced southward along the rail and road corridors of northern Korea. Over the following two weeks, they moved through the northeastern and east-coast cities and into the interior, reaching northern centers over the third and fourth weeks of August. A detailed study drawing on Soviet military records dates the capture of Pyongyang to August 24; other accounts identify August 26 with the advance column's arrival or the establishment of Soviet occupation headquarters. The distinction between operational entry and the subsequent installation of command structures helps explain the different dates. Japanese resistance diminished as units received orders to cease fire and surrender, though the pace of surrender varied from place to place and some Japanese forces remained in their positions awaiting formal instructions. The Soviet advance halted in the vicinity of the 38th parallel, in line with the arrangements discussed in Section 64.16.

Korean civilians in the north experienced this transition in diverse ways. In the northeastern ports, residents lived through air attacks and landings, and many fled, and Japanese civilians in the area faced danger and displacement. In towns and cities farther south, residents saw the arrival of Soviet columns, sometimes after local committees had already formed and Japanese officials had begun to withdraw. Some welcomed the Soviet troops as the army that had ended Japanese rule; others observed them with wariness, and reports from Korean and Japanese sources, as well as later Soviet documents, record incidents of looting and violence by soldiers, which created fear and resentment in some communities. Historians must treat these accounts carefully, since they are scattered and politically charged, but they indicate that the arrival of a foreign army was neither uniformly welcomed nor uniformly feared.

Why did the Soviet forces enter Korea before Japan's surrender? The answer lies in the structure of the Allied war effort. The Soviet Union had its own military and political objectives in the war against Japan. It sought to defeat Japanese forces in Manchuria and northern Korea, to secure territory and ports, and to ensure a role in the settlement. Its commanders were not waiting for a surrender announcement to begin operations. For Koreans, this meant that the first foreign army to arrive on the peninsula was a combat force of an Allied power that had not been part of the Cairo Declaration, and that the northern part of the country came under foreign military control weeks before the south did.

Section 64.14

Northern Korea after the Japanese Surrender

The transition in the north took place in two overlapping stages: first, the formation of Korean local bodies in the days after August 15, often before Soviet troops had arrived; second, the establishment of direct relations between Soviet commanders and these bodies as Soviet forces moved south. Understanding this sequence helps to avoid the assumption that northern Korean political institutions were created by the Soviet Army from nothing.

In Pyongyang, the principal northern city, Korean leaders moved quickly. Cho Man-sik, a Christian nationalist with a long record of nonviolent activism and considerable local prestige, headed a committee for preparing independence for South Pyeongan Province, formed within days of the broadcast. Accounts indicate that the Japanese provincial authorities consulted Korean leaders and agreed to a transfer of administrative responsibilities for maintaining order, though, as in Seoul, the nature and extent of any transfer is understood differently in different sources. In Hamhung and other northern cities, similar committees formed, with leaders from varied backgrounds, including nationalists, Christians, and Communists who had been released or had emerged from hiding. In many northern towns, local Koreans were already acting as the de facto authority by the time Soviet troops appeared.

Pyongyang provides a particularly important example of this sequence. Cho Man-sik and other Korean civic figures had local standing before the Soviet Army established a command presence; their organizations initially filled a political vacuum that existed separately from the Soviet advance. A study based on Soviet military material dates the taking of Pyongyang to August 24, while another account identifies August 26 with the arrival of an advance column and establishment of occupation headquarters. These are not interchangeable documentary claims. The narrative should distinguish an initial entry, the consolidation of a garrison, and the creation of offices that could issue orders to civilian institutions. Calling all of them simply “Soviet arrival” blurs precisely the uncertainty that shaped northern Korean responses.

Commandants faced concrete problems that could not wait for a settled Korean constitution. Japanese soldiers possessed weapons and records; power stations, bridges, coal mines, and rail lines had to be secured; and civilians needed food and protection. Soviet officers therefore had incentives to use existing Korean committees and knowledgeable local staff, while also reserving decisive control over installations and policing for the military. The relationship was not an uncomplicated grant of self-rule. Korean leaders could gain opportunities through Soviet recognition but became vulnerable when commanders changed priorities or preferred other intermediaries. That tension between local initiative and external military authority was present in August even though the institutions of the later North Korean state were not yet formed.

When Soviet forces arrived, their commanders encountered these local bodies and generally worked through them, at least at first. Soviet military authorities issued proclamations addressed to the Korean population, assuring them of the Soviet Army's role in liberating Korea from Japanese rule and permitting the formation of political parties and organizations under military supervision. The Soviet Army's priorities in these early weeks were military: accepting the surrender and disarmament of Japanese forces, securing railways, ports, communications, and industrial facilities, and ensuring that Japanese officials and civilians did not obstruct these tasks. Political questions were secondary, though the commanders also had instructions to cooperate with Korean organizations and to prevent disorder. Soviet officers including Korean-speaking interpreters, among them Soviet citizens of Korean descent, played an important role in communication.

The position of Japanese officials and civilians in the north was precarious. Japanese administrators were removed from authority or confined to specific roles; Japanese troops were disarmed and gathered; and Japanese civilians, many of whom lived in industrial towns and ports, faced shortages and danger as the governing structures changed. In the following months, large numbers of Japanese civilians in the north suffered severe hardship, an aspect of the postwar transition treated in later chapters. At the same time, Koreans took over management of many facilities and public services, in cooperation with or under the oversight of Soviet authorities.

It is essential to distinguish the August transition from the later creation of Communist-dominated governing institutions. In August, northern political life included nationalists, Christians, socialists, and Communists, and Cho Man-sik was among the most prominent leaders. Kim Il Sung and the guerrilla and Soviet-trained Koreans associated with him had not yet returned to Korea, and the main Communist organizers in the north were local activists. The Soviet Civil Administration, the formal bodies that unified northern governance, and the policies of land reform and political consolidation belong to the following months and are treated in Chapter 65. Reading the later northern state back into the first weeks would obscure the real range of political possibilities that existed in the north in August 1945.

Section 64.15

Southern Korea between Japanese Withdrawal and American Arrival

In the south, the interval between August 15 and the American landing on September 8 was a period of ambiguity and improvised arrangements. No Allied army was present, but the Japanese colonial government was also no longer able to command the confidence or obedience of the population. Seoul and the southern provinces thus lived through nearly three weeks in which authority was divided among a weakened colonial administration, a heavily armed but demoralized Japanese military, Korean committees of varying strength, and a population waiting to learn what the Allies would do.

The Government-General remained in place and Governor-General Abe Nobuyuki continued in office, but its leaders were focused on maintaining order, protecting Japanese civilians, and preparing for the arrival of the Allied forces. Japanese military commanders in Seoul, who controlled large forces, were instructed to maintain order until relieved and were reluctant to tolerate challenges to their authority. As described in Sections 64.5 and 64.8, the initial cooperation with Korean leaders gave way within days to restrictions on Korean organizing, as Japanese military and police authorities reasserted control and as indications arrived that the Allies expected the status quo to be maintained until their arrival. Some local Japanese officials, however, continued to cooperate with Korean committees, and the degree of Japanese interference varied by region.

The gap between Japanese military control and Korean political initiative was clearest where the work of government required both compliance and consent. Remaining Japanese authorities could still direct armed soldiers and control strategic installations, yet their orders increasingly lacked moral force among Koreans who expected colonial rule to be over. Korean committees could mobilize volunteers, speak to large gatherings, and sometimes mediate food supply, but they had no legal power to compel Japanese officials to relinquish assets. This produced negotiated, uneven arrangements rather than either a complete power vacuum or a clean transfer of sovereignty. The distinction helps explain why Seoul could display Korean flags and hear Korean leaders speak while the Government-General and Japanese army headquarters continued to operate.

The Americans initially possessed less local knowledge than the committees they were about to encounter. An August 26 diplomatic communication from Manila described planning to rely, at least initially, on Japanese administrative personnel under a military governor, precisely the approach that many Koreans would find objectionable. That communication did not settle eventual occupation policy, and it was not a public promise to retain Japanese rule. It nevertheless documents the divergence between military planners' priority of administrative continuity and Korean activists' demand for political and institutional replacement. When Hodge's troops arrived in September, they inherited not an empty territory awaiting government but a society that had been organizing and arguing about authority for weeks. Contemporary U.S. planning record.

Korean committees filled much of the practical space. In Seoul, the CPKI continued to operate, expand its branch network, and prepare for a national assembly. In provincial centers, local committees assumed police-like and administrative functions with varying degrees of recognition from the remaining Japanese officials. Political organizations also began to re-form: leftists, including Communists, regrouped; conservative nationalists organized their own circles; and religious and civic groups reestablished contact. Negotiations among these groups over cooperation and leadership took place amid rumors about the Allies' intentions, rumors that included expectations that the Soviets, the Americans, or the Chinese might arrive first.

Uncertainty about the Allies was acute. Leaflets dropped by American aircraft in late August informed Japanese forces and the Korean population of the impending occupation and instructed Japanese authorities to maintain order until the Allies arrived. Koreans learned that American forces were coming, but little was known about American intentions regarding Korean political groups. The U.S. XXIV Corps, under Lieutenant General John R. Hodge, was preparing to move from Okinawa to Korea. It would arrive with limited information about Korean conditions and politics and with a primary mission of accepting the surrender and securing the zone, a point that matters for understanding the tensions of the first weeks of occupation described in Chapter 65.

The result was an interval in which Koreans acted but did not govern with recognized sovereign authority. Public order was largely maintained in the south, though with local variations and tension, and the absence of a major collapse of order in these weeks owed something to the efforts of Korean committees and something to the continued presence of Japanese military and police. But the interval also allowed political positions to harden. Competing groups used these weeks to build organizations, claim legitimacy, and shape expectations about the future, so that the American arrival in September would meet a political society already polarizing around fundamental questions: who represented Korea, how quickly independence should come, and what social changes it should entail.

Cause and Effect

Imperial Collapse and the Competition for Authority

Long-term causes

Thirty-five years of colonial rule had suppressed independent Korean political institutions, while generating overseas and underground independence movements with competing programs; wartime mobilization had strained society and weakened confidence in the colonial state.

Short-term causes

Military defeat, the atomic bombings, Soviet entry into the war, and divisions within the Japanese leadership produced a sudden surrender decision, leaving colonial officials little time to prepare.

Immediate context

The Allied powers had no agreed mechanism for transferring sovereignty to Koreans, no recognized Korean government, and no occupation forces in place across the peninsula, while the Soviet Army was already advancing in the north.

Event or transformation

The colonial administration lost legitimacy and much of its coherence. Korean organizations, prisoners, and communities mobilized, and political authority became fragmented and contested.

Immediate effects

Release of prisoners, formation of the CPKI and local committees, public celebration, early efforts at justice and social reform, and the allocation of surrender zones at the 38th parallel.

Long-term consequences

A contest among Korean political projects, set within a foreign military occupation, that would shape the occupations and the establishment of two Korean states in 1948.

Historical complexity: No single factor explains the outcome. Korean mobilization, Allied military decisions, the timing of the surrender, and the unresolved legal status of Korea each shaped the competition for authority. The result was not predetermined by any of them.

Section 64.16

The 38th Parallel: A Military Surrender Line with Political Consequences

The 38th parallel is now so closely associated with Korea's division that it is easy to forget that it began as a staff officers' device for allocating surrender responsibilities. Understanding its origin in the days after August 9 is essential to avoiding the assumption that the division of Korea was planned in advance or that the line was intended as a permanent frontier.

The American proposal arose from the sudden acceleration of events. With Soviet forces fighting in Manchuria and northern Korea, and with Japan's surrender seemingly imminent, American planners in Washington faced the question of where Soviet and American forces should accept the surrender of Japanese troops. Few American units were within reach of Korea, and the Soviet Army was already advancing there. On the night of August 10–11, a small group of American staff officers in the War Department's planning organization, including Colonels Dean Rusk and Charles Bonesteel, was asked to propose a dividing line quickly. Their later recollections describe a search for a line that would place the capital, Seoul, and as much of the peninsula as possible in the American sector, while remaining a line that the Soviets might accept. They selected the 38th parallel, which divided the peninsula roughly in half and placed Seoul and the port of Incheon to the south. These accounts, written years afterward, reflect hindsight, but the general sequence is documented in American records.

The line was included in General Order No. 1, the order drafted in Washington to govern the surrender of Japanese forces throughout Asia, which was sent to the Soviet and British governments and was issued formally on September 2 together with the surrender instrument. The Soviet government, in its reply, proposed changes concerning other regions but did not object to the arrangement for Korea, and Soviet forces halted their advance near the line. In effect, the Soviet Union accepted the proposal. The decision was made by the Allied powers alone. No Korean political representative from any organization was consulted, and most Koreans were unaware of the line in August.

The military logic was straightforward: Japanese forces north of the parallel would surrender to the Soviet commander and those south of it to the American commander. It was an administrative convenience for the surrender process, akin to other zones established for Japanese surrender in China, Southeast Asia, and the Pacific. U.S. diplomatic documents described the arrangement as temporary and military in character. Nothing in the order specified that the zones would become separate administrations or governments, and the Allied governments had not agreed on the political future of Korea.

Geography gave the line significant consequences. The parallel cut across river basins, railways, power transmission lines, and administrative boundaries, and it separated regions that had been integrated in the colonial economy. The southern zone contained roughly two-thirds of the population and the main rice-producing districts and Seoul, while the north held much of the heavy industry, mining, and hydroelectric capacity. These differences should not be exaggerated into destiny, since the colonial economy had linked both regions and both contained agriculture, industry, and cities. But the economic and administrative disruptions caused by the sudden barrier were real, and the weeks that followed saw growing restrictions on movement across the line, a process taken up in Chapter 65.

The distinction between a temporary surrender boundary and a permanent division is the central analytical point. In August and September 1945, the 38th parallel was a line between two Allied zones, expected by many to be removed once Korea's future was settled. It became a political frontier only through the failure of Soviet–American agreement on how to unify the zones, the policies of the two occupation authorities, the choices of Korean political groups, and the escalating Cold War. Reading the permanent division back into the August decision would turn contingent outcomes into foreordained ones.

Section 64.17

Koreans Returning Home and the Movement of Displaced Populations

The end of the Japanese Empire left millions of people far from the places they regarded as home. Millions of Koreans lived or worked outside the peninsula in 1945: in Japan, where the Korean population numbered about two million, many of them laborers and their families; in Manchuria and elsewhere in China; in the Soviet Far East and Central Asia, where earlier generations of migrants and deportees had settled; on Sakhalin; and in military and labor units spread across Japan's far-flung empire. Their experiences of August 1945 differed according to where they were and what conditions they faced.

The hope of immediate return was widespread and, for most, unrealistic. The collapse of the Japanese transport system, damage to ports and railways, shortages of ships and fuel, and the absence of an organized program of repatriation meant that most Koreans could not simply go home on August 15. Some left quickly, by whatever means they could find, including small boats and trains. Many of the first movements were chaotic and dangerous. One incident, the sinking of the Ukishima Maru near Maizuru on August 24, 1945, while carrying Korean laborers and their families toward Korea, killed many passengers. The official reported toll was 549 deaths, including 524 Koreans and 25 Japanese military personnel; survivors and researchers have disputed whether the official count was complete. Investigations and retrospective testimony also dispute the ship's movements and circumstances of the explosion, which apparently involved a mine. The presence of competing accounts does not justify treating a deliberate sinking as established fact. The episode is a reminder that return itself was a hazardous part of liberation.

Records of return movement also require attention to what is being counted. A figure for people registered at Japanese ports does not necessarily include those who arranged their own passage, while a count of arrivals in southern Korea may not include returns to the northern zone or onward movement. The U.S. occupation's subsequent reports document congested ports and attempts to register returnees, but they are records of its administrative system rather than a complete census of every journey. By linking nationality, military status, point of departure, and destination, historians can better explain why estimates differ. The key August fact is logistical: the war ended instantly as a political event, but the transportation network had to absorb the movement of immense populations in both directions. Occupation-era repatriation record.

Most returns occurred over the following months and years, under arrangements set by Allied occupation authorities, the Japanese government, and the occupation forces in Korea. In Japan, Koreans gathered at ports awaiting passage, and by the end of 1946 a large share of those who wished to leave had done so. Restrictions on cash and baggage that returnees could carry, the poor economic situation in Korea, and the political uncertainty led many Koreans in Japan to remain, forming the basis of the resident Korean community in Japan whose history continues in Volume IV. In Manchuria and China, some Koreans returned south, while others remained and became part of the population of the People's Republic of China later on. Koreans on Sakhalin, numbering in the tens of thousands, were largely unable to return under the postwar arrangements, a situation that persisted for decades.

Soldiers, wartime laborers, and prisoners faced distinct problems. Korean men who had served in the Japanese military were scattered across theaters of war, and some were held as prisoners or, in a number of cases, were accused of war crimes as guards or auxiliaries, a complicated legacy of their status as colonial subjects serving an empire. Laborers in mines and factories in Japan, Sakhalin, and elsewhere sought unpaid wages and passage home, and disputes over compensation arose immediately. Women who had been forced into military sexual slavery were often left in remote locations without means of transportation; many returned late, some did not return, and for decades most remained silent about their experiences, a history discussed in connection with later memory politics in Volume IV.

The return of Koreans also affected conditions in Korea. Returnees added to pressure on food, housing, and employment, and brought with them diverse political experiences, including exposure to socialist, nationalist, and labor movements in Japan and China. Their arrival in the following months fed into the political debates and tensions of the occupation. For this chapter, the key point is that liberation did not instantly gather the dispersed Korean population into a free homeland. It began a long and uneven process of return, whose difficulties formed part of the unfinished nature of Korea's liberation.

Section 64.18

The Provisional Government, Overseas Independence Leaders, and Competing Claims to Authority

Chapter 63 examined the major independence organizations abroad and their claims to represent the Korean nation. Their responses to Japan's defeat in August 1945, and the delay before most of their leaders reached Korea, were an essential part of the political situation inside the peninsula. A reader should not imagine that the principal overseas leaders returned in triumph in August. They did not.

The Provisional Government, headquartered in Chongqing, China, with Kim Ku as its leading figure, received the news of Japan's surrender as the culmination of decades of struggle and as a moment of difficulty. Its Restoration Army had been preparing, in cooperation with American intelligence officers, for operations that the sudden end of the war made unnecessary, and Kim Ku's later memoir recalls disappointment that Korea had not achieved liberation through a greater military role of its own. That recollection, written afterward, reflects his own framing of the event, but it points to a real concern among independence leaders: that liberation had come largely through the actions of foreign powers and might weaken their claim to leadership. The Provisional Government nevertheless asserted that it was the legitimate government of Korea and prepared to return and take power.

The separation between the historic prestige of exile organizations and their immediate administrative capacity is central to the story. Provisional Government leaders could invoke a charter, years of diplomacy, and the Korean Liberation Army, but they lacked a domestic tax system, police network, and recognized legal authority in August. Local committees possessed a different kind of strength: they could mobilize neighborhoods and put volunteers into streets or offices, but their formal claim to represent the whole nation was limited. These were not identical sources of legitimacy, and neither could simply be substituted for the other. The problem was compounded by distance. Communications from Chongqing, Washington, and Soviet military centers did not instantly reach Korean villages, and Korean organizations at home could not suspend every administrative decision until overseas leaders arrived.

Nor did every overseas organization seek the same political settlement. Some leaders hoped for a republican succession from the provisional government founded in 1919; others saw liberation as the opportunity for a broader social revolution or for a united-front administration. Many individuals had participated in resistance through Chinese or Soviet military structures whose sponsors expected a continuing role in postwar Korea. These differences explain why recognition mattered so much: acknowledgment of one institution could strengthen one political program over the others before Koreans had held a national election. August 1945 therefore opened a contest not simply among personalities, but among legal claims, military relationships, existing local institutions, and competing definitions of popular representation.

It could not do so immediately. The Allied governments did not recognize it as the government of Korea, and the American position, in particular, was that members would be permitted to return only as private individuals. Transportation and permission had to be arranged through the Chinese and American authorities. The Provisional Government's principal leaders, including Kim Ku, returned to Korea in November 1945, in groups, on American-arranged flights, and subject to the condition of private status. Their delayed return meant that, in the crucial weeks of August and September, leaders inside Korea set the pace of political organization, and the overseas leaders had to enter a political field that had already begun to take shape.

Syngman Rhee, who had long represented the Provisional Government's cause in the United States, remained in Washington in August, where he had lobbied for recognition without success. He returned to Korea in October 1945, with the assistance of American military authorities. Other overseas groups returned on their own schedules: Soviet-connected Korean activists, including soldiers of the Soviet-trained units in which Kim Il Sung had served, reached northern Korea in September and after, and the Communist and nationalist leaders who had been based in Yan'an, China, returned still later, in late 1945 and beyond. Chapters 65 and 77 treat their arrival and rise. In August, none of these figures was present to lead.

Meanwhile, leaders inside Korea, including Yeo Un-hyeong, Cho Man-sik, conservative nationalists in Seoul, and Communists who had survived underground or in prison such as Pak Hon-yong, made their own claims. The result was a field of competing sources of legitimacy: the Provisional Government's historic claim, the CPKI's practical authority and popular mobilization, the standing of leaders with long records of resistance inside the country, and the Allied powers' ultimate authority as the recipients of Japanese surrender. None had decisive legitimacy. The Allies controlled the means of governance but lacked Korean consent and knowledge, while the Korean organizations had popular support and political programs but lacked international recognition. This structural gap, more than the rivalry of individual personalities, shaped the political uncertainty of liberation.

Section 64.19

Liberation without a Settled Government: The Threshold of Occupation

By the end of August 1945, Koreans had accomplished a great deal in a very short time. They had emerged from colonial rule, released political prisoners, restored open political and cultural life, organized local institutions across much of the peninsula, and articulated visions of a sovereign, democratic state. These were genuine achievements, produced in large part by Koreans' own initiative, and they should not be understated in an account that often emphasizes the role of the great powers.

The unresolved questions, however, were equally significant. Authority within Korea remained fragmented among the Government-General, Japanese military and police forces, the CPKI and its branches, local people's committees, rival political groups, and, in the north, the Soviet Army. Essential institutions were still being reorganized and food, finance, and transportation were precarious. Millions of Koreans across the former empire faced uncertain futures. Competing Korean political projects had begun to form: a moderate and left-leaning coalition around the CPKI, conservative nationalists who were preparing to organize separately and awaiting the Provisional Government and the Allies, Communists reorganizing a national party, and, in the north, a mix of local leaders working with Soviet authorities. And the Allied powers had assigned surrender responsibilities along a line that divided the peninsula, with no agreed plan for Korea's political future.

One can distinguish three levels of unfinished authority at the month's end. At the neighborhood and county level, Korean committees were answering practical demands for food, safety, and the operation of institutions. At the national level, the CPKI and rival organizations offered competing plans for representation and reconstruction but did not possess acknowledged sovereignty. At the international level, Allied commanders had negotiated which forces would accept the Japanese surrender without settling how Koreans would govern themselves. Each level affected the others: local effectiveness strengthened a political organization's claim, recognition by an Allied army could alter local power, and the absence of a nationally accepted authority made every regional arrangement more consequential.

This was also a moment in which decisions remained reversible. The 38th parallel divided surrender zones, not two internationally recognized Korean states; the Provisional Government's leaders were still overseas; the Korean People's Republic had not yet been proclaimed; and occupation institutions had not yet enacted their later political and economic programs. Historians know what followed, but people in August could not. A responsible account therefore presents the competing possibilities without suggesting that compromise was guaranteed or that partition was already inevitable. The question of how Soviet and American authorities would respond to Korean political initiatives belongs to Chapter 65, where immediate military necessities became sustained systems of government.

The CPKI's leaders understood that the arrival of Allied forces would change the situation. In early September they convened a national gathering in Seoul and, on September 6, proclaimed the Korean People's Republic, in an attempt to present the Allies with an established Korean government. This step, treated in Chapter 65, was not the same as the CPKI's earlier work; it was a response to the imminent arrival of American forces, and it would be rejected by the American occupation authorities. It illustrates the pressure Korean leaders felt to establish facts before foreign armies arrived.

American forces arrived soon afterward. The U.S. XXIV Corps under Lieutenant General John R. Hodge landed at Incheon on September 8, and on September 9 American forces took formal control in Seoul, where the Japanese surrender in the southern zone was accepted. In the north, Soviet forces were already in control. These events, the beginning of the occupations, are narrated in Chapter 65 and are mentioned here only as the threshold of the next phase. The central point is that American authority arrived in a society already engaged in political organization, and that the authorities of the two zones would begin with very different circumstances and policies.

The central problem that this chapter leaves for Chapter 65 is therefore this: who would exercise governing authority over Korea? Korea had been liberated from Japanese rule, but the question of who would govern a liberated Korea remained unanswered. August 1945 opened multiple political possibilities; it did not determine in advance which would prevail. A unified Korean state, a trusteeship, a divided peninsula, and other outcomes were all conceivable in the last days of August. The story of how some possibilities closed and others emerged belongs to the chapters that follow.

Chapter Summary

Chapter 64 has examined the weeks around Japan's surrender and Korea's liberation, and has argued that August 1945 was simultaneously a moment of national liberation, popular political mobilization, institutional breakdown, and international uncertainty. Koreans organized to govern themselves, but the collapse of Japanese authority occurred within an Allied military framework that did not provide for the immediate transfer of sovereignty to a unified Korean government.

On the eve of liberation, Korean society was exhausted by wartime mobilization, shortages of food, forced labor, military conscription, and heavy policing. Organized independence activity inside Korea was fragmentary, and the larger movements were abroad. Japan's surrender then followed from interacting pressures: the Potsdam Declaration, the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki, the Soviet declaration of war and its offensive beginning August 9, and divisions within Japan's leadership. Historians continue to debate the relative weight of these factors, and no single cause adequately explains the decision.

The Allied powers had promised Korean independence "in due course" at Cairo but had not agreed on a mechanism, a timetable, or a recognized Korean authority. Trusteeship was an informal American idea, not an operational policy. When the surrender came, there was no plan for transferring sovereignty, and Korean leaders were excluded from the decisions that mattered most, including the assignment of surrender responsibilities at the 38th parallel.

The chapter distinguished four dates that are often combined: Soviet operations in Korea began on August 9; Japan announced its acceptance of the surrender terms on August 15, the date Koreans commemorate as liberation; the formal surrender was signed on September 2; and American forces landed at Incheon on September 8 and took formal control in Seoul on September 9. These distinctions show that liberation, military occupation, and national division were related but distinct developments, unfolding on different timetables in the north and the south.

The first days of freedom were marked by Korean initiative. Yeo Un-hyeong negotiated with colonial officials over order, prisoners, and freedom to organize, and the evidence for these talks must be read critically: participants' claims differ from what was implemented, and historians debate whether authority was transferred or created. Political prisoners were released, crowds celebrated and reclaimed public space, and the Committee for the Preparation of Korean Independence formed in Seoul, drawing moderates and leftists, while prominent conservatives stayed apart. Its August 28 declaration set out aims of independence, democratic government, public order, and protection of livelihoods. It must not be confused with the Korean People's Republic proclaimed on September 6.

Liberation was also a social transformation. Within days, hundreds of local committees formed to keep order, distribute food, and take over services and workplaces. They varied widely in membership and purpose, and should not be labeled automatically as Communist. Workers, farmers, women, students, and families brought expectations about land, wages, education, and equality, though few reforms had been achieved by the end of August. The question of collaboration and justice emerged immediately, with disagreements over popular action versus legal process, and over how broadly accountability should extend.

The Soviet Army fought in northeastern Korea from August 9 and advanced southward, reaching Pyongyang around August 24–26. Its early dealings with northern Korea relied on local committees, including Cho Man-sik's committee in South Pyeongan, and the later Communist-dominated institutions belong to Chapter 65. In the south, the interval before September 8 was one of divided authority among a weakened colonial government, an armed Japanese military, and Korean committees. The 38th parallel, proposed by American staff officers on the night of August 10–11 and accepted by the Soviet Union, was initially a military surrender line, not a frontier between states, but its consequences proved far-reaching.

Koreans abroad and across the former Japanese Empire faced long and difficult paths home. Most returned over subsequent months and years, and many did not return at all. The Provisional Government's leaders and other overseas figures returned only from October and November onward, and some later, so that political organization in August was led by leaders inside Korea. Competing sources of legitimacy, from the Provisional Government to the CPKI to the Allied powers' authority, left the question of governance unresolved.

The chapter's closing argument is that Korea had been liberated from Japanese rule, but the question of who would govern a liberated Korea remained unanswered. August 1945 opened multiple political possibilities, and did not determine in advance which would prevail. The creation of two states in 1948 was the product of later decisions and conflicts, examined in Chapters 65 and 66, and should not be read back into the first weeks of freedom.

Chapter Reflection

Chapter 64 matters because it recovers the weeks in which Korea's future was open. Later histories, in both Korean states, tend to treat 1945 either as a triumphant restoration or as the prelude to division. The evidence of August shows something more complicated: a society that had just been released from colonial rule acting with remarkable speed to organize itself, within a military framework in which decisions that would shape its future were being made elsewhere. Understanding that combination is necessary for understanding everything that followed.

The chapter also illustrates a broader pattern in the history of decolonization: the end of an empire does not automatically produce a sovereign state. Between the collapse of colonial authority and the establishment of a recognized government lies a period in which competing groups, foreign powers, and ordinary people negotiate who will rule and on what terms. In Korea, that period was shaped by the absence of a recognized Korean government, by the staggered return of overseas leaders, by the arrival of two armies on different dates, and by a surrender line drawn for military reasons.

The developments of August 1945 shaped later events in specific ways. The people's committees and the CPKI set the terms of a political contest over land, justice, and legitimacy that would intensify under the occupations. The questions of collaboration and accountability would return in the politics of both Korean states. The 38th parallel, born as a surrender line, would harden into the frontier of 1948 and the armistice line of 1953. And the memory of liberation itself, with its distinct names and commemorations in the north and the south, became part of each state's account of its origins.

The next chapter takes up the problem this one leaves open. Chapter 65 examines how Soviet administration in the north and American military government in the south began to answer the question of who would govern Korea, and how, within a changing international order, those answers diverged into two political systems. To read it well, students should keep in mind what August 1945 actually was: a liberation without a settled government, whose outcome had not yet been decided.

Documentary and Scholarly Source Register

Editorial method. The four Primary Source Windows present labeled summaries or paraphrases, not facsimile-transcribed excerpts. The records below establish the provenance of the relevant documents and provide further reading. A source's presence does not mean every statement has been collated against every surviving Korean, Japanese, English, and Russian original. Contemporary official records require the same source criticism as retrospective works; administrative statistics may reflect incomplete categories or political purposes.

  1. National Diet Library, Japan. “Imperial Rescript of the Termination of the War, August 14, 1945.” Digital Constitution resources; original issued August 14, radio broadcast August 15. Document record.
  2. National Diet Library. Chronological resources on the Potsdam Declaration (July 26, 1945), the surrender broadcast, and formal surrender. Historical chronology.
  3. U.S. National Archives. “Surrender of Japan (1945).” The September 2 instrument; distinguishes surrender of Japanese armed forces from the political arrangements that followed. Digitized archival description.
  4. Maeil Sinbo, August 17, 1945. Contemporary report of Yeo Un-hyeong's August 16 speech; includes Yeo's account of the August 15, 8 a.m. meeting and his five demands. National Institute of Korean History, Korean History Database, “자료대한민국사.” Original-language report.
  5. Committee for the Preparation of Korean Independence. “Declaration and Program,” dated August 28, 1945. National Institute of Korean History, Sources for Korean History, with original text and explanatory commentary. Source and commentary.
  6. National Institute of Korean History. New Edition of Korean History, Volume 52, on the establishment and early activity of the CPKI; discusses the August 15 planning and August 17 organizational leadership and notes a related August 26 declaration in the narrative. Historical synthesis.
  7. U.S. Army Forces in Korea. Historical account of political organization after liberation, including an account of Ahn Jae-hong's August 17 public broadcast. Preserved by the National Institute of Korean History. Occupation-era institutional account.
  8. “War and peace in liberated North Korea: Soviet military administration and the creation of North Korean police force in 1945.” International Journal of Asian Studies, Cambridge University Press. The study dates operations at Unggi, Rajin, and Chongjin, the August 17 occupation order, and Pyongyang's entry to August 24. Research article.
  9. Dean Rusk. July 12, 1950, memorandum recalling the August 10–11, 1945, selection of the 38th parallel, reproduced in Foreign Relations of the United States. Retrospective first-person evidence. FRUS record.
  10. General Order No. 1. Allocation of Japanese surrender to Soviet command north of 38 degrees latitude and American command to the south, reproduced in Foreign Relations of the United States, 1945, Volume VI. FRUS record and text.
  11. Douglas MacArthur. Proclamation No. 1 to the people of Korea, September 7, 1945; statement of American military control south of the parallel. NIKH Korean History Database. Document transcription.
  12. U.S. Department of State. Consul General Steintorf's telegram from Manila, August 26, 1945, reporting preliminary plans for the administration of southern Korea and anticipated use of Japanese personnel. NIKH reproduction of FRUS. Diplomatic record.
  13. U.S. Army Forces in Korea. Military government history, “The Textbook Situation,” on the transition away from Japanese school materials in 1945. A retrospective occupation document, not a neutral survey of all Korean schools. Occupation-era account.
