Pansori, Painting, Literature, and Popular Culture

Korean History: Ancient Kingdoms to a Divided Peninsula

Chapter Opening

After hearing a performance of Chunhyangga during his travels in the 1750s, the scholar Yu Jin-han composed a classical Chinese narrative poem dated 1754. That poem survives as one of the earliest detailed witnesses to the Chunhyang story in pansori.

This chapter studies cultural production in Joseon between roughly 1600 and 1800: vernacular literature, the spread of Hangeul, pansori, genre painting, minhwa, satire, storytelling, and entertainment beyond the royal court. Its argument is that these decades saw new subjects and wider audiences in literature, music, performance, and painting.

That change was not simply elite culture becoming popular. Court painters, scholars, professional performers, women, merchants, and ordinary people contributed on unequal terms, with different access to education, patronage, literacy, and recognition.

The evidence is varied and uneven: books, manuscripts, paintings, written accounts of performances, and later records. Much of the best-documented material, especially for pansori, mask dance, and minhwa, is from the nineteenth century or later, so the chapter separates eighteenth-century evidence from later sources.

The chapter moves through four stages. The first seven sections follow literary life: scripts and literacy, the circulation of manuscripts and books, fiction, women's writing, poetry, and satire. The next four turn to storytelling, pansori, and public performance. Five sections then treat painting, from the court bureau to landscape, genre scenes, and decorative work, and the last two ask who paid for and who consumed culture and what the surviving evidence can establish.

Throughout, the chapter distinguishes what a source shows from what later tradition has attached to it. Authorship of well-known works is disputed, dates of performances are uncertain, and paintings are composed images, not photographs. Those cautions are part of the history, not merely qualifications of it.

The chapter draws on the market economy of Chapter 39 and the religious life of Chapter 40, and prepares the social analysis of Chapter 42.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how recovery and commercialization shaped cultural production.
  2. Distinguish the roles of hanmun and Hangeul and their audiences.
  3. Trace how stories circulated through manuscripts, print, and performance.
  4. Analyze fiction and satire as social commentary without equating it with organized opposition.
  5. Describe the form and early evidence of pansori.
  6. Compare court, literati, genre, and minhwa painting.
  7. Assess what genre painting can and cannot reveal.
  8. Distinguish eighteenth-century evidence from later records.
  9. Evaluate who funded and consumed cultural products.

  2. How did literacy differ by gender, status, and script?

Historical Context

Joseon recovered from the Imjin War and the Manchu invasions (Chapters 32–36) through agricultural rebuilding and growing markets (Chapter 39). Literary Chinese stayed the language of government and scholarship, while Hangeul, promulgated in 1446 (Chapter 27), found wider uses. Practical Learning scholars (Chapter 38) paid new attention to Korean society and geography, and religious currents (Chapter 40) left traces in stories and images. The status order of yangban, middle people, commoners, and slaves continued to shape who could take part in cultural life.

Two features of the setting matter most. First, the state and the educated elite still defined cultural prestige: examinations rewarded literary Chinese, the court employed painters through a royal bureau, and scholars judged poetry and painting by inherited standards. Second, growth in towns and markets created paying audiences and a class of professionals, such as storytellers, singers, and some painters, who depended on them. The cultural history of the period lies in the interaction of those two pressures.

The evidence reflects the same imbalance. Writings by officials and scholars survive in quantity, while performers, most women, and poor audiences appear mainly through the accounts of others. Later chapters of the textbook take up the family, gender, and status structures that governed these differences.

Section 41.1

The Cultural Landscape of Later Joseon

After the Imjin War and the Manchu invasions (Chapters 32–36), Joseon rebuilt villages, fields, and institutions, and the recovery described in Chapter 39 brought larger markets and a larger Seoul. Cultural life changed with these conditions. More books were copied and printed, performers found paying audiences in towns and markets, and painters turned to subjects beyond royal ceremony and scholarly landscape. This chapter follows those changes through literature, performance, and painting.

It is better to speak of several overlapping cultural worlds than of one. The court needed ceremonial records, portraits, and music. Scholar households valued poetry, essays, and calligraphy in literary Chinese. Commercial entertainment in towns and markets depended on audiences who paid in coin or goods. Within households, women and other family members read, copied, and recited texts, and made letters and verse. A single person could move among these worlds: a yangban might compose a poem in literary Chinese in the morning and listen to a sung tale in the evening.

The overlap did not mean equality. Access to education, leisure, patronage, and public recognition depended on status, gender, wealth, and place, and the people who made much of the culture studied here, such as professional singers and many painters, held low or middling social rank even when elites admired their work. The chapter therefore avoids the simple story in which an elite culture turned popular. Courtiers, scholars, professionals, women, merchants, and ordinary people all contributed, on unequal terms.

The evidence is varied and uneven. Books and paintings survive in quantity, but most were produced by or for the literate and the comfortable. Performances leave only indirect traces, such as observers' descriptions, and some of the best-documented practices, including pansori, mask dance, and minhwa, are recorded mainly from the nineteenth century onward. The chapter keeps eighteenth-century evidence separate from later material and identifies where the record is thin.

The chapter depends on its neighbors. Chapter 38 treats Practical Learning, Chapter 39 the market economy, Chapter 40 religion, and Chapter 42 family, gender, and status. Here the concern is what was made, performed, read, and seen, who took part, and what the surviving works can establish.

Section 41.2

Hangeul, Hanmun, and the Expansion of Literary Culture

Literary Chinese, called hanmun in Korean, remained the prestigious language of government, scholarship, and the civil service examinations throughout the Joseon period. A well-educated man learned to read and compose in it, and official records, histories, and most formal poetry and essays were written in it. Knowing hanmun marked membership in the educated elite.

Hangeul, promulgated in 1446 (Chapter 27), served a different range of readers and uses. It was not the language of examinations or of the state's formal documents, though the court did use it for some purposes, including translations and notices meant for people who did not read Chinese characters. In later Joseon it was used widely in letters, household writing, devotional texts, and a growing body of fiction and song texts. Women, who were largely excluded from formal education in literary Chinese, were important readers and writers of Hangeul, but they were not its only users: commoners with some schooling, and many men as well, read and wrote it.

Literacy cannot be measured. No census recorded who could read, and the term covers different skills: reading Hangeul, reading characters, writing, and composing in hanmun. These varied by gender, status, region, and occupation. Historians can say that Hangeul was far easier to learn than characters, that it reached readers beyond the examination world, and that it was used in expanding ways in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. They cannot honestly give a literacy rate.

The growth of Hangeul literature did not replace hanmun. The two coexisted, and prestige stayed with hanmun: scholars often wrote fiction and songs in Hangeul but valued their hanmun work more, and some authors wrote in both. An important question for the period is therefore not only which language a work was written in, but who read it, in what language, and through what form of reading, since a story composed in hanmun could be read in a Hangeul translation or heard when read aloud, and a Hangeul text could be copied and read in households far from where it was composed.

Historical Vocabulary
Hanmun and Hangeul

Definition: Hanmun is literary Chinese as written in Korea; Hangeul is the Korean alphabet promulgated in 1446. Historical context: Hanmun was the language of the examinations and of most state and scholarly writing; Hangeul was used in letters, household writing, devotional texts, and much vernacular fiction and song. Why it matters: The choice of script and language indicated audience and standing. Potential misunderstanding: Hangeul literature was not exclusively women's literature, and its growth did not displace hanmun, which kept its prestige.

Section 41.3

Manuscripts, Printed Books, and the Circulation of Stories

Stories reached readers and listeners in several ways. Most circulated by hand copying, which produced many versions of a work, since copyists corrected, shortened, or expanded as they wrote. Texts also circulated by woodblock printing, in which a carved block produced many copies, and by reading aloud, which let people who could not read take part. A household might therefore possess a copied manuscript, a printed book, or both, and a story might be known to people who never saw a page of it.

Copying has an important consequence for historians: many popular works do not exist in one authoritative text but in several surviving versions that differ in wording, length, and sometimes in plot. Trying to recover a single original can be misleading, and for some works the differences among versions are themselves evidence of how the story was received and altered.

Banggakbon are books printed by private publishers for sale, and the Academy of Korean Studies traces such commercial editions to the seventeenth century. Its history lists a Sayo chwison cut at Jeonju in 1648, a Myeongsim bogam cho issued under the name of Son Gi-jo in 1664, and a group of works printed between 1676 and 1686 under the joint name of Jeon I-chae and Park Chi-yu, including Gomun jinbo, Nongga jipseong, and Guhwang boyubang. Most of these early titles are classics, reference works, and practical manuals, so the first commercial printing served learned and practical readers more than it served readers of fiction.

Fiction appears in this record in the eighteenth century. The earliest surviving printed edition of Guunmong is a woodblock edition issued at Naju, whose old name Geumseong appears in its colophon. A newer Academy entry on Naju editions dates it to 1725, while the Academy's older banggakbon article gave 1728, so the date given in older references should be checked. A 2022 bibliographical study reports that the original 1725 impression is no longer extant, though later impressions of the edition survive, so the date belongs to the edition and not to a surviving first printing. The edition is also unsettled in classification: the Academy lists it among Naju's commercial publications, but notes that some scholars regard it as privately sponsored, so it cannot simply be called the first commercial edition. It was printed in literary Chinese, which matters for a chapter about Hangeul reading. A separate Seoul (Gyeongpan) edition of Guunmong is dated to around 1749 on the evidence of printed leaves that survive only as reused backing paper, a bibliographical reconstruction and not an intact edition. Together they show the novel discussed in Section 41.4 in print within a few decades of its composition, in more than one place. Such finds are fragmentary, and they show that printing of novels had begun without showing how many copies were sold or to whom.

Later scholars group the main production centers as Seoul (Gyeongpan), Jeonju (Wanpan), and Anseong (Anseongpan). Books were sold through shops called seopo, and the colophon or publication page of a surviving book sometimes records the shop, its address, and its owner, which is how scholars identify publishers. The Anseong evidence is mostly later than this chapter's period. Studies place most of Anseong's output in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, and one proposes that its printing grew out of calendar printing that may have begun around the mid-eighteenth century, a claim that remains a scholarly proposal. A study of Anseong's fiction identifies three imprints and eleven works represented by thirteen sets of woodblocks, while other work identifies more Anseong firms, including some that printed non-fiction educational books, so each count measures a different thing. Some Anseong editions copied, abridged, or took over Seoul blocks, which shows that the centers were linked.

The best-documented commercial printing of Hangeul fiction therefore dates mainly from the nineteenth century, and this chapter does not extend that fuller picture back to the eighteenth century without evidence. The same caution applies to the lending of books for a fee, associated with the term sechaekbon, for which the evidence is again stronger for the later period.

Costs and access mattered. Paper, ink, labor, and carved blocks made books expensive in a society where many people had little cash, so reading often depended on borrowing, copying, or hearing a text read by someone else. Those who could afford to buy or commission books were disproportionately wealthier households, and their tastes shaped what circulated.

Everyday Life and Social History

Stories in the Household

This is a historical composite, not a documented family. A household with a literate woman might own a copied Hangeul manuscript that was read aloud to relatives of several generations, so that listeners who could not read took part. Copies were borrowed, recopied, and altered, and the stories shared a household with letters, devotional texts, and household accounts. Evidence for such households is indirect and survives mostly from families with the means to preserve papers.

Section 41.4

Vernacular Fiction, Heroes, Dreams, and Social Criticism

Prose fiction was one of the most prominent literary developments of the period, and its history is bound up with the question of script and language. Kim Man-jung (1637–1692), a posthumous child raised by a strict mother, passed the civil examination in 1665, held several offices, suffered political reversals, and died in exile on the southern coast. The Academy of Korean Studies notes that, though a member of the Westerner Noron elite, he took an interest in Buddhism and in books that the orthodox regarded as heterodox, and credits that interest as one condition for his writing fiction. Two of his novels survive: Guunmong ("The Cloud Dream of the Nine") and Sassi namjeonggi ("The Record of Lady Sa's Journey to the South").

Guunmong opens in Tang China, where the young monk Seongjin, a disciple of the master Yukgwan at Mount Heng, meets eight fairies on a stone bridge and feels a stir of worldly desire. Expelled for it, he is reborn as Yang Soyu, who rises through the examinations, wins military victories, and marries two wives and takes six concubines, all of whom had been the fairies. At the height of his success he looks at the ruined tombs of heroes, feels the emptiness of life, and wakes to find himself again the monk Seongjin, who attains enlightenment together with the eight women. Scholars divide over what this means. Some scholars have interpreted the novel as expressing a harmony of Confucian, Buddhist, and Daoist ideas, others as a single Buddhist teaching of emptiness drawn from the Diamond Sutra, and critics reply that it shows only a basic Buddhist sense of impermanence. More recent readings stress the desires of male scholar-officials, a wish for the king's awakening, and stories of consolation and hope. The Academy also notes that, unlike earlier dream-journey tales, the dream here is a worldly success that proves hollow and a reunion that comes only on waking, and that the hero's life follows a heroic pattern in which courtship outweighs struggle.

The textual history is unusually well documented, and it bears directly on this chapter's concern with script and readership. The Academy records more than fifty surviving variants: a literary-Chinese woodblock edition printed at Geumseong (Naju) in 1725, commercial Hangeul editions, Hangeul manuscripts and typeset editions, literary-Chinese manuscripts, and editions with Korean grammatical glosses added to the Chinese. A first generation of scholars held that Kim wrote in Hangeul and that his kinsman Kim Chun-taek later translated the work into Chinese. Jeong Gyu-bok, who found a literary-Chinese manuscript he identified as the earliest, the Nojon text, argued that the original was in Chinese and that the Hangeul texts were translations. On his account the Chinese text developed from the Nojon text (before 1725) to the 1725 edition and a further edition of 1803, with Hangeul versions following each line. The Academy infers from the large number of surviving Chinese and Hangeul copies that the novel was received across divisions of class and gender. Surviving copies establish circulation and textual diversity, but exactly who read each version is hard to reconstruct, so this remains an inference. A 2022 bibliographical study by Lee Yoon-suk, reports that the original 1725 impression is not extant although later impressions survive, and points to evidence of Hangeul readership in the 1680s that bears on the language question. The question of the original language remains a scholarly argument and not a settled fact.

Sassi namjeonggi is a different kind of book. Its heroine, Sa Jeongok, marries the official Yu Yeonsu and, after nine years without a child, urges him to take a concubine. The concubine, Gyo Chaeran, bears a son, conspires with the retainer Dong Cheong, frames Sa, and has her driven from the household, becomes principal wife, and then plunders the estate and brings about Yu's exile. Sa survives through hardship, Yu comes to see the deceit, and the villains are executed and Sa restored. The Academy classifies it as a household novel and a moral novel, and the plot has been read as a veiled appeal to King Sukjong, who deposed Queen Inhyeon and raised Lady Jang to queen, to recognize his error and restore the queen. The same entry argues that the novel's central theme is better seen as Sa's virtue than as the rivalry of a wife and a concubine. It also notes that the novel is among the first in which the wicked characters' worldly desires occupy a large and detailed place, and that dreams drive the plot at several points. The setting is Ming China, and the Academy suggests this was meant to veil a sharp resistance to the present. The political reading should therefore be held alongside, not in place of, the novel's treatment of household order, women's virtue, and the desires of its villains. Versions in several volumes exist in Hangeul woodblock (Seoul) editions, manuscripts, and typeset books, as well as Kim Chun-taek's Chinese rendering.

Hong Gildong jeon ("The Tale of Hong Gildong") tells of a gifted son of a high official and a concubine, who is barred by his birth from full standing in his family and society, leaves home, and becomes a leader of a band of outlaws before finding a place elsewhere. It has often been read as a protest against the restrictions placed on children of concubines. The attribution to Heo Gyun (1569–1618), however, is a real problem, discussed below, and cannot be treated as settled.

These works criticize particular features of the social order, such as the barriers facing children of concubines or the danger of a household disordered by rivalry, but most end within accepted moral frameworks: virtue is rewarded, order is restored, and the hero frequently finds legitimacy rather than overturning the system. They are evidence of discontent and of moral and religious ideas circulating among readers, not of organized programs of reform. Readers' responses probably varied, and surviving texts cannot show how any particular person understood them.

Historical Debate

Who Wrote Hong Gildong jeon?

The question: Is the Hangeul Hong Gildong jeon that survives the work of Heo Gyun, the early seventeenth-century scholar and writer traditionally credited with it?

The traditional attribution: An early statement by Yi Sik names Heo Gyun as author of a work of this title, and later readers took this as establishing his authorship of the tale known today.
The textual problem: The surviving Hangeul versions are considerably later than Heo Gyun's lifetime, and they differ from one another. A 2024 scholarly article by Chung Kil Soo questions even the reliability of the evidence connecting Heo Gyun to an original work of this title. The problem therefore involves several matters at once: the early attribution, the existence and content of any original, and the transmission and reworking of the text before it reached its surviving forms.

Points of disagreement: evidence and interpretation, not chronology alone. Why it matters: If the surviving text is later and reworked, it is evidence of the concerns of its later readers and copyists as much as of an early seventeenth-century author. Student task: Explain why an early attribution does not by itself establish who wrote the surviving text, and identify evidence that could clarify the problem.

  1. What is the difference between an early attribution and an extant text?
  2. How do variant manuscripts complicate the idea of an original?
Section 41.5

Women, Reading, and Literary Expression

Women in Joseon were excluded from the examination system and from most formal schooling, yet some read and wrote extensively. Their writing took several forms: letters to relatives, household and devotional texts, poetry and verse narratives, and, in the palace, memoirs and records. Much of it was in Hangeul, which women of educated families often learned at home, though some women also learned characters and wrote in literary Chinese. Women's literacy therefore varied sharply by family standing and household, and only a minority of women, mostly from elite or palace circles, left writing that survives.

The best-documented example is Lady Hyegyeong (1735–1816 by the Gregorian calendar; sources using the Joseon calendar give her death as 1815), wife of Crown Prince Sado and mother of King Jeongjo, whose memoirs, known as the Hanjungnok, were written in Hangeul at several points late in her life. They are a major source on court life and on the death of her husband in 1762, but they were written decades afterward, by a participant with strong interests in how the events were remembered, so historians treat them as a powerful and partial account, not a neutral record. Palace women's writing of this kind is sometimes grouped as gungjung munhak, "palace literature," a modern scholarly label for texts produced in or about the court by women. The court also relied on women's Hangeul letters and notices for internal communication.

Some women wrote in literary Chinese at a high level. Im Yunjidang (1721–1793), a scholar from a yangban family, composed essays and commentary on Neo-Confucian philosophy in hanmun, and her work shows that a woman could engage with the central scholarly tradition when her family allowed it. She was exceptional, and her survival in the record owes much to her family's willingness to preserve her writing.

A distinct body of women's verse is called naebang gasa, "inner-quarters gasa," a form of long poem associated with the women's quarters of yangban households. Surviving examples include didactic poems on proper conduct and marriage, descriptions of seasonal work and travel, and expressions of loneliness, sorrow, or complaint about married life. Two cautions apply. Many are anonymous, so the claim that a woman wrote a given poem may rest on its subject or voice alone, which is not secure evidence. And a large share of surviving texts was recorded in the nineteenth century or later, so dating them to the eighteenth century requires separate evidence.

Women were also readers of fiction. Hangeul narratives circulated in manuscript within households, and women appear in later sources as eager readers and copyists. This is better documented than the idea that all Hangeul fiction was written for women, which the evidence does not support: authors included men, and readers included men of all ranks. Women's reading and writing must be reconstructed from fragments, mostly preserved by families with the means and motive to keep them, and most women, especially poor and rural women, left no writing that has survived. Chapter 42 returns to the family and status structures that shaped these opportunities.

Section 41.6

Sijo, Gasa, and the Changing World of Korean Poetry

Korean poetry in later Joseon was both written and performed, and its main vernacular forms were sung or recited as well as read. Three need distinguishing. Sijo is a short poem of three lines, treating subjects from nature and scholarly retirement to love, loyalty, and aging. Gasa is a longer form, running on in paired phrases, suited to description, travel, instruction, and narrative. Gagok is not a poetic form but a vocal tradition, in which trained singers performed sijo texts to a set musical structure with instrumental accompaniment.

These were not exclusively elite forms. Scholars composed sijo and gasa in Hangeul even while valuing hanmun poetry more highly, and the forms were also sung and composed by people outside the yangban elite. During the eighteenth century a longer, more narrative and often humorous type of sijo, called sasol sijo, became prominent, and it shows wider subject matter, including the lives of commoners, love, and satire of pretension, than the older scholarly sijo. Authorship of many such poems is anonymous or uncertain.

The development of anthologies is evidence for the changing audience. Cheongguyeongeon, compiled in 1728 by Kim Cheon-taek, a professional singer of non-yangban background, gathered sijo and related song texts for use in performance. Haedong gayo, compiled by Kim Su-jang, was another major collection and exists in stages: a compilation of 1755, an important revision of 1763, and further work through 1769, so a single date should not be attached to it, and Gagok wollyu, of 1876, appears later still. Such collections show that songs were being gathered, organized, and recopied for singers and amateurs, and that professional performers and their circles had become significant custodians of the tradition. They do not by themselves show who listened or how often, and their compilers' choices shaped what survives.

Gasa shows a different range. An example from this period is Kim In-gyeom's Ildong jangyuga, a long travel gasa recording his journey to Japan with the 1763–1764 Joseon diplomatic mission. Travel gasa of this kind give an observer's view of places and people, and they illustrate how a form associated with scholarly poetry could report on the wider world. Like all single-author accounts they represent one traveler's perspective. Poetry in this period thus reached beyond traditional scholarly settings in performers, patrons, and subject matter, though literati remained prominent authors.

Historical Vocabulary
Sijo, Gasa, and Gagok

Definition: Sijo is a short three-line poem; gasa a longer continuous poem; gagok a vocal tradition in which trained singers performed sijo texts with instrumental accompaniment. Historical context: All three were written or sung in the vernacular, with anthologies compiled in the eighteenth century. Why it matters: They show poetry as performed culture with professional participants. Potential misunderstanding: Gagok names a way of singing, not a kind of verse, and these forms were not limited to the yangban elite.

Section 41.7

Satire, Humor, and Literary Criticism of Society

Satire was a way of criticizing social conduct inside the limits of what could be safely written. The most celebrated satires of the eighteenth century come from Park Ji-won (1737–1805), a scholar of yangban birth associated with the Practical Learning reformers whose ideas Chapter 38 discusses. In 1780 he traveled to Qing China as part of an embassy, and his travel record, Yeolha ilgi ("The Jehol Diary"), recorded his observations. He also wrote short prose pieces, among them Yangbanjeon ("The Tale of the Yangban") and Hojil ("The Tiger's Rebuke"), which circulated among his writings. The two pieces do not share a textual home: Yangbanjeon is included in the Bangyeonggak oejeon group within Park's collected writings, while Hojil appears in the Gwannae jeongsa portion of Yeolha ilgi, so neither should be assumed to belong to both collections or to have been composed on the same occasion. All were composed in literary Chinese, and their readership was therefore the educated.

Yangbanjeon tells of an impoverished yangban in debt for grain from the state. A wealthy commoner offers to pay his debt in exchange for the title. A local official drafts a contract listing the demands of the status, which the commoner finds so burdensome that he withdraws, and a second contract that lists privileges instead is rejected by him as robbery. The story mocks the idea that status carries moral worth, and it points at the gap between the ideals attached to the yangban and their conduct, as well as at the commercial power of wealthy commoners. Because it comes from within the elite, it should be read as an elite critique of its own class, not as a view from below.

Hojil takes the form of a tiger's lecture to a self-righteous Confucian scholar, in which the tiger points to human cruelty and hypocrisy and treats the scholar's claims to virtue with contempt. Its target is moral pretension, and it uses the animal's voice as a mask for the author's irony. Like Yangbanjeon, it shows that a scholar could attack hypocrisy through humor, and it was apparently circulated among a literate audience that appreciated its wit.

These texts need careful interpretation. They demonstrate that criticism of status pretension and moral hypocrisy was possible and sometimes admired, and they belong to a broader literary and intellectual world in which educated men debated reform. They are not evidence of an organized movement against the status order, and their authors remained members of that order. Satire also appeared in other forms, including vernacular fiction, sung narrative, and performance, and the later sections of this chapter show that humor at the expense of pretension was not confined to written Chinese prose. The evidence cannot show how most people outside the literate elite understood the same targets, since their views survive, if at all, only indirectly.

Section 41.8

Oral Storytelling and Professional Entertainers

Much of the culture of later Joseon was heard, not read. Professional entertainers, often grouped under the term gwangdae, included singers, acrobats, clowns, and musicians who performed at markets, village festivals, private banquets, and the gatherings of local officials. They held low social standing even when their skills were admired, and their work depended on payment or gifts from audiences and patrons. The category is a broad one: acrobats, comic performers, and narrative singers practiced different crafts, and this chapter does not treat them as a single occupation.

Storytelling of fiction was also a trade. A professional reader, called a jeongisu, recited popular tales to paying listeners in Seoul and other towns. Two separate sources must be kept apart. Jo Su-sam (1762–1849) described the trade itself in Chujae giyi, a series of seven-character quatrains from the early nineteenth century, not necessarily a picture of the eighteenth-century trade, in volume 7 of his collected works, the Chujaejip. The Academy of Korean Studies describes it as the only text that introduces the storyteller (jeongisu) in this way, and the account shows a storyteller who recited novels, worked at regular places in Seoul, and broke off at an exciting moment to collect money before continuing, a practice known as yojeonbeop. This is evidence for the commercial practice of storytelling. A different record, preserved in the Annals of King Jeongjo under 1790, reports that a listener at a Jongno tobacco shop stabbed a professional reader during a recitation. The identification of the tale as the story of General Im Gyeongeop, whose betrayal and death is said to have stirred the listener's anger, comes from a separate account attributed to Sim No-seung, so not every detail of the incident belongs to the royal annals. This reported incident illustrates one contemporary account of intense audience engagement; it does not establish that violence at such performances was common.

Performers learned by apprenticeship: a young person studied under a master, memorized repertory, and practiced voice and technique over years. For narrative singing this is documented in detail only for the nineteenth century. Written texts and oral performance influenced each other. A tale known from a manuscript might be recited, shortened, or elaborated in performance, and a performed tale might later be copied down, so historians cannot treat "oral" and "written" tradition as separate streams.

Evidence for these performers comes largely from observers, usually literate men of higher status, whose accounts reflect their own tastes and sometimes contempt. Performers seldom left their own records. The audiences are therefore easier to describe than the performers' own views of their craft.

Section 41.9

The Origins and Development of Pansori

Pansori is a form of sung narrative performed by one singer, the sorikkun, accompanied by one drummer, the gosu, who beats the rhythm on a barrel drum and calls out encouragement. The singer alternates sung passages (sori, or chang), spoken passages (aniri), and gesture (ballim), working over a set of rhythmic patterns (jangdan) that vary in speed and mood. Much of the art lies in improvisation within a received framework and in the exchange between singer and audience, whose spoken exclamations of approval are part of the performance.

How pansori began is debated. Scholars have proposed connections to narrative songs of ritual specialists, to folk storytelling, and to the repertoire of professional entertainers, and no one theory has settled the question. It is safer to say that a tradition of sung narrative was taking shape in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries than to name a single origin or a first performer.

The earliest strong evidence is a written account from the mid-eighteenth century (see the Primary Source Window below), which shows that the story of Chunhyang was being performed by 1754. Much else that is celebrated about pansori belongs to the nineteenth century: named virtuoso singers, the selection of a standard repertory from a larger earlier one, the development of regional vocal styles, and the patronage of wealthy households and of the court. This chapter treats those developments as the later flowering of a tradition whose eighteenth-century form is only partly known, and warns against reading the nineteenth-century repertory and style back into the earlier period.

Primary Source Window

Yu Jin-han, Manhwabon Chunhyangga, 1754

Creator and date: Yu Jin-han, whose literary name was Manhwadang, often shortened to Manhwa; dated 1754. Type: a seven-character narrative poem in classical Chinese, traditionally described as two hundred lines (gu), composed after the author heard a performance of Chunhyangga. Its original title was Gasa Chunhyangga Ibaekgu, and it survives in Yu's collected writings, the Manhwajip; "Manhwabon" means roughly "Manhwa's version."

Context: Yu was a literate man of scholarly background who turned an oral tale into a poem in the prestigious written language. This makes the poem a record of a performed story filtered through an observer's choices, not a transcript of what the singer sang.

Paraphrase (not a quotation):

The poem tells the story of a young woman, Chunhyang, who refuses the demands of a local magistrate after her relationship with a young official's son, and it follows the consequences of her refusal. One concrete difference noted by the Academy of Korean Studies concerns arrangement: in the poem Chunhyang recounts her sufferings under the magistrate to Yi Mong-nyong when they meet in prison, so those events reach the reader through her telling, whereas later versions arrange the narrative differently. Other portrayals of characters also differ from the nineteenth-century versions.

Analysis: The poem shows that the tale of Chunhyang was already performed and well enough known by 1754 for a scholar to render it in verse, and it preserves a version older than any surviving pansori text. Its differences from later versions show that the story changed over time. Limitations: It cannot show how the singer performed, with what music, gesture, or improvisation, nor can it show the audience's reaction. It is the work of one observer writing in a different language and form. Perspective: a scholar listener; the performer and most of the audience are silent.

Section 41.10

Pansori Narratives: Love, Loyalty, Family, and Social Order

Five stories form the repertory known today: Chunhyangga, Simcheongga, Heungboga, Sugungga, and Jeokbyeokga. Their selection as a standard group reflects nineteenth-century developments, since earlier performers apparently sang a larger and more varied body of tales, so the five should not be taken as the whole eighteenth-century repertory.

Chunhyangga tells of Chunhyang, a young woman of a gisaeng household, who forms a bond with Yi Mong-nyong, the son of a local official, and exchanges pledges of fidelity with him; surviving versions differ on whether this counts as a formal or secret marriage. When he is called away, a new magistrate demands her attention, and she refuses him and is imprisoned and threatened with death until Yi Mong-nyong returns in the guise of a secret royal inspector and punishes the magistrate. It treats fidelity, the dignity of a woman of low status, and the abuse of official power. Simcheongga concerns a blind father and the daughter, Simcheong, who sells herself to sailors as a sacrifice to the sea to obtain the rice her father promised a Buddhist temple in the hope of regaining his sight; she survives, is reunited with him, and his sight is restored; it centers on filial devotion and sacrifice, and also on poverty.

Heungboga contrasts two brothers, the generous younger one and the greedy elder, in a tale of poverty, fortune, and moral reckoning, with strong comic and social elements. Sugungga is an animal fable in which the Dragon King of the sea, ill, sends a turtle to bring back a rabbit's liver, and the rabbit's trick shows how cleverness can defeat power, a story often read as an allegory of rulers and officials. Jeokbyeokga draws on the Chinese tale of the Battle of Red Cliff, familiar to readers of the Romance of the Three Kingdoms, and gives the singer scope for battle narrative and rhetoric.

These narratives could reinforce familiar moral values, such as filial piety and fidelity, while also criticizing greedy or abusive officials and mocking pretension. Humor and exaggeration allowed audiences of differing status to take different meanings from the same performance. The surviving texts, however, are generally later than the first performances and were written down in the nineteenth century or after, so they should not be treated as eighteenth-century scripts, and what can be said about any performer's own intentions is limited.

Section 41.11

Mask Dance, Puppetry, Festivals, and Public Entertainment

Alongside narrative singing, Korean communities enjoyed masked drama. Talchum or talnori is the general term for masked dance-drama, in which performers act out a series of comic scenes, called gwajang, with figures such as an arrogant noble, a servant who mocks him, a corrupt monk, and a quarrelling old couple. Puppet plays, with a character such as Kkoktugaksi in later records, offered similar humor. Many performances were tied to seasonal gatherings and festivals, when villages and neighborhoods came together.

Regional names point to regional traditions. In Seoul and Gyeonggi the masked plays are called sandae-nori; in Hwanghae in the northwest, haeseo talchum, of which Bongsan is the best known; east of the Nakdong River in Gyeongsang, yaryu; and west of it, ogwangdae, with Tongyeong and Goseong among the surviving examples. A further tradition belongs to the village ritual lineage, as at Hahoe in Andong, where masked play was part of a communal shamanic rite and whose carved masks are a National Treasure. Scholars describe two broad lineages, one of village ritual play and one of the sandae-nori of the capital region.

The performers differed too. A scholar cited in press accounts of the Korean mask-dance tradition, Jeon Gyeong-uk, has argued that the original capital-region plays (bon-sandae), associated with centers such as Aeogae, Sajikgol, Gupabal, and Nokbeon, were transmitted mostly by professional entertainers, while the plays that spread from them (byeol-sandae, haeseo, yaryu, and ogwangdae) were carried on by local residents, among them farmers, minor officials, and shamans. Songpa and Yangju are derivative traditions that the National Institute of Korean History dates to roughly the early to mid-nineteenth century, so their transmission differs from that of the original centers. The content varied accordingly. Goseong ogwangdae features the servant Malttugi mocking several yangban, though its present form was shaped by developments around 1910 and so cannot stand for an eighteenth-century Goseong performance; Bongsan satirizes both monks and nobles; and the Yangju byeol-sandae gives particular weight to conflict between yangban and commoners. Ridicule of pretension was therefore common but not uniform, and it took place within a festival setting that allowed temporary license, not in organized opposition.

Evidence from the eighteenth century is thin. An eighteenth-century description survives in the poem Namseong gwanhuija by Gang I-cheon (1768–1801), composed in 1789 from his memory of puppet and masked performances he had seen outside Seoul's South Gate in 1778, with recognizable scenes involving monks and scholars. The Nakseongyeondo in the Hwaseong seongyeok uigwe, printed in 1801, depicts entertainment at the celebration of the fortress's completion in 1796, so such plays reached state events as well as villages; the date of the event and the date of the book should be kept apart. But the fullest scripts, such as O Cheong's Bongsan text, were recorded in the twentieth century, and many of the best-known forms are described as taking their present shape in the later nineteenth century. This chapter therefore uses the plays to illustrate what kinds of public entertainment existed and who performed them, and it avoids reconstructing a particular eighteenth-century performance.

Section 41.12

Court Painters, Literati Artists, and Artistic Patronage

Painting in Joseon was produced by people of different standing for different purposes. The royal painting bureau, the Dohwaseo, was a small office whose institutional rules appear in the Gyeongguk daejeon law code. According to the Academy of Korean Studies, it provided for one senior supervisor (jeje) and two byeolje of junior sixth rank as regular officials, and for twenty painters (hwawon) in the category of miscellaneous posts, with three further posts of low military rank for those who continued to serve after their terms ended. The office stood in the Gyeonpyeong ward of the capital, in what is now the Gongpyeong-dong and Gyeonji-dong area of central Seoul. The original code also provided fifteen painting students (hwahak saengdo) under the Ministry of Rites, and the revised Sokdaejeon of 1746 doubled that number to thirty, while the Daejeon tongpyeon of 1785 raised the number of authorized painters from twenty to thirty. The Academy notes that hwawon were treated as little different from lowly craftsmen, so that in the painting office's earlier organization the fifth-rank post of byeoljwa, the highest a painter could reach, was filled where possible by a scholar-official who knew painting. That post belonged to the office before its reorganization and downgrade to the sixth-rank Dohwaseo in 1470, whose later structure included byeolje officials. The code of 1865 formally codified a post of painting instructor (hwahak gyosu), though instructors are documented earlier, so instruction did not begin in 1865.

Painters entered by examination. The test (chwijae) examined bamboo, landscape, figures, birds and animals, and flowers and plants. Its subjects were therefore the genres of appreciation painting valued by scholars, though much of a court painter's actual work was practical. Hwawon painted royal portraits and portraits of meritorious officials, made the paintings that recorded state ceremonies and banquets, illustrated the ceremonial records (uigwe), drew maps, and made decorative screens for palace rooms and weddings. Some accompanied official missions: Kim Yu-seong, a hwawon, went to Japan with the 1763–1764 diplomatic embassy, the same mission that Kim In-gyeom described in his travel gasa (Section 41.6). Hwawon were also called on to paint for royal relatives and scholar-officials, and these side commissions produced much of what art historians now value.

Scholar-painters, usually yangban, painted as an expression of learning and character, often landscapes, bamboo, and plum, and many looked down on painting done for pay. The categories overlapped: hwawon were professionals of middle status who nonetheless trained in the scholarly genres, and scholars collected and commissioned their work. Training for hwawon ran through the bureau's examination and its student painters. In 1783 Jeongjo formalized the chabi daeryeong hwawon system (also read jabi), initially selecting ten painters for special royal assignments, so it was a system of painters picked for royal duties, not simply a training program.

Patronage shaped subject, but the evidence has limits. The court's commissions are well documented because ceremonial paintings and uigwe survive with records. For works that hwawon made for other patrons, the buyer is usually unknown, so historians describe the patronage structures, court demand, elite collecting, and private commissions, without assigning a known purchaser to each painting. The three painters in the next sections, Jeong Seon, Kim Hong-do, and Shin Yun-bok, worked in different positions within this system, and only two of them belonged to the Dohwaseo.

Section 41.13

Jeong Seon and True-View Landscape Painting

Jeong Seon (1676–1759) came from a yangban family of modest means and held several local offices, and he was not a Dohwaseo painter. He first climbed the Diamond Mountains in 1711 and returned to them repeatedly, and Mount Geumgang became the subject he painted most. He is associated with jingyeong sansuhwa, "true-view landscape painting," which depicts identifiable Korean places rather than idealized Chinese scenery. Reference accounts describe it as a tradition of painting real scenery that existed in earlier Korean painting, to which Jeong added brush methods of the Southern School newly fashionable in the eighteenth century, in a setting of scholars who traveled to famous sites and valued things Korean.

Geumgang jeondo, "Complete View of Mount Geumgang," is dated 1734, when Jeong was fifty-nine by traditional Korean counting (fifty-eight by elapsed years), and is a National Treasure. It shows the whole inner Diamond Mountains within a round composition, seen from above as though from the air. Sharp rock peaks are drawn with thin, long vertical strokes dragged downward, while soft earthen slopes are made with ink dots, a contrast that commentators read as a balance of hard and soft, and the whole is set in light ink and color on paper. The painter does not copy what the eye would see from one place. He selects, compresses, and reorganizes the view to convey the mountain's character.

Inwang jesaekdo, "Clearing after Rain at Mount Inwang," is dated 1751, painted when he was seventy-six by traditional counting (seventy-five by elapsed years), in ink on paper about 79 by 138 centimeters. Its inscription gives the date as the last ten days of the leap month of the sinmi year, and it is now in the National Museum of Korea. The foreground is seen from above and the upper mountain from below, which gives the sense of standing before the mountain. The granite rocks are built up in dense, repeated layers of ink and broad vertical strokes made with a tilted brush, giving weight and mass. Inwang is in fact a pale granite hill, so the black rock is the painter's rendering of the mountain's force and not its color. Mist after rain is left as empty paper, and pines, trees, and houses in the foreground tie fog and peaks into one scene.

Tradition connects the painting to the illness of Jeong's friend, the poet Yi Byeong-yeon (1671–1751), who died on the twenty-ninth day of that leap month, and a story says Jeong painted it with him in mind. That link rests on later tradition, and recent scholarship questions it, so it should not be treated as established. "True-view" did not mean photographic realism or a rejection of Chinese painting. Jeong used methods from earlier Chinese and Korean practice to interpret actual sites, and the shift lies in subject and attention. His manner was continued by followers, and later in the century other approaches overlapped with it: Gang Sehwang called for a more realistic style informed by Western methods, and Kim Hong-do worked in several modes, so Korean painting did not move along a single line of increasing realism.

Historical Vocabulary
Jingyeong sansuhwa

Why it matters: it made the Korean landscape a central subject. Potential misunderstanding: it was neither photographic nor a break with Chinese painting traditions.

Section 41.14

Kim Hong-do and the Painting of Everyday Life

Genre painting in the sense of pictures of daily activity existed in Korea before the eighteenth century, but the Metropolitan Museum's essay by Eleanor Soo-ah Hyun notes that these earlier works were generally idealized images of yangban life. In the eighteenth century the subject shifted toward the commoner, and, interestingly, literati artists were the first to draw attention to non-elite activities. Professional court painters then brought the genre to maturity. The essay credits Kim Hong-do (born 1745; his death date is uncertain, given as about 1806 by the Met and as after 1816 in a National Museum record), known as Danwon, a court painter of middle-people (jungin) background and a favorite of Yeongjo and Jeongjo for his range: he painted landscapes, Buddhist and Daoist subjects, portraits, scholars' objects, flowers, and animals.

His best-known genre work is the album now called Genre Paintings by Danwon, twenty-five leaves in the National Museum of Korea. Its subjects are ordinary men and women at work and leisure, some set in landscape and some on blank grounds, with figures arranged within a coherent space. The faces are individually drawn, not repeated, and the essay singles out humor, liveliness, and narrative force. The wrestling scene, Ssireum, shows spectators ringed around two locked wrestlers on an empty ground, and the schoolroom scene shows a weeping pupil before a teacher and amused classmates. A contemporary, the painter and critic Gang Sehwang (1713–1791), reportedly praised Kim's depictions of street scenes, ferry crossings, shops, and examination scenes as work that left viewers astonished.

Kim Deuk-sin (1754–1822), of a family of painters, entered the Dohwaseo at twenty and was among the court painters chosen for a portrait of Jeongjo, as Kim Hong-do also was. His Chasing a Cat shows a lower-class couple pursuing a cat that has stolen a chick. The Met essay describes him as continuing Kim Hong-do's comedy of situation while giving scenes a more articulated setting and a more naturalistic mode. The album of twenty-five leaves is presented by the National Museum as Kim Hong-do's work, though scholars have debated its precise attribution, a caution that does not remove the value of the compositions as evidence.

The Met essay ties the rise of genre painting and true-view landscape to wider change. After the fall of the Ming dynasty in 1644, Joseon scholars questioned China's role as the center of civilization and turned to native subjects, Practical Learning examined land, class, and the economy, and vernacular writing in Hangeul increased across social classes. The two painting types are cited as evidence of a growing emphasis on a Korean cultural identity. This is one interpretation, and the shift in painting cannot be explained mechanically by the Ming collapse.

What can the paintings establish? They show that painters and their patrons found ordinary activities worth depicting, and they supply detail of clothing, tools, and gesture. They are not documentary records. The scenes are composed, the figures individually staged, hardship is often softened, and the intended audience is usually unidentified. The paintings therefore tell more about what was considered a pleasing and meaningful subject than about the actual conditions of labor, and the commoners they show did not choose how they were seen.

Section 41.15

Shin Yun-bok, Urban Leisure, and Gender in Painting

Shin Yun-bok (about 1758–after 1813), known as Hyewon, was also a Dohwaseo painter, and the Met essay stresses that his subject matter differs entirely from Kim Hong-do's. Where Kim portrayed the lives of everyday men and women, Shin became famous for pictures of yangban leisure, especially involving gisaeng, entertainers of low legal status. Double-Sword Dance, from the album Genre Paintings by Hyewon, plainly shows this world of leisure. His figures are fashionably dressed and drawn with fine, flowing line and delicate color, set in sparse gardens, pavilions, and streams.

Scene from a Dano Day is the most explicit. Women bathe and arrange their hair by a stream during the Dano festival, with bare legs and breasts shown, while two young monks watch from behind rocks. The Met essay calls it arguably Shin's most risqué painting and notes that the voyeuristic situation sexualizes the image. It also notes that this overt eroticism, seen also in his paintings of amorous couples, was daring in a Confucian society that prized women's chastity and men's self-restraint and regarded sexual explicitness and excessive leisure as leading to moral corruption. The art historian Saehyang Chung has studied the iconography of common women washing clothes by a stream that lies behind the scene.

The essay asks whether such paintings are commentaries on yangban lifestyle and mores, and historians do not agree on the answer. They may idealize, flirt, satirize, or reflect a patron's taste, and the purchasers of individual works are generally unidentified. The viewpoint is that of male painters and male viewers, and the women portrayed, including gisaeng of low status, appear as seen by others, not as they might have described themselves. The paintings are evidence for tastes and for how an urban society pictured pleasure, not for the lives of those depicted.

Section 41.16

Minhwa, Decorative Painting, and Popular Symbolism

Minhwa, often translated "folk painting," is a later art-historical category, popularized by the twentieth-century collector and critic Yanagi Sōetsu, and refers to decorative paintings made for households and other settings outside the prestigious court and scholar traditions, though the boundary was porous. The tradition includes tigers with magpies (hojakdo), flowers such as peonies, birds, fish, mountain-spirit pictures, character paintings, and groups of long-life symbols, painted on folding screens, doors, and sheets hung in rooms. Many carried wishes for protection, long life, prosperity, many sons, and success. Museum labels date well-known examples of the tiger and magpie to the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, while most surviving screens of other types are nineteenth-century. The imagery of minhwa drew on older stories and beliefs, so its motifs cannot be treated as inventions of the late period.

The clearest case of exchange between court and popular painting is chaekgeori, pictures of books and prized objects. A museum account traces it to Qing paintings of display cabinets, duobaoge, a proposed influence that Korean painters substantially adapted, and ties its late-eighteenth-century vogue to Jeongjo, who valued rule through books. The more specific chaekgado shows books and objects on shelves in a bookcase, and its beginnings are presumed to lie in screens made by court painters under Jeongjo, with Jang Han-jong (1768–1815) and, in the nineteenth century, Yi Hyeong-rok (born 1808) among the best-known court painters of the type. Their patrons were at first at the top of society.

The type spread. A museum account says that from the later nineteenth century chaekgeori reached wealthy families distant from scholarship and then commoners, and that its subjects grew more varied. Folk examples are generally smaller, and many drop the bookcase and show books, writing implements, antiques, fruit, and vases on a desk or table, so not every chaekgeori shows shelves. The objects in the pictures have also been read as evidence of late-eighteenth-century change: a larger yangban stratum, economic revival, and interest in Qing goods. Such screens were displayed in men's reception rooms and studies, and that placement says as much about their owners' self-image as about the pictures.

The producers and buyers of minhwa are hard to identify. Painters were often anonymous, and surviving examples point to a range of settings, from modest households to elite and court patronage, so low cost and low status cannot be assumed for every piece. Elite designs and popular decorative traditions also influenced each other. Because so much of the evidence is nineteenth-century or later, this chapter treats minhwa as a tradition with earlier roots that is documented mainly in later works and does not date a given screen to the eighteenth century without evidence.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Culture in Later Joseon

What changed: Hangeul literature, sung narrative, true-view landscape, and genre scenes became prominent. What continued: the prestige of hanmun, court patronage, and the status order. When: gradually, with much of the evidence from the late eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. Who experienced change most directly: urban audiences, professional performers, and court painters; rural and poor people experienced more continuity. Why it matters: it shows innovation within an unequal order.

Section 41.17

Markets, Patronage, and the Expanding Cultural Audience

Culture in later Joseon was paid for through several different channels, and the evidence shows which ones were documented. The court paid salaried painters in the Dohwaseo and commissioned portraits, ceremonial paintings, and uigwe (Section 41.12). Scholar-officials and wealthy households commissioned or collected paintings: chaekgeori screens were displayed in men's reception rooms and studies, and the court painters who made the earliest ones worked for patrons at the top of society (Section 41.16). Performers and storytellers lived by audience payment. The jeongisu of Jo Su-sam's account broke off at a tense moment to collect money before continuing, and singers such as Kim Cheon-taek, a professional of non-yangban background, compiled song anthologies that circulated among singers and amateurs (Section 41.6).

Books followed a different route. Early commercial printing was of classics and practical manuals, sold through bookshops (seopo), and printed fiction appears only in small and uncertain numbers in the eighteenth century, with the denser evidence of commercial fiction publishing belonging to the nineteenth (Section 41.3). Much reading depended on borrowing, copying, or hearing a text read aloud, so a household's reading was not the same as its book purchases. Public festivals and mask dance depended on a different economy again: villagers were performers, spectators, and sometimes supporters of a communal celebration (Section 41.11), and the plays reached state occasions as well as villages.

Markets enlarged these audiences without removing their limits. Seoul's growth and regional commerce, examined in Chapter 39, increased demand for entertainment and goods, but those with cash and leisure still shaped much production. The poorest could hear a story or watch a festival more easily than they could own a book or a painting, and performers, many painters, and the women portrayed in paintings were rarely the ones who set the terms of what was made. The relationship also ran in several directions: court styles moved into popular painting, popular storytelling reached a scholar's poem, and vernacular and literary traditions borrowed from each other.

The evidence limits what can be said. For most individual works the patron or buyer is unknown, so historians describe the structures of court demand, elite collecting, and paying audiences without assigning a known purchaser to each work. Regional differences, such as the later association of the southwest with pansori styles, point to varied local circumstances that the surviving sources reveal only in part. Categories used by historians, such as minhwa, are later art-historical labels, associated with twentieth-century collectors and critics, and should not be assumed to have been the terms used by the painters and customers of the eighteenth century.

Section 41.18

Cultural Creativity, Social Boundaries, and the Legacy of Later Joseon

Later Joseon was culturally creative. Hangeul literature expanded beside literary Chinese, narrative fiction found readers in manuscript and print, sung narrative took shape, professional entertainers gathered paying audiences, and painters turned to recognizable landscapes and to everyday and urban life. Elite and popular traditions overlapped and influenced one another, as a scholar's poem on a sung tale, a court painter's screen that became a household fashion, and a satire of yangban pretension all show. The period also reflected a widening interest in Korean places, people, and language, though that interest is one interpretation of the evidence and not a single cause of the cultural change.

The same period kept firm boundaries. Status, gender, and wealth governed who was educated, who was paid, and who was remembered. Professional singers and many painters held modest or low rank, even when scholars and kings admired their work. Women's writing survives chiefly from privileged and palace households, the women in many paintings are seen through male eyes, and the lives of poor and rural people appear mostly through the accounts of others. Satire and ridicule of pretension were widespread, yet they came from scholars and from festival settings that allowed temporary license, not from organized opposition to the status order.

The surviving books, paintings, and performance records show what was made, by whom when that is known, and for what kinds of audience. They cannot show how most people understood these works or what the performances actually sounded like. Much of what is celebrated, especially in pansori, mask dance, and minhwa, is documented chiefly from the nineteenth century, so earlier practice has to be inferred from a few precise traces: Yu Jin-han's 1754 poem, Jo Su-sam's early-nineteenth-century account of a storyteller, Gang I-cheon's 1789 poem on performances he had seen in 1778, and the dated paintings of Jeong Seon, alongside Kim Hong-do works whose exact dating or attribution is debated. Those unknowns are part of the evidence, not a gap to be filled by assumption.

The chapter's themes carry forward. Chapter 42 examines the family, gender, and status structures that governed who took part in this culture, and the performance and publishing traditions developed further in the nineteenth century, when much of the surviving record begins.

Chapter Summary

After the wars of the early seventeenth century, recovery and commerce created new audiences for books, performance, and pictures, but status still governed access.

Hangeul expanded alongside literary Chinese, which kept its prestige. Stories circulated by copying, print, and reading aloud, with the better-documented commercial book trade belonging to the nineteenth century.

Fiction and satire criticized status barriers and hypocrisy, yet generally operated within accepted moral frameworks. The authorship of Hong Gildong jeon and the original language of Guunmong, whose 1725 Naju edition was printed in literary Chinese, remain debated.

Women read and wrote in Hangeul and, rarely, in hanmun, but the surviving record is selective and often late.

Poetry circulated as performed culture, through sijo, gasa, gagok, and anthologies compiled in the eighteenth century.

Pansori emerged from sung narrative, with Yu Jin-han's 1754 poem the key early evidence and most refinement belonging to the nineteenth century. Mask dance and puppetry are known mainly from later records.

Painting diversified: Jeong Seon made Korean landscape a major subject, Kim Hong-do and Shin Yun-bok depicted daily and urban life, and minhwa served household aspirations.

The central historical lesson is the interaction of expansion and hierarchy. New audiences, new subjects, and new professions appeared, yet they developed inside a status order that decided who could be educated, paid, and remembered.

A second lesson concerns evidence. The best-documented forms, such as pansori repertories, mask dance, and minhwa, are mostly later than the period they illuminate, and early traces such as Yu Jin-han's poem or Jo Su-sam's verse account show how much of the story is reconstructed from an observer's choices.

Markets enlarged audiences without removing inequalities. Surviving evidence shows what was made and valued, not the full inner life of the society that made it.

Chapter Reflection

Later Joseon was a period of cultural experiment, in which new subjects and audiences appeared inside an unequal social order.

The chapter also teaches a method: separate when something began from when it is best documented, and ask whose perspective a source preserves.

Its themes of status, gender, and criticism lead into Chapter 42, and its popular traditions continued to develop in the nineteenth century.

The chapter also shows how a society can be inventive and unequal at once. The same period that produced satire of yangban pretension, sung tales of an abused woman defying a magistrate, and paintings of schoolboys and wrestlers also kept the barriers that placed performers and many painters at low social standing. Reading these works well means holding both facts in view.
