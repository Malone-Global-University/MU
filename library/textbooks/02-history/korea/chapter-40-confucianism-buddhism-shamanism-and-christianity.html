Confucianism, Buddhism, Shamanism, and Christianity

Korean History: Ancient Kingdoms to a Divided Peninsula

Chapter Opening

Neo-Confucianism dominated Joseon's official moral and political order, but it did not monopolize religious life.

This chapter examines how Koreans experienced, practiced, defended, contested, and transformed religious life between roughly 1600 and 1800, with the early nineteenth century as a transition.

It treats Confucian ritual, Buddhist institutions, shamanic and village religion, and the emergence of Korean Catholicism as intersecting parts of one society, not four separate doctrines.

The chapter's central argument is that official orthodoxy and lived practice coexisted, and that Catholicism introduced new possibilities and conflicts.

Evidence includes court and judicial records, temple and gazetteer records, ritual manuals, and Chinese-language Catholic writings. Most were produced by elites and officials, so ordinary practice must often be inferred.

Intellectual debates belong to Chapter 38, family and status to Chapter 42, and the major persecutions to Chapter 45.

Historical Context

Joseon's founders adopted Neo-Confucianism as official ideology and restricted Buddhism. The Imjin War and Manchu invasions (Chapters 31–36) devastated temples, villages, and communities and shaped religious recovery.

Contact with Qing China carried Chinese-language books, including Catholic writings, into Korea, a channel also used by merchants and envoys.

Section 40.1

The Religious Landscape of Later Joseon

The invasions of the 1590s and 1620s–1630s left Joseon with devastated villages, damaged temples, and a court intent on restoring moral and social order. In that setting Neo-Confucianism was the state's official ideology, but religious life was broader than any official doctrine. Historians therefore distinguish three levels: official orthodoxy, expressed in royal and state ritual and in Confucian institutions; institutional religion, notably Buddhist monasteries and their clergy; and household and community practice, including ancestral rites, shamanic ceremonies, and village rituals.

These levels overlapped. A scholar might uphold Confucian ritual in public, a woman of the same household might sponsor prayers at a temple, and a village might hold a communal rite for a guardian spirit. Religious affiliation was not necessarily exclusive, and the modern idea that a person belongs to one religion fits this society poorly. The chapter therefore asks what people did, not only what they professed.

Neo-Confucian authority did not eliminate other traditions for several reasons. The state regulated and criticized Buddhist and shamanic practice but lacked the means to end it, and both met needs that Confucian ritual did not, such as funerary and memorial services, healing, and prayers for family welfare. Official criticism is also poor evidence that a practice disappeared; a ban or complaint often shows the opposite.

Elite prescriptions and ordinary behavior differed. Ritual manuals and court regulations set out what proper conduct should be, while farmers, women, and the poor often had neither the means nor the inclination to follow elite models exactly. The gap between norm and practice is a theme of the whole chapter.

The evidence has clear limits. Court records show state ritual and official debate; ritual manuals show prescriptions; temple records and local documents show institutions, land, and patrons; and gazetteers describe shrines and customs. Almost all were written by officials, scholars, or clergy. They are strongest for institutions and weakest for the beliefs of ordinary people, which historians must infer from indirect traces such as complaints, regulations, and surviving ritual sites.

Section 40.2

Neo-Confucianism and the Religious Authority of the State

Joseon's rulers treated ritual as a foundation of legitimacy. By performing correct ceremonies for ancestors, land and grain, and the sages, the king presented his rule as part of a moral and cosmic order, not simply as the exercise of force. Ritual also defined hierarchy: who performed which rite, where, and with what music and offerings expressed rank within the court and between the kingdom and its neighbors.

The core of the system was a code of state ceremonies. The Gukjo oryeui, completed in 1474 under Seongjong by Sin Suk-ju, Jeong Cheok, and others after earlier attempts under Sejong and Sejo, set out the five categories of state ritual: auspicious or sacrificial (gilrye), celebratory (garye), ceremonies for guests (binrye), military (gunrye), and mourning (hyungrye). It drew on Chinese precedent and earlier Korean custom and, with the Gyeongguk daejeon law code, became a basic canon, later supplemented by continuations such as the Gukjo sok oryeui. Its compilers sought to replace the earlier mixture of native, Buddhist, and Confucian ceremony with Confucian order.

Among the sacrificial rites were those at the royal ancestral shrine, the Jongmyo, and at the Sajik altars for the spirits of land and grain. The Jongmyo housed the tablets of deceased kings and queens, where the ruler performed ceremonies accompanied by music and dance, and the Sajik altars linked the dynasty to agricultural prosperity. The code also provided for rites to mountains and rivers, to agricultural pioneers, and to Confucius. These institutions were established well before 1600; what belongs to this chapter's period is their maintenance and revision after the wars.

Royal ritual remained a living institution, not a fixed tradition. Rulers of the eighteenth century, including Yeongjo and Jeongjo, are associated with revisions to ceremonial practice, and supplements to the oryeui reflect changing needs. The extent of particular reforms should be checked against the ritual compilations themselves, since reference works do not describe them uniformly.

Historians sometimes call Confucian ritual an ideology rather than a religion, but the distinction is too simple. These ceremonies involved sacrifice, offerings to spirits, prescribed purification and procedure, and ideas about the relation of the living to ancestors and to cosmic forces. The code also reached ordinary people only in part: it was an official, court-centered system, with provisions such as the seasonal ancestral rites and mourning for officials and commoners as subordinate sections. State ritual was therefore both a source of royal authority and a model that officials hoped would shape wider behavior.

Section 40.3

Confucian Learning, Local Elites, and Moral Authority

Beyond the court, Confucian authority rested on local institutions. Private academies (seowon) combined teaching with the commemoration of revered scholars. They emerged in the sixteenth century, with Baegundong Seowon, founded in 1543, often cited as the first, and multiplied in later Joseon. Each held ceremonies honoring a Confucian sage or a local worthy, so the academy was at once a school, a shrine, and a gathering place for a scholarly network.

Royal recognition mattered. A seowon that received a royal tablet (saaek) became a chartered institution (saaek seowon), gaining prestige and, in many cases, privileges such as support and exemptions. Academies also became centers of local elite authority and of scholarly and political networks, and they could be sources of conflict over land, cost, and privilege.

The Seowon deungnok, a six-volume manuscript apparently compiled by the Board of Rites (Yejo), shows how the state handled academies. It covers 1642 to 1742 and reached a uniform arrangement by the mid-eighteenth century. It collects reports, petitions, and memorials from officials and students, the Board's own recommendations, and the king's replies, on the founding, royal recognition, abolition, and ceremonies of academies and shrines, on repairs and costs, on tax exemptions, and on grants of land and slaves, as well as actions against illegal conduct by people connected with them. Its period runs from the time when founding an academy was made subject to central permission, which sharply increased petitions, to the order of 1741 to demolish shrines of local worthies, portrait halls, and living-person shrines built after 1714. The compilation itself took shape as Yeongjo moved to demolish and control local academies. Academies and shrines mattered for rites, for education, for the political activity of local scholars, and for the order of rural society, and the government watched them closely. The record therefore shows seowon not only as ideal institutions but as bodies that petitioned, held property, enjoyed privileges, and could be disciplined, and it shows the state's ambivalence about them.

Village-level moral regulation took the form of communal compacts. A hyangyak (district compact) and, in smaller units, a dongyak (village compact) set rules for mutual assistance, discipline, and local order. The Academy of Korean Studies describes the dongyak as an organization created after the mid-Joseon period by resident elites to maintain a status order centered on themselves and a tax system suited to them, and as a successor to older cooperative associations. In the seventeenth century, with the spread of villages of a single lineage, dongyak often rested on lineage ties.

A well-documented case is the Buindong dongyak, begun in 1739 (Yeongjo's fifteenth year) in the Daegu area of Gyeongsang by Choe Heung-won. Its aim was to stabilize the community and prevent peasants from fleeing because of heavy taxation. It was administered through a village office with a chief chosen from the yangban, officers from the yangban or middle people, and lower officers from commoners. Members were forbidden to leave or move elsewhere, and on fixed days in spring and autumn the whole village assembled to hear the compact explained. Seating in the upper hall distinguished scholarly rank and legitimate from illegitimate descent, while the lower hall distinguished commoners from slaves. The compact also established storehouses for taxes and for relief and funeral assistance, and its provisions were borrowed from a compact written in Yean after the Imjin War.

The case shows how Confucian moral authority worked in practice: it offered real relief to distressed households and, in the same document, reinforced distinctions of rank. It also illustrates the limits of the evidence, since the compact shows what its founder prescribed, not how consistently residents complied. The intellectual history of Confucianism belongs to Chapter 38, and court politics to Chapter 37; the focus here is on moral and religious authority in local life.

Historical Vocabulary
Seowon and Jongmyo

Seowon were private Confucian academies that taught students and held ceremonies for revered scholars, some chartered by royal tablet as saaek seowon; Jongmyo was the royal ancestral shrine holding the tablets of kings and queens. Misunderstanding: Confucian institutions were not merely schools or government offices; ritual was central to them.

Section 40.4

Ancestor Veneration, Mourning, and Confucian Ritual Life

Ancestor veneration lay at the center of Confucian religious practice in households. Families honored forebears with spirit tablets, offerings, and memorial ceremonies, and with prolonged mourning when a parent died. These rites expressed filial piety, a duty to parents extending beyond death, and carried public as well as private significance, since conformity signaled proper conduct and social standing.

The ritual models came largely from Zhu Xi's Family Rituals, an influential Chinese guide to the rites of coming of age, marriage, mourning, and ancestor worship, which Joseon scholars studied and debated. The state code also included seasonal ancestral rites for officials and commoners, held in the second, fifth, eighth, and eleventh months, and a section on mourning for officials and commoners, though it was primarily concerned with the court.

Practice varied. Manuals describe prescribed ritual, which must be distinguished from evidence of what all families actually did. Households adapted the ideals unevenly: a wealthy yangban lineage could maintain a family shrine and conduct elaborate ceremonies, while poorer families had fewer resources for offerings, tablets, and extended mourning. Regional customs and Buddhist and popular habits also shaped how rites were observed, so the spread of Confucian ritual among ordinary households was gradual. Chapter 42 treats the resulting changes in inheritance, marriage, and gender hierarchy.

Ritual interpretation could also become a matter of high politics. The Yesong controversies of 1659 and 1674 concerned how long members of the royal family should wear mourning after the deaths of King Hyojong and later Queen Inseon. Officials and factions disagreed over what Confucian rules required, including whether the king should be treated as an eldest or a younger son, and the rulings that followed affected the standing of factions. The cases show that a question about the length of mourning could carry dynastic and political consequences. The factional history belongs to Chapter 37; the point here is how ritual interpretation shaped authority.

This background helps explain the ancestral-rites conflict of 1791, discussed in Section 40.15. For Joseon officials, ancestral rites were not private customs but an obligation tied to family, moral order, and the state. A person who abandoned them could therefore be seen as attacking the social order, which is why disputes over rites carried such weight.

Section 40.5

Buddhism under Neo-Confucian Rule

The Joseon state restricted Buddhism: it limited monastic institutions and clergy and reduced official patronage, and Confucian officials criticized it. Buddhism nevertheless survived. Monks were mobilized for labor and military roles during and after the wars, and temples recovered gradually after the invasions.

Section 40.6

Buddhist Monasteries, Monks, and Religious Communities

Monasteries were rebuilt in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries with support from lay patrons, including elite women, and drew on income from land and crafts such as papermaking. Monks also owed labor to the state. Monasteries linked mountain communities with lay worshippers, so their significance extended beyond the clergy.

Section 40.7

Buddhist Devotion, Ritual, and Everyday Religious Life

Lay people used temples for prayers, merit-making, funeral and memorial rites, and the printing and veneration of scriptures and images. Women were prominent patrons. Monasteries thus provided religious services that Confucian ritual did not, which helps explain Buddhism's persistence despite official disapproval.

Section 40.8

Sacred Mountains and the Blending of Religious Traditions

Mountains were sacred places. From about the seventeenth century, shrines to the mountain spirit (sansin) and the Seven Stars (Chilseong) were incorporated into temple life, showing interaction between Buddhism and local practice rather than the mere survival of unrelated older beliefs.

Section 40.9

Shamanism, Mudang, and Ritual Specialists

Ritual specialists called mudang mediated with spirits in ceremonies (gut), healed, and divined. They had low social standing and drew elite criticism, yet households of many classes sought them. Practice varied by region: some practitioners were spirit-possessed, others inherited their roles.

Historical Vocabulary
Mudang and Gut

Mudang are ritual specialists who mediate with spirits; gut are their ceremonies. Misunderstanding: "shamanism" is a modern scholarly label; practices and practitioners varied widely.

Section 40.10

Village Religion, Household Spirits, and Communal Ritual

Villages held rites for guardian spirits at local shrines and tree or stone markers, and households honored protective spirits of the home. Agricultural and fishing communities held ceremonies for harvests and sea voyages, and festivals brought communities together, with distinctive regional practices.

Everyday Life and Social History

One Household, Several Practices

A composite, not a record of a family: a household might hold ancestral rites in the Confucian manner, pay for prayers at a temple, and join a village ritual, seeing these as compatible. Sources are indirect.

Section 40.11

Religious Coexistence, Regulation, and Social Difference

Confucian officials criticized shamanic and Buddhist practice, yet both continued. Women, excluded from much formal Confucian ritual leadership, were prominent in other religious activities, and social groups differed in which traditions they emphasized. Coexistence reflected demand and the limits of regulation.

Section 40.12

Western Learning and the Introduction of Catholic Ideas

Chinese-language writings by Jesuits reached Korea through Qing China, and some Korean scholars read them as Western learning. Reading about Catholic ideas is not the same as conversion, and early interest was largely scholarly. Their intellectual reception is covered in Chapter 38.

Historical Vocabulary
Seohak and Cheonju

Seohak ("Western learning") covered European knowledge arriving in Chinese-language books; Cheonju ("Lord of Heaven") was the Korean term for the Catholic God. Misunderstanding: interest in Seohak did not imply conversion.

Section 40.13

The Formation of a Korean Catholic Community

In 1784 Yi Seung-hun was baptized in Beijing and returned to Korea, and with Yi Byeok and others helped form a lay-led Catholic community without clergy. In 1785 authorities discovered a gathering at the home of Kim Beom-u in Myeongnye-dong and investigated, revealing official suspicion and the varied social standing of those involved.

Section 40.14

Catholic Beliefs, Conversion, and Religious Authority

Catholic teaching centered on a single Lord of Heaven and an afterlife, which differed from Confucian and Buddhist assumptions. Converts included members of different social groups, among them women and non-elites. Catholic practice created tensions with established expectations about ritual, family, and authority.

Section 40.15

Ancestral Rites, Religious Conflict, and Early Persecution

In 1791 the Jinsan Incident arose when Yun Ji-chung, reported to have abandoned the ancestral rites for his mother, was prosecuted with his relative Gwon Sang-yeon, and both were executed. Authorities treated the refusal of ancestral rites as a violation of Confucian morality. In 1794 the Chinese priest Zhou Wenmo arrived, and in 1795 efforts were made to arrest him. The larger persecutions belong to Chapter 45.

Cause and Effect

The Jinsan Incident

Long-term causes: Confucian ritual orthodoxy and reading of Western learning. Short-term causes: formation of a Catholic community after 1784. Trigger: refusal of ancestral rites. Effects: executions and heightened suspicion. Long-term: later persecutions (Chapter 45). Complexity: no single cause.

Section 40.16

Continuity and Change in Joseon's Spiritual Life

Around 1800 Joseon's religious life combined an official Confucian order, persistent Buddhism, shamanic and village practice, and a small, newly persecuted Catholic community. Institutions changed less than habits of coexistence, while Catholicism posed new challenges leading toward nineteenth-century conflicts.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Religious Life around 1800

Continuities: Confucian state ritual, Buddhist temples, shamanic and village practice. Changes: temple recovery, blending of mountain-spirit worship, and a new Catholic community. Change was gradual and uneven.

Historical Debate

An Unprecedented Challenge?

The question: Did Catholicism challenge Joseon's moral order as something wholly new, or expose tensions already present?

New challenge: A foreign faith with a different view of ritual and authority.
Exposed tensions: Disputes over ritual, orthodoxy, and status already existed.

Why it matters: It shapes how persecution is understood. Student task: Identify evidence that could distinguish the views.
