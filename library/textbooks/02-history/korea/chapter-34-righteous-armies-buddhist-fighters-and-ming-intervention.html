Righteous Armies, Buddhist Fighters, and Ming Intervention

Korean History: Ancient Kingdoms to a Divided Peninsula

Chapter Opening

By the summer of 1592, Japanese armies held Hanyang and Pyongyang and had marched through most of the peninsula in a matter of weeks. Yet holding a city is not the same as holding a country. In the countryside between the captured capitals, a different war was beginning: one fought by provincial scholars and their retainers, by surviving garrison troops, by monks summoned from mountain monasteries, by villagers who hauled grain and stones, and, before long, by tens of thousands of soldiers sent across the Yalu River by the Ming empire.

Chapters 32 and 33 described two different movements in the opening phase of the Imjin War. Chapter 32 traced the collapse of Joseon's conventional defense: the Japanese landings in the spring of 1592, the rapid advance, the fall of Hanyang and Pyongyang, the flight of the court northward, and the disruption of civilian life. Chapter 33 turned to the sea, where Admiral Yi Sun-sin's fleets disrupted Japanese maritime movement and made the supply of a far-flung army more difficult. This chapter turns to the land war after that first shock and asks how a campaign that began as a Japanese advance became a contested occupation, then a coalition counteroffensive, and finally a stalemate pressed against the southeastern coast.

The answer developed here is deliberately not a story of one savior. Popular memory, in Korea and elsewhere, has often reduced the transformation of the war to a single hero, a single people rising in unison, or a single act of foreign rescue. The surviving evidence supports none of these simplifications. Local armed groups known as uibyeong ("righteous soldiers") made the occupation expensive and insecure. Surviving government units and officials supplied commanders, fortresses, and the machinery of requisition. Buddhist monks, whom the Joseon state had marginalized for two centuries, formed military and labor organizations at the court's request. Civilians carried the grain, built the walls, buried the dead, and sometimes defended the fortresses. And the Ming empire, for reasons of its own, committed field armies far larger than anything Joseon's local mobilizations could assemble. The interaction of these forces, not any one of them, changed the war.

This chapter is also about limits. Local resistance could delay and bleed occupying armies, but at Geumsan and at the second siege of Jinju it could be destroyed by concentrated force. Ming intervention made the recapture of Pyongyang possible, but the Ming army suffered a sharp reverse at Byeokjegwan, bargained with Japanese commanders, drew heavily on Korean provisions, and did not pursue Joseon's objective of expelling the invaders at any cost. The recovery of the capital in the spring of 1593 left the Japanese armies intact and entrenched on the southeastern coast. The war entered a stalemate that no party had planned.

The evidence for these events is rich but uneven in its biases. Court records preserved in the Annals of King Seonjo (Seonjo Sillok) report victories and defeats in the language of officials who needed to justify themselves to a king in exile. Retrospective memoirs, notably the Jingbirok of Yu Seong-ryong, offer a senior official's reflections written after the fighting. Local biographies, gazetteers, and later commemorative writings preserve the memory of individual militia leaders, often in the language of Confucian loyalty and sometimes with the heightened outlines of later memorialization. Chinese official histories and Japanese campaign records provide counter-perspectives, though all three bodies of evidence tend to magnify the achievements of their own side. This chapter reads these sources against one another and says plainly when numbers, dates, and anecdotes rest on thin or retrospective foundations.

A word on dating. Korean sources of the period use the lunar calendar, and the sequence of campaigns in this chapter is most reliably cited by lunar month. Where this chapter gives lunar months it says so; where it gives seasons or Gregorian months, these are approximate correspondences. Figures for armies, casualties, and the dead at sieges are especially unreliable and are given as ranges, as traditional figures, or not at all.

Finally, this chapter places the Imjin War within the multinational setting that the outline requires. By 1593 the war involved Joseon, Ming China, and the Japanese forces of Toyotomi Hideyoshi, and it drew in Buddhist institutions, provincial elites, and civilian communities across the peninsula. Korea was neither a passive battlefield on which outside powers fought nor a nation that expelled an invader on its own. It was a society whose state had nearly failed and then, with allies and at terrible cost, helped to contain the invasion. Chapter 35 will follow how that stalemate ended, how a settlement failed, and how the war resumed in 1597.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Distinguish among occupation, administration, and control, and explain why Japanese possession of major cities in 1592 did not amount to control of Joseon.
  2. Define the uibyeong accurately, explaining why they were a family of regional mobilizations rather than one national army or a modern mass movement.
  3. Compare the militia leadership, social bases, and results of resistance in Gyeongsang, Jeolla, Chungcheong, Hwanghae, and Hamgyong.
  4. Explain what guerrilla and irregular warfare could and could not accomplish, and how land resistance interacted with the naval pressure described in Chapter 33.
  5. Analyze why a Neo-Confucian state that had restricted Buddhism turned to Buddhist networks in 1592, and what the monks actually did.
  6. Trace the sieges of Jinju, the fighting at Cheongju and Geumsan, and the defense of Haengju as case studies in cooperation, sacrifice, and military limits.
  7. Assess why Ming China intervened, distinguishing strategic self-interest, tributary obligation, and uncertainty about events in Korea.
  8. Evaluate the roles of the failed Zu Chengxun expedition, the Li Rusong expedition, the recapture of Pyongyang, and the setback at Byeokjegwan in the allied campaign.
  9. Interpret why the Japanese evacuated Hanyang in 1593 and why the evacuation contracted rather than ended the Japanese presence.
  10. Evaluate competing interpretations of what changed the land war, and justify an integrated account using evidence.
  11. Apply source criticism to official reports, retrospective memoirs, and commemorative traditions such as the stories associated with Haengju.
  12. Connect wartime exposure to Ming military organization to the establishment of the Training Agency in 1593.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did a war that began with the collapse of Joseon's conventional armies become a prolonged, multinational struggle that Japanese occupation forces could no longer control?
  2. What is the difference between holding cities and controlling a territory, and why does that difference matter for understanding the Imjin War?
  3. Who joined the uibyeong, why did they join, and why should historians resist describing them as a unified national uprising?
  4. Why did a state that had restricted Buddhism rely on Buddhist fighters and laborers in a crisis, and what changed and did not change afterward?
  5. Why did the Ming empire intervene, and how did its priorities both converge and conflict with those of Joseon?
  6. Why did the allied success at Pyongyang not produce a rapid march to the southeastern coast?
  7. What does the contrast between the first and second sieges of Jinju reveal about the strengths and limits of local resistance?
  8. How should historians treat commemorative stories and traditions, such as those surrounding Haengju, when the evidence for specific details is retrospective?
  9. How did coalition warfare change the Joseon state's own military institutions?

Historical Context

Students approaching this chapter should carry forward the outcome of Chapters 31 through 33. Joseon in 1592 was a long-lived Neo-Confucian dynasty (Chapters 25 and 26) whose military institutions had not been tested by large-scale war for generations. Its government rested on civil officials recruited through examinations, a status order in which the yangban elite dominated local society (Chapter 29), and a provincial structure in which governors, military commanders, and magistrates controlled local resources. Chapter 31 examined factional court politics, Hideyoshi's unification of Japan, failed intelligence, and uneven military preparation. Chapter 32 traced the collapse that followed: Japanese forces, armed with large numbers of firearms and experienced in a century of Japanese civil war, advanced from Busan to the northern peninsula, Seonjo left Hanyang, and the court moved toward the Yalu frontier.

Chapter 33 then described the naval war. The Japanese strategy had presumed that a land army advancing north could be supplied in part by sea along the western coast. Joseon fleets under Yi Sun-sin and other commanders, using ships suited to coastal combat and heavy artillery, defeated Japanese squadrons in a series of engagements and made unrestricted maritime movement impossible. That outcome did not by itself end the invasion, and Chapter 33 cautioned against reducing the entire war to naval action. This chapter picks up the other half of the problem: what could be done on land, where the Japanese held the principal cities and roads, and where the Joseon state's regular forces had largely failed.

Three elements of the Joseon landscape matter especially. First, provincial society had a dense network of local elites, kinship groups, and scholarly communities, many of whom held land, retainers, and moral authority that could be turned toward organizing armed groups. Second, the peninsula's mountainous terrain, river crossings, and walled towns offered defensive positions that a prepared garrison could hold. Third, the Joseon state, though dispersed and damaged, had not disappeared: the court at Uiju retained legitimacy, provincial governors continued to issue orders, and local officials still controlled tax records and granaries. These features meant that the occupation, however swift, rested on a society that could resist in many forms.

On the continental side, Ming China had long treated Joseon as its closest tributary state (Chapter 30). The tributary relationship carried ritual and diplomatic obligations on both sides, but it did not automatically commit the Ming to defend Joseon militarily. Ming decision-makers in 1592 were also contending with other pressures, including a major mutiny in the northwest, and they received uncertain and conflicting reports from the peninsula. Whether and how to intervene was a genuinely open question, and Section 34.9 treats it as one.

Finally, the religious and social setting matters. Joseon's official ideology was Neo-Confucian, and the dynasty had restricted Buddhist institutions and the status of the clergy, though Buddhism persisted in mountain monasteries and in popular and elite devotion (Chapter 40 treats the broader religious landscape). The concept of a "righteous army" drew on Confucian language about loyalty to the ruler and moral duty in time of crisis. When monks and commoners joined or supported militia forces, they stepped into a framework of obligation whose vocabulary was elite and Confucian, even where their own motives were more varied.

Section 34.1

From Occupation to a War of Resistance

The opening weeks of the Imjin War created a misleading picture of Japanese success. Japanese columns moved from the southern ports toward Hanyang along several main routes, took the capital without sustained resistance, and pressed on to Pyongyang and into the northeast. A map drawn at midsummer 1592 would show an arc of Japanese-held cities from the southeastern coast to the northern frontier. But that map would record where Japanese armies stood, not what they controlled. Historians of occupation distinguish three related conditions. Occupation means that an army is physically present in a place. Administration means that an occupier can collect revenue, appoint or coerce officials, and issue orders that local people obey. Control means that an occupier can move people, messages, and supplies through the territory without serious interference. In 1592 the Japanese armies occupied many towns, administered some of them for short periods; control beyond occupied towns, garrisons, and major communication routes was uneven and often insecure.

Several features of the invasion produced this gap. The Japanese advance had been rapid, and its columns outran the supply arrangements designed to follow them. Chapter 33 showed that the maritime component of those arrangements came under heavy pressure from Joseon naval forces. The overland component, long roads through mountainous country and across rivers, passed through districts whose officials had fled but whose communities had not disappeared. Garrisons had to be spread across a long line of communications to hold the cities and protect messengers and convoys. Every small garrison and every convoy was a target for any group that could muster weapons and local knowledge.

The Joseon response was initially fragmentary. Chapter 32 described the collapse of regular defenses and the flight of the court. But the dissolution of central authority did not mean the end of the state's capacities. Provincial governors and military commanders who survived reorganized troops. Local magistrates, some of whom had fled and some of whom stayed, still held records and granaries. And individual members of the provincial elite, scholars and former officials who lived in the countryside, began calling on their kinsmen, retainers, tenants, and neighbors to form armed groups. These were the first uibyeong. They arose not from a central order but from local circumstances, and the court at Uiju sometimes learned of them only after they had formed.

The war that emerged in the second half of 1592 therefore had at least four layers. The first was the Japanese field armies, large and experienced but widely dispersed and increasingly concerned about supply. The second was Joseon's surviving regular forces, which rebuilt around fortresses, provincial commands, and the navy. The third was the militias and monk forces, locally rooted and uneven in quality. The fourth, arriving from the summer and in force by winter, was Ming intervention. Each layer affected the others. Local militias made Japanese communications dangerous, which made the Japanese concentrate in cities and fortified positions, which in turn gave regular and Ming armies a more definable target. This chapter follows those interactions across fifteen sections and ends in the stalemate of 1593.

One interpretive caution applies throughout. The story of the transformation of the war is often told in nationalist or heroic registers that emphasize a unified people's resistance. The evidence supports a more complicated picture. Some communities resisted, some fled to mountains and islands, some cooperated with Japanese forces under coercion or for advantage, and some changed course as the military balance shifted. Resistance was widespread but uneven, and different regions experienced the war in different ways. This chapter treats that unevenness as part of the history, not as a deviation from it.

Section 34.2

What Were the Uibyeong?

The Korean term uibyeong is usually translated "righteous soldiers" or "righteous army." The word ui carries a Confucian moral sense of righteousness or duty, particularly the duty to act on behalf of a rightful ruler and community when official structures fail. To call a force ui was to claim that it acted from principle rather than for profit or coercion. The term recurs in Korean history, in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries as well, but those later movements had different social bases, goals, and circumstances and should not be read back into 1592 (Chapters 54 and 56 return to the question of resistance and identity in the modern period). In the Imjin War, the uibyeong were not a single organization, did not answer to a single commander, and were not a modern nationalist mass movement.

Who were they? Many of the best-known leaders belonged to the provincial scholar-elite. Some were former officials, some were scholars who had passed or sought to pass the examinations, and some were wealthy landholders. They were well placed to organize because they commanded the loyalty of kin, tenants, and servants, owned weapons and horses, knew local geography, and could invoke Confucian arguments about loyalty. Several of the major leaders discussed in this chapter fit that profile. But the forces they led were broader than the elite. The rank and file included peasants, retainers, servants, enslaved or bonded people (nobi) in some cases, hunters who knew the mountains, artisans, local military personnel who had lost their units, and, in some regions, men who sought to avoid the exactions of the occupation or to protect their own villages.

Motives were correspondingly varied. For some leaders, loyalty to King Seonjo and the moral language of righteous action were central, and their writings and later biographies articulate this clearly. For others, defense of home communities and family networks mattered most. Protection of property and local economic and political bases was a practical concern for landholders whose estates lay in the path of the invaders. Hostility to occupation, driven by the violence and requisitions of Japanese troops, motivated ordinary villagers. And some participants were drawn in by pressure from local leaders, by the state's later offers of official posts and exemptions, or by simple necessity. Korean historical scholarship notes both the importance of local elite leadership and the desire to defend local bases; these were not mutually exclusive, and treating them as such flattens the evidence.

The relationship between uibyeong and the state changed over time. At first the militias formed spontaneously, sometimes in tension with the remnants of provincial government. Some governors and commanders regarded unofficial armed forces with suspicion, particularly when militia leaders enjoyed local prestige that rivaled their own, and there were disputes over command, over the use of provisions, and over who deserved credit. As the war continued, the court recognized successful militia leaders with official titles and appointments, authorized them to draw on provincial stores, and in many cases incorporated their followers into regular units. This process of recognition, reorganization, and absorption is one of the important institutional consequences of the war, and Section 34.15 and the Continuity and Change feature return to it.

The extent of militia participation varied greatly by region and has been difficult to quantify. Later compilations list a large number of named militia leaders across the peninsula, but these lists were compiled after the war, in a context of commemoration and of reward, and the numbers of men under each leader are often given in round or traditional figures that cannot be verified. This chapter therefore speaks of militias by region and by case study, and avoids totals that the sources cannot support.

Historical Vocabulary
Uibyeong (義兵), "righteous soldiers"

Definition: Locally raised armed groups, usually organized by provincial elites and composed of kin, retainers, tenants, and neighbors, that fought against the Japanese invasion in 1592–1598 on their own initiative or with later state recognition.

Historical context: The term draws on Confucian ideals of righteousness and loyalty to the ruler. In the Imjin War, uibyeong formed first in the occupied or threatened provinces and operated alongside, and sometimes in friction with, regular Joseon forces.

Why it matters: The uibyeong show how an apparently collapsing state generated resistance from below. They also show the importance of local elite leadership in premodern Korean society.

Potential misunderstanding: The uibyeong were not a single national army, not a spontaneous uprising of an undifferentiated people, and not the same as later uibyeong movements against Japanese encroachment in the early twentieth century. They varied by region, leadership, size, and effectiveness.

Section 34.3

Gwak Jae-u and Resistance in Gyeongsang

Gyeongsang, the province of the first Japanese landings, was among the earliest regions in which militias formed, and it produced the most widely remembered militia leader of the war, Gwak Jae-u. A wealthy member of the local elite from the Uiryeong area, Gwak raised a force in the fourth lunar month of 1592, using his own resources to equip retainers and neighbors. Korean historical reference works identify his force as one of the earliest spontaneous uibyeong formations in the war. Gwak's later reputation as the "Red Robe General" (Hongui janggun) is associated with the red clothing attributed to him and his men; later accounts say that his appearance and the use of decoys confused Japanese scouts, though the details of these tactics come from accounts compiled after the war, and students should treat them as part of a heroic tradition as well as a plausible record of how a small force might create uncertainty.

Gwak's military importance rests less on this color than on geography. Uiryeong lay near the confluence of the Nam and Nakdong rivers, in a district through which Japanese forces moving west from the Gyeongsang interior sought to reach Jeolla, the southwestern province often called Honam, which was rich in grain and which Joseon authorities needed to remain outside Japanese control. In the early summer of 1592, a Japanese force attempted to cross the Nakdong in the Jeongamjin area to advance toward Jeolla. Korean sources describe Gwak's forces contesting these crossings and, together with other Gyeongsang militias, helping to block the advance. The effect was strategic out of proportion to the size of the engagements: Jeolla's resources remained available to Joseon, and its naval and land forces were later able to operate from them. The exact scale of the fighting and the exact role of Gwak's force relative to others, including regular troops and neighboring militias, is difficult to establish, and Korean official historical materials emphasize the blocking function rather than a decisive battle.

Gwak's relationships with Joseon authorities were also instructive. Provincial officials were wary of independent armed leaders, and Gwak had disputes with the provincial administration before the court and officials more sympathetic to his cause recognized and supported him. The senior provincial official Kim Seong-il, who had gone to Japan as a deputy envoy two years earlier, was among those who worked to reconcile such tensions and to support the militias. Such episodes show that militias were not simply an uncomplicated popular addition to the war effort but a force whose standing was negotiated with officials who feared both defeat and the loss of their own authority.

Gwak was not alone. Kim Myeon of Goryeong and Jeong In-hong of Hapcheon were among the other early Gyeongsang militia leaders, and each raised forces in his own district, harassed Japanese detachments, and cooperated at times with Gwak and with the provincial administration. Their histories remind us that the Gyeongsang resistance was a network of local leaders with their own followings and their own rivalries, not the extension of one man's authority. Later memorial writing tended to concentrate credit on a small number of famous names. Reading the surviving records, the more accurate picture is of a regional pattern of overlapping mobilizations, with Gwak the most famous among them.

The Gyeongsang cases also illustrate the limits of militia success. Gwak's forces were most effective in terrain suited to ambush and obstruction, along river crossings and mountain approaches. They could not meet a Japanese field army in open battle and win, and they did not try to. Their value lay in making movement difficult and in preserving the ground on which regular Joseon forces and, later, allied armies could operate. In this sense Gwak is a good example of the argument of this chapter: effective, regionally significant, but part of a layered resistance rather than the single cause of its success.

Section 34.4

Resistance across the Provinces

Beyond Gyeongsang, militias formed across the peninsula, but with strikingly different characters. In Jeolla, which had not been overrun, mobilization took place under conditions of relative security and under the eye of a functioning provincial government. Go Gyeong-myeong, a senior figure of the Honam elite, raised a force and led it northward. His career, which ended with his death in fighting in the Geumsan area in the summer of 1592, shows that Jeolla's militias were often prepared to move beyond their home province to meet the Japanese on the roads toward the capital and toward the Honam grain lands. Their motives combined defense of their own province with loyalty to the king and a strategic sense that Honam could be defended in Chungcheong.

In Chungcheong, the province bordering the Hanyang region and Jeolla, Jo Heon organized one of the best-known militias of the war. A scholar-official with a record of principled opposition at court, Jo gathered followers and drew on provincial Confucian networks. His force's fate at Geumsan is treated in Section 34.7, but the character of his militia, a scholar-led force with a strong ideological vocabulary, and its cooperation with monk forces, makes Chungcheong an important case. Kim Cheon-il, whose later role at the second siege of Jinju appears in Section 34.14, was also an early militia leader and took part in operations in the central regions before moving south, which shows that individual militia leaders could move far from their homes as the war's geography changed.

In Hwanghae, which lay on the road between Hanyang and Pyongyang and was occupied by Japanese forces during the northern advance, militias operated in a more contested environment. Japanese garrisons were closer, and local forces tended to adopt more obviously guerrilla methods, harassing communications and attacking smaller detachments, rather than assembling larger standing units. The evidence for this region is thinner than for Gyeongsang and Jeolla, and generalizations should be cautious.

The northeast, Hamgyong, offers a pointed contrast. Chapter 32 described the capture of two royal princes there and the disintegration of authority in a frontier region whose officials and population had complicated relations with the central state. In that setting, some local people cooperated with the Japanese, and some turned captives over to them, while others resisted. Jeong Mun-bu organized a militia that fought to retake towns from Japanese and from collaborators and recovered localities in the region. The Hamgyong case reminds students that resistance was a response to the conditions of a particular region, including its frontier culture, its ties to the state, and the pressure of the invaders on it, not a uniform response to an identical stimulus. Some local people had little reason to feel loyalty to a distant court that had treated their region as a peripheral frontier, and loyalty and betrayal in the northeast cannot be reduced to simple moral categories.

Across these regions, then, the uibyeong were a family of regional mobilizations. Social structure, distance from the Japanese, the condition of local government, the presence of effective elite leaders, and the nature of the terrain all shaped what resistance looked like. These factors shaped resistance, but not in a simple linear pattern: heavily invaded Gyeongsang produced major militias, while some other occupied regions produced smaller or more dispersed forces. Some regions produced little organized resistance at all. This variety is not a defect in the story but one of its main findings.

Section 34.5

Guerrilla War: Roads, Rivers, Intelligence, and Supply

It is useful to ask what irregular forces could realistically accomplish. Militias raised from kin and neighbors, equipped with whatever arms could be gathered and trained little or not at all, were generally ill suited to defeating large Japanese field formations in conventional battle. Japanese units carried large numbers of firearms and had considerable experience of coordinated infantry combat. Militia success, where it occurred, depended on terrain, surprise, local knowledge, and avoiding direct confrontation with the strongest enemy forces.

Within these constraints, militias could do a number of things. They could attack small detachments, messengers, and foraging parties, which forced the Japanese to move in larger and slower groups. They could contest river crossings and mountain passes where a small number of defenders could delay a larger force. They could burn or seize warehouses and cut communications. They could gather intelligence about Japanese movements and pass it to regular Joseon commanders, and they could guide government troops through terrain they knew well. They could also recover isolated towns where Japanese garrisons were small, restoring a nominal Joseon presence and, with it, a source of local grain and manpower. All of these actions were incremental. None could expel the invaders, but together they altered the conditions under which the Japanese operated.

The most important effect was on concentration. The more insecure the countryside, the more Japanese commanders had to hold their forces in fortified cities and strongpoints, to garrison key locations heavily, and to escort convoys. This reduced the ability of Japanese armies to maneuver and exploit the initial advance. It also meant that, by the end of 1592, the Japanese occupied a series of defended positions rather than a territory.

This section's argument must be connected to Chapter 33. Naval denial and land resistance were distinct mechanisms. Joseon naval victories made Japanese maritime logistics more difficult by denying free movement along the western coast, but they did not by themselves close the sea to every Japanese vessel or supply every need. Local land resistance made the long overland lines of communication less secure but did not sever them. Neither mechanism alone "cut Japan off." Together they imposed a progressively greater cost on maintaining a far-flung occupation. This is a statement about attrition and burden, not a claim that the Japanese were starved out or defeated by either mechanism alone, and the chapter's later sections show how Ming armies, weather, disease, and supply conditions entered the picture as well.

Irregular resistance also carried costs for the communities that supported it. Japanese commanders and troops responded to attacks with reprisals, and villages suspected of aiding militias risked destruction. Joseon's own requisitions fell on the same communities that were exposed to Japanese violence. Where militias were large, they competed with government troops and each other for provisions. The history of guerrilla resistance in the Imjin War is therefore not simply a story of effective harassment but also of the strain placed on a rural population by two armies, one foreign and one its own.

Section 34.6

Buddhist Fighters in a Confucian State

One of the most striking features of the wartime mobilization is the participation of Buddhist monks. Joseon was founded as a Neo-Confucian state (Chapters 25 and 26). Its early rulers restricted the property, institutions, and prestige of Buddhist temples, limited the ordination of monks, and excluded the clergy from the capital and from elite social status, even though Buddhist practice persisted in the countryside, in mountain monasteries, and among many members of the elite and royal household. In the crisis of 1592, the same state turned to those marginalized Buddhist networks for fighters, laborers, and organizers. The apparent paradox dissolves when one recognizes that the monasteries were among the few institutions in rural Korea with organized communities, stored provisions, and leaders able to command obedience, and that Joseon had no ready substitute for them in the emergency.

The central figure was Hyujeong, known as Seosan Daesa, one of the leading Buddhist teachers of the day. In the seventh lunar month of 1592, King Seonjo called on him to mobilize monks for the defense of the kingdom, and Hyujeong issued a call to Buddhist communities across the country. Korean historical material places the nationwide expansion of organized monk forces after this summons. Hyujeong, advanced in years, took an overall leadership role, and his disciples took field command. The most famous was Yujeong, known as Samyeong Daesa, who raised monk forces in Gangwon and later commanded them in northern campaigns, including the fighting around Pyongyang, and who participated in later negotiations with the Japanese (Chapter 35 returns to these). Other monk leaders, including Yeonggyu, who appears in Section 34.7, led their own contingents in Chungcheong and elsewhere. Some monks had already organized forces before the royal summons, so the call may be better understood as the state's recognition and coordination of a movement already underway than as its origin.

What did the Buddhist forces do? They fought, but fighting was only part of their work. Monk units guarded fortresses, especially mountain fortresses whose geography they knew. They transported provisions, repaired and constructed defenses, collected and stored supplies, and provided the labor that sieges and campaigns required. Some monks took part in major assaults, including the recapture of Pyongyang in 1593 (Section 34.10), and in the defense of Haengju (Section 34.12). In the south, monks also served in connection with naval operations. The line between combat, labor, and logistics was thin, and the Buddhist contribution is best understood as a broad contribution of organized manpower and skills, not simply as the presence of a few warrior-monks.

The motives of monks, like those of the uibyeong, were varied. Buddhist teachings supported protection of the realm and the idea of a ruler as protector of the dharma, and some monks and writers framed their service in those terms. Monasteries also had practical interests in the survival of the state, since Japanese forces plundered and burned temples. Monastic communities were obliged under existing Joseon arrangements to supply labor, and the monks' service in the war can be seen in part as a heightening of an existing, and often resented, obligation. Whether any individual monk acted from religious conviction, from loyalty, from obligation, or from some combination cannot generally be recovered from the sources.

A common misconception must be corrected. Wartime Buddhist service did not end Joseon's Neo-Confucian restrictions on Buddhism. The monks' contributions earned individual honors and, in some cases, positions and privileges, and the war's aftermath left monasteries with a somewhat different place in the state's organization of labor, especially in the building and garrisoning of fortresses. But the dynasty's ideological commitments and social hierarchies remained in place. The Buddhist contribution to the war is a case of the state using institutions it had marginalized, not of a change in the state's basic stance. Chapter 40 returns to the broader religious landscape of later Joseon.

Historical Vocabulary
Uiseunggun (義僧軍), "righteous monk soldiers"

Definition: Organized forces of Buddhist monks, and the laborers attached to them, that took part in the defense of Joseon during the Imjin War as combatants, fortress guards, transport workers, and builders.

Why it matters: The monks' wartime role shows how an ideologically Confucian state drew on Buddhist institutions it had restricted, and it demonstrates the breadth of non-elite and non-professional participation in the war.

Potential misunderstanding: Monk forces did more than fight, and their service did not reverse the dynasty's restrictions on Buddhism. Portrayals of the monks as a unified religious army exaggerate their organization and their direct combat role.

Section 34.7

Cheongju and Geumsan: Cooperation and Sacrifice

The joint operations of Jo Heon's militia and the monk force led by Yeonggyu form a valuable case study because they show militia and Buddhist forces acting together, achieving a notable success, and then suffering catastrophe. Jo Heon, a scholar-official from Chungcheong, had gathered a militia of followers drawn from the province's Confucian networks. Yeonggyu, a monk, led a force of monks based in the same region. In the eighth lunar month of 1592, the two forces cooperated in an operation against Cheongju, a city of strategic importance on the roads of the central peninsula that had been held by Japanese forces. Korean official historical materials describe their joint action as retaking the city, with the combined force of militia and monks driving the Japanese garrison out. The recovery of Cheongju restored a regional administrative center to Joseon hands and removed a Japanese strongpoint close to Jeolla and to routes toward Hanyang.

Jo Heon then moved toward Geumsan, a town that lay on the road into Jeolla and was held or threatened by Japanese forces advancing from the central peninsula toward Honam. The strategic logic was the same one that had shaped Gyeongsang resistance: Jeolla's grain and manpower had to be protected, and a Japanese force at Geumsan could threaten them. Jo Heon intended to attack, and the commander of the regular forces in the region, who judged that a militia assault would fail, advised delay and coordination. The sources indicate that Jo Heon, committed to moral and political duty and to the credibility of the militia cause, chose to proceed. The militia and the monks attacked Japanese forces outside Geumsan around the eighteenth day of the eighth lunar month. They fought courageously and were destroyed. Jo Heon, Yeonggyu, and most of their forces were killed. Later tradition numbers the dead at about seven hundred and honors them with a tomb and shrine at Geumsan, the Tomb of the Seven Hundred Righteous Warriors, though the figure is a traditional one and should not be treated as an exact count.

Historians read the episode in several ways. The strategic result was mixed. The cumulative fighting at Geumsan helped check Japanese pressure toward Jeolla and contributed to the withdrawal of forces from the area, and Korean historical material stresses the importance of this check, but the cost was the loss of a large part of the militia and monk forces of the region and of two of their most prominent leaders. Some historians and commentators have criticized the decision to attack without adequate preparation. Others stress that the sacrifice became a powerful example for later mobilization. Both observations may be true. The case is useful precisely because it blocks a romantic reading of the militias as invincible guerrillas. Resistance could be effective and still be extraordinarily costly.

The relationship between the militia and the regular forces in this episode also shows the problem of command. Jo Heon's force operated beyond the control of the provincial commander, and the disagreement between them was not only tactical but also a conflict between a moral conception of duty and a more cautious professional judgment. Conflicts of this kind recur in the story of the uibyeong, and they help explain why the court and provincial governments, as the war went on, tried to bring the militias under regular command.

The Cheongju and Geumsan story also illustrates the cooperation across social boundaries that the war produced. A Confucian scholar-official and a Buddhist monk, whose institutions had stood in tension for two centuries, fought and died side by side. That cooperation reflected necessity as well as shared purpose, and it did not erase the social and ideological differences between the groups. But it suggests how the emergency created temporary alliances that the ordinary operation of the Joseon social order would not have produced.

Section 34.8

The First Siege of Jinju, 1592

Jinju, in Gyeongsang near the Nam River, was a fortified provincial city that controlled a main route into Jeolla. If Japanese forces could take it, the road into the grain-rich southwest lay open. For that reason Jinju mattered more strategically than its size alone would suggest, and both sides understood it. In the tenth lunar month of 1592, a large Japanese force, including contingents under commanders such as Hosokawa Tadaoki, advanced on the city. The defense was commanded by Kim Si-min, the magistrate of Jinju, who had spent the preceding months strengthening the city's defenses and preparing its garrison.

The defenders were a mixture of regular soldiers, militia members, local officials, and civilians. Korean sources, including the official report preserved in the Seonjo Sillok discussed below, emphasize the garrison's preparations with gunpowder weapons, including artillery and firearms, and the cooperation of defenders on the walls. Outside the city, militia forces, among them Gwak Jae-u's, harassed the besiegers and created confusion about the number and position of relieving forces, which increased the pressure on the Japanese commanders. Civilians inside the walls carried materials, supplied the defenders, and took part in repairing damage. The siege lasted several days, with repeated Japanese assaults that the defenders repelled, and ended with the Japanese withdrawal. Kim Si-min was wounded during the fighting and died shortly afterward, which makes the victory a costly one for Joseon, and numbers on both sides are given in the sources with a precision that cannot be trusted. Korean sources describe the defenders as numbering a few thousand and the attackers as much more numerous, but the specific figures vary and reflect wartime and later commemorative reporting.

The first siege of Jinju is often grouped in later Korean memory with the naval victory at Hansan (Chapter 33) and the defense of Haengju (Section 34.12) as one of the war's great victories. That grouping reflects later commemorative tradition, and students should note how such groupings shape memory by fixing a small number of events as emblems of the war. The siege is nevertheless historically significant on its own terms. It protected the approaches to Jeolla in the autumn of 1592, when Japanese forces were still attempting to extend their position, and it showed that a prepared fortress with a competent commander and gunpowder weapons could resist a major assault.

The siege is also important because it breaks down a simple distinction between government troops and volunteers. The defense depended on the integration of regular troops, local officials, militia forces outside the walls, and civilians within them. The garrison was not a purely professional army, and the militias were not a purely independent force. Their cooperation was the source of the defense's strength, and its coordination required a commander and a provincial administration that could organize it. Kim Seong-il, the provincial official who had supported the militias and who was involved in organizing the defense of the region, shows how the state's surviving machinery interacted with local mobilization.

Section 34.9

Why Ming China Entered the War

The scale of the Ming commitment to Korea was the most important change in the war after the summer of 1592, and it raises the question of why the Ming empire intervened. King Seonjo, from his place of refuge in the northwest, repeatedly asked for Ming assistance, and Joseon sent envoys to the Ming court to describe the situation and to request troops. The tributary relationship between the two states supplied a framework for these requests: Joseon acknowledged Ming suzerainty, and the Ming emperor was expected to protect a loyal vassal. But tributary obligation alone did not compel the Ming to commit large armies to a distant campaign, and the decision was not automatic.

Ming decision-makers weighed several considerations. They were uncertain about what was happening in Korea. Early reports were conflicting, and some Ming officials suspected, at least initially, that Joseon might be cooperating with the Japanese or that its account of events was exaggerated. They were also concerned about security. Japanese armies in Korea, and Hideyoshi's stated ambitions for the continent, raised the prospect that a Japanese-held Korea would pose a direct threat to the Ming frontier in Liaodong, and many Ming officials argued that it was safer to fight in Korea than on Ming territory. There were also competing demands on Ming resources, including a serious mutiny in the northwest that diverted attention and military commanders. The Ming decision was thus a mixture of obligation, strategic self-interest, and calculation, and neither extreme interpretation fits. The Ming did not intervene out of pure altruism, but neither was its participation incidental or reluctantly forced.

The first intervention was small and unsuccessful. In the summer of 1592, a Ming force from Liaodong under Zu Chengxun crossed the Yalu and attacked Pyongyang, held by Konishi Yukinaga's army. The attack took place in poor conditions, and the Ming force was defeated, with Zu Chengxun himself withdrawing. Korean historical sources explicitly distinguish this failed first relief force from the much larger expedition that followed. Its significance was nonetheless considerable. It demonstrated to Ming authorities that a small force from the frontier garrison would not suffice, it indicated the strength and tactical competence of the Japanese, and it shaped the Ming decision to prepare a larger campaign.

The failure also opened a period of diplomacy. A Ming negotiator, Shen Weijing, met Japanese commanders and arranged a truce that held for some weeks while the Ming prepared its main army. This diplomatic track, which would recur in 1593 and after, shows from the start that Ming policy combined military and diplomatic means, and that Ming officials were willing to talk to the Japanese in ways that did not always correspond to Joseon's preferences. Joseon, whose territory was the object of the negotiations, was often marginal to them, which is a theme that Chapter 35 develops. Ming commanders also took with them their own assumptions about Korean competence and loyalty, which affected relations in the allied camp.

The scale of the Ming force that crossed the Yalu in the winter of 1592–1593 is variously reported, and sources give figures in the tens of thousands, with Chinese and Korean accounts differing. What matters for this chapter is that the Ming brought a field army on a scale Joseon could not immediately match, including substantial infantry, cavalry, and artillery, and that this force, in combination with Joseon troops and Buddhist and militia forces, made large-scale offensive operations possible for the first time.

Section 34.10

Li Rusong and the Recapture of Pyongyang

The main Ming expedition was commanded in the field by Li Rusong, an experienced Liaodong military leader who had recently campaigned elsewhere for the Ming, and was overseen by the senior civil official Song Yingchang. It crossed the Yalu in the winter of 1592–1593 and moved south toward Pyongyang with a Joseon contingent attached and with coordination provided by Joseon officials, among them Yu Seong-ryong, who was responsible for supplying and guiding the allied armies and for liaison between the two courts. Buddhist forces under Hyujeong and Yujeong also joined the campaign, and Joseon regular troops participated under their own commanders.

The assault on Pyongyang took place in the first lunar month of 1593, around February. Konishi Yukinaga's forces held the walled city and had fortified its approaches. Ming forces arrived in large numbers and brought artillery, which they used to bombard the defenses, along with infantry and cavalry, and the allied armies attacked from several directions over a period of days. Joseon troops took part in the assault, and the Buddhist forces are recorded as having participated in fighting on key ground near the city. After heavy fighting, in which both sides suffered considerable losses, Konishi's army abandoned Pyongyang and withdrew southward, a retreat that Korean and Chinese accounts report as taking place at night and under pressure from the allied forces. The exact casualties and the sequence of assaults are reported differently in different sources.

The recapture of Pyongyang was a turning point. It removed the principal Japanese foothold in the north, restored a major city to Joseon, and ended the Japanese advance. It also triggered a more general Japanese withdrawal from forward positions in the north: other Japanese forces, including those under Kuroda Nagamasa, pulled back from positions in Hwanghae, and Kaesong was recovered by the allies. Korean sources identify the 1593 campaign as a major turning point in the war, and the judgment is reasonable. The war was no longer a Japanese advance. The question after Pyongyang was how far, how fast, and at what cost the allied forces could press the Japanese southward.

The achievement should be assigned carefully. Ming artillery, infantry, and cavalry, in numbers local forces could not match, made the assault possible. But the allied army depended on Joseon guides, intelligence, provisions, and, in the Joseon contingent and Buddhist forces, on troops that took part directly. The fortified cities and roads the allies advanced across had been made more difficult for the Japanese by months of militia harassment and naval pressure. This is the integrated account that the chapter's Historical Debate (Section 34.11) develops, and Pyongyang is the best evidence for it: no single factor explains the Japanese loss of the city.

Section 34.11

The Allied Advance and the Battle of Byeokjegwan

The victory at Pyongyang did not produce an effortless march to Busan. After the city fell, Li Rusong advanced south rapidly, reaching Kaesong and pressing toward Hanyang, where the main concentration of Japanese forces under commanders including Kobayakawa Takakage and Tachibana Muneshige lay. Late in the first lunar month of 1593, a Ming vanguard moving ahead of the main body, including a body of cavalry, encountered Japanese forces in the area of Byeokjegwan, north of Hanyang. The result was a serious Ming setback. The Japanese counterattacked effectively on ground that limited the Ming cavalry's advantages, and the Ming forces suffered heavy losses and fell back. Li Rusong himself is recorded as having been in danger, and his army withdrew northward to regroup around Paju and Kaesong.

The causes of the reverse should be stated cautiously. Accounts point to the Ming vanguard's rapid advance, its small size relative to the Japanese force it met, the terrain, and perhaps overconfidence after the victory at Pyongyang, and they also credit Japanese resistance and tactical skill. Whether reconnaissance failed or the Ming simply overextended, or whether information was incomplete in ways that cannot now be reconstructed, is not firmly established. What is clear is the consequence. Korean historical accounts explicitly connect Byeokjegwan with the slowing of the Ming offensive and a stronger turn toward negotiation. After Byeokjegwan, Ming commanders were more cautious about pushing against concentrated Japanese forces, more concerned with supply and with the condition of their troops, and more receptive to a diplomatic settlement.

Byeokjegwan is therefore an important corrective. The chapter must not replace a "Yi Sun-sin won everything" myth with a "the Ming army simply liberated Korea" myth. Ming armies were capable and numerous, and they made Pyongyang possible, but they were not invincible, and they had their own interests. Their commanders were wary of losses, aware of the long supply line that stretched back to Liaodong, and conscious that their government had other concerns. These interests did not always match those of Joseon, whose leaders wished to press the Japanese to the sea. The tension between the two aims, an allied army that wished to end the war without fighting its way to the coast and a host government that wished to expel the invaders, defines much of the diplomacy and military history of 1593.

The setback did not return the war to Japanese initiative. The Japanese army, though it had held at Byeokjegwan, was also under strain from supply difficulties and the insecurity of its communications, and its commanders were concentrating in Hanyang and nearby positions. The war settled for a time into a standoff around the capital, in which allied and Japanese forces watched one another, raided one another's provisions, and negotiated.

Historical Debate

What Changed the Land War after the Summer of 1592?

The question: Between the Japanese advance of the spring and summer of 1592 and the Japanese withdrawal from Hanyang in 1593, the land war changed character. Historians differ on what produced the change.

Interpretation A — Local-resistance-centered: Some accounts, especially in nationalist and commemorative traditions, emphasize the uibyeong and monk forces. They argue that local resistance made the occupation untenable, denied the Japanese the countryside, protected Jeolla, and forced concentration and withdrawal, so that Ming intervention completed a process local forces had begun.
Interpretation B — Ming-intervention-centered: Other accounts, including some Chinese-centered narratives, emphasize the Ming expedition. They argue that only large, trained field armies with artillery could defeat Japanese forces in set battle, that Pyongyang was a Ming victory, and that local forces were auxiliary and could not have turned the war.
Interpretation C — Integrated coalition: A third approach, which this chapter finds best supported, emphasizes the interaction of layers: navy and militia raised the costs of occupation and supplied intelligence, guides, and provisions; surviving regular forces held key fortresses; and Ming armies provided the mass to break concentrated Japanese positions. On this reading, neither local resistance nor Ming intervention was sufficient alone.

Evidence: The competing views draw on the same sources: Korean annals and memoirs, Chinese military and diplomatic records, Japanese campaign accounts, and local biographies. They differ in whom the sources emphasize and in how they weigh the quantitative and the cumulative effects of resistance.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns interpretation and weighting more than chronology. Differences also arise over terminology (how far "uprising" and "liberation" are appropriate terms) and over the reliability of figures for militias and Ming armies.

Why it matters: How historians weigh these factors shapes the national and international memory of the war, including who is credited and who is minimized, and it affects how one understands the Joseon state's capacity and the character of the Ming-Joseon relationship.

Student task: Using evidence from Sections 34.3 through 34.13, construct the strongest case for each interpretation, then explain why you find the integrated interpretation more or less persuasive. Identify one piece of evidence that would most clearly distinguish among the three.

Section 34.12

Haengju: Fortress, Firepower, and Civilian Participation

The fortress of Haengju stood on a hill beside the Han River, northwest of Hanyang, where it commanded approaches to the capital and to the allied position to the north. Gwon Yul, a Joseon general who had previously fought in Jeolla and Chungcheong, moved his force there in the early part of 1593 as part of the effort to pressure the Japanese in the capital. In the second lunar month of 1593, around March, a large Japanese force attacked Haengju in an attempt to remove a threat to its position in Hanyang. The fighting lasted a long day, with repeated assaults.

The defenders held. They had the advantage of terrain and fortifications, and they used firepower, including the missile and artillery weapons available to Joseon forces. Korean accounts describe the use of hwacha fire carts and other gunpowder and projectile weapons, but the specifics of these weapons and how effective they were should be treated cautiously, as the principal descriptions come from Korean sources that had reason to emphasize them. The garrison included regular troops and monk soldiers, among them forces associated with the monk Cheoyeong, who took part in the defense. When ammunition ran short, the defenders improvised, using stones and other materials, and the fortress's supply of materials depended on labor from inside and around it.

That labor leads to the famous stories about Haengju. A widely told tradition holds that women carried stones to the defenders in their skirts, and the Korean term for a type of apron-like skirt, haengju chima, is popularly linked to the story. These traditions, like many commemorative stories, were recorded long after the event, and the name's connection to the battle is better understood as a memory tradition than as an established fact. They require source criticism. The surviving contemporary evidence does not allow historians to confirm the story's details or to identify the women involved. But the broader point behind the stories is secure: defending a fortified position required military and civilian labor together. Stones, water, food, wounded men, and repairs all had to be moved, and the people who did that work included many who left no name in the records.

The Japanese failed to take Haengju, and the defense strengthened the allied position near Hanyang. It was also significant as a Korean-led success, in which Joseon forces rather than Ming armies held the field. In later memory Haengju was grouped with Hansan and the first siege of Jinju as one of the three great victories of the war, a tradition that, as with Jinju, reflects commemoration. The battle's practical importance was to demonstrate that Joseon regular forces, properly led and fortified, could resist concentrated Japanese attacks, and to add to the pressure on the Japanese position in Hanyang during the weeks before its evacuation.

Everyday Life and Social History

Civilians, Provisions, and the Burden of Two Armies

For most people in Joseon in 1592–1593, the war was not a campaign of famous commanders but a daily problem of survival, labor, and obligation. Armies of every kind, Japanese, Joseon regular, militia, monk, and Ming, depended on local grain and local labor, and the same village could be asked to support several forces in the course of a season. Joseon officials requisitioned grain and men to feed their own armies and the Ming expeditionary force, whose arrival in large numbers placed heavy burdens on the provisioning system. Yu Seong-ryong and other officials struggled to supply the Ming armies, and the strain of doing so was a persistent theme in allied relations.

Civilians performed a range of tasks that blur the line between civilian and military labor. They hauled supplies along roads and rivers, a form of portering that was heavy and dangerous. They built and repaired fortress walls and mountain strongholds. They nursed the wounded, buried the dead, and tried to protect their families. Some joined militias or served as guides and informers. Many fled to mountains or islands, abandoning fields that then went unplanted, and refugees crowded into places that seemed safer. Missing relatives, family separation, and the loss of male labor affected households long after fighting moved elsewhere. Captured people, taken to Japan or enslaved within the war zone, are treated in Chapter 35.

The damage to agriculture compounded the suffering. Crops were destroyed or left unharvested, grain was seized by several armies, and disease, spread by crowding and malnutrition, afflicted soldiers and civilians alike. Sources from the recovered capital and elsewhere record severe famine and epidemic in 1593 and the following year, including reports of desperate measures to survive. Because most records were produced by officials and elites, they tell more about government responses than about the experiences of peasants, women, and enslaved people, whose experiences historians must infer from scattered references, memorial traditions, and later local sources.

Social status shaped these experiences. Elite families could sometimes flee to safer districts or draw on resources, while commoners and the unfree bore much of the labor and risk. For nobi and other bonded people, military service or labor in the war sometimes offered a path to improved status when the state rewarded such service, which meant that the war briefly opened possibilities of mobility even as it imposed heavy burdens. Chapter 42 returns to the long-term social changes of the following centuries.

Section 34.13

Japanese Withdrawal from Hanyang

In the spring of 1593, the Japanese armies that had held Hanyang since the previous summer abandoned the capital. Korean historical material places the evacuation in the fourth lunar month, around May, after the Pyongyang counteroffensive and subsequent military pressure. The withdrawal was not a rout following a single battle. It was the outcome of an accumulation of pressures, and understanding why requires disaggregating them.

Supply was central. The Japanese concentration of forces in and around Hanyang placed heavy demands on provisions that had to come from the surrounding countryside or from the south along roads exposed to militia attack. Naval constraints limited what could be brought by sea. Allied forces and Korean militias worked to deny the Japanese local grain, and sources record an allied raid on Japanese grain stores in the Yongsan area of the capital that reportedly damaged their provisions, though the details and attribution are difficult to establish with certainty. Disease and attrition, including illness among crowded and underfed troops, are also noted in sources, though their scale cannot be measured with precision. Japanese commanders also had to consider the risk that allied forces, reinforced and supplied, would press them in the capital, and the pressure from Haengju and the surrounding allied positions added to that risk.

Diplomacy also mattered. Ming representatives, among them Shen Weijing, negotiated with Japanese commanders, and the talks included proposals for Japanese withdrawal from the capital and for the return of captured Korean princes. Ming commanders, wary after Byeokjegwan and interested in ending the war without the costs of a decisive battle, were receptive. Joseon officials were not enthusiastic and did not control the talks. The allied armies followed the Japanese withdrawal but did not press it hard, and Joseon commanders, frustrated, complained that the Ming were allowing the Japanese to escape. These complaints reflect a real divergence of aims.

The result was a contraction of the Japanese perimeter toward the southeast. Japanese forces retreated along the main roads to the coast, where they established or reinforced fortified positions, often described as Japanese-style fortresses, around the southeastern ports. The allied coalition entered the capital and pursued but proved unable or unwilling to destroy the Japanese armies outright. The capital that the allies recovered had suffered severe damage, including burned buildings and heavy loss of life, and its recovery was only the beginning of a long reconstruction. The court, still at Uiju and later returning toward the capital, faced a ruined city and a starving population.

The meaning of the withdrawal is therefore double. It was a major success for the allied coalition and for Joseon: the capital was recovered and the Japanese position in the north collapsed. But it left the Japanese presence in Korea intact, with large armies in fortified coastal bases from which they could not easily be dislodged. This pattern, of allied success short of expulsion, defines the situation that Section 34.15 describes.

Cause and Effect

The Japanese Withdrawal from Hanyang, 1593

Long-term causes

The extended Japanese lines of supply and communication, the naval constraints described in Chapter 33, the persistent insecurity of the countryside produced by militia resistance, and the Ming decision to intervene in force.

Short-term causes

The recapture of Pyongyang, the allied advance, the defense of Haengju, shortages of provisions and illness, and Japanese concern about being cut off or pressed in the capital.

Immediate trigger or context

Negotiations between Ming and Japanese representatives that offered a way to withdraw without a decisive battle, after Byeokjegwan had made the Ming receptive to settlement.

Event or transformation

Japanese forces evacuated Hanyang in the spring of 1593 and withdrew along the main roads toward the southeastern coast.

Immediate effects

The allies recovered a devastated capital. The Japanese perimeter contracted toward the southeast. Famine and disease persisted, and the allied armies did not destroy the Japanese forces.

Long-term consequences

A stalemate with Japanese forces entrenched on the southeastern coast, a prolonged negotiation, and, in 1597, a renewed invasion (Chapter 35).

Historical complexity: No single factor explains the evacuation. Supply difficulties, allied pressure, diplomacy, and attrition interacted, and historians disagree about their relative weight, in part because Japanese, Korean, and Chinese sources emphasize different elements. The withdrawal was a contraction by choice under pressure, not a defeat in battle, and for that reason it did not settle the war.

Section 34.14

The Second Siege of Jinju, 1593

The second siege of Jinju, in the sixth lunar month of 1593, provides a bitter contrast to the first. Within weeks of the Japanese evacuation of Hanyang, a Japanese force turned against the city that had defeated it the previous autumn. Hideyoshi is generally reported to have wished to avenge the earlier failure and to secure the road into Jeolla, and a much larger Japanese force than in 1592 advanced on Jinju. The sources give very large figures for the attackers, but those figures vary and reflect wartime and commemorative reporting, so this chapter does not adopt a specific total. What is clear is that the Japanese came with overwhelming numbers and with preparations for a prolonged assault, including measures to overcome the fortress's defenses.

The defenders included soldiers, uibyeong, officials, and a large number of civilians who had taken refuge in the city, which made the siege an event that combined military defense with the fate of a crowded community. Kim Cheon-il, a militia leader whose earlier career is noted in Section 34.4, took part in the defense, along with Hwang Jin and Choe Gyeong-hoe, officers associated with the garrison, and others. The Allied forces nearby, including Ming and regular Joseon troops, did not come to Jinju's relief, a failure that Korean accounts note and that reflects both the caution of the allied commanders and the strategic situation after the Hanyang evacuation. Whether relief was feasible, and who bears responsibility for its absence, is one of the disputed questions of the episode.

The siege lasted several days. Hwang Jin, a respected commander, was killed by enemy gunfire during the fighting, a blow to the defenders' leadership. Heavy rain, according to accounts, damaged a section of the wall, and the Japanese broke into the fortress. The defense collapsed, and the fighting inside the city was followed by a massacre of defenders and civilians. Kim Cheon-il and others are recorded as having died, and Korean tradition says he ended his life by throwing himself into the Nam River. A tradition associated with a gisaeng entertainer named Nongae, who is said to have drowned a Japanese officer by embracing him and leaping from a rock above the river, is a famous part of the siege's commemoration. Like other commemorative traditions, it was recorded and elaborated after the war and should be read as a memory tradition requiring source criticism, not as a confirmed account of what happened.

The scale of the catastrophe should be stated without false precision. Traditional figures for the dead run to tens of thousands, and these numbers are heavily shaped by wartime and commemorative reporting. They cannot be confirmed, and modern historians generally treat them with caution while recognizing that the loss of life among soldiers and civilians was very large. The fall of Jinju devastated the region, and the city was left in ruins.

The contrast between the two sieges, Jinju defended successfully in 1592 and destroyed in 1593, teaches an important lesson. Local resistance and fortress defense could delay and damage occupation forces, protect strategic ground, and inflict losses, but they could not guarantee victory against a concentrated army determined to prevail. The first siege showed what preparation, firepower, and cooperation could do against an attack of limited scale. The second showed what happened when the attacker brought overwhelming force and the defenders lacked relief. Official Korean material distinguishes the two outcomes explicitly. The fall of Jinju did not, however, produce a Japanese advance into Jeolla: the Japanese, having taken the city, did not press further, and they withdrew to their coastal positions. The result was a costly demonstration of Japanese power that did not change the strategic situation of the stalemate.

Multiple Perspectives

The Experience of the Land War: Seven Positions

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns that historians have identified across many sources. They are not quotations from any named historical actor, and the groups did not possess equal power, information, or freedom of action.

An uibyeong leader (provincial scholar-elite): Organizing a force draws on the loyalty of kin and tenants, expresses a duty to the king, and protects the family's land and standing. Success brings prestige and possible state recognition; failure may mean the death of followers and ruin. Relations with provincial officials who fear rival authority require constant negotiation.
An ordinary rural recruit: Service may come from local obligation, pressure from a landlord or lord, or the wish to protect a village. The recruit has little say over strategy and bears heavy risk. A promised reward or a change in status may matter, but so may simple survival, and the recruit's own reasons rarely appear in the record.
A Buddhist fighter or monastic laborer: Service combines religious ideals of protecting the realm with the practical need to preserve a monastery and meet existing labor obligations. The monk's contribution may be carrying provisions or repairing a mountain fortress as much as fighting, and the social position of the clergy in a Confucian state does not change.
A Ming soldier or officer: Fighting in a distant country for an emperor whose interest is the security of the Liaodong frontier, the Ming soldier depends on a supply line stretching back across the Yalu and on Korean provisions. Caution after Byeokjegwan reflects concern for losses, and the war's purpose is Ming security as much as Joseon's survival.
A civilian inside a besieged fortress: The civilian has sought refuge in a place that offers walls but no certainty. Labor, shortages, and fear dominate, and the fate of the fortress determines the fate of families. Civilians had little voice in decisions and left almost no records of their own.
A Joseon provincial official: Charged with supplying armies, reporting to a distant court, and balancing the demands of regular forces, militias, and allied commanders, the official faces requisition and administration under extreme strain. Reports shape rewards and punishments, and the official's own standing depends on outcomes.
A Japanese garrison soldier: Stationed in a hostile country far from supply, the soldier faces insecure roads, local resistance, disease, and shortages. The war's purpose is set in Japan by Hideyoshi, and many soldiers experience the campaign as hardship and attrition. Japanese records of such experience are limited and mostly come from commanders.
Section 34.15

From Counteroffensive to Stalemate, 1593

By the middle and later months of 1593, the war had reached a new shape. Japan no longer controlled the broad territorial arc it had traversed in 1592, but it remained entrenched in fortified positions along the southeastern coast, from which it could not easily be dislodged and which it could supply by sea from Japan. Ming and Joseon forces had recovered much of the northern and central peninsula, including Pyongyang and Hanyang, but they had not expelled the invaders. Neither side could achieve its aims by force alone, and neither was prepared to abandon them.

The internal structure of the Joseon war effort was changing as well. The militias were increasingly being recognized, reorganized, or absorbed into state structures. The court conferred official titles on prominent leaders, drew their followers into regular formations, and placed them under provincial and national command. Buddhist forces continued to perform military and logistical tasks, especially in fortress construction and garrison duty. Ming commanders increasingly favored negotiation, and diplomacy came to occupy the center of allied strategy. The Joseon court, which wished to continue the fight, found itself in the awkward position of depending on allies whose objectives differed from its own.

One of the most important institutional results was the creation of the Training Agency, the Hunryeondogam, in 1593. The experience of fighting alongside Ming forces, whose organization, drill, and use of specialized units contrasted with the shortcomings of the pre-war Joseon army, convinced some Joseon leaders that reform was necessary. Yu Seong-ryong and others urged the creation of a new force trained in Ming-influenced methods, including drill in the use of firearms, bows, and close-quarters weapons. Korean historical material directly connects the experience of the Ming forces and the 1593 lull in fighting to Joseon's adoption of new military organization. The Training Agency was a significant development but belongs here chiefly as a consequence of coalition warfare. Its later history, and the larger changes in the Joseon military in the seventeenth century, are treated in later chapters of Part VI and Part VII and should not overwhelm the account of 1593.

The central unresolved problem was that Japan remained in Korea. Negotiations between the Ming and Japanese sides began in earnest, and for the next several years the war was a matter of truce, diplomacy, and local skirmishing rather than of large campaigns. Neither side obtained an acceptable settlement: Hideyoshi's demands were incompatible with the Ming's conception of its tributary order, and Joseon was largely excluded from the bargaining. The apparent truce would collapse into a renewed Japanese invasion in 1597. That story, the failure of the settlement, the second invasion, and the war's end, is the subject of Chapter 35.

This chapter has argued that the transformation of the land war in 1592–1593 resulted from the interaction of several forces. A state that had nearly failed generated resistance at several levels, from local militias and monk forces to surviving regular troops and fortified civilian communities. These forces, together with naval pressure, made the occupation expensive and insecure. Ming intervention then supplied the mass armies that made a counteroffensive possible. The result was a contested war between a coalition and an entrenched occupier, and it was not an easy victory for anyone.

Continuity and Change Over Time

The Joseon Military and Society in Wartime

What Changed

  • The court recognized and absorbed locally raised militias, giving unofficial forces official standing
  • Buddhist monks moved from a marginal position to an organized role in defense, fortress construction, and labor
  • Joseon established the Training Agency in 1593 and began adopting Ming-influenced drill and organization
  • The relationship with the Ming deepened in a direct military form that went beyond ritual tributary diplomacy

What Stayed the Same

  • The Neo-Confucian ideological framework and the social dominance of the yangban elite remained in place
  • Buddhism remained officially restricted, despite wartime service
  • Requisitions and labor obligations continued to fall heavily on commoners and the unfree
  • The court retained its legitimacy and its administrative structure, despite the severity of the crisis

When and how did change occur? Change was gradual, uneven, and contested. Militia absorption and military reform proceeded through 1593 and after, with disputes between officials and local leaders. Who experienced it most directly? Militia leaders, monks, soldiers, and commoners saw the greatest change in obligation and status, while the central court and elite experienced more continuity in their social position. Why does the pattern matter? It shows a society that adapted institutionally under pressure without abandoning its governing ideology.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how the Imjin War's land campaign changed from a Japanese advance into a contested occupation, then a coalition counteroffensive, and finally a stalemate. Its central argument is that the transformation cannot be attributed to one savior. Local militias, surviving government forces, Buddhist fighters, civilians, the functioning Joseon state, naval pressure, and Ming intervention interacted. Local resistance made occupation expensive and insecure, Ming intervention supplied the mass field armies for a major counteroffensive, and Joseon forces and communities provided intelligence, supplies, local knowledge, manpower, and fortified resistance.

The chapter began by distinguishing occupation, administration, and control. Japanese armies held Hanyang, Pyongyang, and the main roads in 1592 but did not securely or uniformly control the countryside. Their long lines of communication ran through districts where local people could, and did, resist, and these lines were also strained by the naval pressure described in Chapter 33. The war that emerged had four layers: Japanese field armies, Joseon's surviving regular forces, militias and monk forces, and Ming intervention.

The uibyeong were a family of regional mobilizations, not a single national army. Their leaders were often provincial scholar-elites who drew on kin, retainers, and neighbors and framed their actions in Confucian language of loyalty, though the rank and file included commoners and others with varied motives. The cases of Gwak Jae-u and the Gyeongsang militias, Go Gyeong-myeong in Jeolla, Jo Heon in Chungcheong, and Jeong Mun-bu in Hamgyong show how resistance varied with region, leadership, terrain, and the condition of local government. Some regions mobilized strongly, others little, and the narrative of a unified people rising as one is not supported by the evidence.

Guerrilla and irregular resistance could attack small detachments, contest crossings and passes, gather intelligence, guide government troops, and force the Japanese to concentrate behind fortified positions. It could not defeat large Japanese field armies in set battle. Together with naval denial, it imposed a progressively greater cost on the occupation, though neither mechanism alone cut Japan off. Resistance also burdened the communities that supported it, which faced both Japanese reprisals and Joseon requisitions.

Buddhist monks, whom the Neo-Confucian Joseon state had marginalized, were mobilized after the court called on Hyujeong in the seventh lunar month of 1592. Under Hyujeong, Yujeong, Yeonggyu, and others, monk forces fought, guarded fortresses, transported provisions, repaired defenses, and provided labor. Their wartime service did not end Joseon's restrictions on Buddhism. The joint action of Jo Heon's militia and Yeonggyu's monks at Cheongju, and their destruction at Geumsan, shows both the effectiveness and the cost of irregular resistance.

The first siege of Jinju in 1592 demonstrated that a prepared fortress with a competent commander, gunpowder weapons, regular troops, militia outside the walls, and civilians within could resist a major assault and protect the approaches to Jeolla. It also broke down a simple distinction between government troops and volunteers. The official report on the siege illustrates how Joseon officials understood and credited collective defense, while also reminding students of the limits of official reports as evidence.

Ming intervention resulted from a mixture of tributary obligation, strategic self-interest, and uncertainty about events in Korea. The first, small expedition under Zu Chengxun failed at Pyongyang in the summer of 1592, but a much larger army under Li Rusong, accompanied by Joseon forces and Buddhist contingents and coordinated by Joseon officials such as Yu Seong-ryong, retook Pyongyang in early 1593. The recapture was a turning point, but it was not the product of Ming power alone. It depended on the conditions that local resistance, naval pressure, and Joseon cooperation had created. The Ming vanguard's reverse at Byeokjegwan slowed the offensive and strengthened Ming interest in negotiation, which prevents any simple account of the Ming army as the liberator of Korea.

Gwon Yul's defense of Haengju showed Joseon regular forces, firepower, monk soldiers, and civilian labor holding a fortress against concentrated assault, and the stories about women carrying stones illustrate the need for source criticism of commemorative traditions. In the spring of 1593, the Japanese evacuated Hanyang under the combined pressure of allied forces, supply difficulties, disease and attrition, local resistance, and negotiation. The evacuation contracted the Japanese perimeter toward the southeast without destroying the Japanese armies. Ming and Joseon commanders differed over how hard to press the withdrawal.

The second siege of Jinju in the summer of 1593 ended in the fortress's fall and a large loss of life among defenders and civilians. The contrast with the first siege shows that local resistance could delay and damage occupation forces but could not guarantee victory against a concentrated army, especially without relief. This chapter has not adopted any specific death toll, because surviving figures are shaped by wartime and commemorative reporting.

By late 1593 the war had reached a stalemate: Japan held fortified southeastern coastal positions, Ming and Joseon forces had recovered much territory without expelling the invaders, militias were being absorbed into state structures, and Ming commanders favored negotiation. The experience of coalition warfare contributed to the establishment of the Training Agency in 1593. The unresolved problem, that Japan remained in Korea and negotiations offered no acceptable settlement, leads to the renewed invasion of 1597 and the war's end, the subjects of Chapter 35.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it explains how a state that had nearly collapsed became one participant in a layered, multinational war. It shows that the collapse of conventional armies does not end resistance, that occupation is harder to sustain than conquest, and that the forces that resist an invader are rarely the single, unified people of later national memory. The resistance of 1592–1593 was uneven, locally rooted, often elite-led, dependent on the work of commoners, monks, and civilians, and bounded by what a poorly equipped militia could achieve.

The chapter also reveals the interaction between a society's internal resources and external intervention. Neither Joseon's local mobilizations nor the Ming expedition was sufficient alone. The Ming armies made the counteroffensive possible, but they relied on Korean provisions, guides, and troops and pursued aims of their own. Joseon forces and communities shaped the conditions under which the Ming could advance, but they could not themselves retake the cities or expel the Japanese. Understanding that interdependence, and the tensions within it, is essential to any accurate account of the war, and it avoids both the nationalist myth of unaided Korean victory and the opposite myth of simple foreign rescue.

The developments of 1593 shaped later events in several ways. The stalemate on the southeastern coast, and the failure of the negotiations that followed, set the stage for the renewed invasion of 1597 (Chapter 35). The experience of coalition warfare contributed to military reform, and the absorption of militias and the wartime use of Buddhist institutions left traces in Joseon's organization of labor and defense. The memory of Jinju, Haengju, Geumsan, and the uibyeong became a durable resource for later Korean thinking about resistance, though, as this chapter has shown, that memory has often simplified a more complicated record. The Manchu invasions treated in Chapter 36 would test the lessons of this war again under different circumstances.

Finally, the chapter contributes to the textbook's larger commitments. It treats Korea as an organizing subject without making it a passive battlefield for outside powers, it keeps the multinational character of the conflict visible, and it insists on source criticism where heroic tradition and official report blur the evidence. The war was fought by identifiable communities and individuals, some famous and most unnamed, and an honest history of it recognizes both what they achieved and what they could not.
