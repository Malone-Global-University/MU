Court Politics and the Road to War

Korean History: Ancient Kingdoms to a Divided Peninsula

Chapter Opening

In 1590 a Joseon embassy crossed the Korea Strait to congratulate Toyotomi Hideyoshi on the military reunification of Japan. The envoys waited for months while their host finished a campaign in the east, were finally received in Kyoto, and carried home a letter whose wording troubled them. By the spring of 1591 they were back at court, and the two senior men, who disagreed about what they had seen, were asked the one question that mattered: what did Hideyoshi intend? About a year later the question was answered by ships off Busan.

This chapter is the hinge between two parts of the textbook. Part V (Chapters 25–30) described the early Joseon order: a Neo-Confucian monarchy with a trained scholar-official class, a status society, a Ming-centered diplomatic world, and managed relations with Jurchen and Japanese neighbors. Part VI opens with the largest war the peninsula had experienced in centuries. Chapter 31 explains how Joseon arrived at that threshold. It does not narrate the invasion. The landings, the fall of Busan and Dongnae, the flight from Hanyang, and the first Ming response belong to Chapter 32; Admiral Yi Sun-sin's wartime campaigns belong to Chapter 33.

The chapter is organized around one controlling question: why did war become possible in 1592, and why was Joseon unable to translate warning signs into adequate preparation? Answering it requires attention to three states. In Japan, a generation of civil war ended in a consolidation under Hideyoshi that created a new kind of ruler with a large, experienced, firearm-equipped military establishment and ambitions that reached beyond the archipelago. In Joseon, a court that had been shaped by a recent rise of scholar-officials was divided into political associations, while a military system designed for a different kind of threat had weakened. In Ming China, the senior power in the East Asian diplomatic order was preoccupied elsewhere and only gradually understood what was being planned.

Between Joseon and Hideyoshi stood the island domain of Tsushima, introduced in Chapter 30 as an intermediary border society. Its Sō rulers depended on Korean trade and also owed obedience to the new Japanese ruler. When those two obligations collided, the Sō tried to reconcile demands that could not be reconciled. They are among the central actors of this chapter.

The chapter also treats several popular explanations with caution. One says that Joseon ignored all warnings. In fact the court received information from several channels and took some measures. A second says that factionalism alone caused the disaster. Factional politics shaped appointments and the reading of evidence, but it operated alongside fiscal and administrative limits, real uncertainty, and strategic assumptions that were reasonable until they failed. A third is the familiar binary in which one envoy predicted war and another said there would be none. As Section 31.8 shows, what the sources preserve is more complicated, and much of the familiar story was shaped by later political memory.

Historians reconstruct these years from sources that must be read critically. They include the Veritable Records of King Seonjo (Seonjo Sillok) and its later revision, the memoir of the statesman Yu Seong-ryong, the war diary of Yi Sun-sin, the surviving text of Hideyoshi's letters, Japanese and Jesuit reports, and Ming records. Most Korean accounts of the prewar years were compiled or revised after the disaster, and many were written by people who already knew how the story ended. Part of the work of this chapter is to distinguish what contemporaries knew from what later writers believed they should have known.

A note on dates. Korean and Japanese sources of this period use lunar calendars, and the two do not always agree by a day. The chapter gives years and seasons where precision is unnecessary and, where a day matters, gives the Korean lunar date together with its Gregorian equivalent. The Japanese landing at Busan occurred on the 13th day of the fourth lunar month of 1592, which corresponds to 23 May in the Gregorian calendar. This chapter ends on the eve of that day.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why Hideyoshi's consolidation of Japan changed the strategic environment facing Joseon.
  2. Describe the structure of late sixteenth-century Joseon court politics, including the emergence of Easterners and Westerners and the later division of the Easterners.
  3. Distinguish securely documented features of Hideyoshi's continental ambitions from later interpretation of his motives.
  4. Analyze Tsushima's position between Joseon and Hideyoshi and the incentives of the Sō and their agents to soften or reinterpret demands.
  5. Trace the diplomatic exchanges of 1587–1591, including the Tsushima missions of 1587 and 1589 and the Joseon embassy of 1590.
  6. Evaluate the evidence for what Hwang Yun-gil, Kim Seong-il, and Heo Seong reported, and explain how later historiography shaped the popular account.
  7. Assess the problem of intelligence under uncertainty, including the roles of Tsushima, merchants, rumor, and Ming sources.
  8. Describe Joseon's military system on the eve of war, including command arrangements, service obligations, weaponry, and fortifications.
  9. Identify measures of preparation actually undertaken in 1591–1592 and explain why they proved inadequate.
  10. Interpret the geography of the southern coast and the roads to Hanyang as strategic variables.
  11. Contextualize Joseon's position between Ming China, the Jurchens, and Japan.
  12. Evaluate multi-causal explanations against single-cause explanations of Joseon's unpreparedness.

Historical Context

By the 1580s the Joseon dynasty was nearly two centuries old. Its institutions, described in Chapters 25–30, rested on a monarchy advised by high officials in the State Council and the Six Ministries, a censorial system that could criticize officials and sometimes the king, and a civil examination system that produced a literate governing class. The state's foreign relations followed a pattern sometimes summarized as sadae, serving the greater, toward Ming China and gyorin, neighborly relations, toward the Jurchens and Japan. Tribute missions to Beijing were regular. Relations with Japanese were limited and regulated, centered on the trading enclave at Busan and on Tsushima.

That arrangement had been shaped by earlier episodes. The agreement of 1443 (the Gyehae agreement) set the number of ships that the Sō of Tsushima could send annually. The Japanese-resident disturbances of 1510 and the large coastal raid of 1555, led Joseon to restrict trade and to make the Border Defense Council (Bibyeonsa) a permanent organ. Joseon officials therefore had a settled view of Japan as a divided land of daimyo, merchants, and pirates, with whom a limited, controlled relationship was the right policy. Japanese political fragmentation had made this a reasonable view. The shogunate in Kyoto had long ceased to command the regional lords, and no Japanese ruler had sent a Ming-recognized embassy to Joseon in a way that required Joseon to treat the Japanese state as a unified diplomatic partner.

Within Joseon, the decades after 1567 were politically unusual. Seonjo, who ascended as a grandson of King Jungjong through a collateral line, was the first Joseon king to come to the throne in this manner, and he presided over the triumph of the sarim, scholar-officials associated with a strict Neo-Confucian program who had been purged repeatedly in earlier decades. Their victory did not produce unity. Within a decade the scholar-officials were divided into associations that historians call bungdang (factions), and the divisions shaped the court's debates over appointments, policy, and, eventually, diplomacy with Japan.

Section 31.1

A New Strategic World after 1580

Chapter 30 described the early Joseon order as a system of managed relations: tribute to Ming, ritual correspondence and limited trade with Tsushima, and wary dealings with the Jurchens of the northern frontier. For roughly a century that system worked because Joseon's neighbors were, in different ways, either subordinate to Ming or politically fragmented. Japan's long period of civil conflict, the Sengoku era, meant that Joseon did not deal with a single Japanese government. It dealt with Tsushima, with regional lords who sent occasional ships, and with the trade interests of western Japan.

After about 1580 that assumption became outdated. Within a decade Japan passed from fragmentation to a unified military hierarchy under a single ruler. At the same time a new Jurchen leader, Nurhaci, began consolidating a following in Manchuria from 1583, while Ming China, still the dominant power, was entering a period of administrative drift under the Wanli emperor. The new strategic environment was not one in which Joseon was inherently doomed. It was one in which institutions and assumptions designed for the old environment faced new pressures. The question explored in this chapter is how quickly those pressures were recognized.

Two further technological and commercial changes mattered. Since the arrival of Portuguese traders in the 1540s, firearms known in Japan as tanegashima (matchlock arquebuses) had been copied and manufactured at scale, and Japanese commanders developed tactics and logistics around them in decades of warfare. Maritime commerce also linked western Japan to Southeast Asia, Ryukyu, and Fujian. Joseon, by contrast, had maintained a deliberately restricted maritime posture toward Japan, which narrowed its opportunities to observe these developments.

Section 31.2

Joseon Court Politics in the Late Sixteenth Century

The scholar-officials who came to dominate the court in Seonjo's early reign shared a commitment to Neo-Confucian moral government, but they differed on appointments, on the interpretation of ritual, and on loyalty to different teachers and scholarly lineages. Traditional accounts date the division of the political class into Easterners (Dongin) and Westerners (Seoin) to about 1575, associated with a dispute over an appointment in the Ministry of Personnel between Kim Hyo-won and Sim Ui-gyeom. The labels derived from the districts of Hanyang in which the leaders lived. Easterners were associated especially with followers of the scholars Yi Hwang and Jo Sik; Westerners looked to followers of Yi I and Seong Hon.

These groups were not political parties in the modern sense. They had no formal membership, platform, or electoral base. They were networks tied by teacher-student relations, kinship, marriage, regional ties, and shared positions on specific issues, and they operated through the offices that mattered: the Three Offices of remonstrance and scholarship, the Ministry of Personnel's control over recommendations, and the State Council. A king could play one group against another, and Seonjo did. Personnel recommendation was where the competition was fiercest, because an official's rank and reach depended on it.

Historical Vocabulary
Bungdang (factions)

Definition: A term for the political associations of scholar-officials in Joseon from the later sixteenth century on, often translated "factions." The word later carried a strongly negative sense in Confucian political thought because it suggested pursuit of private interest at the expense of the public good.

Historical context: The Easterner–Westerner division of 1575 was followed by further subdivisions. After the events of 1591 the Easterners split into Southerners (Namin) and Northerners (Bukin). These labels were also geographical in origin, not descriptions of the groups' regional bases.

Why it matters: Faction labels help students follow disputes over appointments and policy, but they can mislead. Individuals moved between positions, factions were internally divided, and the sources that apply the labels were often compiled by later partisans.

Potential misunderstanding: Factions were not modern parties and should not be treated as the sole explanation of any policy outcome.

Two crises in the years immediately before the war sharpened the divisions. In 1589 an Easterner official named Jeong Yeo-rip was accused of plotting rebellion. The investigation, led by the Westerner statesman Jeong Cheol, grew into a purge known as the Gichuk affair, in which a large number of Easterners and associates were executed, killed in custody, or exiled. Estimates are commonly given in the hundreds, and sometimes about a thousand. In 1591 Jeong Cheol himself fell from favor after raising the unresolved question of the royal succession, since Seonjo had not named an heir. His fall restored Easterners to influence, and the Easterners then divided over how harshly to treat Jeong Cheol's supporters: the lenient became the Southerners, the severe the Northerners.

The consequence for the topic of this chapter is that the embassy to Japan was decided in 1589–1590 in a court shaken by the Gichuk purge, and the envoys' reports were heard in 1591 in a court being reshaped by the fall of Jeong Cheol. Such conditions did not determine what officials believed. They did affect who spoke, who was believed, and who had an interest in a particular answer. Whether this was a principal cause of unpreparedness is the subject of the Historical Debate later in this chapter.

Section 31.3

Toyotomi Hideyoshi and the Unification of Japan

The political order that Joseon had known as Japan's was dissolving even as Joseon refined its policies toward it. During the Sengoku period, regional lords (daimyo) fought to extend their domains. Oda Nobunaga, from central Japan, defeated rivals partly through the use of massed matchlock infantry and the control of key commercial cities, and by 1582 dominated much of central Japan. When he was killed in a coup in that year, his retainer Toyotomi Hideyoshi defeated the conspirator and emerged as the leading figure among Nobunaga's successors.

Hideyoshi was of low social origin, as later records and his own letters acknowledge, and rose through military service. He consolidated power through a mixture of war, alliance, and institutional design. In 1583 he defeated a rival at Shizugatake; in 1584–1585 he reached an accommodation with Tokugawa Ieyasu; in 1585 he received the high court title of Kampaku, imperial regent; and in 1585 and 1587 he brought Shikoku and Kyushu under control. In 1590 he subdued the Hōjō family at Odawara, and the northeastern lords then submitted to him. This did not make Japan a centralized state in the later sense. Daimyo kept their domains, but they now held them within a hierarchy that Hideyoshi controlled.

Several measures reshaped the military and fiscal basis of the regime. Cadastral surveys measured land by productive capacity, giving daimyo assessments that translated directly into military obligations. A "sword hunt" in 1588 disarmed much of the peasantry, and edicts began to separate warrior from commoner status. In 1588 Hideyoshi also issued an order against piracy, which later accounts connected with his claim to control Japan's seas. The result was a mobilization system that could place large numbers of experienced soldiers, many with firearms, under a single command.

For Joseon, the significance was structural. A decade earlier, Tsushima had been one of several parties along the Japanese side of the strait that dealt with Korea. By 1590 it was a subordinate within a hierarchy headed by Hideyoshi, which meant that the political authority across the strait had changed fundamentally, even though the contact points, the Busan enclave and the Tsushima ships, looked the same.

Section 31.4

Hideyoshi's Continental Ambitions

Hideyoshi's aims toward the continent are documented in several kinds of sources, though not all with equal reliability. Reports by Jesuit missionaries record that in the mid-1580s he spoke of conquering China and of using Korea as a path. His letters in the early 1590s to the Ryukyu kingdom and, in 1591, to the Spanish authorities in the Philippines demanded recognition of his authority. Orders in 1591 to build a large staging castle at Nagoya in Hizen province, on the coast of northern Kyushu facing Tsushima, and to assemble troops and ships are among the clearest evidence that an overseas campaign was being planned in earnest. His letter to the Joseon king, discussed below, is the most direct evidence about the message he intended Joseon to receive.

Motives are more disputed. Historians have proposed several overlapping explanations. One emphasizes the need to employ and reward a large military class after unification, since land within Japan was already allocated. Another points to Hideyoshi's personal ambitions and his desire for glory beyond what any previous Japanese ruler had attained. A third stresses commercial and diplomatic aims, including a wish to restore trade with Ming, which had been interrupted in the sixteenth century. A fourth views the project as a kind of political consolidation: a foreign war would test and bind the daimyo to him. None of these explanations excludes the others, and the surviving evidence does not allow a confident ranking.

What the evidence does allow is a conclusion about Joseon's position. Whatever the exact balance of motives, Hideyoshi's stated plan assumed that Joseon would either cooperate in a march toward Ming or be treated as an obstacle. Joseon could not accept the first without breaking its fundamental loyalty to Ming, and it had no reason to believe the second would be tolerated. Diplomacy therefore began with incompatible premises.

Primary Source Window

Hideyoshi's Letter to the Joseon King, 1590

Creator: issued in Hideyoshi's name, probably drafted by Zen monks serving his government · Date: late 1590 · Type: Diplomatic letter in Classical Chinese, preserved in Korean records

Context: The letter was delivered to the Joseon envoys in Japan in reply to the Joseon king's message of congratulation. It is known principally through the version transcribed in Korean sources, since the Japanese original does not survive in a form that allows a complete comparison.

Paraphrase: The following paraphrases the letter's general content. It is not a quotation.

The letter recounts the writer's humble beginnings and portents said to have accompanied his birth, announces that he has brought all of Japan under his authority, and states that he intends to cross the sea and reach Ming. It invites Joseon to join him and presents early cooperation as a sign of good will.

Analysis: The letter treats Joseon as a party that should cooperate with a Japanese ruler's plan. It is written in the language of a ruler addressing a subordinate state, which Joseon officials regarded as inappropriate. Korean accounts record that the envoys objected to its wording about Ming and that Gensō and others in the Sō party intervened to soften or alter phrasing before the envoys departed. Different Korean accounts record the wording and alterations differently, so the exact content of what Joseon read is uncertain.

Limitations: The text does not specify a timetable, the scale of forces, or whether Joseon's refusal would bring war. It also does not tell us how Hideyoshi's advisers understood Joseon's likely response.

Perspective: The letter represents Hideyoshi's regime. It does not represent Joseon's view of Japan, Tsushima's position, or any Ming perception.

Questions for students:

  1. What does the letter say about its author's intentions toward Ming and toward Joseon?
  2. Who created it, and why might Zen monks have drafted it?
  3. What purposes might its tone and framing have served?
  4. What can historians learn about Hideyoshi's regime from it?
  5. What can they not establish from this source alone, and why does the problem of differing transcriptions matter?
Section 31.5

Tsushima between Joseon and Hideyoshi

Tsushima is a mountainous island whose agricultural land cannot feed its population. Its lords, the Sō, had for centuries depended on trade with Korea, and they held Joseon-granted titles and trading rights under the 1443 arrangement. In practice their economy depended on regular access to Busan. At the same time, after 1587 they were obliged to obey the ruler who now commanded Kyushu.

After the Kyushu campaign of 1587, Hideyoshi assigned the Sō the task of bringing the Joseon king to Japan to pay homage, treating Joseon as a state that might be made subordinate. Joseon would never have accepted such a premise. The Sō understood this, and their problem was to avoid both disobeying Hideyoshi and provoking Joseon. A first mission in 1587, led by an emissary named Tachibana Yasuhiro, was received but not answered with a promise of homage. The Sō lord Yoshishige and then his successor Yoshitoshi, who held the domain from 1588, were helped by the monk Gensō, an experienced Zen diplomat, and by Yanagawa Shigenobu. Yoshitoshi's father-in-law was Konishi Yukinaga, a powerful commander in Hideyoshi's service, whose influence and own interests reinforced the Sō's dependence on the new regime.

A second effort in 1589 took the form of a mission to Hanyang led by Gensō and Yoshitoshi. According to Korean records, the Joseon court demanded the return of Korean men who had taken part in raids on the southwest coast in 1587, including a man named Sa Hwa-dong, as a condition of further dealings. The Sō delivered a number of such men. The Joseon court, seeing this as evidence of the Sō's good faith, agreed to send an embassy. Japanese gifts presented on that occasion are recorded to have included muskets and peacocks.

The central point is that the embassy was framed differently by each side. To the Sō and probably to Hideyoshi, it could be presented as an act of homage or a first step toward submission. To Joseon, it was a courtesy mission to a neighboring ruler who had unified Japan, in the style of earlier embassies. The Sō had every reason to keep these two interpretations apart. Their strategy of softening, translating generously, and leaving ambiguities unresolved was rational for a small domain caught between two powers. It also meant that neither side heard the other's demands in plain terms.

Section 31.6

Diplomacy Begins to Fail

Diplomatic failure in these years did not come from a single refused message. It accumulated from incompatible assumptions, different ranks and titles, and gaps in translation. Joseon's court could not easily classify Hideyoshi. He was not a king, and he did not hold the imperial title, which belonged to the emperor in Kyoto. His own title, Kampaku, was a court office for a regent. Joseon officials who wanted to avoid treating him as a sovereign equal could point to his low birth. Hideyoshi, for his part, expected recognition as the supreme authority in Japan.

The conventions of correspondence made things worse. Letters between East Asian courts used a vocabulary of rank, tribute, and moral hierarchy. A phrase that one side regarded as a polite courtesy could read to the other as a demand for submission. The Sō as intermediaries translated and sometimes altered phrases. Reports of what was said at one end passed through a third party before reaching the other. When Joseon officials eventually read a letter that spoke of entering Ming, they responded with alarm. The reply they sent, according to accounts, was composed in conventional courteous terms and did not accept the proposal.

Historical Vocabulary
Gyorin and Tongsinsa

Definition: Gyorin (neighborly relations) was the term for Joseon's policy toward Japan and the Jurchens, as distinct from sadae, service to the greater, directed toward Ming. A tongsinsa was an envoy sent to convey good will to a neighboring ruler, literally an envoy of communication and trust.

Historical context: Joseon had sent such envoys to Japan in the fifteenth century. By the 1580s no mission had gone for roughly a century, and by that time the Japanese authority that had once received them no longer exercised real control.

Why it matters: The 1590 embassy was a revival of an old form under new circumstances. Students should not assume that either side shared a view of what the form meant.

Potential misunderstanding: "Tribute" in this period was not a single fixed institution. Its meaning depended on who used the word and what each party expected to follow from it.

Section 31.7

The 1590 Joseon Embassy to Japan

After debate at court, Joseon appointed three principal officials. Hwang Yun-gil, a Westerner, was senior envoy. Kim Seong-il, an Easterner and a follower of Yi Hwang's school, was deputy envoy. Heo Seong, also an Easterner, was secretary. The party sailed from Busan to Tsushima in 1590, escorted by the Sō party, and traveled through the Inland Sea to Sakai and Kyoto. Hideyoshi was away in eastern Japan, completing the siege of Odawara and then pacifying the northeast, so the envoys waited for months.

They observed what they could. The route took them past ports, castles, and troop movements in an obviously militarized society, even if the envoys' interpretation of what they saw varied. Late in 1590 Hideyoshi, now back in Kyoto, received them at his palace. The Joseon accounts record a formal reception with gifts and with the return of the letter. After the audience, the envoys waited at Sakai for a reply to be prepared, while the letter's wording was negotiated through Gensō and others. They returned via Tsushima and reached Busan in the spring of 1591.

The embassy was Joseon's most important opportunity to see Japan directly. It was also a limited one. The mission was not a covert military reconnaissance expedition, and its members had little freedom to inspect Japanese preparations, although observing political conditions and Hideyoshi's intentions was part of its purpose. They were guided by Japanese hosts and constrained by diplomatic protocol and by language: communication relied on written Chinese and interpreters. They saw samurai, castles, and ceremony, but they did not see the staging works at Nagoya, which were not yet begun. What they could assess was Hideyoshi's character, the mood of his court, and the wording of his letter. That is exactly what they reported.

Tsushima continued to press after the embassy's return. Korean records indicate that Gensō and others returned to Joseon in 1591 with messages that made the demand for passage through Korea more explicit, and that Joseon refused to agree. At that point, Joseon had enough information to know that Hideyoshi proposed a campaign against Ming and expected Joseon's help. What it lacked was reliable information about timing and scale.

Section 31.8

What Did the Envoys Actually Report?

The popular account is compact: Hwang Yun-gil returned and warned the court that Japan would invade; Kim Seong-il returned and said there was no sign of war; the court, dominated by Kim's faction, listened to Kim; and disaster followed. The story has the shape of a morality tale, and that is a reason to examine its sources.

The earliest layer of evidence is the entries in the Veritable Records of King Seonjo (Seonjo Sillok), which preserve the court's discussions. The next is Yu Seong-ryong's memoir, the Jingbirok, which discusses a conversation in which he questioned Kim Seong-il privately. A third is the revised edition of the Veritable Records (Seonjo Sujeong Sillok), compiled in the mid-seventeenth century. This is important because the original Seonjo Veritable Records were compiled in the early seventeenth century under a court in which Northerners held influence, and the revision was produced decades later under a court in which Westerners dominated. Each version reflects its compilers' affiliations and their knowledge of what happened next.

Taking these together, historians can say several things with reasonable confidence. The envoys did disagree. Hwang Yun-gil's assessment was alarmed. Kim Seong-il challenged it, arguing that Hwang's reports were stirring needless panic and that the evidence for an invasion was not conclusive. Later summaries commonly place Heo Seong, despite his Easterner affiliation, closer to Hwang's assessment, so the dispute should not be reduced to a simple division along factional lines. Sources differ on whether Kim denied that war could ever come or doubted its imminence and scale, and the Jingbirok portrays him as more hesitant than the later summary suggests. The disagreement also concerned means of judgment: Hwang relied on impressions of Hideyoshi's character and bearing, while Kim saw reasons for doubt.

Two further points complicate the moral tale. First, the court did not simply believe Kim and do nothing, as the measures in Section 31.11 show. Second, after the invasion began Kim Seong-il served as a provincial governor in Gyeongsang and died in 1593 while organizing resistance, so the later image of him as a complacent official sits uneasily with his conduct once the war began. Debates about whom to blame served the political interests of later factions, as the Historical Debate explores.

Section 31.9

Information, Intelligence, and Uncertainty

Joseon had no standing intelligence service. Its information about Japan came chiefly from Tsushima, from envoys on rare occasions, from merchants and fishermen, from captives and returnees, and from reports forwarded by southern officials. The principal informant, Tsushima, was also a party with its own interests, which meant that its reports were shaped by what its leaders wished Joseon to believe.

Other evidence existed but was ambiguous. Reports that Hideyoshi had demanded supplies and cooperation from Ryukyu, and merchants' accounts from Fujian of Japanese preparations, circulated in the Ming world. The Japanese residents of the Busan enclave had, according to later accounts, mostly left by the eve of the invasion, a sign that something had changed, though its meaning was clear only afterward. Rumors of impending war spread among the population. A court faces a difficulty in such conditions: in any year there are warnings, most turn out to be false, and acting on all of them has costs.

The conceptual problem was also important. What Joseon feared, from experience, was raiding: the 1555 and 1587 raids had been serious but regional. What the evidence now suggested was an invasion on a different scale, requiring an army to cross the strait and march through the peninsula toward Ming. Nothing since the Mongol era had resembled this. Officials who judged the likelihood from past patterns were reasoning plausibly from available experience, and the structure of the problem meant that a rare event with enormous consequences was difficult to weigh against the ordinary cost of preparation.

Section 31.10

Joseon's Military System on the Eve of War

The Joseon military had been designed in the fifteenth century for border defense and internal order, and it had adapted over time. At the center, the Border Defense Council coordinated policy after 1555. Regional commanders held army and naval posts in each province. Local magistrates were responsible for registers of men liable for service and for the upkeep of fortresses, granaries, and arsenals.

Command in an emergency followed a system known as jeseung bangnyak, which replaced an earlier system of regional garrison commands. Under it, in a crisis the troops of many districts would march to a designated assembly point, where a commander dispatched by the court would take charge. The Border Defense Council, by contrast, became a more permanent institution only after the 1555 raid. The idea was flexible: a concentrated force could be deployed against a raiding party. The weakness was that it required the assembled men to arrive in time and that a commander who did not know the men would lead them.

Historical Vocabulary
Jeseung bangnyak

Definition: A system of emergency command in which local forces from several districts assembled at a designated place under a commander appointed by the central government.

Historical context: Developing from the early sixteenth century, especially after the 1510 Three Ports uprising, the system modified the older jingwan arrangement by concentrating local forces at strategic points under centrally dispatched commanders. It was meant to counter coastal raiding with a concentrated force.

Why it matters: Against a fast-moving large army, the system's reliance on assembly and on a new commander, rather than a standing local force, was a serious weakness.

Potential misunderstanding: The system was not incompetent; it was designed for a different kind of threat.

The manpower base was weaker than the registers suggested. Military service was in principle an obligation of commoner males, but by the sixteenth century substitution and banggun supo had spread: men increasingly avoided direct service by providing cloth or other payments. These arrangements helped support military institutions in some cases but were also widely abused, including by local commanders who accepted payments and kept them, so nominal registers increasingly overstated the men actually available. Training was irregular, and many officers had little combat experience, though the northern frontier, where Jurchen incursions continued, had produced officers who did. Archery remained prominent, and Joseon possessed an established gunpowder-artillery tradition, including cannon and smaller handheld chongtong. What it lacked was Japan's extensive force of infantry trained with matchlock arquebuses. The Japanese gift of muskets in 1589 apparently did not lead to large-scale adoption.

Fortifications were another area of unevenness. Joseon relied heavily on mountain fortresses (sanseong) as places of refuge, while many walled district towns in the south had low or decayed walls. Maintaining walls required forced labor and money, which local officials were reluctant to demand from a population already burdened by taxes. A later tradition credits the scholar-official Yi I with advocating the preparation of roughly 100,000 troops in 1583, shortly before his death in 1584. The precise form of this proposal and its later representation require caution, since the story reflects later reassessments, but it illustrates an argument that the existing system needed strengthening.

Section 31.11

Attempts at Preparation

It is incorrect to say Joseon did nothing, but preparation was uneven rather than absent. Official records indicate that some defensive works were curtailed after the embassy debate, when the reassuring assessment carried weight. As additional warnings accumulated during 1591, including messages from Tsushima, the court again ordered inspections, appointments, fortification, training, and other measures. Fortresses in the southern provinces of Gyeongsang, Jeolla, and Chungcheong were ordered repaired or expanded, and provincial officials mobilized labor to do so. Senior officers were sent to inspect defenses and review troops. Appointments were made to key posts, and some of the officers chosen later proved effective.

The most consequential appointment for the later war was that of Yi Sun-sin. In early 1591, on the recommendation of Yu Seong-ryong, Yi was promoted from a district magistracy to command the Left Jeolla naval district, a jump in rank that critics at court questioned. His diary, which begins at the start of 1592, shows him inspecting fortifications, training crews, repairing and building ships, and testing cannon, including on a newly constructed turtle ship, in the days before the war. These activities are discussed here only as evidence of preparation; his campaigns belong to Chapter 33. Other appointments, including that of Gwon Yul as a district magistrate in Jeolla, also later proved significant.

Preparations had limits. The labor demanded for fortifications was resented. Some officials argued that walls were useless against a force that could not be stopped in open battle, while others argued that wall-building diverted effort from training. Commanders in the south were distributed unevenly in capability, and the central assumption of the command system, that troops would assemble at a designated point under a commander sent from the capital, had not been tested against a large invasion. The measures taken were thus significant but fragmentary.

Historical Debate

Did Factional Politics Cause Joseon's Unpreparedness?

The question: To what extent did factional conflict at court explain Joseon's failure to prepare adequately for the invasion of 1592?

Interpretation A — Factionalism as principal cause: Traditional accounts, shaped by the seventeenth-century revision of the Veritable Records and by later moral readings, treat factional rivalry as the central cause: officials aligned reports and recommendations with factional interests, and the court's attention was absorbed in internal disputes. This reading was also used by some colonial-era writers to portray factional politics as a characteristic weakness of Joseon.
Interpretation B — Factionalism as a factor among others: Many historians now argue that the evidence for factional distortion is weaker than it appears: the disagreement of the envoys was real but not simply partisan, the court took defensive measures, and the factional narrative was shaped by later compilers with their own interests. They stress structural causes, including the limits of the military system, fiscal and labor constraints, and the difficulty of interpreting ambiguous evidence.
Interpretation C — The problem of recognition: A further view emphasizes the conceptual difficulty: officials grounded their judgments in past experience of Japanese raiding and in a Ming-centered view of the region, and they did not readily imagine an invasion of this scale. On this view, factions affected how arguments were made but did not create the underlying uncertainty.

Evidence: The principal sources are the two versions of the Veritable Records, Yu Seong-ryong's memoir, and records of appointments and fortification orders. Each is compromised in different ways.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns causation and interpretation, and to some extent the reliability of sources.

Why it matters: If factionalism was the principal cause, the war can be read as a moral failure of the Joseon elite. If it was one factor in a multi-causal situation, the episode becomes a case study in institutional strain and strategic surprise, and the explanation changes how students should understand the dynasty's capacity and its subsequent recovery.

Student task: Identify one piece of evidence that supports each interpretation, and one source of bias that affects it. What additional evidence would help decide among them?

Section 31.12

The Southern Coast and Strategic Geography

The strait between Tsushima and Busan is about fifty kilometers wide, a short crossing in good weather. Busan was Joseon's main contact point with Japan: a harbor with a garrison and, nearby, the Japanese trading enclave (waegwan) where licensed Japanese traders lived and worked under Joseon supervision. A few miles inland lay Dongnae, a walled district town that was the administrative center of the area. These places formed the front line, and they were well known to the Japanese, especially the Sō, who had traded and traveled there for generations.

From Dongnae the main road ran north-northwest through the southeastern interior toward Hanyang, passing Yangsan, Miryang, Daegu, Seonsan, and Sangju, crossing the Sobaek range at Joryeong pass, and continuing through Chungju toward the capital. Other roads ran west toward Jinju and the Jeolla provinces and along the eastern coast. The mountain ranges of the interior channeled movement along a few corridors, and the passes were defensible. The Naktong River valley provided supply routes but also an avenue for advance.

The seas were equally important. The southern and western coasts were studded with islands and harbors, and naval districts controlled by Gyeongsang and Jeolla commanders guarded them. Joseon's agricultural wealth lay heavily in the southwest, and grain moved north by sea. The sea lanes along the western coast linked the south with Hanyang and with the Ming frontier. A power that could interrupt those lanes could threaten Joseon's supply and communications, and a power that could not would have to rely on overland routes. These considerations became decisive in the war and belong to later chapters. For now the point is that the invasion's geography was understood, in principle, by both sides.

Section 31.13

Ming, Jurchens, and the Wider Geopolitical Setting

Ming China was the senior power in the regional order and Joseon's political and cultural reference point. The Ming court under the Wanli emperor, who reigned from 1572, was entering a period of reduced imperial engagement in routine government after the death of the grand secretary Zhang Juzheng in 1582. Ming had its own commitments: campaigns against rebellion in the southwest, pressure along the northern frontier, and, in early 1592, a major military mutiny at Ningxia in the northwest. Ming's attention was therefore divided when the first warnings arrived.

Information reached Ming through several channels. Ming had cut off official trade with Japan in the sixteenth century, but merchants from Fujian traveled to Japan, and Ryukyu was a tributary of Ming with its own links to Japan. Joseon also faced a dilemma about informing Ming. It owed Ming loyalty and information, but it also knew that reports suggesting that Japan might cross Korea could invite Ming suspicion that Joseon was in league with Japan. Joseon had recently spent years of diplomacy resolving a dispute over how a Ming reference work described the Yi royal house's descent, and it was sensitive to its standing at the Ming court. The record shows that Joseon debated how to present the matter.

The result was that, in early 1592, the threat appeared to both courts to be primarily a Joseon–Japanese problem. Ming was aware of Hideyoshi's intentions in outline, but Ming officials had not decided to commit forces, and they were uncertain whether Joseon would be a reliable partner. The Jurchen frontier added to Joseon's problem: after the Nitanggae raid of 1583 and with Nurhaci's rise, the northern provinces needed forces and experienced officers, which meant that some of Joseon's best commanders were not in the south. Joseon thus faced a potential two-front problem, though the northern threat was less acute in 1592.

Section 31.14

On the Eve of Invasion, Spring 1592

By the spring of 1592 the Japanese side had completed a vast mobilization. Nagoya in Hizen, built within a few months, served as the center of a staging area where contingents from many domains assembled with ships and supplies. The force was organized into nine divisions totaling roughly 150,000–160,000 troops, though figures vary by source, deployed in successive contingents with further reserves behind. Konishi Yukinaga, with the Sō contingent from Tsushima, commanded the first division, and Katō Kiyomasa and Kuroda Nagamasa led the next two. Hideyoshi did not intend to cross immediately himself. Tsushima, which had spent years as intermediary, now became a forward base.

Joseon's side was a mixture. The court had warnings and had acted on some of them. Fortresses had been repaired, though unevenly. Yi Sun-sin and other naval commanders were building ships and training crews. Officers had been sent south, though the troops they might lead had not assembled. Politically, the court was still adjusting to the fall of Jeong Cheol and the division of the Easterners, and the question of the royal succession remained unsettled. Many officials continued to doubt that any invasion would be on the scale or with the speed that Hideyoshi planned. The southern magistrates and commanders were at their posts but were scattered, and the main strength of the army was not assembled.

The gap between what Joseon knew and what it needed to know was therefore not a matter of total ignorance. Joseon knew that Hideyoshi had unified Japan, wrote of entering Ming, expected cooperation, and had been refused. It knew that some Japanese had left Busan and that preparations were rumored. It did not know, and had little means of knowing, that the first division would sail within weeks with tens of thousands of veteran troops, that they would be followed by many more, and that they would drive deep into the peninsula faster than the command system could respond.

In the weeks that followed, the assembled fleet and its first contingents prepared to cross from Tsushima. Chapter 32 opens with their arrival off Busan on the 13th day of the fourth lunar month, and with the collapse of the southern defenses that followed.

Continuity and Change Over Time

The Strait Order, c. 1500–1592

What Changed

  • Japan passed from regional fragmentation to a unified military hierarchy under one ruler, between about 1582 and 1590.
  • Tsushima moved from a semi-independent trading intermediary to a subordinate required to carry out Hideyoshi's demands.
  • The Joseon political class divided into factions, and in 1591 the Easterners divided further.
  • Japanese military capacity, especially firearms and mobilization, grew far beyond anything Joseon had faced from raiders.

What Stayed the Same

  • The Busan enclave, Tsushima ships, and ritual correspondence continued, so the contact points looked familiar.
  • Joseon's loyalty to Ming and its Ming-centered view of the region persisted.
  • By the later sixteenth century, emergency assembly under jeseung bangnyak and increasingly cloth-based military obligations had become entrenched features of the military system, and both remained in place on the eve of war.
  • The southern coast and road network remained as geographic constants.

Why the pattern matters: Change occurred unevenly. The surface continuity of diplomatic forms made the underlying change in Japan harder to see. Those who experienced the change most directly were the Sō, the Japanese commanders, and the southern officials. Those who experienced greater continuity were most of the officials and population of the interior, for whom the strait remained distant.

Cause and Effect

Why War Became Possible, and Why Joseon Was Not Ready

Long-term causes

Japanese political consolidation after a century of civil war; the spread of firearms; Joseon's settled view of Japan as fragmented and raid-prone; a military system built around emergency assembly; the decay of service obligations.

Short-term causes

Hideyoshi's victories of 1587–1590 and his continental plans; Tsushima's failed mediation; factional conflict from 1589 to 1591; the envoys' divided reports; a very short interval for decision.

Immediate trigger

Completion of the Nagoya staging base and the assembly of the invasion force in the spring of 1592.

Event or transformation

The crossing of the invasion fleet in the spring of 1592; its arrival at Busan opens Chapter 32.

Immediate effects

Rapid collapse of southern defenses, the court's flight from the capital, and the opening of a war in which Ming would intervene (Chapters 32 and 34).

Long-term consequences

Devastation of Joseon society, military reform, a changed relationship with Ming, and the later crisis with the Manchus (Chapters 35 and 36).

Historical complexity: No single cause is sufficient. Hideyoshi's ambitions made war possible; the failure of mediation, the structure of Joseon's defenses, uncertain evidence, and political division determined how ready Joseon was. Chronological sequence is not causation: factional conflict preceded the war but did not by itself explain unpreparedness.

Everyday Life and Social History

Households, Labor, and the Border

For ordinary people in the southern provinces, the road to war was mostly a matter of obligations. Commoner households owed tax, cloth, grain, and labor. Where a man was assigned to military service, his household might pay cloth in place of his service, and orders to repair fortifications meant additional corvée labor in the agricultural season. Such demands were resented, and they were one reason local officials hesitated to impose them. Women managed household production and, when men were called away, much of the farm's work. Enslaved people (nobi) were part of the labor force and, in some cases, of local military mobilization, though the sources are thin.

At the border, daily life included trade. Japanese merchants and their Korean partners dealt in cloth, grain, copper, silver, and other goods through the Busan enclave, and fishermen on both sides of the strait knew its waters. When Japanese residents left the enclave, people in the port noticed. On Tsushima, where the population relied heavily on the sea and on trade with Korea, the shift toward war meant requisitions, the building of ships, and the prospect of the loss of a livelihood. The sources for these experiences are scarce and mostly come from officials and chroniclers, so historians must infer them indirectly from administrative records, later memoirs, and archaeology.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has asked why war became possible in 1592 and why Joseon was unable to turn warning signs into adequate preparation. The answer has been multi-causal: a transformed Japan, a Joseon order built for different conditions, an intermediary whose position became untenable, uncertain evidence, and a court divided at a critical moment.

The strategic environment changed after about 1580. Japan moved from decades of civil war to a unified military hierarchy under Hideyoshi, whose military organization, firearms, and ambitions were new to the region. Joseon's assumptions about Japan, formed by a century of dealing with Tsushima and coastal raiders, were reasonable for the older world and ill suited to the new one.

Joseon's court was shaped by the rise of scholar-officials and by factional division. The Easterner–Westerner division of about 1575, the Gichuk purge of 1589, and the fall of Jeong Cheol and the split of the Easterners in 1591 coincided with the key diplomatic decisions. Faction mattered for appointments and for how evidence was read, but it was not the whole explanation.

Hideyoshi's plans to reach Ming are documented in his letters, in Jesuit reports, and in the building of the staging base at Nagoya. His motives remain debated, and several explanations are plausible. What is clear is that his stated plan required either Joseon's cooperation or its subordination, and that Joseon could accept neither.

Tsushima was caught between the two. Dependent on Korean trade and obliged to Hideyoshi, the Sō and Gensō tried to reconcile incompatible demands by softening wording and leaving ambiguities. The strategy was rational for a small domain, but it prevented either side from hearing the other plainly.

The embassy of 1590 gave Joseon a rare view of Japan and a letter that troubled the envoys. Their reports diverged, but the familiar story of one envoy predicting war and another denying it is a simplification shaped by later political memory. Sources disagree on what exactly was said, and the court did not simply do nothing.

Intelligence was fragmentary and mediated by interested parties. Joseon had no standing intelligence service and relied heavily on Tsushima. Rumors, signs such as the departure of Japanese from Busan, and reports from Ming-world channels circulated. The conceptual problem was as important as the informational one: officials accustomed to raids did not readily imagine an invasion of this scale.

The military system rested on emergency assembly under a commander sent from the capital, on registers that overstated available manpower, and on uneven fortifications. The court made real efforts in 1591–1592: repairs, inspections, appointments such as that of Yi Sun-sin, and naval preparations. These were significant but incomplete.

The southern coast, the road to Hanyang, and the sea lanes shaped the strategic problem. Ming and the Jurchen frontier added complications: Ming's attention was divided and Joseon hesitated over how to inform it, while the northern frontier absorbed experienced officers. By spring 1592 the Japanese staging was complete, Joseon had warnings and partial preparations, and the landing was imminent.

The chapter's larger lesson is methodological. The prewar years are known mainly through sources written by people who knew the outcome, and historians must separate what contemporaries could know from what later writers thought they should have known. Doing so replaces a simple moral story with a more instructive account of institutional strain, uncertainty, and strategic surprise.

Research or Library Extension

MGU Library Research Project

Constructing a Source Dossier on the 1590 Embassy

This project asks you to assemble a dossier of sources about the 1590 embassy and to analyze how different accounts shape our understanding of it.

  1. Identify sources. Using library resources, locate translations or scholarly discussions of the Seonjo Sillok, the Seonjo Sujeong Sillok, the Jingbirok, and Japanese or Jesuit sources on Hideyoshi's plans.
  2. Document each source. For each, record author, date of composition, political context, and likely audience.
  3. Compare accounts. Note where the sources agree, where they diverge, and which divergences can be explained by the compilers' circumstances.
  4. Assess reliability. Identify what each source can and cannot establish, and what evidence is missing.
  5. Write your findings. In a 4–6 page essay, present a reasoned account of what the envoys reported and why, using careful language such as "sources suggest" or "this remains uncertain."

Library resources to use: Korean and Japanese historical source collections in translation, scholarly studies of the Imjin War, reference works on Joseon institutions, and the MGU History Library's materials on East Asian diplomacy.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a state can be warned and still be unready. The failure of 1591–1592 was not the absence of information but the difficulty of interpreting incomplete information through assumptions formed in a different strategic world. That difficulty is a recurring problem in the history of diplomacy and war, and the Joseon case allows students to study it with an unusually rich body of sources.

The chapter also reveals the importance of intermediaries. Tsushima's attempt to hold together two incompatible orders delayed a crisis and, in doing so, may have reduced the clarity of the signals each side received. This points to a wider pattern: border societies often absorb conflicts between larger powers, and their incentives shape the information that moves between them.

Finally, the chapter shows how historical memory is made. The story of the two envoys, the moral weight given to factions, and the picture of a court that ignored warnings all took shape after the disaster, in compilations produced by people with political stakes. Reading them critically does not excuse errors, but it replaces a tidy narrative with a more accurate and more useful one. Chapter 32 now turns to what happened when the invasion began, and later chapters will show how Joseon, having been surprised, rebuilt its defenses and its relationship with Ming.
