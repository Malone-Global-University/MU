Assimilation, Collaboration, and Korean Identity

PUBLICATION EDITION — Consolidated historical and documentary revision, 6 November 2026.

Korean History: Ancient Kingdoms to a Divided Peninsula

Chapter Opening

In October 1937 the Government-General of Korea distributed a short pledge of loyalty to the Japanese emperor, to be recited in schools, offices, and public gatherings. A child could memorize it in minutes. Whether reciting it changed what the child believed is a question the pledge itself cannot answer.

The Imperial Subject Oath is a convenient entry point to this chapter because it shows both the ambition and the limit of assimilation. Colonial authorities wanted more than obedience. They wanted Koreans to understand themselves as subjects of the Japanese emperor, to speak Japanese as the national language, to register household names in Japanese form, to attend ceremonies at Shinto shrines, and to join organizations that tied everyday conduct to imperial war aims. Yet the oath could be required, recited, and recorded without anyone being able to require what lay behind the words.

This chapter concentrates on the years from 1936 to 1945 while tracing the roots of assimilation back to annexation in 1910. It examines four related processes: the policies and institutions designed to produce loyal imperial subjects; the participation of Koreans in administering, legitimizing, or promoting colonial goals; the everyday responses of people living under unequal power, from outward compliance to refusal; and the preservation and transformation of Korean language, religion, family practice, and historical memory.

These processes should not be collapsed into one another. A Korean police officer, a writer producing wartime propaganda, a family registering a new household name, and a schoolchild reciting an oath stood in very different positions, with different powers, obligations, and alternatives. Neither “collaborator” nor “patriot” is an adequate single label for them. The chapter therefore asks what each person or institution actually did, when, with what authority, and under what pressure.

Throughout, the chapter distinguishes imperial rhetoric from imperial practice. The slogan naisen ittai, usually translated “Japan and Korea as one body,” promised unity, yet colonial society retained unequal legal status, political representation, and social standing. The evidence includes statutes, ordinances, school records, newspapers, police files, memoirs, and later testimony. Each records a different kind of behavior, and none can be read as a transparent window onto private belief.

The chapter depends on its neighbors. Chapter 57 covered the growth of schools, newspapers, and mass culture; Chapter 59 covered political independence movements; Chapter 61 treats Japanese continental expansion; and Chapter 62 treats wartime labor, military service, and sexual slavery. Here the focus is the ideology, institutions, and cultural pressure through which colonial power tried to reshape Korean identity.

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  2. Explain the meaning of kōminka and naisen ittai and analyze the contradiction between these claims of unity and unequal colonial institutions.
  3. Distinguish the 1938 and 1943 education ordinances and their effects on Korean-language instruction.
  4. Analyze the legal structure and implementation of the 1939–1940 name policy.
  5. Assess the escalation of shrine attendance requirements and the range of religious responses.
  6. Evaluate how newspapers, literature, film, and broadcasting were transformed by wartime control.
  7. Differentiate collaboration, institutional participation, constrained accommodation, and coerced compliance using dated evidence.
  8. Interpret the relationship between cultural preservation and political resistance, including the Korean Language Society case.
  9. Compare the kinds of evidence historians use and explain why public compliance does not reveal private belief.
  10. Contextualize the legacies of assimilation for Korean identity after 1945.

Historical Context

Japan annexed Korea in 1910, governing through a Government-General that answered to Tokyo. Students should already know from Chapters 55–57 the main outline of colonial administration, cultural rule after 1919, and the growth of schools, newspapers, and mass culture. Chapter 59 covered organized independence movements. This chapter takes up the attempt, strongest after 1936, to remake Korean loyalty and identity.

Two background points matter. First, assimilation as an aim was present from the start, but its methods and intensity changed. Second, colonial law preserved distinctions between Koreans and Japanese, so the call for unity operated inside an unequal legal order. The wartime mobilization of labor and soldiers is treated in Chapter 62, and Japanese continental expansion in Chapter 61.

Section 60.1

Assimilation and Colonial Rule after Annexation in 1910

Japanese rule began in 1910 with annexation, not with a program of equal integration. The Government-General in Seoul, headed by a military governor-general, held legislative and executive power and was answerable to Tokyo, not to any Korean body. Koreans were colonial subjects of the Japanese emperor, but the laws governing them differed from those governing residents of the home islands, and Koreans did not enjoy equivalent political rights. Assimilation, in the sense of making Koreans resemble and identify with Japanese, was therefore an ideological aim operating inside a system of legal difference.

Early policy emphasized public order, police supervision, and the subordination of Korean political institutions, topics covered in Chapter 55. Education was an early instrument. The first Chōsen Education Ordinance of 1911 differentiated schooling for Koreans from that for Japanese residents, stressed Japanese-language instruction and loyalty, and limited most Korean students to elementary and vocational training. Historians generally read this as an assimilationist goal paired with deliberate restriction of advancement.

The point for this chapter is chronological. Assimilation did not appear suddenly in 1937. It had institutional roots in the earliest decade of rule, then changed in intensity and method as political circumstances changed. Treating it as one unchanged policy from 1910 to 1945 obscures that history.

The institutional contradiction was evident in education and law. The Government-General legislated by ordinance for Korea, while the Japanese Diet did not operate as an elected legislature representing residents of the colony. Schools, courts, police and registers administered a population classified as imperial subjects but governed through a distinctive colonial regime. Japanese language, the emperor's place in moral instruction and official hierarchy were introduced as practical signs of belonging. Nevertheless, learning the prescribed language did not open an equal path to political participation. This matters because assimilation was never simply a cultural encounter between neighboring societies: it was a project backed by an asymmetry of authority.

A complete history also needs the Korean side of the encounter. Parents might seek Japanese-language education because it offered access to government employment or commercial work without endorsing colonial rule. A school could produce capable bilingual graduates while remaining institutionally unequal. Official demands for deference coexisted with Korean family customs, religious institutions and community languages. Some young Koreans became adept at navigating these worlds, and others lacked the opportunity to attend school at all. The variation explains why later intensification did not proceed across a blank social landscape: it worked through familiar schools, registers and officials, while attempting to broaden their claims over conduct and belief.

Section 60.2

Cultural Rule after 1919: Accommodation, Surveillance, and the Limits of Pluralism

The March First Movement of 1919, discussed in Chapter 56, convinced colonial leaders that rule by overt military force alone was unsustainable. Under Governor-General Saitō Makoto, policy shifted toward what is often called cultural rule. Civilian-style policing replaced the gendarmerie in name, a limited number of Korean-language newspapers and magazines received permission to publish, and the second Education Ordinance of 1922 formally reduced educational differences between Koreans and Japanese.

These measures opened real if narrow space for Korean publishing, associations, and public debate, much of it examined in Chapter 57. They also coexisted with censorship, police surveillance, and the unchanged principle that Korea was governed from Tokyo. Cultural concessions should not be confused with recognition of Korean sovereignty or political equality.

For assimilation, cultural rule mattered in two ways. It showed that colonial authorities could pursue loyalty through selective tolerance as well as pressure. It also created institutions, including Korean-language newspapers, private schools, and religious bodies, that later became targets when policy hardened in the late 1930s.

The policy of cultural rule allowed Korean actors to build institutions that the colonial state could monitor but not fully control. Licensed newspapers became meeting places for debate about education, historical language and social reform. Mission schools and churches cultivated distinct institutional cultures. Cultural nationalists advanced projects in the belief that educating Koreans would help prepare for future self-government. Yet obtaining authorization to publish or hold a meeting did not protect a group when its arguments challenged imperial interests. The authorities retained powers of inspection, suspension, arrest and censorship, and the expanded police system could reach into local associations.

This mixture matters for the later history of coercion. A Korean-language newspaper could become a source of public influence in the 1920s and face a demand to cease publication in 1940; a school permitted to teach some Korean could lose that curricular space under revised ordinances; a church that developed a public life could be required to incorporate imperial observances into worship-related settings. In other words, the late-1930s campaign reclassified activities once tolerated as obstacles to unity. Students should resist portraying cultural rule either as genuine political autonomy or as wholly fictitious: its openings were limited but consequential, and their subsequent closure changed everyday opportunities.

Section 60.3

Manchuria, Imperial Expansion, and the Intensification of Assimilation, 1931–1936

Japan’s seizure of Manchuria in 1931, treated in Chapter 61, changed Korea’s position in the empire. The peninsula became a forward base, an industrial supplier, and a corridor to the continent. Authorities in Seoul increasingly judged Korean political reliability a strategic matter, not only an administrative one.

Governor-General Ugaki Kazushige, who served from 1931 to 1936, promoted rural revitalization and moral or social education campaigns aimed at discipline, thrift, and harmonious relations between Japanese and Korean subjects. These were assimilation measures in a broad sense, but they did not yet involve the sweeping language, name, and ritual requirements of the late 1930s. This period should be read as an intermediate stage: pressure rose, the strategic stakes grew, and the institutional groundwork for later campaigns was extended, including expanded expectations for shrine participation examined in Division IV.

Korea's expanding strategic role altered official expectations of its population. A peninsula linked by rail and shipping to northern China provided a corridor for soldiers and goods and, increasingly, a production base for military industries. This was not itself an assimilation ordinance, but it made questions of loyalty seem urgent to colonial planners. Political discussion, household conduct and schooling could now be evaluated through the language of emergency, service and national unity. The shift preceded the outbreak of full-scale war in China in 1937, although the subsequent war greatly intensified it.

Ugaki's rural and moral campaigns reflected more than economic concern. Officially encouraged thrift, household reconstruction, work discipline and communal order attempted to place local institutions under more regular supervision. Rural revitalization often addressed real indebtedness and declining income, but it could also redirect grievances away from landlord–tenant conflict and toward prescribed personal discipline. These were not identical to mandatory name registration or abolition of Korean-language instruction: institutions, intensity and legal powers varied. The historically useful connection lies in administrative technique—officials learned to work through village leaders, associations and recurring household assessments that later total-mobilization leagues adapted to more explicitly ideological goals.

Section 60.4

Governor-General Minami Jirō and the Reorientation of Colonial Policy, 1936–1937

Minami Jirō, a former army minister, became governor-general in 1936. His administration promoted a program of imperial integration, advanced under slogans associated with national polity, unity of Japan and Korea, and endurance. The appointment coincided with the approach of full-scale war in China in July 1937, and the demand for visible Korean loyalty sharpened accordingly.

Historians should be careful about attributing the change to Minami alone. Policy reflected decisions in Tokyo, the military’s growing weight in government, the preferences of settler interests in Korea, and wartime requirements. Minami’s tenure is best understood as the period when these pressures converged into an explicit and far more intrusive program, whose main instruments the following divisions examine: schools, names, shrines, propaganda, and mass organizations.

Minami did not begin with a fully formed sequence of measures. His government announced a language of imperial solidarity that was subsequently turned into distinct ordinances, campaigns and organizations. The 1937 oath offered a compact formula of loyalty; the 1938 education changes altered school structures; the 1939 name measures reworked registration; and mass organizations connected schools, employers and neighborhood groups to officials. That chronology shows an accumulating system, not a single decree. War demands, military personnel shortages and expanding administrative capacities shaped how the program changed from year to year.

A second historical distinction concerns authority. General policies could be announced at the top while local enforcement took place through school principals, village heads, employers, police officers, church administrators and family registrars. These intermediaries interpreted, applied and sometimes increased pressure beyond what formal statutes stated. For example, a law may specify a registration procedure without directing a shopkeeper to refuse service to noncompliant customers; contemporary reports of such pressures require separate evidence. The governor-general's program therefore has to be studied at two scales: its ideological design and its experienced operation within local hierarchies.

Section 60.5

Kōminka and Naisen Ittai: Imperial Subject-Making and the Claim of Unity

Kōminka is usually translated “imperial subject-making” or “imperialization.” It named the project of transforming Koreans into loyal subjects of the emperor through language, education, ritual, and daily conduct. Naisen ittai, “Japan (naichi) and Korea (Chōsen) as one body,” was the accompanying slogan of unity. Together they expressed the official claim that Koreans and Japanese shared a single imperial community.

The claim of unity conflicted with colonial practice. Koreans remained subject to distinct legal arrangements, lacked representation comparable to residents of the home islands, and faced systematic disadvantage in employment, schooling, and administration. Imperial rhetoric cannot be cited as proof that equality existed. A central analytical task of this chapter is to compare what authorities said about unity with what institutions actually delivered, a theme developed in Division X.

Official statements of 1937–1939 articulating naisen ittai are valuable evidence because they tie cultural and political identification explicitly to imperial loyalty. Students should read them as statements of purpose by the colonial state, not as descriptions of Korean attitudes. The Primary Source Window below identifies the policy-statement corpus; its paraphrase still requires reconciliation with a specific dated original before publication.

The expression 'one body' did political work precisely because equality had not been achieved. It encouraged Koreans to imagine common allegiance to the emperor while the system continued to distinguish the colony from the home islands. One could be obligated to salute the flag, study Japanese and accept greater demands for service without enjoying equivalent democratic representation. Officials sometimes described measures in the language of equalization, yet similar school names or public rituals did not erase differences in funding, social rank or access to senior positions. The proper historical test is whether a particular rule actually changed an institution, not merely whether official language promised unity.

The government's rhetoric was not automatically accepted on its own terms by everyone who repeated it. Some officials presented unity as a solution to colonial tensions; some Korean public figures used its promise to argue for fuller rights; some families adopted the vocabulary necessary to navigate work and school. Others regarded it as a demand to abandon separate identity. These reactions cannot be deduced from the slogan alone. Comparing a dated public policy declaration with legislation, household records and contemporary reports illustrates the gap between political aspiration, enforcement and the attitudes of those compelled to participate.

Section 60.6

From the 1911 and 1922 Education Ordinances to the Assimilation Curriculum

The first Chōsen Education Ordinance (1911) set up a separate, shorter and more vocational track for Koreans, with Japanese-language instruction and moral training central. The second ordinance (1922), issued in the era of cultural rule, nominally brought the school systems closer, extending the length of Korean elementary schooling and using shared curricula. In practice, schools for Koreans and Japanese residents remained largely separate and unequally funded, and limited places restricted Korean access to secondary and higher education.

Chapter 57 covers the expansion of schooling and literacy. For present purposes the point is institutional: by the mid-1930s the Government-General already controlled curricula, textbooks, teacher certification, and school inspection. That machinery made later, sharper assimilation measures administratively feasible.

The 1911 ordinance was constructed around differentiated education. Korean pupils were offered a narrower educational ladder, heavily weighted toward elementary and occupational training, than the institutions open to settler children. In 1922 the colonial government revised the system amid its stated cultural-rule reforms, extending courses and adopting some nominally comparable categories. Yet language, residence, tuition, competition for places and institutional allocation continued to shape who progressed. There was no inevitable progression from school expansion toward freedom; the same apparatus that educated Korean readers also trained pupils in officially sanctioned loyalty.

By the mid-1930s the administrative channels were already in place: ministries and inspectors specified curricula, schools adopted approved textbooks, examinations rewarded prescribed knowledge and teacher careers depended on official recognition. These arrangements gave later assimilation policies a reach beyond police prohibitions alone. A pupil could encounter official ideology every morning without ever attending a political rally, while a parent could have an educational reason to comply with rules that contradicted family convictions. In evaluating school regulations, students should compare the wording of an ordinance with enrollment statistics, inspector reports and class schedules—three different kinds of evidence.

Section 60.7

The Third Chōsen Education Ordinance of March 1938

The third ordinance, promulgated on March 4, 1938, restructured schooling under the banner of unified educational principles. School categories for Koreans were renamed to match those used for Japanese residents, so Korean elementary schools, for example, took the name used by their Japanese counterparts. The ordinance emphasized clarity of national polity, Japan–Korea unity, and endurance, and it strengthened Japanese-language and moral instruction.

It also changed the status of Korean as a school subject. Korean was made optional in the formal framework, and in many schools it was dropped or marginalized in practice. This differs from the 1943 ordinance, which removed it entirely (§60.11). Formal unity of school names did not create equal resources, and Korean pupils continued to face scarcer places, larger classes, and fewer routes to advancement.

The third ordinance, promulgated on 4 March 1938, renamed Korean school categories to match the Japanese system. The symbolic force was considerable: a school formerly marked as a Korean 'common school' was now described using the terminology of elementary schooling in Japan. But the legal act did not automatically supply classrooms, teachers or money. School location and admission remained important sources of inequality, and schools with the same official title could serve populations with very different resources. It is therefore misleading to call the new nomenclature the abolition of educational discrimination.

Korean-language education illustrates both the reach and limits of reform. The ordinance made Korean optional within the formal curriculum, while Japanese, imperial history, geography and moral instruction were reinforced. This changed who was authorized to decide whether Korean was taught and enabled its practical disappearance in many settings even before the 1943 legal removal. Contemporary class schedules would reveal implementation more accurately than the ordinance alone. Teachers faced inspection and professional risk, but they did not all respond identically. Pupils also differed in fluency and access: urban students already exposed to Japanese-language institutions faced an instructional change different from that of a rural beginner.

Section 60.8

Japanese as the Imperial National Language and the Suppression of Korean

Japanese was promoted as kokugo, the “national language.” Authorities used instruction, official communication, and institutional expectations to extend its use. Policy moved by stages from incentives, such as advantages for Japanese speakers in employment and advancement, toward restriction of Korean in formal education. Campaigns encouraging households to use Japanese at home, and recognition of “national language–using” families, extended pressure into private life.

Language policy should be distinguished from linguistic outcome. Koreans learned Japanese at very different rates by age, sex, class, and region, and many Koreans, especially in rural areas and among older people, spoke little of it. Formal suppression did not eliminate Korean as a spoken language (§60.48).

The administrative use of the term kokugo—'national language'—was a claim about political membership, not a neutral description of linguistic distribution. Japanese had important advantages in government documents, examination systems, schooling and many desirable jobs. A young person might learn it to improve prospects, while older relatives continued to use Korean in village and domestic life. Campaigns promoting Japanese conversation extended into work and household settings, but their effectiveness depended on teachers, local officials, time, exposure and the willingness of adults. Calling a household a 'national-language-using family' documented eligibility or official recognition, not necessarily all the conversations held within it.

This produced a layered linguistic environment. Public ceremonies might demand Japanese formulae even where ordinary work continued in Korean; educated writers might publish in Japanese to reach available venues, yet retain Korean as the language of intimate thought or literary production; some children became intermediaries for parents who could not understand official notices. Such differences discourage any easy equation between Japanese-language proficiency and political loyalty. Historians should separate language instruction, official restrictions, actual language use and the meaning attached to each by individual speakers.

Section 60.9

Textbooks, Imperial History, Moral Education, and the Construction of Loyalty

Textbooks and moral instruction presented Japanese history, geography, imperial mythology, and the figure of the emperor as the framework for belonging. Ceremonies tied to the school calendar reinforced these lessons. The aim was to change how pupils understood their political community and Korea’s place within it.

Historians use surviving textbooks, school regulations, and inspection reports to reconstruct this content, but these sources show what was intended to be taught. They do not show what individual students absorbed, doubted, or reinterpreted at home.

Textbooks provided the most systematic means of translating ideology into the daily school routine. Lessons presented the emperor and imperial polity as an overarching frame of obligation; morality courses linked proper conduct to service; geography could situate Korea within the empire's territorial imagination. The political significance lies in repetition across subjects: pupils encountered the same hierarchy in stories, ceremonies, calendars and classroom rules. Teachers might be required to display imperial portraits, guide ritual observances and monitor whether pupils used prescribed language. Schooling thereby fused literacy and knowledge with social discipline.

Yet an approved book is not a record of comprehension. Students could memorize a passage to obtain marks while interpreting it differently outside school. Attendance also varied by age, gender and region, leaving many young Koreans outside regular classrooms for much of the period. Research must therefore pair a precisely identified textbook edition with inspection reports, attendance registers and recollections—while recognizing that testimony recorded decades afterward may reflect liberation-era memory. The key question is not whether the empire printed a lesson, but how the lesson was delivered, to whom, with what consequences for education and what evidence of response.

Section 60.10

The 1941 National Schools and the Restructuring of Elementary Education

In 1941 elementary schools were reorganized as kokumin gakkō, “national schools,” following the same change in Japan. The new framework emphasized imperial loyalty, physical training, and preparation for wartime service. It is distinct from both the 1938 and 1943 ordinances: it renamed and reoriented elementary education within the existing legal structure, while the 1943 ordinance revised the wider school system and removed Korean from the curriculum.

The 1941 national-school change should be distinguished from the earlier renaming under the 1938 ordinance. The designation kokumin gakkō aligned Korean elementary institutions with a wider Japanese educational reform that stressed the creation of disciplined imperial subjects. Curriculum, physical exercises, school ceremonies and collective activities were tied more closely to wartime priorities. For a pupil, the change could be visible in a new school name, altered subjects, regularized assemblies and pressure to learn the forms of respect associated with imperial institutions. It did not amount to a promise that all Korean children could now attend school.

Compulsory universal elementary schooling remained an unrealized project. Official discussions in 1942 and a formal decision in 1943 anticipated implementation in 1946, after a preparatory period, but the Japanese empire surrendered in August 1945. Thus it is inaccurate to count planned compulsory attendance as an achieved colonial benefit. Wartime shortages and the growing use of students in activities outside ordinary instruction also affected the quality of education. When comparing 1941 with 1943, the former concerns the form and purposes of elementary schools; the latter restructures the wider education system under the exigencies of war.

Section 60.11

The Fourth Chōsen Education Ordinance of March 1943

The fourth ordinance, promulgated March 8, 1943, restructured schooling for wartime. Korean was removed as a curriculum subject, Japanese-language instruction was strengthened, and secondary education was reorganized and shortened, with greater weight on practical and military-related training. The change completed a legal progression: optional in 1938, absent from the formal curriculum in 1943.

The ordinances of 1938 and 1943 must not be conflated. A student in 1940 might still encounter some Korean instruction; after 1943 there was none in the official program.

The fourth Chōsen Education Ordinance of 8 March 1943 was not simply an amendment to language teaching. It was part of a wartime attempt to align schooling more closely with Japan's revised educational institutions, to shorten certain secondary courses and to organize pupils for heightened national service. Korean, made an optional formal subject in 1938, was removed from the curriculum. Other subjects were adjusted to reinforce Japanese language and imperial civic formation. These changes came while schools faced disruption, resource scarcity and pressure to send students into mobilization programs.

The legal date is only one layer of the history. Instructions implemented during 1943–1945 altered the school year, curriculum practice and student obligations in ways that require examination of subsequent regulations and local reports. The Government-General's promulgation of an ordinance established authority; it does not show that every school could fulfill all prescribed activities, particularly as war conditions deteriorated. This section must also retain the boundary with Chapter 62: the educational and ideological design belongs here, while students' detailed experiences of coerced work and military service belong to the total-war narrative.

Section 60.13

Korean Surnames, Lineage, Household Registration, and Colonial Legal Identity

Korean surnames (seong) identified patrilineal descent, and women ordinarily kept their own surname at marriage. Japanese family law instead organized people into households (ie) under a household head, with a household name (shi) shared by its members. Colonial authorities recorded Koreans in household registers that classified them by legal status and regulated family relationships, so registration served both administrative and symbolic ends.

Registration also marked the legal line between Korean and Japanese subjects: the two groups were recorded in different registers, and moving between them was restricted. This helped sustain legal inequality inside a rhetoric of unity (§60.54).

Registration rules show the colonial distinction clearly. Koreans were entered in Korean household registers, regulated by an ordinance of the early 1920s, and Japanese residents in the register used in Japan. Movement between the two was restricted and was mainly possible through marriage or adoption. The register therefore recorded legal status, and a person’s entry in it determined which laws applied. Later name policies changed how people were named, but they did not merge the registers or erase this line (§60.54).

The distinction between a Korean surname and a Japanese-style household shi was central to the government's intervention. Korean surnames situated individuals within enduring descent lines, often associated with a clan origin, and ordinarily did not change when a woman married. The Japanese household name system organized the name of a legally registered family unit in a different way. Colonial authorities had already rendered Korean family relations into an administrative register, but the later policy sought to change the terms through which households appeared before the state. The official proposal was therefore not a minor spelling adjustment.

Registration also created an archive of belonging. A legal document could tell an official the household head, dependents, lineage, marital status and name used for administrative purposes. Such records mattered in property relations, schooling and employment, yet do not provide a transparent account of how family members described themselves at home. One registered household could contain people with different attitudes toward the policy, and a younger person's opportunity to choose was often limited by decisions of the head. Historians should ask which entries were legally required and which were modifications chosen—or pressured—after the initial registration.

Section 60.14

The November 1939 Name Policy and the Revised Chōsen Civil Ordinance

On November 10, 1939, the Government-General promulgated revisions to the Chōsen Civil Ordinance, together with rules on changing given names. Two measures must be separated. First, the revised ordinance required every household to establish a household name (shi). A household could declare a shi derived from its existing surname, and if it made no declaration by the deadline, the household head’s surname became the shi by default. Second, changing to a Japanese-style name (kaimei) was legally a separate, formally optional procedure requiring application and permission.

Officials justified the measures as steps toward unity and as fitting the Japanese household system. Historians read them as an instrument of symbolic assimilation. Summarizing the policy as a law requiring all Koreans to adopt Japanese names is inaccurate; the legal structure was more complicated, and the gap between legal option and social pressure is the heart of the matter (§60.15).

Officials explained the measures in two ways. One was that the household-name system fit the unity of Japan and Korea. The other was that the policy responded to Korean wishes, an official claim that historians treat with caution because the evidence for such wishes comes largely from sources produced or controlled by the state. The ordinance also relaxed adoption rules, allowing a son-in-law to be adopted as heir, which departed from Korean lineage custom.

A further point concerns records. Registration of a household name did not remove the lineage surname, which continued to appear in the family register. A household’s new public name and its inherited lineage identity could therefore coexist on paper, a fact that shaped how families understood what they were doing.

The measures promulgated on 10 November 1939 comprised separate legal components: a revision of the Chōsen Civil Ordinance introducing household shi and provisions governing changes of names. Under the mechanism effective 11 February 1940, the household head was to designate and report a shi within six months. If no declaration was filed, the existing surname of the household head operated as the shi by law. This is not equivalent to a legal command that all households choose newly invented Japanese-sounding surnames. The supplementary permission procedure for altering a personal name was distinct again.

Understanding that distinction does not diminish the policy's coercive context. Government publicity presented new naming as an expression of imperial unity, while local authorities, schools and workplaces could impose powerful pressures on families to comply in visibly Japanese forms. The legal provision that allowed an unchanged surname-derived designation did not guarantee social freedom to rely on it. An evidence-based treatment should place the two 1939 decrees beside implementation circulars, registration statistics and accounts of discriminatory enforcement. In that comparison the difference between formal choice and effective choice becomes historically concrete.

Section 60.15

The Implementation of Sōshi-Kaimei, February–August 1940

Registration opened on February 11, 1940, and closed on August 10. Registration was initially slow. Authorities then intensified publicity and applied administrative pressure through local officials, schools, and employers. Official figures reported that roughly four-fifths of households had declared a new shi by the deadline, though students should treat such figures as official statistics whose compilation reflected the pressure they measured.

Pressure reported in historical studies included warnings about schooling for children and obstacles in administrative procedures and employment. Because registration affected every household, the policy reached ordinary people far more directly than ceremonies or propaganda.

Later testimony and scholarly studies describe a range of pressure: warnings that children could be refused entry to school or face discipline, difficulty obtaining official documents and rations, obstacles to employment and promotion, and public labeling of unregistered households as disloyal. Local officials and school staff were charged with raising registration numbers, which created incentives for zeal. The sharp rise in registrations as the deadline approached is generally read as evidence of these pressures.

Postwar accounts also recall individuals who chose names meant as satire or protest. The details of such stories vary, and they should be treated as memory, not as contemporary documentation. They are useful as evidence of how the policy was remembered.

The registration period from 11 February through 10 August 1940 generated a campaign of instructions and persuasion. Officials emphasized completion rates and often connected naming with claims of Japanese–Korean solidarity. In practice the decision fell especially heavily on household heads, even though the resulting official identity affected spouses and children. Families weighed lineage continuity, employment, school records and fear of adverse administrative treatment. Registration forms establish what was recorded, not whether a family acted with enthusiasm.

Contemporary examples of pressure—from public notices to social exclusion—must be evaluated with dates, locations and sources. It is defensible to characterize the policy as coercive in operation, but not to invent a universal penalty that applied to every family. Some households selected a Japanese-style shi, others relied on a form related to their existing surname; individuals differed in changing their given names. Published aggregate compliance rates can show the scale of the registration campaign but cannot automatically disclose motives or uniformity of enforcement. The source question is therefore dual: what did the law require, and how did institutions make an ostensibly limited choice difficult to exercise?

Section 60.16

Names, Public Identity, Family Memory, and Personal Responses

Responses varied. Many families, concerned about children’s schooling, jobs, or access to rationed goods, registered a new shi. Some chose a shi that preserved the lineage surname or a lineage reference in a Japanese-style form, often interpreted as a way of keeping ancestral identity visible. Others resisted or delayed. Individuals sometimes used different names publicly and within the family.

Because the lineage surname generally remained in the family register as a separate record, registration did not erase ancestry, although it altered public naming. Later testimony and memoirs offer valuable but retrospective evidence, shaped by postwar memory.

Families weighed several concerns: the schooling and careers of children, the security of the household head, and loyalty to ancestors and lineage. Some drew on their clan seat or the reading of their surname to choose a household name that kept a link to their lineage. Others delayed until the deadline. Who in the family decided, and what children and elders thought, is difficult to know from registration records alone.

The records show outcomes, not reasons. To reconstruct reasons, historians turn to memoirs, interviews, and diaries, each shaped by later circumstances, including postwar pressure to explain or defend choices.

Names condensed several kinds of identity into a few characters. A household register linked a family to official institutions; a clan name or genealogical record linked people to kinship and ancestral memory; the name used at work or school could be conditioned by colonial expectations. For some individuals, adopting a Japanese-form name was a calculation concerning livelihood and status rather than an abandonment of Korean identity. Others resisted a new designation as a symbol of colonial encroachment. A family's administrative entry should not be turned into a single account of everyone in that household.

The intergenerational effects were particularly important. Older relatives might remember names used before the ordinance, while children experienced a new school record as ordinary. After liberation some families sought restoration of Korean forms, but restoration could involve legal work, identification problems and differences between jurisdictions. Historians benefit from comparing pre- and post-1940 records, oral histories and official correspondence, carefully separating retrospective indignation from the contemporaneous choices available to each person. A record of a changed name is evidence of a bureaucratic outcome; it does not by itself reveal consent, political affiliation or private loyalties.

Section 60.17

Family Law, Marriage, Kinship, and the Limits of Imperial Integration

The same revisions made it possible for a Korean household to adopt a son-in-law as heir, a practice resembling Japanese custom, and they expanded Japanese-style rules on household headship. Officials promoted Japanese–Korean marriage as a sign of unity, yet such marriages remained a small minority, and legal and social distinctions between the two populations persisted.

The family law changes show how assimilation reached household authority and inheritance. They did not produce legal equality, and later chapters on liberation return to the question of how these registers and names were treated.

The most visible symbol of intermarriage rhetoric was the 1920 marriage of Crown Prince Yi Un, the last heir of the Korean royal house, to the Japanese princess Nashimoto Masako. It was arranged by the imperial government and served as a symbol of unity. Ordinary intermarriage remained uncommon and was shaped by the legal rules on the registers. Official promotion of such marriages did not change the underlying legal and social inequality.

The household-name measure occurred within a broader contest over family law. The colonial legal order combined Japanese statutory concepts with selectively recognized Korean customary arrangements. Officials presented some revisions as reform or modernization while also seeking compatibility with Japan's registered household system. Changes involving adoption, household succession and marriage touched questions of property, lineage and the authority of household heads. The significance of these provisions cannot be assessed simply by asking whether a new law was more 'modern'; it matters who could assert a claim and whose preferences were subordinated.

State publicity also used examples of marriages between Koreans and Japanese to symbolize unity. Such marriages were historically real, but they were not proof that the two populations possessed equal social standing, legal experience or political rights. Families encountered different administrative expectations and forms of prejudice. Nor did all women or younger relatives possess equal bargaining power within their own households. This section should thus analyze two unequal relations simultaneously: imperial differences between settlers and colonized people, and hierarchies of gender and age within family structures. The result is a more precise explanation of why administrative integration did not create equal citizenship.

Section 60.18

Shinto Shrines, Chōsen Jingū, and the Imperial Sacred Landscape

Japanese settlers established shrines in Korea soon after annexation. Chōsen Jingū, the principal state shrine, was completed on Namsan in Seoul in 1925. Shrines carried imperial symbolism and were used in school and civic ceremonies. Their presence, however, differs from the later coercive requirement that Koreans participate. Before the mid-1930s shrines largely served Japanese residents and official ceremonies, and links between schools and shrines already existed.

Shrines multiplied along with the Japanese population in Korea, and regulations issued by the Government-General from the 1910s governed their establishment. Chōsen Jingū, on Namsan overlooking Seoul and enshrining the sun goddess Amaterasu and the Meiji emperor, was meant to serve as the central shrine of the colony. It linked the colony to the imperial sacred geography of Japan and became a site for official ceremonies, including those involving school groups. Before the mid-1930s most Korean participation was limited, and shrine rites were often defended as expressions of loyalty and public order.

Chōsen Jingū, consecrated in Seoul in 1925, established an imposing sacred presence connected to Japan's imperial state. Its ceremonies and spatial prominence helped make imperial allegiance visible, particularly to officials, settler communities and school groups. The shrine belonged to a wider network of Japanese religious and civic institutions across the peninsula. Its importance did not depend on Koreans understanding Shinto doctrines: government authorities increasingly described participation as an act of civic respect. That framing made refusal appear to officials as a political breach, even where resisters understood the rite as worship of another deity.

The chronology is essential. The construction of shrines and occasional school visits preceded the comprehensive pressure of the mid-1930s; an early school excursion is not itself evidence that every Korean had been compelled to attend. Shrine histories, maps, school diaries and ceremonial photographs can reconstruct where and when visits occurred, but photographs alone cannot show whether participants agreed with the rituals. The most significant tension arises when an imperial government reserves to itself the power to decide whether a ceremony is religious for all inhabitants, overriding the interpretation of people asked to participate.

Section 60.19

The Escalation of Shrine Attendance Requirements during the 1930s

After 1931 and especially from 1935, the Government-General increased pressure on schools and organizations to participate in shrine ceremonies, and expanded local shrines. Officials described attendance as an expression of civic loyalty, not religious worship. That was an official claim; many Christians and others understood shrine ritual as religious, and the conflict turned on this question.

The crisis began in earnest in November 1935, when the governor of South Pyeongan Province required school heads in Pyongyang to attend a shrine ceremony. After 1936, a policy sometimes described as one shrine per township extended shrine facilities to local communities. Authorities treated absence as disloyalty. In the early 1940s households were also encouraged to keep shrine altars, though the extent of compliance is debated.

The official definition of attendance as a civic act of reverence was a legal and political choice that allowed the state to demand participation without admitting that it interfered with religious freedom, which the colonial constitutional framework nominally protected.

The shrine-attendance issue accelerated during the early and middle 1930s, especially in schools and mission institutions. Provincial officials and school inspectors increasingly demanded that teachers and pupils attend ceremonies as demonstrations of loyalty. The official distinction between civic observance and religion was central: it allowed authorities to claim that refusal was improper conduct rather than an exercise of conscience. The practical conflict surfaced prominently at Pyongyang in November 1935, when mission-school principals declined to bow at a shrine following a school principals' meeting.

The difference between a written rule and enforcement needs careful scrutiny. A principal could face loss of official recognition even if a broad statewide order had not yet been publicized uniformly. Students could face pressure from school authorities as well as government representatives. Missionary letters, government announcements and Korean church records provide contrasting evidence; none alone is sufficient to reconstruct every school's response. The disciplinary campaign widened the idea of school administration from classroom standards to management of ritual participation, thereby laying groundwork for the more sweeping imperial-subject policies introduced after 1936.

Section 60.20

Christian Schools, Church Resistance, and the Shrine-Worship Crisis

The dispute became public in November 1935 at Pyongyang, when mission school heads, including George Shannon McCune of Soongsil and V. L. Snook of Soongŭi Girls’ School, declined to require students to attend shrine ceremonies. McCune was removed from his post in 1936. Several Presbyterian mission schools closed rather than comply, and authorities increasingly pressed churches directly.

In September 1938, the Presbyterian General Assembly meeting in Pyongyang adopted a resolution accepting shrine attendance as a non-religious civic ceremony, under heavy police pressure. Conflict continued: some pastors and believers refused, and imprisonment and church closures followed.

Responses by missions differed. After refusing to comply, the Northern Presbyterian mission decided to close its schools, and schools such as Soongsil and Soongŭi in Pyongyang closed in 1938. Other missions took different courses at different times, and many missionaries had left by 1940–1941 as relations between Japan and the Western powers worsened. The closures ended a major channel of private Korean education.

The pressure on the 1938 Presbyterian assembly included police surveillance of the meeting. That fact complicates the meaning of its resolution as a statement of belief and shows why institutional records must be read alongside accounts of the conditions under which they were produced.

The 1935 Pyongyang conflict provides unusually specific evidence. At a November meeting convened by the provincial governor, the missionary educators George S. McCune and Velma L. Snook were among those who refused the required shrine ceremony. Officials described their objection as a mistaken classification of civic ritual as religion; the educators insisted that the ceremony carried religious significance incompatible with their Christian commitments. Administrative retaliation threatened their right to operate or direct schools. The dispute thus became a test of whether private religious institutions could preserve doctrinal autonomy while continuing to educate students under colonial law.

The Presbyterian General Assembly's September 1938 decision unfolded under much stronger pressure. Section 60.21

Korean Protestant and Catholic Responses: Refusal, Accommodation, and Institutional Survival

Responses differed by denomination and person. Methodist leadership accepted shrine attendance earlier. In 1936 the Vatican issued guidance that Catholics could attend shrine ceremonies as civic acts, which shaped Catholic practice. Among Protestants, missionaries, pastors, and lay believers split between refusal, accommodation to preserve institutions, and open support. One denomination’s decision did not establish what all its members believed.

Methodist leaders accepted shrine attendance earlier, and the Vatican’s 1936 instruction treated attendance as a civil act, which shaped Catholic practice. After 1938 resistance continued among Protestants. Church histories report that a large number of believers were detained and that some died in custody, but the figures cited vary widely and rest partly on later church sources, so they should be treated cautiously.

Within denominations, positions varied from principled refusal to accommodation intended to keep schools and churches open, to open support. These choices were made by pastors, missionaries, and lay people with different responsibilities and risks.

Responses among Protestant churches varied by denomination, leadership and locality. Some congregations held that bowing before a shrine was incompatible with worship of the Christian God; others accepted the state's argument that the gesture was a nonreligious civic obligation. Mission authorities weighed the future of schools and churches against the demands of conscience, and some institutions chose closure rather than compliance. Korean church leaders were not merely passive recipients of foreign missionary opinions. They could disagree among themselves, and the pressures exerted on church administrators did not distribute evenly to parishioners.

The Catholic institutional response also followed a distinct trajectory, shaped by guidance on state ceremonies, episcopal authority and the complex status of Shinto ritual in imperial policy. It cannot be described accurately by simply transferring a Presbyterian account to all Christians. Police surveillance and imprisonment of those who continued to refuse—including pastor Ju Gi-cheol—demonstrated serious consequences for some dissenters. Researchers must distinguish a church's official public accommodation, an individual minister's refusal and the ordinary believer's participation under pressure. A government compliance register could classify all three within one institution even where their convictions and consequences differed profoundly.

Section 60.22

Buddhist Institutions, Temple Administration, and Imperial Religious Policy

Buddhist temples had been regulated since the Temple Ordinance of 1911, which organized head temples under Government-General supervision. Japanese Buddhist sects also established missions in Korea. Korean Buddhist leaders responded variously: some used cooperation to protect institutions, some adopted Japanese-style practice, and some took part in imperial ceremonies and propaganda. Distinguishing institutional adaptation from active advocacy requires evidence on particular leaders and dates.

A 1911 temple ordinance placed Korean temples under a system of head temples supervised by the Government-General, which approved appointments. Japanese Buddhist sects established missions in Korea, and their practice, especially the acceptance of married clergy, became a contested issue within Korean Buddhism. Some Korean Buddhist leaders cooperated with colonial authorities to protect temple property and institutions, while others sought to preserve distinctive traditions. Temples also took part in ceremonies for soldiers and in patriotic campaigns, which should be distinguished from administrative adaptation.

Korean Buddhism had already been reorganized through colonial temple administration, giving the Government-General influence over institutional property and leadership before the heightened assimilation campaigns. The Temple Ordinance and subsequent supervisory practices made monasteries dependent on official approval in matters of appointment and administration. Japanese Buddhist denominations had their own activities on the peninsula, but Japanese religious influence should not be confused with Shinto shrine policy: Buddhism and state-sponsored shrine rites occupied different places in the imperial religious order.

Some Buddhist leaders and temple bodies cooperated with official campaigns, while others concentrated on teaching, ritual continuity or local congregational life. The presence of a monk at a public ceremony is not enough to establish a temple's ideological commitment; dated statements, organizational records and administrative correspondence are better guides. Changes in institutional finance and temple property also shaped the range of available decisions. Buddhism's encounter with imperialization was thus simultaneously religious, legal and organizational, not a single nationwide choice between refusal and collaboration.

Section 60.23

Cheondogyo, Confucian Traditions, Local Religious Practices, and the Limits of Religious Assimilation

Cheondogyo, the successor to Donghak, Confucian associations, and shamanic and household ritual traditions faced regulation and pressure to demonstrate loyalty. Ancestral rites and local practices continued in homes and communities despite official pressure. As with language, religious assimilation produced visible compliance in public settings while much practice persisted in private life.

Cheondogyo, the successor movement to Donghak, had split into factions and faced pressure to adopt imperial slogans. Confucian bodies were encouraged to form federations under official guidance in the late 1930s. Authorities also campaigned against practices they labeled superstition. Ancestral rites, household observances, and village rituals nonetheless continued, since they were embedded in family life and difficult to monitor. These practices show the limits of assimilation, though they should not automatically be classed as resistance (§60.53).

Cheondogyo, Confucian institutions and local devotional practices entered the assimilation crisis from different starting points. Cheondogyo had a history of Korean religious reform and involvement in the March First Movement, but its adherents and leaders did not all follow one political path in the 1930s and 1940s. Confucian ceremonies and household ancestral rites connected family identity to lineage and the ritual calendar. Officials could tolerate aspects of these traditions while demanding additional imperial ceremonies, creating situations of combination and tension rather than simple replacement.

Everyday local practices also had a geographical dimension. Village and household rituals were often sustained by elders, women's labor and kin networks beyond the routine view of colonial administrators. Such continuity could preserve distinctive memories and associations without functioning as an organized political campaign. Conversely, an institution that openly contested official demands might incur sanctions even if its members were not affiliated with nationalist parties. In this context, state power attempted to define which rituals were compatible with imperial loyalty, while participants interpreted the same practices through inherited religious and communal meanings.

Section 60.24

From Colonial Censorship to Wartime Information Control

Censorship existed throughout colonial rule, through licensing, prepublication review, and bans on content. After 1937 control changed in kind as well as degree. Authorities restricted what could be said and also required publishers to carry approved messages, support campaigns, and use Japanese. Editors and publishers faced pressure to take part in propaganda as a condition of continuing to operate.

Colonial press control rested on permit requirements, prepublication review, and police power to ban issues and punish editors. Korean-language dailies were permitted only from 1920, alongside the Government-General’s own Korean-language organ, Maeil Sinbo. Wartime rules widened these powers: a temporary speech-control ordinance issued in December 1941 broadened licensing and punishment, and newspapers were consolidated, leaving Korean readers with little independent print. The shift from forbidding certain content to requiring approved content matters. Editors were expected to run official campaigns, carry slogans, and eventually publish in Japanese.

The movement toward wartime information control changed the relation between government and publisher. Earlier censorship had already permitted seizure, suspension and deletion. During the late 1930s the regime increasingly pressed editors to publish material actively supportive of wartime objectives. Official guidance, licensing, paper supply, publishing associations and the threat of police action could all affect what reached readers. The outcome was not merely silence about prohibited subjects but systematic amplification of approved narratives about military service, national unity and sacrifice.

A distinction is needed between Korean-language publication and independent Korean public opinion. The government-affiliated Maeil Sinbo continued to publish in Korean, while the major privately owned Korean-language dailies were closed in August 1940. This left an official-language channel without the autonomous editorial institutions that had helped build a Korean public sphere in the 1920s. Later security regulations, including those introduced in December 1941, tightened the wartime political environment, but their legal titles and provisions must be cited precisely rather than aggregated under a vague 'speech-control law.' Newspapers reveal state messaging as well as the choices forced upon editors, and a study of their final issues should compare contemporary editorial statements with later accounts of coercion.

Section 60.25

The Closure of Dong-A Ilbo and Chosun Ilbo in August 1940

The two largest Korean-owned dailies, permitted since 1920, had already been disciplined: Dong-A Ilbo was suspended in 1936 after publishing a photograph of the marathon winner Son Gi-jeong (Sohn Kee-chung) with the Japanese flag removed from his uniform. In 1940 the Government-General pressed both papers toward closure, and both ceased publication on August 10, 1940. Their closure removed the main independent Korean-language daily news from public life.

Whether owners fully agreed or were coerced is a matter for the documentary record, which shows sustained official pressure and negotiation.

Both papers had been founded in 1920, Dong-A Ilbo by Kim Seong-su and associates and Chosun Ilbo by a separate group of owners. They had built large readerships and had been repeatedly censored and suspended. In 1939–1940 officials pressed for consolidation and closure, citing wartime paper shortages and the need for unified information. Reports mention a financial settlement. The owners’ room to refuse was extremely limited, since the authorities controlled licenses and paper supplies. The closure left Maeil Sinbo as the only Korean-language daily, and with it the main space for independent Korean-language public discussion ended.

The August 1940 closures followed months of explicit pressure. Historical research based on colonial press records describes Government-General efforts from January to consolidate the two private dailies with the official Maeil Sinbo, along with threats and arrests affecting newspaper personnel. The final August 10 issues were thus not routine business decisions made in an open publishing market. Their disappearance ended an important institutional space through which Koreans had debated schooling, language, culture and social reform under the already restrictive conditions of cultural rule.

The closure should not be told as a complete end to Korean writing. The official Maeil Sinbo remained, and other printed materials still circulated under changing conditions. But the character of the public sphere changed when newspapers with independent editorial histories no longer appeared. When reconstructing the event, the final printed issues and the Government-General's policy must be read together: a farewell editorial might be written under duress, while later reminiscences might exaggerate the newspaper's independence during earlier years. Both source limitations belong in a textbook history.

Section 60.26

Publishers, Magazines, Literary Associations, and Japanese-Language Publications

Korean-language literary journals, including Munjang and Inmun Pyeongnon, were forced to close in 1941. A Japanese-language magazine, Kokumin Bungaku (National Literature), took their place, edited by Choe Jae-seo. Debates arose among writers over whether to write in Japanese at all.

A Japanese-language journal, Kokumin Bungaku, edited by Choe Jae-seo, took their place, publishing works and essays in support of wartime culture. The language question was not simple: some Korean writers wrote in Japanese because it widened their readership. Kim Sa-ryang, for example, wrote in Japanese and reached a national Japanese audience in 1940. Choice of language therefore cannot by itself indicate a writer’s politics.

The literary world was not simply shut down at once. Publications were restricted, combined, renamed or redirected toward wartime themes. Some Korean writers shifted to Japanese-language outlets because access to Korean-language publication narrowed; others chose Japanese as a vehicle for particular literary or intellectual projects. The magazine Kokumin Bungaku, launched in 1941, explicitly promoted a literary program situated within imperial citizenship. The closure or consolidation of literary magazines disrupted networks of publishers, critics and readers as well as the careers of individual authors. Paper shortages, censorship and institutional sponsorship shaped publication opportunities. The writer Kim Sa-ryang, whose Japanese-language writing achieved recognition around 1940, is an instructive case: language of publication cannot serve as a proxy for collaboration without analysis of a particular text, its themes and the author's broader conduct. A convincing literary history therefore identifies titles, publication dates and rhetorical choices rather than judging an entire corpus by language alone.

Section 60.27

Writers, Poets, Intellectuals, and the Politics of Wartime Literature

Writers responded in different ways: some stopped publishing, some wrote in Korean until it became impossible, and some produced works supporting the war. Yi Gwang-su, prominent in earlier nationalist literature, publicly supported imperial policy in the 1940s, as did Choe Chae-seo in his editorial role. Each case needs dated texts and evidence of the conditions of publication.

Writers faced a narrowing range of options: silence, writing in Korean for private circulation, writing within official limits, or active support of the war. Individual cases differ and need dated texts. A writer might produce a wartime essay under commission and also leave private writings that suggest other views, and later memory of writers has often been shaped by postwar politics. Responsible assessment requires reading both the surviving texts and what is known of the writer’s circumstances.

For writers and intellectuals, wartime collaboration was visible most clearly in identifiable public work. A dated essay praising service or an official speech urging enlistment provides different evidence from mere membership in a literary association. Choe Chae-seo's 'Requirements of National Literature,' published in the November 1941 issue of Kokumin Bungaku, articulated a program that placed literature within an imperial framework. Its argument can be examined through its published title, editorial setting and language, while remembering that discussion in later historical scholarship may mediate access to the full original issue.

The choices of Yi Gwang-su, Choe Nam-seon and other prominent intellectuals also changed across decades. Their early anti-colonial or nationalist writing was historically real; their later public cooperation with imperial campaigns must be treated with equal evidentiary seriousness. In November 1943 the two visited Meiji University to encourage Korean students to enter wartime service, an event recorded in studies of wartime cultural mobilization. That act should not be diluted by their earlier reputations or made the sole determinant of every other work they produced. Other writers continued to negotiate ambiguous or critical expression. Source criticism must attend to published wording, contemporary censorship, sponsorship and the consequences of each intervention.

Section 60.29

Radio, Music, Theater, Sports, and the Propagation of Imperial Culture

Broadcasting, songs, theater, and sports organizations were used to promote imperial identification. Audiences differed in choice: attendance at ordered events was compelled, while listening to radio or watching a film was more voluntary, though the available content was controlled. These differences matter in judging participation.

Radio from Seoul ran Japanese and Korean-language channels, and wartime programming promoted patriotic themes. Songs, theater, and exhibitions were enlisted for campaigns. Sports had a particular complexity: the Korean marathoner Son Gi-jeong won the 1936 Olympic marathon representing Japan, and Korean newspapers’ treatment of his victory brought the 1936 suspension of Dong-A Ilbo. Participation in commercial culture differed from compulsory attendance at organized events, and the room for audience choice varied accordingly.

Broadcasting offered colonial officials an especially useful medium because a station could transmit approved language, music and announcements simultaneously across separated locations. Korean and Japanese radio services had developed before the war, but the tightening propaganda system placed new weight on news presentation, patriotic songs and official appeals. The potential breadth of the medium should not be confused with universal reception: radio ownership, electricity and listening locations remained unequal, while collective listening might bring broadcasts to people without receivers.

Theater and music organizations also encountered pressure to contribute to sanctioned public programs. Some performers joined officially sponsored associations or participated in fundraising events; others continued regional or popular cultural forms in altered circumstances. Sport could be appropriated for imperial rhetoric as well, although Son Gi-jeong's 1936 Olympic victory shows how the same event could sustain different meanings: Japanese authorities claimed an imperial triumph, while many Koreans saw a Korean athlete's achievement. The chapter must distinguish that earlier case from specific wartime propaganda performances and avoid implying that all entertainment was equally state-directed.

Section 60.30

The Imperial Subject Oath of October 1937

The Imperial Subject Oath was introduced in October 1937 in two versions, one for children and one for older students and adults. Its text, in short sentences, pledged loyalty to the empire and to the emperor and stressed unity and endurance. Recitation was required in schools, offices, and gatherings. The oath sought to make imperial identification a routine act.

The oath’s two versions used short, simple clauses meant to be memorized and recited by children and adults. Schools began each day with recitation, and offices and gatherings opened with it. Because the text was brief and the requirement constant, its effect was cumulative: the aim was habit more than persuasion. The Government-General also established a monthly Patriotic Day, from September 1937, when flag-raising and shrine visits were organized, tying the oath to a regular calendar.

The Imperial Subject Oath was distributed for recitation from October 4, 1937; an October 5 Dong-A Ilbo report described its use in schools and associations. The government issued different versions for younger pupils and for older students and adults. Both linked identity with loyalty to the emperor, collective unity and endurance. The children's version used especially direct sentences, suitable for repetition in assemblies. Its pedagogical function lay in the regular act of speaking: schoolchildren were expected to rehearse imperial belonging publicly in a disciplined group.

The oath is a primary document that can be examined without inferring the emotions of every child who recited it. Its three points reveal the qualities officials wanted to promote, while the historical report establishes distribution and prescribed use. They do not show how many students understood its political language or privately agreed with it. A diary or a teacher's recollection might illuminate reception, but those sources would require individual evaluation too. The difference between text, institutional practice and belief is the central historical method of this chapter, not a reason to neglect the oath's coercive nature.

Section 60.31

Flag Ceremonies, Palace Bowing, Imperial Holidays, and Daily Discipline

Daily and ceremonial practice expanded: raising the Japanese flag, bowing toward the imperial palace (kyūjō yōhai), singing the Japanese anthem, and observing imperial holidays. These applied in schools, offices, and community gatherings and made loyalty visible.

Rituals such as bowing toward the imperial palace and singing the Japanese anthem were performed in schools, offices, and community gatherings, and imperial holidays added to the calendar of ceremonies. Their power lay in repetition and public visibility. A person’s participation could be observed and recorded, so that abstaining was noticeable, while sincere belief remained impossible to monitor.

The official ritual calendar organized visible loyalty through repeated actions. School assemblies, flag observances, the Japanese anthem, bowing toward the imperial palace and ceremonies tied to imperial holidays conveyed a public hierarchy in which the emperor stood at the center. These observances were not mere decorations around other lessons; their repetition associated ordinary time with state-defined belonging. Ritual participation also generated records that can mislead the historian. An inspector might report that every pupil bowed; a photograph might show an entire street or classroom performing an approved ceremony. Neither document establishes enthusiasm. Some people may have found these practices normal, some complied to avoid sanctions, and others objected on religious or national grounds. The historically defensible claim is that the authorities attempted to make loyalty observable and enforceable. Evaluating its deeper success requires evidence beyond the official schedule and visual record.

Section 60.32

The National Spiritual Mobilization Movement of 1938

The National Spiritual Mobilization League of Korea was established on July 7, 1938, with Government-General leadership and local branches. It linked imperial ideology to everyday conduct and encouraged campaigns on thrift, saving, and loyalty.

The League was led by Government-General officials, with Korean notables, including some prominent nationalist figures, given titular and public roles. It conducted campaigns for thrift, savings, labor service, and loyalty ceremonies through provincial and local branches. Its membership figures reflected enrollment as much as willingness. The League is a useful example of institutional participation: a name on a roster or a title on a letterhead shows a person’s presence in the organization but not their motives.

The National Spiritual Mobilization League of Korea was publicly inaugurated on July 7, 1938, the anniversary of the outbreak of full-scale war with China. Preparatory meetings and founder statements had already supplied an organizational and ideological program. Its September 22, 1938 platform, reproduced in historical scholarship, brought together the exaltation of the imperial spirit, Japan–Korea unity, cooperation with wartime economic policy, labor service and support for soldiers. The league was therefore not only an abstract 'spiritual' movement: it linked values and rituals with practical expectations of production and sacrifice.

A nationwide hierarchy connected the center to provincial and local organizations. Recruitment totals, attendance at inaugural events and the number of affiliated groups reveal the scale of organization but not spontaneous consent. Representatives of religious, professional and social organizations could join a structure created under explicit governmental sponsorship, while ordinary members might be enrolled through community institutions rather than a freely chosen political allegiance. The league illustrates how a state could attempt to transform loyalty into an administrative routine, using existing associations as conduits for messages and local supervision.

Section 60.34

Patriotic Neighborhood Groups, Local Officials, and the Monitoring of Everyday Life

At the lowest level, neighborhood patriotic groups organized households for meetings, campaigns, and demonstrations of loyalty. Local officials and community leaders served as intermediaries, so that pressure came from neighbors and officials as well as the central state. Individual participation ranged from compulsion to eager organizing, which can only be separated through case-specific evidence.

The base of the system was the neighborhood patriotic group, typically of about ten households, formed from 1938 and incorporated in 1940 into a neighborhood association structure that also distributed rations and passed on official notices. Group leaders were local residents, often under pressure to deliver turnout. These arrangements made attendance and contributions visible to neighbors, and a household’s standing could depend on its participation. They also placed local intermediaries between orders from above and the households below, which shaped how enforcement felt in practice.

The small neighborhood unit, frequently described through the term aekukban, linked government instructions to households. Groups often organized around roughly ten households, but size and boundaries varied, and officials used them for announcements, meetings, community discipline and, increasingly, coordination of collection and rationing. These organizations made state policy tangible: instead of encountering only a governor-general's decree, a resident dealt with a nearby leader who could ask for attendance, savings, materials or confirmation that a household had heard an instruction.

A neighborhood group could also create complicated local relationships. Its leader might be a respected resident, an official intermediary or someone trying to protect neighbors from harsher demands; participants might cooperate for practical reasons without believing the propaganda. Lists, minutes and collection accounts can show what the association demanded and recorded, but they rarely register private hesitation. The distinction between policy and experience must therefore be maintained at the smallest scale. Rationing, labor requisition and the wider mechanics of total war are developed in Chapter 62; the present concern is the institutional channel through which such demands reached households.

Section 60.35

Defining Collaboration: Cooperation, Accommodation, Coercion, and Complicity

“Collaboration” (chinil, literally “pro-Japanese”) is both an analytical and a moral term in Korean public life. Historians who use it need criteria. Four distinctions are useful. Voluntary collaboration involves initiative in advancing colonial aims. Institutional participation means holding a position in colonial or officially sponsored bodies. Constrained accommodation means complying outwardly to protect livelihood, family, or institutions. Forced compliance describes actions taken under direct threat or without meaningful choice, as in the case of schoolchildren.

Four questions help apply these categories to a person: what authority did they hold, what alternatives were realistically available, what did they gain, and what were the consequences for other Koreans? Conduct also changed over time, so a person’s record in 1925 may differ from 1943. Social background, wealth, education, or Japanese-language ability alone is not evidence of collaboration.

Applying the framework requires attention to timing. The pressure on Koreans was far greater in 1943 than in 1925, and a person’s public conduct could change as circumstances changed. A record from one year therefore cannot stand for a whole career. Historians also need to separate the postwar vocabulary of “pro-Japanese” conduct, shaped by later political disputes, from the categories used by people at the time.

Evidence matters at every step. Useful sources include published statements with dates, membership and leadership lists of officially sponsored bodies, personnel and gazette records, donation and fundraising lists, and memoirs and trials after 1945. Each has limits: lists show names but not willingness, statements were censored, and postwar testimony was often given in self-defense or accusation. A responsible assessment states which kind of evidence supports each claim.

Collaboration should be defined through conduct and context, not inherited identity or social position. Historians need to ask what an individual did, whether it advanced colonial administration or wartime objectives, which powers and benefits were involved, what pressures existed and how the activity affected others. A Korean writer producing an enthusiastic wartime recruiting essay acted differently from a household head submitting a mandatory form; an officer enforcing police orders had an authority not possessed by a schoolchild. A reliable analysis identifies the action before considering possible motives.

The vocabulary is also historically situated. Later Korean efforts to name and investigate pro-Japanese collaboration developed under the politics of liberation, division and state formation, and these later categories can illuminate as well as distort individual life histories. One individual might have participated in nationalist activity in one decade and imperial propaganda in another. Assessing such a trajectory requires a chronology of writings, appointments and responsibilities, not an attempt to assign a timeless essence. Coercion, self-interest, conviction and institutional inertia often interacted, but the difficulty of establishing motives is not a reason to avoid evaluating documented public behavior.

Section 60.36

Korean Officials, Police, Bureaucrats, and Local Administrative Intermediaries

Koreans served in the colonial bureaucracy, police, and local government, as clerks, police officers, township heads (myeon), and, in smaller numbers, in higher posts. Many positions were subordinate: Koreans were concentrated in lower ranks and in roles that placed them between the state and their neighbors. Officeholders implemented orders, sometimes harshly, and sometimes softened them or provided warnings. Assessing any individual requires evidence of what they did in office, not simply that they held it.

The colonial administration placed Japanese officials in the senior posts of the Government-General and the provinces, while Koreans filled many lower and middle positions and held posts as county heads and township heads. Consultative bodies, such as the Central Advisory Council of Korean notables and provincial and municipal councils created from the 1920s, had advisory or limited powers, and franchise rules restricted who could vote. These offices gave some Koreans visibility and influence without giving Koreans control of policy.

Policing shows the range of roles. Korean constables carried out routine duties on the streets and in villages, and some officers served in units that watched for political activity. These are different positions with different powers. A constable enforcing a registration deadline, a township head passing along quotas, and an officer interrogating a suspected nationalist acted under the same system with very different degrees of initiative and consequence.

The evidence includes official gazettes, personnel directories, court and police records, and memoirs. They show who held office and when, but not always what a person did in office or how the community judged them. The experience of those under their authority sometimes records leniency, warnings, or abuse, and each case needs its own documentation. The later political careers of former officials belong to the chapters on liberation and occupation.

Korean officials served within a hierarchy whose legal authority remained Japanese. Police employees, local administrative staff, school officials and translators could carry out everyday tasks that made colonial rule workable. Their participation varied in importance. An office clerk entering household data did not have the same decision-making power as a senior official directing a discriminatory policy or a police investigator interrogating political suspects. Administrators could advance careers and gain relative security, yet could also face scrutiny and restrictions imposed on Koreans by Japanese superiors.

A serious case study should identify a person's appointment, responsibilities and documented decisions. The mere presence of a Korean name in a departmental roster does not establish active ideological support; by contrast, dated orders or public speeches supporting repression provide more direct evidence. These positions also created local dilemmas. An intermediary might transmit official instructions to neighbors with whom he had family and community relations. Local memory may later portray him as a collaborator or as someone who mitigated harm. Historians must test such claims with documents and distinguish an actor's intent from the actual effects of his official functions.

Section 60.37

Landlords, Business Leaders, Industrialists, and Economic Collaboration

Korean landowners and business owners took part in colonial economic institutions, government-sponsored associations, and wartime campaigns for savings and production. Commercial activity under colonial rule is not in itself collaboration. Evidence of collaboration requires documented support for imperial programs, such as holding office in mobilization organizations or publicly urging participation. Some figures appear in the record both as supporters of Korean institutions and as participants in wartime campaigns, which shows why single labels fail.

Colonial rule created business opportunities for some Koreans and restricted others. Landlords benefited from rice policy and tenancy arrangements discussed in Chapter 58, and some held local posts. Holding land or running a firm is not collaboration, and many owners also funded schools, newspapers, and cultural projects.

Wartime made the line harder to draw. Controlled materials, credit, and licenses meant firms needed official goodwill to operate. Owners were pressed to buy government bonds, join savings campaigns, make “defense” donations, and take posts in mobilization associations. Evidence of collaboration lies in documented acts such as leadership in these associations or public appeals, not in prosperity. The same person can appear in the record as a supporter of Korean institutions and as a participant in wartime campaigns, which is why single labels mislead.

Commercial participation under colonial rule varied from ordinary efforts to survive to significant involvement in state-sponsored campaigns. Landlords, merchants and manufacturers depended on law, transport and markets provided or regulated by the colonial administration. Some prospered through close connections with officials and contracts, while others faced the same constraints as enterprises elsewhere in the colony. Profit alone cannot establish political allegiance, especially where Koreans had few options outside the imperial market. What matters for collaboration analysis is the evidence of voluntary leadership, material provision, public advocacy or unusually close institutional partnership in advancing imperial policy.

Wartime contracts and enterprise committee records could document the scale and beneficiaries of economic participation, but figures require context. A government order to deliver goods at controlled prices is not the same as an entrepreneur independently recruiting workers or celebrating conquest. A Korean business association's statement might have been drafted under official pressure even where its leaders also sought favors. To interpret business collaboration adequately, students should identify the company, owners, date, contractual obligation and policy served, then compare the conduct with the alternatives available under an increasingly coercive war economy.

Section 60.38

From Nationalism to Imperial Advocacy: Yi Gwang-su and Choe Nam-seon

Yi Gwang-su, author of the 1917 novel Mujeong, took part in drafting the February 8, 1919 declaration of Korean students in Tokyo. Choe Nam-seon drafted the March First Declaration of Independence and was imprisoned. In the late 1930s and 1940s both publicly supported imperial policy: Yi adopted a Japanese-style name in 1940, and in 1943 both urged Korean students in Tokyo to volunteer for military service. Scholars debate their motives, which can only be inferred, while their public statements and dates are documented.

Their earlier nationalist roles and later advocacy both belong to the record. Neither erases the other.

Yi Gwang-su and Choe Nam-seon were among the best-known figures in early twentieth-century Korean intellectual life. Their earlier contributions to nationalist literature, publishing and public debate were significant, making their later support for imperial causes particularly contested after liberation. The history must be chronological. Specific essays, appointments, speeches and travel reveal changes in public behavior more reliably than general statements that they 'turned pro-Japanese.' Choe's involvement with colonial historical institutions and later wartime roles, for instance, differed in form from Yi's promotion of imperial identity in literary and political writing.

A particularly instructive episode occurred on November 24, 1943, when the two addressed Korean students in Japan in support of wartime service, including a visit associated with Meiji University. This is a dated public intervention, not merely a rumor about private views. Comparing it with their own earlier arguments about Korean identity permits analysis of ideological change without erasing either phase. The source record also demands caution: accounts written after 1945 could emphasize betrayal or rationalization. Public advocacy can be assessed from the action and its words even if the degree of inner conviction remains inaccessible.

Section 60.39

Writers, Educators, Scholars, and Artists in Imperial Cultural Organizations

Writers, scholars, and artists joined officially sponsored bodies, wrote commissioned propaganda, or lent their names to campaigns. Membership, public leadership, commissioned work, and personal conviction are different things, and evidence on each differs. Some participated under pressure and later withdrew from public life; others took leading roles.

Cultural organizations offered Korean writers, scholars, and artists both pressure and opportunity. A writers’ association formed in 1939, with Yi Gwang-su as chairman, was reorganized in 1943 under closer wartime direction, and its members were asked to contribute to patriotic events and publications. State-sponsored art exhibitions, which dated from the 1920s, increasingly featured war-related themes.

Roles within these bodies differed. A person might hold a title, sign a statement, attend a meeting, accept a commission, or write enthusiastically on their own initiative. Some withdrew from public life or stopped publishing; others became leading voices. Evidence includes journals, membership lists, exhibition catalogs, and later accounts. Titles and signatures show participation, but the degree of conviction and pressure must be weighed case by case.

Wartime literary and cultural organizations often mixed professional opportunity, institutional protection and political obligation. Their officers could issue public endorsements, choose approved subjects or recruit performers and writers into patriotic events; ordinary members might have much less influence. The fact of joining an organization must therefore be assessed alongside a person's formal position and published work. The strongest evidence is a dated statement or participation in a specific event, not an unqualified membership roster.

Choe Chae-seo's editorial activity at Kokumin Bungaku illustrates a deliberate ideological program for literature, whereas Kim Sa-ryang's Japanese-language fiction cautions against assuming that writing in Japanese automatically demonstrates acceptance of imperial aims. Religious and educational leaders likewise faced professional constraints while making different kinds of decisions. The task is to reconstruct how cultural institutions became channels of state demands without treating everyone employed in them as morally identical. It is possible to describe coercive conditions accurately while also recognizing that some individuals exercised substantial initiative in propagating imperial objectives.

Section 60.40

Religious Leaders, Institutional Accommodation, and Public Support for Imperial Policies

Religious bodies took part in shrine attendance, patriotic fundraising, and imperial ceremonies. Institutional decisions differ from individual dissent: clergy, educators, and lay believers in each tradition included both supporters and refusers (§60.20–60.22).

Religious organizations were drawn into wartime campaigns. Following the September 1938 decision of the Presbyterian General Assembly, a delegation visited the Ise Shrine in Japan. Churches and temples took part in patriotic fundraising, prayer services for soldiers, and ceremonies. These institutional acts reflect decisions by leaders under heavy pressure and, in some cases, active support.

Dissent also existed. The Pyongyang pastor Ju Gi-cheol refused to accept shrine worship, was repeatedly imprisoned, and died in prison in 1944. Clergy, educators, and lay believers in every tradition differed from their leadership, and some quietly avoided participation. A denomination’s official position never establishes what every member believed.

Religious leaders occupied a particularly complicated position because churches, temples and other bodies managed their own members, property and educational institutions while operating under colonial supervision. An official religious resolution endorsing shrine observance or patriotic contributions could protect institutional access but could also help normalize the policy. A leader organizing support for imperial campaigns had greater agency than a lay member whose congregation had already adopted a policy. The evidence must distinguish who drafted a statement, who formally approved it, who dissented and who carried it into practice.

The Presbyterian shrine controversy demonstrates why the procedure matters as much as a vote. The 1938 assembly decision came under police supervision, and a contemporary missionary account reported restrictions on delegates who wished to oppose it. That circumstance limits the interpretation of a unanimous-looking resolution, while not eliminating the possibility that some leaders supported accommodation. Buddhist and other religious organizations followed different institutional paths, and their histories require their own records. The critical comparison is among positions of authority, documented public advocacy and the consequences of institutional action for ordinary members.

Section 60.41

Public Appeals for Wartime Service and the Politics of Collaboration

Speeches, articles, advertisements, and organizational campaigns encouraged Korean military and labor service. Promoting mobilization differs from being compelled to take part. The systems of volunteer recruitment, conscription, labor mobilization, and their human consequences are treated in Chapter 62.

Appeals for service took the form of speeches, newspaper articles, advertisements, pamphlets, and lecture tours. The army special volunteer system began in April 1938, followed by measures later in the war, including a student volunteer campaign in 1943 and conscription in 1944. These measures and their human consequences are treated in Chapter 62.

For this chapter the key point is the language of persuasion. Appeals urged Koreans to prove their loyalty through service and promised recognition in return, while the underlying inequality discussed in Division X remained. “Volunteering” took place in a setting where local officials, schools, and employers pressed for results, so the word itself cannot be taken as evidence of free choice. Those who made the appeals, those who relayed them, and those who were targeted stood in very different positions.

Public appeals for wartime service reveal collaboration more clearly than broad labels when authors, venues and dates can be established. The government sought prominent Korean voices who would urge enlistment, military participation and support from families. A newspaper appeal could present duty as patriotic honor and identify participation as proof of equal imperial membership, even though the underlying political system remained unequal. Speakers were not necessarily responsible for the details of military or labor deployment, but their words could help legitimize policies that placed young Koreans under increasing pressure.

Such appeals should be distinguished from the experiences of the people addressed. Students in Japan who were urged to enter military service faced deadlines, surveillance, career uncertainty and threats of sanctions; their reactions cannot be inferred from the enthusiasm expressed by an invited speaker. The primary record of a recruiting speech shows the advocate's public position, whereas petitions, diaries and administrative files are needed to reconstruct audience responses. The relationship to Chapter 62 is straightforward: this section studies legitimating language and public intellectual participation; Chapter 62 studies recruitment systems, coercion and the lives of those mobilized.

Section 60.42

Households and the Everyday Experience of Assimilation

Policy fell differently within a household according to age and position. A child faced school requirements, an employed adult faced workplace expectations, and a household head carried name registration. The same family could contain people in very different positions.

Within one household the same policy fell differently. A school-age child was required to speak Japanese and recite the oath, the household head carried responsibility for registration, and an elder might have little Japanese at all. Generational gaps in language and schooling sometimes meant that children understood the new institutions better than their parents, which altered family authority. Religious observance, ancestral rites, and employment added further pressures, so a family could comply publicly in one area while keeping custom in another.

The household was where several state policies converged. A parent had to negotiate school enrollment, official language expectations, new registration forms, local ritual observances and later demands circulated through neighborhood associations. Family members experienced these policies unequally: a child might face Japanese-language lessons daily while grandparents retained Korean-language community ties; an employed adult might depend on official approval; women could be made responsible for presenting the household as orderly and patriotic. The experience cannot be captured by treating 'the Korean family' as a unit with one intention.

Bureaucratic pressure also changed over time. A household might have welcomed a child's access to education in one decade and found its religious and naming practices restricted in the next. The same person could comply with a registration rule while preserving family genealogies or teaching Korean privately. A carefully labeled composite can help students visualize these layers, but a scholarly edition should compare the illustration with real records: school schedules, household registers, neighborhood notices and dated correspondence. Their combined evidence supports the claim of pressure without inventing an individual diary or asserting knowledge of private feelings.

Section 60.43

Women, Domestic Life, Imperial Motherhood, and Wartime Social Organizations

Propaganda promoted ideals of the disciplined, self-sacrificing mother and domestic manager, and women were organized in wartime associations. These ideals differ from women’s actual circumstances: women worked in agriculture, factories, and households, and economic need shaped what they did far more than ideology.

Women were addressed as mothers and household managers whose duty was to raise loyal children, save, and support the war. Women’s associations organized campaigns, and some prominent educators and writers, including Kim Hwal-lan and Mo Yun-suk, publicly backed wartime campaigns. Such figures have since been assessed through the framework of §60.35, with attention to their positions and statements. Meanwhile most Korean women worked in farming, factories, and households, and the ideal of the domestic imperial mother described few lives as they were actually lived. The mobilization of women for labor and military sexual slavery is treated in Chapter 62.

Imperial wartime ideology assigned women a distinctive public role as mothers, wives, educators and organizers of domestic austerity. Officially approved organizations promoted thrift, health, household order and acceptance of military service by sons and husbands. The rhetoric of motherhood could be presented as honorable national service while transferring the burdens of wartime disruption to families. Women also worked in schools, offices, factories and fields, so an exclusive emphasis on motherhood would obscure their economic and organizational roles. Gender shaped the forms of mobilization without reducing all women's lives to them.

Named advocates require dated evidence. Kim Hwal-lan, associated with Ewha's educational leadership, publicly supported imperial service in an essay identified as 'Conscription and the Resolve of Peninsula Women' in the December 1942 issue of Sin Sidae. Mo Yun-suk also participated in wartime literary and public campaigns, but her actions should be tied to particular publications or organizational records rather than assumed from later reputation. The public speeches of prominent figures must not be treated as representative of all Korean women, many of whom had little institutional power and faced unavoidable demands. Comparing official appeals with women's actual working conditions brings the distinction between propaganda and experience into focus.

Section 60.44

Youth, Masculinity, Physical Training, and Expectations of Imperial Service

Schools and youth organizations emphasized physical training, discipline, and ideals of masculine service. This cultural preparation anticipated military mobilization, whose operation is covered in Chapter 62.

Schools and youth training programs, including youth training centers established in the 1930s, used military-style drill, group exercise, uniforms, and camps to build discipline and an ideal of masculine service. These programs were meant to prepare young men for the volunteer and conscription systems and to link physical culture with loyalty. How individual youths understood them varied: some embraced the opportunities and prestige, others endured them as obligation. The cultural preparation is the subject here; recruitment and its consequences belong to Chapter 62.

The wartime ideal of youth placed loyalty, discipline, physical fitness and readiness for service at the center of schooling and extracurricular activity. Drill, ceremonial participation and state-sponsored youth organizations connected the classroom to a broader political hierarchy. For boys, official rhetoric particularly emphasized martial service and the defense of the empire, while girls were often addressed through household discipline and supportive public work. These patterns did not exhaust the roles of young women or all individual experiences, but they shaped the expectations attached to age and gender.

Training institutions, school schedules and official photographs demonstrate what activities were prescribed. They do not tell us whether a pupil regarded a drill as patriotic, burdensome or merely a requirement to complete. University-age students and younger schoolchildren also occupied different administrative positions. The policies surrounding student recruitment for military service intensified in 1943; the full story of that mobilization belongs in Chapter 62. Here the principal question is how schools and youth institutions prepared a language of duty that could later be applied to wartime bodies and careers.

Section 60.45

Urban and Rural Differences, Social Class, and Unequal Exposure to Assimilation

Exposure was uneven. Urban professionals, students, and civil servants met Japanese-language schooling, propaganda, and organizations more directly than many rural farmers, whose daily lives were governed by local officials and village routines. Industrial workers faced workplace discipline. Class, region, sex, and age shaped how far assimilation reached.

The rural revitalization campaign of the 1930s organized villages under official guidance, and village officials were the key link to the state. Japanese-language courses for adults were held in many villages. Urban students and civil servants, by contrast, met schooling, propaganda, and organizations daily. Industrial workers in northern factories faced workplace discipline and company-run campaigns. Women, the elderly, and the poor in remote areas were often least reached by schooling and were most dependent on what local intermediaries reported or required.

The intensity of assimilation differed by place and occupation. Officials could supervise urban schools and large workplaces more frequently than dispersed rural households, although local village networks extended their reach. Urban professionals had greater exposure to Japanese as an administrative and employment language, while rural adults often retained Korean as the principal spoken language. Material inequalities also shaped the stakes: someone hoping for a government post might feel pressure to display fluency and ceremonial compliance, whereas a farmer dealing mainly with neighbors experienced different forms of inspection.

Regional contrasts should be grounded in institutional geography rather than stereotypes about northern or southern Koreans. A map of shrines, schools, transport connections and government offices can show where enforcement was feasible, but cannot establish a simple map of belief. Households shifted between urban and rural worlds through migration, military obligations and seasonal employment. The same national policy thus encountered distinct social structures. Comparative local evidence—school attendance, language campaigns, registrars' records and neighborhood association minutes—should anchor any claim that a policy was implemented more intensively in one locality than another.

Section 60.46

Public Compliance, Private Belief, and the Difficulty of Reconstructing Personal Experience

Attendance records, registration documents, school reports, and photographs show behavior, not conviction. Memoirs and testimony reflect later perspectives and postwar pressures. Historians therefore should not infer private beliefs from compulsory public conduct.

Photographs of ceremonies, attendance lists, and school reports show who was present and what was recorded, not what individuals thought. Memoirs and interviews offer inner perspective but were composed decades later, often by people who had been children and whose memories mixed pride, ambivalence, and later political judgment. Diaries and letters are valuable but rare and often self-censored. A cautious historian describes behavior as behavior and treats statements about belief as claims requiring independent support.

Public compliance is a weak measure of private belief when the state can punish refusal. An oath recital, approved photograph, registration declaration or organizational membership list establishes that an act was recorded; it does not by itself show enthusiasm, comprehension or consent. Yet historians should not assume that all participants privately opposed the regime either. People could accept some claims about reform, opportunities or belonging while rejecting other demands. The methodological obligation is to preserve uncertainty rather than fill it with a preferred national story.

Different records pose different problems. Police reports were compiled within an institution charged with identifying threats; personal diaries were shaped by their author's outlook and might be self-censored; memoirs written after liberation were influenced by retrospective loyalties and political disputes. Comparing sources can sometimes reveal how an event looked from different social positions. It rarely provides direct access to an entire community's interior life. Students should therefore separate an observable act, a contemporaneous expressed opinion, an inferred motive and a later remembered account, marking each step explicitly.

Section 60.47

Material Incentives, Institutional Pressure, Punishment, and the Limits of Choice

Employment, schooling, rationing, official approvals, and fear of surveillance shaped decisions. Actions taken to protect family security differ from those showing initiative in advancing colonial aims, even where outward behavior looks the same.

Control over rice, cloth, fuel, and other goods expanded from about 1939, and distribution often passed through the neighborhood associations, tying household supplies to local standing. Savings campaigns set quotas. Jobs and promotions favored those who spoke Japanese and displayed loyalty. Penalties ranged from social pressure to police action under the Peace Preservation Law, with a 1941 ordinance allowing preventive detention of those regarded as political risks. The pattern of incentives and penalties shaped decisions without determining them, and acts taken to protect a family differ from acts taken to advance the system.

The experience of pressure could involve both inducement and threat. Opportunities for schooling or employment might favor visible Japanese-language competence, while a family could face scrutiny for avoiding local ceremonies or declining participation in prescribed campaigns. As the war intensified, neighborhood committees connected ideological expectations with household savings, material collections and distribution systems. Some decisions were made under threats of punishment; others involved seeking a livelihood within institutions that the colonial government controlled. The presence of alternatives must be assessed for the particular place and date.

Formal statutes did not exhaust the consequences of refusal. A rule might say that a name change was optional, but local officials, employers or schools could make noncompliance costly. Conversely, not every report of a sanction can be generalized across the entire peninsula. Evidence should distinguish legal penalties, discretionary administrative measures, reputational pressure and the reactions of private employers or neighbors. This differentiated account makes the colonial system more intelligible than a binary choice between voluntary collaboration and total compulsion.

Section 60.48

Korean as a Spoken and Written Language under Increasing Restrictions

Korean remained the language of homes, markets, villages, and churches even as its place in schools, offices, and print shrank. Restricting formal Korean instruction is different from the disappearance of the language: most Koreans, especially older people, women, and rural residents, continued to speak Korean daily, and Japanese proficiency varied widely.

Official estimates in the early 1940s placed the share of Koreans with some comprehension of Japanese at roughly one-fifth of the population, though such figures depend on official definitions and campaign pressure. Proficiency varied by age, sex, schooling, and place: younger urban men were far more likely to speak it than older rural women. Korean thus continued as the working language of the great majority at home, in markets, in villages, and in most religious services. What shrank was its public and institutional status.

The suppression of Korean in official schooling and major newspapers did not remove it from conversation. Korean continued to be spoken in homes, markets, work settings and religious communities, with differences among generations and localities. Japanese gained importance for examinations, official communication and upward mobility, but its diffusion was unequal. Children might serve as interpreters of documents for older relatives; some writers moved between two literary languages; and the meaning of using either language depended on context. The survival of spoken Korean should not obscure the institutional violence of reducing its formal public roles.

Public and private domains are not completely separable. Language used in a neighborhood association, church or market could become politically sensitive where officials tried to enforce Japanese-only practices. A Korean-language note might be an ordinary personal communication or an act of deliberate cultural preservation depending on its circumstances. Evidence of language survival includes private letters, literary manuscripts, oral recollections and later testimony, but the biases of each remain important. An education ordinance tells us what was prescribed in schools, not the full linguistic life of a population.

Section 60.49

Orthography, Dictionaries, and the Korean Language Preservation Movement

The Korean Language Society (Joseon Eohakhoe), formed from an earlier research society of 1921, published the Unified Korean Orthography in 1933 and worked on a comprehensive dictionary. Standard spelling mattered for schooling, publishing, and shared written communication, and so for national identification, as discussed in Chapter 57.

The Society grew from earlier language research groups, including a society founded in 1921 and renamed in 1931, and it began compiling a comprehensive dictionary around 1929. Its 1933 orthography was widely adopted by Korean newspapers and publishers and in many schools until formal instruction ended. Its standardization work, which continued with a standard-language collection in 1936 and rules for transcribing foreign words in 1940, gave Korean a stable written form at the moment when its public use was being restricted. For many contemporaries this made the language project a form of cultural preservation, a view the colonial police later converted into an accusation.

Standardizing Korean writing was an extended scholarly project, not an emergency measure invented in response to the 1942 arrests. Earlier research societies, orthography committees and dictionary collaborators worked to clarify spelling, usage and vocabulary for a wider reading public. The 1933 Unified Korean Orthography was one significant milestone, followed by work on loanword conventions and the compilation of an extensive dictionary. Such efforts required gathering words from different regions, organizing entries, preparing definitions and negotiating scholarly disagreement. They also involved printers, donors and publishers beyond a small circle of professional linguists.

The dictionary project demonstrates why cultural institutions could acquire political significance without every editorial task being a political declaration. Standardization makes a language more teachable, printable and reusable; in the context of imperialization this could help sustain a distinct Korean-language public. The Language Society's 1940 application for publication of a dictionary comprising roughly 160,000 entries and thousands of illustrations, described by later historical research, shows that the work approached a material publishing stage before police intervention. Research should distinguish the surviving 1933 publication from dictionary manuscripts later seized and recovered; they are different source objects with different dates.

Section 60.50

The Korean Language Society Incident of 1942–1943

In October 1942, police in Hamhŭng, investigating a case that began with a student’s diary, arrested people linked to the Society and extended the investigation to its members. Dozens were arrested, and police charged them under public-order law with using language work to advance a nationalist movement. Dictionary materials were confiscated. Yi Yun-jae died in custody in December 1943 and Han Jing in February 1944; others were tried and imprisoned until liberation.

The police framing of the Society as a political conspiracy differs from its documented scholarly activity. The recovery of dictionary manuscripts in September 1945 allowed the dictionary to be completed after liberation.

The case began in the summer of 1942 in Hamhŭng, where police investigating a schoolgirl’s diary traced a connection to a Society member working on the dictionary. From October 1942 police arrested Society members in waves, and dozens were detained and interrogated. Police alleged under public-order law that the Society’s language work was cover for a nationalist movement aiming at independence. Those arrested reported harsh interrogation, and the two deaths in custody, Yi Yun-jae in December 1943 and Han Jing in February 1944, are among the best-documented consequences. Some defendants were tried in 1944 and their convictions upheld on appeal in early 1945. Surviving prisoners were released after liberation.

The recovery of the dictionary manuscripts in a rail freight warehouse in Seoul in September 1945 made it possible to complete and publish the dictionary, whose first volume appeared in 1947, and the manuscripts became a symbol of cultural survival. Students should note that the Society’s scholars did pursue a project with national meaning, but that does not make the police allegation of an armed or organized independence plot accurate. The case turned on the colonial state’s power to define cultural work as political crime.

The Language Society Incident developed from an investigation associated with a student diary and the arrest of Jeong Tae-jin in 1942. Police then arrested members of the society and seized documents connected to dictionary work. Historical accounts differ in their totals because some count people detained, others those investigated or charged. A comprehensive reconstruction must identify the precise counting category, but the repression is undeniable. Proceedings extended into 1944–1945, and several people received prison sentences. Yi Yun-jae died in custody in 1943 and Han Jing in 1944, linking the prosecution to permanent human losses.

The episode is particularly instructive because scholars who compiled spelling and dictionary entries were accused of political offenses. The colonial state interpreted language standardization through its fear of nationalism and opposition to imperialization. Yet the private motives of every contributor cannot be inferred from the accusation. The National Institute of Korean History preserves detailed accounts of the confiscation and later recovery of manuscript materials; the recovered dictionary manuscript was found after liberation, enabling publication of the first volume in 1947. The long chronology therefore runs from research and lexicography, to police disruption, to postliberation cultural reconstruction—not merely one arrest date.

Section 60.51

Literature, Oral Traditions, Historical Memory, and Korean Cultural Continuity

Songs, stories, family customs, and local memory persisted, and some writers continued to write in Korean until publication became impossible. Not every cultural act was organized political resistance. Cultural continuity is a historical phenomenon in its own right.

Cultural continuity took many forms. Poets and writers continued to compose in Korean, often for private circulation. The poet Yun Dong-ju wrote Korean-language poems during the war years, was arrested in Japan in 1943 on charges linked to nationalist activity, and died in a Fukuoka prison in February 1945. His poems were published after liberation. The Buddhist monk and poet Han Yong-un, who had been among the 1919 signers of the independence declaration, died in 1944 and is remembered for refusing accommodation. Oral songs, folktales, and family customs persisted in daily life without being organized or political, and treating all of them as resistance would blur important distinctions.

Cultural continuity involved more than language societies. Songs, religious practices, family stories, oral traditions and knowledge of local history circulated through community networks even where large-scale publishing was censored. Literary figures such as Yun Dong-ju and Han Yong-un later came to symbolize forms of Korean cultural dignity under colonial pressure, but their biographies and works should be analyzed within their own contexts, not converted wholesale into illustrations of one resistance strategy. Some texts speak overtly to political experience; others preserve identity through language and imagery without presenting an organized political program.

The survival of cultural forms can be difficult to measure. Colonial authorities usually recorded prohibited activity more readily than ordinary household continuity. After 1945 survivors sometimes remembered events through the urgent language of national liberation, emphasizing resistance where earlier practices had been more ambiguous. A sound cultural history uses manuscripts, editions, family archives and dated accounts to establish what existed at the time. It also recognizes that cultural traditions changed while being preserved, adapting to urbanization, migration and the bilingual circumstances created by empire.

Section 60.52

Religious Refusal, Community Solidarity, and Cultural Resistance

Documented refusals included believers who declined shrine attendance, educators who resisted new rules, and local groups that quietly avoided campaigns. Open protest, institutional resistance, quiet nonparticipation, and private custom are different forms and carried different risks. Some refusers were imprisoned.

Refusal took forms ranging from open protest to quiet avoidance. Some believers refused shrine attendance and were imprisoned, as in the case of the pastor Ju Gi-cheol. Others avoided participation by absence, delay, or illness, or by minimal compliance. Villagers could ignore campaigns without leaving a record. The risks differed sharply: open refusal could lead to prison, while quiet non-participation could be overlooked, and the evidence for such acts is far thinner for that reason.

Refusal could take visible or quiet forms. Some pastors declined shrine observance despite official pressure; certain schools closed rather than comply; families continued using Korean cultural practices even as the state demanded public conformity. These acts differed in immediate purpose. Refusing a rite because of Christian theology was not necessarily the same as endorsing a political nationalist organization, though imperial authorities might treat both as challenges to loyalty. Community solidarity could provide practical support to people under sanction, while fear for livelihoods could limit how openly others defended them.

Historians also face selection bias: dramatic arrests and closures appear in institutional records, whereas quiet noncompliance may leave almost no trace. It is therefore unsound to construct totals of resisters from a few prominent biographies or to treat all unrecorded behavior as consent. Better studies combine case evidence with the legal circumstances of enforcement and the religious or social relationships that made refusal possible. The chapter's claim is that assimilation remained contested, not that every act of maintaining Korean customs was a coordinated independence campaign.

Section 60.53

Cultural Preservation, Political Nationalism, and the Limits of Assimilation

Preserving Korean language and customs and pursuing political independence overlapped but were distinct. A person could keep Korean traditions without belonging to an independence organization, and activists disagreed about what national culture meant. Colonial police sometimes treated cultural activity as political, as the Language Society case shows.

The relationship between cultural work and political aims was disputed within the Korean nationalist camp. Advocates of cultural nationalism held that language, education, and economic self-strengthening were foundations of independence, while socialists and others criticized such efforts as accommodating colonial rule. Colonial police nevertheless frequently collapsed the two, treating cultural societies as political threats, while many Koreans preserved their language and customs with no connection to any organization. Keeping these three groups distinct is necessary for an accurate account.

Preserving Korean cultural identity and seeking the overthrow of Japanese rule were related but not identical projects. A dictionary compiler could understand the work as professional scholarship, national service or both. A household could continue its language and ancestral rituals without joining a political organization. Conversely, activists in organized independence movements could disagree over which traditions should define a future Korean society. The distinction prevents the chapter from flattening a wide population into a single political identity.

This does not render cultural policy politically insignificant. The regime sought to restructure the institutions that reproduced language and historical memory precisely because these practices helped sustain attachments outside imperial ideology. A measurable legal restriction on Korean instruction is therefore evidence of the state's aims, whether or not every speaker experienced the restriction as a conscious attack. Chapter 59 explains political organization; the present chapter shows how identity could remain active within everyday social practices, including settings that did not generate overt protests.

Section 60.54

Imperial Unity and the Persistence of Unequal Colonial Citizenship

Koreans were asked to show loyalty to an empire in which their political position differed from that of residents of the home islands. Differences persisted in representation, administration, social standing, and employment. In April 1945 Japan amended the House of Representatives election law to allot 23 seats to Korea and amended the House of Peers arrangements; there was no election for the proposed Korean constituencies before surrender. The gap between slogan and law is evidence of the policy’s limits.

Koreans living in Korea sent no representatives to the Imperial Diet during most of the colonial period. Korean men resident in Japan proper could vote after the 1925 expansion of male suffrage if they met the age and one-year residency conditions; this franchise did not extend to Korean districts. Pak Chun-geum won election to the House of Representatives from Tokyo in 1932. Titles in the Korean colonial peerage did not automatically convey membership in Japan’s House of Peers, although three Koreans received separate imperial appointments to that chamber between 1932 and 1943. Inside Korea, advisory bodies and limited local councils gave little real authority. Japanese officials in Korea also received pay supplements that Korean officials did not, a difference that made inequality a matter of everyday administration. The April 1945 proposal allocated 23 lower-house seats to Korea, but elections for these constituencies never occurred (§60.56).

The language of common imperial subjecthood concealed unequal channels of political participation. Koreans residing in Korea did not elect delegates to the Japanese House of Representatives during the colonial period. After Japan's 1925 expansion of male suffrage, however, eligible Korean male residents in Japan proper could vote under relevant residence and age rules; Pak Chun-geum was elected to the Diet from Tokyo in 1932. This exception matters because it disproves a careless claim that no Korean ever held a Diet seat while leaving the colony's exclusion from ordinary national representation intact.

The House of Peers presents a separate legal issue. Receiving a Korean colonial noble title did not automatically make someone a member of Japan's upper chamber; particular Koreans received individual imperial appointments. A later proposal allocating twenty-three House of Representatives seats to Korea appeared in 1945, but no election in those constituencies occurred before Japan's surrender. Together these distinctions show the difference between limited individual participation and representation of the colonized population as a polity. Claims about equality should also be compared with occupational hierarchies, settler privilege and the distribution of administrative authority.

Section 60.55

Japanese Settlers, Colonial Privilege, and Conflicting Visions of Integration

Settlers held economic and social advantages. Some supported stronger loyalty requirements for Koreans but resisted changes that might weaken their own position. Settler interests were one reason the promise of equality remained unfulfilled.

Colonial figures put the Japanese settler population at approximately 758,600 in 1943 and 712,600 in May 1944; the population was heavily urban and held disproportionate access to influential positions in commerce, industry, and administration. Settler newspapers and associations often supported strong demands for Korean loyalty while resisting reforms that might open positions to Koreans or reduce settler influence. These divergent positions mean the “settler view” was not uniform: some advocated greater equality, and many individual settlers had close relationships with Korean neighbors and colleagues. Even so, the structure of colonial privilege gave settlers a stake in the continuation of difference.

Japanese settlement was a fundamental part of colonial Korea's social structure. Settlers worked in administration, commerce, education, finance and other fields, and their numbers grew over time. Historical population counts reported roughly 758,600 Japanese residents in 1943 and 712,600 in May 1944; the date and category must be given rather than an undifferentiated assertion of 'three-quarters of a million.' Their institutions provided important networks of influence, but settlers were not one political class with identical personal positions on integration. Professionals, small merchants, large business owners and officials occupied different places in the colonial hierarchy.

The contradiction of naisen ittai appeared clearly where policy promised common belonging but residents encountered unequal social status and influence. Some settler interests supported loyalty campaigns while opposing changes that might weaken their advantages in appointments or business. Others advocated greater institutional incorporation of Koreans. Evidence for those positions must come from specific settler organizations, newspapers or recorded speeches. The existence of competing views did not eliminate the overarching inequality built into colonial government; it explains how official rhetoric could be contested even among those who benefited from the system.

Section 60.56

Imperial Obligations, Promised Recognition, and Wartime Subjecthood

Officials argued that military service and other obligations would earn Koreans recognition as full subjects. Obligations grew faster than rights. The experiences of those actually mobilized are covered in Chapter 62.

Officials argued that duties such as military service would earn Koreans the status of full subjects. In 1942 the authorities publicly announced a plan to introduce compulsory elementary education in 1946; the Government-General formally resolved in 1943 to pursue that schedule, but Japan’s 1945 defeat prevented the scheme from taking effect. Military conscription of Koreans began in 1944. Rights lagged behind obligations: promises of Diet representation were made only in 1945, and equality in pay, status, and opportunity was not realized. The argument that sacrifice would bring recognition thus asked Koreans to demonstrate loyalty before any equal standing was granted.

The empire increasingly demanded obligations while delaying or withholding political recognition. In 1942 officials announced plans for compulsory elementary schooling, formally elaborated in 1943 with an intended 1946 start, but the project was not realized before surrender. The same period brought announcements and implementation of Korean military conscription and additional appeals to students. In April 1945 the government proposed parliamentary representation from colonial constituencies, including twenty-three House seats for Korea, yet the projected election was overtaken by defeat. These events did not form a sequence in which equal rights simply preceded public obligations.

The point should be made with careful chronology. In some areas, school categories had been formally unified while resources remained unequal; in others, military duties were imposed while formal representation remained prospective. Propaganda could present service as a privilege of shared nationality, but the conditions experienced by recruits require separate evidence. This section evaluates the contradiction at the level of political doctrine and institutional policy. The coercive details of recruitment, service and labor mobilization are the assigned subject of Chapter 62.

Section 60.57

The Collapse of Colonial Assimilation Policy in August 1945

Japan’s surrender in August 1945 ended the authority of the assimilation regime. The Government-General continued administering until the formal surrender to American forces in September. In October 1946 the American military government issued an ordinance allowing the restoration of Korean names. Korean-language schooling and publishing resumed, and institutions adjusted. The detailed history of liberation and occupation belongs to Chapters 64–66.

The immediate transition was uneven. On August 15 and in the following days, Korean political groups formed committees to take over local order, while the Government-General sought to arrange a controlled handover. The formal Japanese surrender in Seoul took place on September 9, and American military administration began soon after. In southern Korea, the U.S. military administration directed the reopening of public primary schools for September 24, 1945, and its September 29 ordinance required Korean-language instruction. Chosun Ilbo resumed on November 23 and Dong-A Ilbo on December 1, 1945. Official shrine attendance ended; the precise dismantling chronology of Chōsen Jingū should be established separately from site-specific records. U.S. Military Government Ordinance No. 122, issued October 23, 1946, provided for restoration of Korean names in southern Korea while preserving the validity of legal acts previously performed under Japanese-style names. These changes reversed the outward forms of assimilation quickly, but the people who had worked in colonial institutions, the language habits of a generation of schoolchildren, and the disputes about responsibility did not disappear (§§60.60, 64–66).

Japan's surrender in August 1945 abruptly removed the legitimacy and ultimately the authority of the Government-General's assimilation program, but institutional change was not instantaneous or uniform. Korean committees formed in August, and military control in the southern zone passed through the formal Japanese surrender in Seoul on September 9. Schools shifted toward Korean-language instruction under the authorities that replaced the colonial administration, but reopening dates, curricula and staffing depended on the emerging occupation systems. In the southern zone the newly established military government directed elementary schools to reopen in late September and restored Korean-language teaching.

The newspaper sphere also changed over time rather than on August 15 alone. Chosun Ilbo resumed on November 23, 1945, and Dong-A Ilbo on December 1. Households confronted the problem of legal names remaining in official records; in the southern occupation zone Ordinance No. 122 of October 23, 1946 supplied procedures for restoring Korean names. The prominent Chōsen Jingū ceased to embody a living state cult, but precise claims about the date and mechanics of every building's demolition require evidence. Liberation ended colonial policy, not the administrative, material and personal consequences it had left behind.

Section 60.58

Sources for the History of Assimilation: Laws, Propaganda, Police Files, and Personal Testimony

Different sources capture different things. Statutes show intent, school and registration records show compliance, propaganda shows what the state wanted said, police files show what investigators alleged, and memoirs and oral history show later memory. Evidence of loyalty needs context, and no single source type answers questions about belief.

A research approach to this chapter might proceed in steps. Begin with a statute or ordinance to establish what the state required. Compare it with a school or registration record to see what was implemented, with a newspaper to see how the policy was publicized under censorship, and with police and court files to see how dissent was framed. Then turn to diaries, letters, and memoirs, reading each for what its author knew and wanted to say. Finally, consider what the whole set omits: women, rural people, and children left the fewest records of their own. Evidence of apparent loyalty always needs this contextual reading.

The primary sources for this subject frequently record the same event in incompatible registers. An ordinance specifies legal duties; an implementation circular instructs officials; a newspaper report presents an authorized public account; a school register may document attendance; a police dossier constructs a suspected offense; and a memoir written much later interprets an experience from the standpoint of survival and political memory. None can be substituted automatically for another. A historian assessing the 1940 name policy, for instance, should read the legal articles alongside dated enforcement reports and evidence from particular families.

Source criticism begins with identification: author or issuing body, exact date, publication or file title, intended audience and provenance. It continues with translation. The Korean rendering of a Japanese legal term can be accurate in general while obscuring a distinction important to the law, as with surname and household shi. Students should identify which statements are direct quotations from a verified text and which are paraphrases or later scholarly reconstructions. Conclusions about private belief should be modest where evidence shows only outward conduct; conclusions about documented policy enforcement can be firmer where multiple independent records agree.

Section 60.59

Historiographical Debates over Collaboration, Resistance, and Colonial Modernity

Scholars differ on how to weigh exploitation, modernization, and cultural change under colonial rule, and on how to define collaboration. Some stress structural coercion, others individual responsibility, and others the ambiguities of “colonial modernity.” Sound conclusions rest on dated actions, not inference from social background.

Three broad interpretive frames shape current scholarship. One stresses exploitation and resistance, emphasizing coercion and Korean opposition. A second stresses colonial modernization, emphasizing economic and institutional change under Japanese rule. A third, often called colonial modernity, argues that modernization and domination were bound together and studies how everyday life, cultural forms, and identity were made within that setting. Some historians use the idea of a “gray zone” to describe the wide range of ordinary conduct between open collaboration and open resistance. Each frame highlights evidence that the others tend to overlook, and disagreement among them is partly about emphasis and definition rather than about the underlying facts.

Historical writing about colonial Korea has employed different explanatory frames. Studies of imperial coercion and anti-colonial resistance emphasize structures of domination and cultural survival. Work on colonial modernization measures schools, infrastructure and industrial change while facing difficult questions about benefit, ownership and political authority. Scholarship on colonial modernity examines how new institutions and subjectivities arose inside unequal imperial relationships. None of these frameworks automatically answers whether a particular author or official collaborated; that judgment requires a more closely specified account of actions and circumstances.

Scholars such as Carter J. Eckert, Gi-Wook Shin and Michael Robinson have contributed to debates about development, nationalist discourse and colonial society, while research by Takashi Fujitani examines race, assimilation and wartime mobilization in the Japanese empire. Their arguments are not interchangeable and should be assigned to specific publications rather than invoked as an undifferentiated consensus. A useful 'gray zone' approach recognizes constrained choice without allowing moral ambiguity to erase clear cases of public propaganda and coercion. Students should compare what question each scholar asks, which evidence the argument rests upon and how the chosen categories affect the interpretation.

Section 60.60

Assimilation and Korean Identity after Liberation

After 1945, disputes over accountability, language, names, and national identity shaped both Korean states. In South Korea a special committee investigated collaboration in 1948–1949, and later commissions in the 2000s revisited the question. Continuing disputes show how the legacy of assimilation remains part of Korean historical memory. The detailed political history is treated in later chapters.

In South Korea, a law on punishing anti-national acts was passed in 1948 and a special committee began investigating collaborators, but it was disrupted in 1949, and few were punished. Many former officials and police stayed in public life. In the Soviet zone, a different set of purges and reforms removed many former officials. In later decades, South Korean commissions and research institutions returned to the question, including a 2009 compilation of individuals identified as pro-Japanese. Linguistic and cultural legacies were also contested, from Hangeul-first education and the reduction of Japanese loanwords to continuing debates over memory. The detailed political history belongs to Chapters 64–66 and Volume IV.

The end of empire opened debates over how Korean society should identify, replace or retain personnel, institutions and practices shaped by thirty-five years of colonial rule. Language education, household records, professional hierarchies and religious organizations could not be remade simply by announcing liberation. Individuals who had publicly advanced Japanese policy sometimes retained expertise and influence, while those persecuted for preserving Korean language or rejecting shrine observance gained recognition. The political systems emerging in the northern and southern occupation zones addressed these problems differently.

In southern Korea the 1948 law punishing anti-national acts established a legal basis for investigating collaboration, and the special investigation committee was forcibly disrupted amid political conflict in 1949. Later researchers continued to assemble documentary evidence, including the 2009 Dictionary of Pro-Japanese Collaborators, a historical compilation of 4,389 individuals rather than a roster of judicial convictions. Such sources reveal the persistence of controversy, but cannot be projected backward onto every colonial-era decision. The lasting historical task is to judge documented conduct within its period while recognizing that postliberation politics reshaped the labels, institutions and public memory through which that conduct has been understood.

Chapter Summary

Assimilation developed in stages. Present from annexation, it became more intrusive after 1931 and especially from 1936, when Minami Jirō’s administration and wartime needs produced a program of imperial subject-making.

The program worked through schools, names, shrines, the press, and mass organizations. The 1938 and 1943 ordinances changed the place of Korean in schooling, the 1939–1940 name policy reached every household, and shrine attendance produced a crisis for churches and schools.

Colonial authorities demanded unity while maintaining legal inequality. Imperial rhetoric cannot be taken as proof of equality.

Korean responses ranged across collaboration, institutional participation, constrained accommodation, forced compliance, and refusal. Judging them requires dated evidence of conduct, authority, and alternatives.

Cultural continuity was widespread, but it was not always organized resistance. The Korean Language Society case shows police defining cultural work as political crime.

After 1945 the legal basis of assimilation collapsed, while disputes over accountability and identity continued into the postliberation period.

Collaboration, in this chapter’s analysis, is a spectrum of conduct and not a status. Officials, businesspeople, writers, clergy, and ordinary householders occupied positions with very different powers and choices, and the evidence for each must be dated and specific.

The Language Society case showed that the colonial state could define cultural activity as political crime, while the many forms of everyday continuity showed that assimilation reached only part of Korean life. Preserving Korean language and custom and organizing against colonial rule overlapped but were distinct.

The legal basis of assimilation ended in August 1945, but its legacies in language habits, institutional memory, and disputes over accountability continued to shape both postwar Korean states.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter shows how an empire attempted to reshape identity and how far that attempt reached. Laws and rituals could be imposed; belief could not be commanded.

The chapter also shows why careful categories matter. Treating all Koreans as patriots or collaborators erases the range of choices under unequal power.

The legacies of this period run through later chapters on liberation, division, and the memory of colonial rule in both Korean states.

The chapter’s lasting lesson is that identity is shaped by institutions but not fully controlled by them, and that historians must read evidence of compliance with care. The distinctions it develops, between rules and enforcement, behavior and belief, and cultural continuity and organized resistance, apply well beyond Korea.

It also prepares for what follows. Chapter 62 examines the mobilization that these ideological programs justified, Chapter 63 follows those who continued the independence struggle abroad, and Chapters 64–66 treat the liberation and the political order that emerged, in which memory of colonial conduct remained a contested subject.
