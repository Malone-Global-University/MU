Ancient Israel and Judah

A History of the Middle East: From Ancient Civilizations to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

In 1868, a German missionary named F. A. Klein learned of a large inscribed black basalt stone standing among the ruins of Dhiban, east of the Dead Sea in what is now Jordan. Before European and local rivals could agree on how to remove it intact, local Bedouin, suspicious of foreign interest, built a fire beneath the stone and poured cold water over it, shattering it into fragments in the hope of dividing the reward among more households. Enough fragments were eventually recovered, together with a paper "squeeze" impression taken before the stone broke, to reconstruct most of its thirty-four lines of Moabite text. The inscription is a royal victory monument carved for King Mesha of Moab in the ninth century BCE, and it directly names Omri, king of Israel, describes the succession of his son, and refers to Omri's "house" while recounting Israelite domination of Moab; on one widely discussed but partly damaged line, it also contains what many scholars read as an early reference outside the Hebrew Bible to the "House of David."

The Mesha Stele is not a biblical text. It was carved by order of a Moabite king who had no interest in flattering Israel or Judah; if anything, it commemorates his success in throwing off decades of Israelite domination remembered elsewhere, in different form, in the biblical books of Kings. Its survival, its violent recovery, and the decades of scholarly argument over how to read its damaged lines illustrate, in miniature, the central historical problem this chapter must confront: ancient Israel and Judah are known to us through several radically different kinds of evidence — the extensive, richly detailed, but theologically shaped narrative of the Hebrew Bible; the material remains recovered by more than a century of archaeological excavation across the highlands, valleys, and cities of the southern Levant; and a comparatively small but historically decisive body of inscriptions left by neighboring kings, officials, and empires who had their own reasons for mentioning, boasting about, or lamenting their dealings with Israelite and Judahite rulers. None of these bodies of evidence, taken alone, can reconstruct the full history of these two kingdoms. Read together, and read critically, they allow historians to write a history that is neither a paraphrase of the biblical narrative nor a dismissal of it, but something more demanding: a reconstruction built from testing different classes of evidence against one another.

Chapter 17 traced the transformation of the Iron Age Levant after the collapse of the Late Bronze Age international system examined in Chapter 16 — the emergence of Phoenician city-states along the coast, Aramaean kingdoms in the Syrian interior, Philistine polities on the southern coastal plain, and new alphabetic writing systems that spread rapidly across the region. Israel and Judah belong squarely inside that same world. They were not an isolated biblical island surrounded by nameless neighbors; they were two of several Iron Age Levantine kingdoms, sharing language families, material culture, diplomatic practices, and recurring conflicts with Phoenician, Aramaean, Ammonite, Moabite, Edomite, and Philistine neighbors, and, from the ninth century onward, increasingly drawn into the orbit of the expanding Neo-Assyrian Empire examined fully in Chapter 19.

This chapter's task is therefore twofold. It must reconstruct, as carefully as the evidence allows, the political, social, economic, and religious history of highland communities that grew, over several centuries, into two distinct monarchic states — a northern kingdom called Israel, centered eventually at Samaria, and a smaller southern kingdom called Judah, centered at Jerusalem. And it must make the process of reconstruction itself part of the history being taught, because the methodological questions surrounding Israel and Judah — how much weight to give a later, edited religious text; how to date destruction layers and monumental architecture; how to read royal inscriptions that are themselves propaganda — are not incidental technical footnotes. They are central to understanding why historians disagree, sometimes sharply, about matters as basic as whether a substantial "United Monarchy" existed under David and Solomon in the tenth century BCE.

The chapter's chronological arc runs from the emergence of a dense network of small highland villages in the Iron Age I southern Levant, roughly the twelfth and eleventh centuries BCE, through the appearance of distinct Israelite and Judahite polities, the growth of the northern kingdom under the Omride dynasty, the parallel and slower growth of Judah around Jerusalem, and finally the crisis brought by Neo-Assyrian expansion — the destruction of Samaria and the incorporation of Israel into the Assyrian provincial system in the late eighth century BCE, and Judah's survival, battered but intact, after Sennacherib's invasion of 701 BCE. The chapter closes not with a king but with a historiographical question, because that is the most honest way to end a chapter whose central achievement should be to teach students how historians actually reconstruct a distant and contested past.

Two boundaries deserve notice before the chapter begins. This chapter examines Israel and Judah primarily from their own vantage point as Levantine kingdoms living under growing pressure from Mesopotamian empires; Chapter 19 examines the internal workings, ideology, and collapse of the Neo-Assyrian Empire itself, including the general Assyrian practice of deportation as an instrument of imperial policy. And this chapter ends at the close of the eighth and the crisis of 701 BCE; the Babylonian destruction of Jerusalem, the exile, and the broader collapse of the Iron Age political order in the seventh and sixth centuries BCE belong to Chapter 20. Readers should keep both boundaries in mind as they follow this chapter's narrative toward, but not past, the edge of catastrophe.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how Israel and Judah emerged from the broader transformation of Iron Age highland settlement in the southern Levant described in Chapter 17.
  2. Distinguish among the major classes of evidence used to reconstruct the history of Israel and Judah — biblical narrative, settlement archaeology, radiocarbon chronology, royal inscriptions, and comparative Near Eastern texts — and explain the strengths and limitations of each.
  3. Describe the material and social character of early Iron Age highland villages and evaluate what can and cannot be inferred from material culture about ethnic or group identity.
  4. Summarize the biblical account of Saul, David, and Solomon and compare it with the independent evidence bearing on tenth-century state formation in the southern Levant.
  5. Explain the major positions in the scholarly debate over the scale, character, and even existence of a substantial "United Monarchy," without treating any single position as definitively settled.
  6. Compare the political, economic, and geographic development of the northern kingdom of Israel and the southern kingdom of Judah as distinct states.
  7. Analyze the Mesha Stele as an external royal source and explain what it reveals, and what it does not reveal, about Israelite power in the ninth century BCE.
  8. Describe the administrative, economic, and social organization of Israelite and Judahite kingship, including officials, taxation, landholding, and social hierarchy.
  9. Explain the historical development of Yahweh worship, household religion, and religious centralization, distinguishing monolatry and exclusive Yahwism from later monotheism.
  10. Situate Israel and Judah within their network of Levantine neighbors, including Moab, Ammon, Edom, Aram-Damascus, Phoenician cities, and Philistine polities.
  11. Trace the process by which Neo-Assyrian expansion led to the destruction of the northern kingdom and Judah's survival as a diminished, tributary state after 701 BCE.
  12. Evaluate competing historiographical approaches to ancient Israel and Judah and articulate why historians resist both uncritical acceptance and wholesale rejection of the biblical narrative as historical evidence.

Historical Context

Chapter 17 examined the transformation of the Levant following the collapse of the Late Bronze Age palace system described in Chapter 16, a collapse whose causes remain debated but whose consequences — the disappearance of several major Bronze Age polities, population dislocation, and the emergence of new political communities across the eastern Mediterranean and the Levant — are well documented archaeologically. Along the coast, this transformation produced the Phoenician city-states; further inland, it produced Aramaean kingdoms in Syria; and along the southern coastal plain, it produced the Philistine city-states, whose material culture points to connections with the wider Aegean and eastern Mediterranean world even as their language and much of their later material culture became increasingly Levantine over succeeding generations.

In the central highlands of the southern Levant — the hill country west of the Jordan River, running roughly from the Judean hills in the south through the hills of Samaria in the north — archaeological surveys conducted from the 1970s onward documented a dramatic increase in the number of small rural settlements beginning in the twelfth century BCE, continuing through the eleventh century, a period conventionally labeled Iron Age I. These settlements were generally small, unwalled or lightly fortified, agriculturally based, and organized around extended households; their material culture, including a distinctive house type often called the "four-room house" and a general absence of pig bones in faunal assemblages at many though not all sites, has been the subject of extensive scholarly discussion about whether and how it can be used to identify the population of these villages as "Israelite." This chapter takes up that discussion directly in Section 18.3, because it is foundational to everything that follows.

The earliest and, for over a century, only widely accepted reference to a people called Israel outside the biblical text itself comes from Egypt: the Merneptah Stele, a monumental inscription carved for the pharaoh Merneptah around 1208 or 1207 BCE, commemorating a military campaign in Canaan and listing, among the conquered peoples and places, one identified by a hieroglyphic determinative marking it as a population group rather than a city-state, and read as "Israel." The inscription tells historians that a group called Israel existed in or near Canaan by the very end of the thirteenth century BCE, and that it was significant enough for an Egyptian king to claim credit for defeating it. It tells us essentially nothing about that group's political organization, territory, religion, or relationship to the highland villages archaeologists would later excavate. Bridging the gap between this brief, boastful Egyptian reference and the fully formed monarchic kingdoms described in the biblical books of Samuel and Kings is one of the central challenges this chapter addresses.

Students should also understand, before proceeding, that the chronology of the Iron Age southern Levant has itself been a major and long-running archaeological controversy, sometimes called the "Iron Age chronology debate" or, more specifically, the dispute over "high" versus "low" chronology for tenth-century strata at key sites. Because dating a destruction layer, a gate complex, or a monumental building to the tenth century versus the ninth century can determine whether that structure is attributed to Solomon, to a later Israelite king, or to the Omride dynasty of the ninth century, seemingly technical disagreements over pottery typology and radiocarbon calibration carry substantial historical weight. This chapter does not resolve that debate — no textbook honestly could — but it will flag, at the relevant points, where chronological uncertainty bears directly on historical interpretation.

Section 18.1

Israel in the Iron Age Southern Levant

The story this chapter tells does not begin with kings. It begins with a demographic and settlement transformation across the central hill country of the southern Levant that archaeological surveys, conducted intensively from the 1970s onward across the highlands of Samaria, Judah, and Benjamin, have documented in considerable detail. During the twelfth and eleventh centuries BCE — the period archaeologists generally label Iron Age I — the number of small rural sites in this highland zone increased sharply compared with the preceding Late Bronze Age, when the highlands had been sparsely settled relative to the fortified Canaanite city-states of the lowlands and valleys examined in Chapters 15 and 17. Estimates of total highland population during this period vary considerably depending on methodology, but the broad pattern of intensified settlement is not seriously disputed.

These new highland villages were generally small — often housing a few dozen to a few hundred people — unfortified or only lightly defended, built around extended-family compounds, and oriented toward mixed agriculture and pastoralism suited to the hill country's terraced slopes and marginal rainfall. Chapter 17 already introduced the broader Iron Age Levant of which this highland world was a part: Phoenician cities rebuilding coastal trade, Aramaean polities consolidating in inland Syria, and Philistine cities establishing themselves on the southern coastal plain. The highland villages of Iron Age I belong to this same regional transformation rather than to an isolated or separate historical process. Where the population of these villages came from remains debated: possibilities proposed by different scholars over the past half-century have included indigenous Canaanite population movement from the collapsing lowland city-state system into the hills, the settlement of pastoral or semi-nomadic groups already present in the region, the arrival of population elements from outside Canaan, and various combinations of these processes operating simultaneously in different places. Most specialists today regard highland Iron I society as substantially continuous with earlier Canaanite populations of the region, rather than as the product of a single, unified migration or conquest, though this remains an area of active scholarly discussion.

The earliest and, for well over a century, the only universally accepted reference to a people called "Israel" outside the biblical text comes from Egypt. The Merneptah Stele, a monumental granite inscription carved to commemorate the military achievements of the pharaoh Merneptah, who ruled at the very end of the thirteenth century BCE, includes among a list of defeated peoples and places in Canaan an entity written with a hieroglyphic determinative used elsewhere in Egyptian texts to mark a population group rather than a settled city or territory — commonly read and translated as "Israel." The inscription is dated with reasonable confidence to approximately 1208 or 1207 BCE, making it, at minimum, evidence that a group known to Egyptian scribes as Israel existed somewhere in or near Canaan by that date, and that this group was significant enough for an Egyptian king to claim a military victory over it in a formal royal monument.

What the Merneptah Stele does not tell historians is equally important. It does not specify where, precisely, this Israel was located; it does not describe its political organization, whether tribal, village-based, or otherwise; it does not indicate whether the group practiced a distinctive religion; and it does not establish any direct continuity between the Israel named by Merneptah's scribes and the highland villages archaeologists excavate in later Iron Age I strata, although most historians consider some relationship between the two extremely likely given the chronological and geographic proximity. The gap between this brief, hostile, and highly formulaic Egyptian reference and the fully elaborated monarchic narrative of the biblical books of Samuel and Kings — separated by roughly two to four centuries depending on how one dates the material in question — is precisely the gap this chapter's remaining sections must attempt to bridge using multiple, independently assessed classes of evidence.

Section 18.2

Reconstructing Israel and Judah: Text, Archaeology, and Inscription

Before this chapter narrates any king, it must establish how historians actually reconstruct the history of Israel and Judah, because the methodology is inseparable from the history itself. Four broad classes of evidence bear on this reconstruction, and each carries distinctive strengths and distinctive limitations.

The first is the Hebrew Bible, principally the books of Joshua, Judges, Samuel, and Kings, supplemented by Chronicles and by the prophetic books, which together constitute by far the most detailed narrative source for the history of Israel and Judah that survives from antiquity. These texts preserve genuine historical memory, administrative terminology, place names, and, in some cases, material that appears to draw on royal court records, genealogies, and annalistic sources no longer independently extant. They are not, however, contemporary neutral chronicles composed by disinterested observers recording events as they occurred. The books of Samuel and Kings, in the form in which they survive, reached something close to their present shape only after extensive composition, editing, and theological shaping carried out over centuries, with much of the final editorial work associated by most scholars with the period of the Babylonian exile and its aftermath, examined in Chapter 20 — centuries after the events described in this chapter's earlier sections. This does not mean the texts are historically worthless; it means they must be read as literature with a theological and political purpose, composed and repeatedly edited by authors interpreting earlier traditions for the concerns of their own, later times, and tested wherever possible against independent evidence rather than accepted as a straightforward factual record.

The second class of evidence is archaeology: the excavated remains of settlements, fortifications, administrative buildings, water systems, tombs, pottery, agricultural installations, and everyday material culture recovered from sites across the southern Levant over more than a century of fieldwork. Archaeology can establish settlement patterns, population estimates, the presence or absence of monumental construction at particular sites and periods, evidence of destruction and its approximate date, trade connections revealed through imported goods, and much about the economic and social organization of ordinary life that texts, focused overwhelmingly on kings and elites, rarely describe. Archaeology's central limitation is that material remains do not speak for themselves; they must be dated, typically through a combination of stratigraphy, pottery typology, and radiocarbon analysis, and interpreted, and both dating and interpretation are subject to ongoing scholarly revision. The dating of tenth-century strata at key sites — including debates over whether particular monumental gate complexes and administrative buildings should be attributed to the reign of Solomon or to later, ninth-century Israelite kings — has been one of the most consequential and long-running technical controversies in the field, sometimes called the "high chronology/low chronology" debate, because a shift of a few decades in dating can move a structure from one dynasty, and one kingdom's formative period, to another.

The third class of evidence is inscriptional material produced by Israel, Judah, and their neighbors: royal monumental inscriptions such as the Mesha Stele examined later in this chapter, administrative ostraca (inscribed pottery sherds used for everyday record-keeping), seals and seal impressions bearing personal and official names, and, from the Assyrian and Babylonian empires, royal annals and administrative records that mention Israelite and Judahite kings by name. This evidence is generally contemporary with the events it describes, which gives it considerable historical value, but it is almost always fragmentary, often damaged or incompletely preserved, and, in the case of royal monumental inscriptions produced by kings boasting of their own achievements, shaped by the same propagandistic purposes that affect royal inscriptions throughout the ancient Near East, discussed further in Chapters 9 through 12 and 19.

The fourth class of evidence is comparative: the wider historical, linguistic, and archaeological context of the Iron Age Levant established in Chapter 17, which allows historians to situate developments in Israel and Judah alongside parallel and comparable developments among Phoenician, Aramaean, Ammonite, Moabite, Edomite, and Philistine neighbors, testing whether patterns observed in Israel and Judah are historically plausible in light of what is independently known about state formation, administration, and religious practice elsewhere in the same region and period.

No single class of evidence is sufficient on its own. Biblical narrative without archaeological and inscriptional control risks reproducing later theological and political agendas as though they were straightforward historical fact. Archaeology without textual evidence can establish material patterns but struggles to explain political events, royal succession, or the specific historical developments that gave those patterns their significance. This chapter's method, consistent with the approach taken by the great majority of current historical and archaeological scholarship on ancient Israel and Judah, is to treat all four classes of evidence as partial, to look for points of convergence and divergence among them, and to be explicit, at every stage, about how confident historians can reasonably be in a given reconstruction.

Historical Vocabulary
Maximalism, Minimalism, and Centrist Archaeology

Historians and archaeologists of ancient Israel are sometimes loosely grouped, for descriptive convenience, into broad interpretive tendencies. Maximalist approaches, more common in earlier twentieth-century scholarship, tend to treat the biblical narrative as substantially reliable unless directly contradicted by other evidence, and to interpret archaeological and inscriptional material in ways that support the biblical account wherever plausible. Minimalist approaches, associated with a body of scholarship that became prominent from the 1990s onward, tend to treat the biblical narrative as a much later literary and theological construction with limited direct value for reconstructing events before the exile, and to require independent, non-biblical evidence before accepting biblical claims about earlier periods as historical.

Most working archaeologists and historians today, including the great majority whose research this chapter draws upon, occupy positions that resist both labels — sometimes loosely described as centrist — accepting that the biblical text preserves genuine and often valuable historical memory while insisting that specific claims require testing against archaeological, inscriptional, and comparative evidence rather than acceptance or rejection as a whole. This textbook adopts that centrist, evidence-testing approach throughout this chapter, treating "maximalism" and "minimalism" as historically important positions in an ongoing scholarly debate rather than as a choice students must make once and for all.

Section 18.3

Highland Villages, Kinship, and Early Israelite Identity

The Iron Age I highland villages introduced in Section 18.1 were small, agriculturally self-sufficient communities whose material remains have been studied intensively for what they might reveal about the emergence of Israelite identity. A characteristic dwelling type of this period and region, commonly called the "four-room house" or "pillared house," consists of a rectangular structure divided into three long parallel spaces, often with pillars separating a central area from side rooms, and a broad room across the rear — a plan well suited to combining household living space, storage, and the sheltering of animals within a single family compound. Versions of this house type appear at a large number of highland sites across several centuries and have sometimes been treated as a marker of Israelite ethnic identity, though most current scholars caution that architectural forms can spread through practical utility, regional building tradition, and cultural contact as readily as through shared ethnicity, and that the four-room house appears, in some times and places, at sites not otherwise identified as Israelite.

A second, more debated marker is the relative scarcity or absence of pig bones in faunal assemblages recovered from a number of highland Iron Age I sites, in contrast to some lowland and coastal sites, particularly certain Philistine settlements, where pig consumption appears more common. Some scholars have proposed that this pattern reflects an early dietary taboo consistent with later biblical food restrictions and might therefore serve as an ethnic marker distinguishing highland Israelite communities from Philistine and other neighbors. Other scholars caution that pig-keeping patterns can be explained by ecological and economic factors — pigs require more water and shade than sheep and goats and are poorly suited to marginal highland environments regardless of the population's ethnic identity or religious practice — and that the pattern is neither universal across all highland sites nor entirely absent from all lowland ones. This textbook presents both explanations as live possibilities rather than treating either as settled, and uses the debate itself to illustrate a broader methodological point: material culture can suggest patterns of identity, but historians must be cautious about equating any single artifact type, house plan, or dietary practice directly and simply with ethnic self-identification, since group identity is a social and ideological phenomenon that material remains reflect imperfectly and indirectly.

What can be said with more confidence is that highland Iron Age I society was organized substantially around kinship: extended households, clustered into larger kin groups sometimes described in later biblical tradition using terms often translated as "clan" and "tribe," formed the basic units of social, economic, and probably political organization in the absence of strong centralized state institutions. Villages practiced terrace agriculture adapted to the hill country's steep slopes, cultivating grain, olives, and grapes, alongside pastoralism focused on sheep and goats; archaeological evidence for the development of hillside terracing, cisterns for water storage, and rock-cut agricultural installations across this period points to a population investing substantial communal labor in adapting the highland environment to sustained settlement, a pattern of environmental adaptation consistent with this textbook's broader treatment, introduced in Chapter 2, of the region's landscapes as constraining but not determining human historical development. Religious practice at this stage appears to have been organized substantially at the household and local level, a pattern examined further in Section 18.9, rather than through the centralized temple institutions that would develop only later, alongside the growth of monarchic states.

The process by which this dispersed, kinship-organized highland society came to identify itself, and to be identified by others, as "Israel" — a process historians sometimes call ethnogenesis, the historical formation of a group identity rather than an assumption that such identities exist as fixed, timeless facts — remains only partially understood, and this chapter does not claim to resolve it. What can be said is that the Merneptah Stele attests to a group called Israel by the end of the thirteenth century BCE, that highland settlement intensified dramatically across the following two centuries, and that by the time independent evidence becomes more substantial in the ninth century BCE, examined in Section 18.6, a kingdom calling itself Israel, ruled from Samaria, is unambiguously attested in both biblical and non-biblical sources. The centuries between these two points remain the most historically uncertain in this chapter, and honest historical method requires acknowledging that uncertainty rather than papering over it with confident narrative detail borrowed uncritically from later biblical tradition.

Historical Vocabulary
Ethnogenesis and Highland Settlement

Ethnogenesis refers to the historical process by which a group comes to understand itself, and to be understood by others, as a distinct people — through shared territory, kinship claims, language, religious practice, political organization, or some combination of these factors, developing and changing over time rather than existing as a fixed, unchanging essence. Historians studying early Israel treat ethnogenesis as a gradual and only partly documented process rather than a single founding event, cautioning against both the assumption that a fully formed "Israelite" identity existed from the moment highland settlement began and the opposite assumption that no meaningful group identity existed before the rise of a centralized monarchy.

This approach parallels the caution this textbook has applied elsewhere to group identity in the ancient Middle East — for instance, in Chapter 17's treatment of Phoenician, Aramaean, and Philistine identity formation in the same period — and reflects a broader historical principle introduced in this textbook's opening chapters: modern and even biblical-era group identities should not be projected uncritically backward onto earlier, less documented periods.

Section 18.4

Saul, David, Solomon, and the United Monarchy Debate

The biblical books of Samuel and Kings present a coherent and dramatic narrative of state formation: Saul, from the tribe of Benjamin, is anointed as the first king over a confederation of Israelite tribes in response to Philistine military pressure and internal calls for centralized leadership; Saul's reign ends in military defeat and his death, and David, a former commander in Saul's service, succeeds him, first over Judah and then, according to the narrative, over a united kingdom of Israel and Judah together, conquering Jerusalem and establishing it as his capital; David's son Solomon inherits a stable and prosperous kingdom, undertakes major building projects including a royal temple in Jerusalem, engages in international trade and diplomacy, and presides over what the biblical text portrays as the kingdom's territorial and economic high point, before the kingdom divides into separate northern and southern states after his death, a division examined in Section 18.5.

This narrative is detailed, literarily sophisticated, and contains material — including administrative lists, place names, and passages many scholars regard as drawing on older court records or annalistic traditions — that most historians consider to preserve genuine historical memory in some form. It is not, however, a contemporary chronicle. The books of Samuel and Kings reached their surviving form through a long process of composition and editing that most scholars date, in its major phases, to periods well after the tenth century BCE, and the narrative's literary structure — presenting Saul as a flawed, divinely rejected king, David as an ideal though imperfect ruler chosen by divine favor, and Solomon's later apostasy as a cause of the kingdom's division — serves clear theological and political purposes connected to later concerns about legitimate kingship, dynastic continuity, and the causes of national catastrophe, purposes examined further in Section 18.12.

Independent evidence for a substantial, centralized tenth-century kingdom spanning both the northern and southern highlands remains limited and contested. On one side, several archaeologists have argued that monumental gate complexes and fortifications at sites including Hazor, Megiddo, and Gezer, traditionally associated with a biblical claim that Solomon built or fortified these cities, support the existence of a centralized state capable of major public works projects across a substantial territory in the tenth century — a position sometimes associated with a "high chronology" for these strata. On the other side, other archaeologists, applying different pottery typologies and radiocarbon results, have argued for a "low chronology" that would place these same monumental structures several decades to roughly a century later, depending on the site and chronological model, in the ninth century, attributing them instead to the Omride kings of the northern kingdom of Israel examined in Section 18.6 — a dating that would remove the primary archaeological evidence for a powerful, unified tenth-century state and instead support a more modest, more regionally limited tenth-century polity centered on Jerusalem, if a state-level polity of any real scale existed there at all during this period.

Jerusalem itself presents a particularly difficult case. Excavations in the area identified as the City of David have revealed evidence of occupation and some monumental construction that different excavators have dated variously to the tenth century or to later periods, but the relatively limited scale of excavated tenth-century remains at Jerusalem, compared with the biblical description of Solomon's extensive building program, has led some scholars to argue that Jerusalem in this period was a modest highland town rather than the capital of a large, wealthy, internationally significant kingdom, while others caution that later building activity, continuous habitation, and the practical difficulty of excavating beneath a living, religiously significant city may have destroyed or obscured much of the relevant tenth-century evidence, meaning that absence of extensive remains cannot be treated as conclusive proof of the absence of a substantial tenth-century settlement.

What can be said with reasonable confidence differs somewhat for each of the three traditional figures. Jerusalem became established at some point as a significant highland center associated with a ruling dynasty later called the "House of David," and the existence of that dynasty has meaningful external support: a separate Aramaic inscription fragment from Tel Dan, discussed in Section 18.6, refers to the "House of David" in a securely ninth-century context, making a founding figure named David considerably better attested than Saul or Solomon individually. A further possible reference to the "House of David" has been proposed in a damaged line of the Mesha Stele, also discussed in Section 18.6, but this reading remains a genuinely disputed epigraphic proposal — some specialists argue that recent imaging supports it, while others regard it as still hypothetical given the stone's fractured condition at exactly that point — and should not be treated as independently confirmed. Saul and Solomon, by contrast, rest far more heavily on the biblical narrative itself and on archaeological reconstruction than on any comparable external inscriptional attestation; their historical existence in some form is plausible to most historians, but very little about their reigns can be independently verified. The scale, wealth, territorial extent, and administrative sophistication of any tenth-century Israelite-Judahite state remain genuinely uncertain and actively debated among specialists using the same body of archaeological evidence. This textbook does not resolve that debate, and students should be suspicious of any account, in either direction, that claims greater certainty than the evidence supports.

Historical Debate

The United Monarchy Problem

The question: Did a substantial, centralized "United Monarchy," spanning both the northern and southern highlands under David and Solomon, exist in the tenth century BCE, and if so, how large, wealthy, and administratively developed was it?

Interpretation A — A substantial tenth-century state existed: Some archaeologists and historians argue that monumental architecture at Hazor, Megiddo, and Gezer, the securely ninth-century Tel Dan reference to an established "House of David" (together with the more disputed possible Mesha Stele reading), and the broad coherence of later biblical administrative traditions together support the existence of a real, though perhaps regionally more limited than later tradition suggests, tenth-century kingdom under David and Solomon, with Jerusalem as a functioning political and religious center.
Interpretation B — Tenth-century political organization was limited and localized: Other archaeologists and historians argue that a "low chronology" dating of the relevant monumental strata to the ninth century, combined with the modest scale of excavated tenth-century remains at Jerusalem itself, points instead to a much smaller, more localized tenth-century polity — plausibly a regional chiefdom or early state centered on Jerusalem and Judah, without the extensive, wealthy, internationally significant kingdom described in the biblical narrative, with the "House of David" phrase in external inscriptions referring to a real but comparatively modest founding dynasty rather than evidence for an empire.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the same excavated monumental architecture, the same inscriptional fragments, and the same biblical text, differing chiefly in how they date key archaeological strata and how much historical weight they assign to later biblical elaboration.

Why it matters: This debate is not merely technical. It shapes how historians understand the origins of Israelite and Judahite statehood, the relative historical priority of the northern and southern kingdoms, and how much continuity to assume between the tenth-century political landscape and the better-documented ninth- and eighth-century kingdoms examined later in this chapter.

Student task: Identify what kind of new evidence — archaeological, inscriptional, or otherwise — would most effectively help resolve this debate, and explain why current evidence has proven insufficient to settle it.

Section 18.5

From One Monarchy—or Several Early Polities—to Israel and Judah

Whatever the precise scale of tenth-century political organization in the highlands, the historical record becomes considerably firmer from the ninth century BCE onward, when two distinct kingdoms — a northern kingdom called Israel and a southern kingdom called Judah — are attested independently in inscriptions left by neighboring rulers as well as in the biblical narrative. The biblical account explains this division as a single dramatic rupture following Solomon's death, when the northern tribes rejected the harsh policies of his son Rehoboam and established a separate kingdom under Jeroboam. Historians generally treat this narrative as preserving a genuine memory of political division, while cautioning that the biblical framing — presenting a prior unity that then fractured due to specific royal misjudgment — may itself simplify a more gradual or more regionally contingent process, particularly if, as the debate examined in Section 18.4 suggests, tenth-century centralized unity was itself more limited than later tradition claims.

Whatever the precise mechanism, Israel and Judah should be understood, from the ninth century onward, as two distinct states with substantially different resources, geography, populations, and international relationships, rather than as two symmetrical halves of an original whole. The northern kingdom of Israel controlled a larger, more fertile, and more agriculturally productive territory, encompassing the hill country of Samaria, the fertile Jezreel Valley, and access to major trade routes connecting the coast, the Jordan Valley, and inland Syria; it also bordered directly on Phoenician territory to the northwest and Aramaean Damascus to the northeast, giving it both greater economic opportunity and greater exposure to larger, more powerful neighbors. Judah, by contrast, occupied a smaller, more rugged, and more agriculturally marginal territory centered on the Judean hill country south of Jerusalem, with less direct access to major international trade routes and, for much of its early history, a smaller population and more modest resource base.

These differences in size, wealth, and geographic exposure help explain a pattern that will recur throughout the remainder of this chapter: the northern kingdom of Israel generally appears in the ninth and eighth centuries BCE as the larger, wealthier, more internationally significant, and more politically volatile of the two kingdoms, experiencing several changes of ruling dynasty through usurpation and civil conflict, while Judah, under a more stable and long-lived royal dynasty tracing its legitimacy to David, developed more gradually and remained, for much of this period, a comparatively minor regional power overshadowed by its northern neighbor. Historians studying this period must therefore resist a tendency, encouraged by the biblical text's eventual focus on Jerusalem and Judah as the vehicle for later Israelite and Jewish religious and historical memory, to treat Judah as the "main" kingdom and Israel as a secondary or derivative one. For most of the period covered by this chapter, the reverse was closer to the historical reality.

Section 18.6

The Northern Kingdom of Israel and the Omride State

The most substantial and best-documented early phase of northern Israelite statehood is associated with the Omride dynasty, founded by King Omri in the early ninth century BCE and continued by his son Ahab and subsequent members of the family. The biblical narrative credits Omri with establishing Samaria as his new royal capital, a hill strategically located in the heart of the northern kingdom's territory; archaeology independently demonstrates substantial early ninth-century royal construction there, including a monumental palace complex and fortifications, consistent with Samaria's emergence as the capital of a well-resourced, administratively sophisticated royal court, though the excavated buildings themselves carry no inscription naming their builder, so the identification of this construction specifically with Omri rests on the convergence of archaeological dating with the biblical account rather than on an inscription confirming his name. Further building activity at Samaria and at other sites across the kingdom, including fortifications and water systems, is traditionally or commonly attributed to Ahab's reign, though, as with Omri, the precise attribution of individual structures to a named king depends on dating strata against the biblical chronology rather than on direct inscriptional confirmation.

The Omride kingdom engaged extensively with its neighbors through both warfare and diplomacy. Biblical narrative describes conflict between Israel and Aram-Damascus during this period, and Assyrian royal inscriptions from the mid-ninth century, describing the Battle of Qarqar in 853 BCE, list "Ahab the Israelite" among a coalition of Levantine rulers who combined forces to resist the westward advance of the Neo-Assyrian king Shalmaneser III — an early and historically significant point of contact between Israel and the Mesopotamian imperial power that would, over the following century, come to dominate and eventually destroy the northern kingdom, a process examined fully in Section 18.11 and Chapter 19. This Assyrian inscription, independent of both the Bible and Moabite tradition, provides valuable external confirmation that Israel under Ahab was a militarily significant regional power capable of contributing meaningfully to a multi-state coalition.

The single most important external source for Omride Israel, however, is the Mesha Stele, examined in detail in the Primary Source Window below. The inscription, commissioned by King Mesha of Moab in the mid-ninth century BCE, describes decades of Israelite domination over Moab beginning under Omri, followed by Mesha's successful revolt and recovery of Moabite territory and independence. Read alongside the biblical narrative in the books of Kings, which also describes conflict between Israel and Moab in this period though with differing emphasis and detail, the Mesha Stele demonstrates that Omride Israel possessed sufficient military and administrative capacity to impose tribute and political domination over a neighboring Transjordanian kingdom for a sustained period — a degree of regional power that fits with, and helps to confirm independently, the archaeological picture of a well-resourced, administratively developed ninth-century Israelite state centered on Samaria.

The Omride dynasty ended violently, according to biblical narrative, in a coup led by Jehu, a military commander who overthrew the ruling family in a purge remembered in considerable, dramatic detail in the book of Kings and associated by many scholars with a fragmentary Aramaic inscription from Tel Dan that appears to describe the killing of Israelite and Judahite kings, though the precise relationship between the biblical account and the inscription's fragmentary, disputed text remains a subject of scholarly discussion. Jehu's dynasty subsequently ruled Israel for several generations, during which the kingdom continued to experience alternating periods of strength and vulnerability in its relationships with Aram-Damascus and, increasingly across the eighth century BCE, the expanding Neo-Assyrian Empire, developments that set the stage for the kingdom's eventual destruction examined in Section 18.11.

Primary Source Window

The Mesha Stele, mid-9th century BCE

Creator: Commissioned by King Mesha of Moab · Date: mid-ninth century BCE · Type: Royal monumental victory inscription, carved in the Moabite language and script on a basalt stele, discovered at Dhiban (ancient Dibon) in 1868

Context: The inscription commemorates Mesha's building projects and, at its center, his successful revolt against decades of domination by the northern kingdom of Israel, which the text attributes to policies begun under King Omri. Like all ancient royal monumental inscriptions, it was composed to glorify its royal patron and to credit his achievements to the favor of his national god, Chemosh, rather than to provide a neutral historical account.

Paraphrase: The inscription describes Omri, king of Israel, as having oppressed Moab for many years, attributes this oppression to the anger of the Moabite god Chemosh against his land, and describes Mesha's subsequent military campaigns to recover Moabite territory and towns, rebuild fortifications, and dedicate captured spoils to Chemosh, framing the entire narrative as a demonstration of Chemosh's favor restored to Moab through Mesha's leadership. A damaged portion of the text has been read by a number of scholars as containing a reference to the "House of David," though the reading remains debated due to the stone's fragmentary condition at that point.

Analysis: The Mesha Stele is historically valuable precisely because it is not an Israelite or Judahite source: it is Moabite royal propaganda, composed to celebrate Mesha's own achievements and to credit them to his own national god, with no interest in flattering or fairly representing Israel. Its confirmation of Omride domination over Moab, read alongside the differing account in the biblical books of Kings, allows historians to compare an external and an internal source describing related events, illustrating both genuine points of overlap and the different emphases each text brings to a shared historical situation. As discussed in Section 18.4, its possible reference to a "House of David" is one of the more significant pieces of external evidence bearing on the existence of an early Judahite royal dynasty, though the damaged state of the relevant line requires appropriate caution.

Questions for students:

  1. Why is the Mesha Stele particularly valuable as historical evidence precisely because it was not written to flatter Israel?
  2. How does the inscription's attribution of Moab's earlier subjugation to the anger of the god Chemosh resemble or differ from how the biblical text explains political events through divine causation?
  3. What are the risks of relying on a damaged, partially illegible line of text — such as the disputed "House of David" reference — as historical evidence?
  4. How does comparing the Mesha Stele with the biblical account of Israelite-Moabite conflict illustrate the broader method described in Section 18.2?
Section 18.7

Judah, Jerusalem, and the Growth of a Southern State

Judah's development as a state proceeded more slowly, and for longer remained more modest in scale, than that of its northern neighbor, a pattern consistent with the geographic and resource disparities described in Section 18.5. Archaeological evidence for substantial royal administration, monumental construction, and widespread literacy in Judah becomes considerably more visible from the eighth century BCE onward, a pattern some scholars have linked partly to the disruption and, after 722/721 BCE, destruction of the northern kingdom, examined in Section 18.11, which may have sent population, administrative expertise, and religious traditions southward into Judah, contributing to Jerusalem's growth as a larger and more administratively developed center during the late eighth and seventh centuries BCE.

Jerusalem itself expanded significantly during this period. Archaeological evidence indicates substantial growth of the city beyond its earlier, more limited core, along with major infrastructure projects, most famously the rock-cut Siloam Tunnel, an engineering project that redirected the city's water supply from the Gihon Spring to a pool within the city walls, associated by many scholars with defensive preparations undertaken under King Hezekiah in anticipation of the Assyrian campaign examined in Section 18.11. An inscription discovered within the tunnel itself describes the moment two teams digging from opposite directions met underground — one of the relatively rare surviving examples of a non-royal, technical inscription from ancient Judah, offering a glimpse of engineering achievement and labor organization rather than royal propaganda.

Judah's administrative development in this period is further attested by a distinctive body of evidence: stamped jar handles bearing the Hebrew letters commonly transliterated as "LMLK" (belonging to the king), found by the hundreds at sites across Judah and dated to the late eighth century BCE, generally associated with Hezekiah's reign and interpreted by most scholars as evidence of a centralized royal system for collecting, storing, or distributing agricultural produce, quite possibly connected to preparations for the Assyrian crisis of 701 BCE. Such administrative seal impressions, together with a growing body of inscribed ostraca recording everyday transactions, military correspondence, and administrative instructions from various Judahite sites, provide historians with valuable, contemporary, non-royal evidence for how the Judahite state actually functioned in practice — evidence that complements, and sometimes provides a useful check on, the far more selective and theologically shaped narrative of the biblical text.

By the late eighth century BCE, then, Judah had developed into a genuine, administratively capable state with a functioning bureaucracy, significant urban growth at Jerusalem, and the capacity to undertake major public infrastructure projects — a considerable development from whatever more modest tenth-century polity may have existed in the same region, discussed in Section 18.4. This growth occurred, however, under the shadow of an expanding Neo-Assyrian Empire that had already destroyed Judah's larger and wealthier northern neighbor, a crisis examined fully in Section 18.11.

Section 18.8

Kingship, Administration, Economy, and Social Hierarchy

Both Israelite and Judahite kingship, as it developed from the ninth century BCE onward, combined military leadership, religious patronage, and administrative authority in ways broadly comparable to other Levantine monarchies examined in Chapter 17. Kings maintained royal households, court officials, and, in the case of the larger northern kingdom especially, standing military forces; biblical and, to a more limited degree, inscriptional evidence attests to officials responsible for taxation, corvée labor — the mandatory, unpaid labor service owed to the crown, often for building and infrastructure projects — record-keeping, and the administration of royal estates. Seal impressions bearing the names and titles of officials, recovered archaeologically from both kingdoms, provide independent evidence for the existence of a functioning class of royal administrators and scribes, supporting the biblical narrative's frequent references to court officials, though such evidence rarely allows historians to reconstruct the precise scope of royal bureaucratic authority in detail.

Economically, both kingdoms depended overwhelmingly on agriculture — grain cultivation in valley and plain areas, terraced cultivation of olives and grapes in the hill country, and pastoralism, particularly sheep and goat herding, across marginal and semi-arid zones. Taxation, most plausibly rendered in a combination of agricultural produce, corvée labor, and, for wealthier households, silver or other valuables, supported the royal court, military forces, and building projects; the LMLK jar handles discussed in Section 18.7 are widely interpreted as physical evidence of a centralized royal system for collecting, storing, or distributing agricultural produce in late eighth-century Judah, though the precise administrative function of the system remains debated. Trade, though more modest than that of larger Phoenician or Mesopotamian economies, connected both kingdoms to regional networks: Israel's access to the Jezreel Valley and routes toward the coast and Phoenicia gave it particular commercial advantages, while Judah's position gave it more limited but still significant access to trade routes running through the northern Negev toward Edom, the Red Sea, and Arabian trade networks discussed in Chapter 17.

Social hierarchy in both kingdoms encompassed the royal family and court elite; a landed and administrative class of officials, military commanders, and priests; a broad population of free landholding farmers and herders who formed the demographic and economic backbone of both societies; and, at the bottom of the social hierarchy, various forms of dependent labor and debt servitude. Biblical prophetic literature, particularly texts associated with the eighth-century prophets Amos, Hosea, Isaiah, and Micah, contains extensive and vivid criticism of social inequality, indebtedness, the loss of ancestral landholdings by small farmers to wealthier creditors, and the exploitation of the poor by elites — evidence that, used cautiously as testimony to social conditions rather than literal statistical description, points to real and apparently growing social stratification and economic pressure on smallholding households during the eighth century BCE, a period of substantial economic growth for elites alongside evident hardship for at least some portions of the rural population. Women's roles, only partially visible in a textual record produced overwhelmingly by and about male elites, encompassed household management, textile production, agricultural labor, and religious practice at the household level, discussed further in the Everyday Life feature below; royal women, including queen mothers, appear in the biblical narrative as occasional but significant political actors, particularly in matters of royal succession.

Section 18.9

Yahweh, Temples, Household Religion, and Religious Change

Religious practice in Israel and Judah, as historians now generally reconstruct it, developed gradually and unevenly over the period covered by this chapter, rather than beginning as, or quickly becoming, the fully developed monotheism later associated with post-exilic Judaism and examined further in Chapter 20. The god Yahweh appears to have been worshipped as the principal national deity of both kingdoms from an early period, closely associated with royal legitimacy, military victory, and covenant traditions binding the deity to the ruling dynasty and, in various formulations, to the wider population. Archaeological and inscriptional evidence, however, complicates any assumption that Yahweh was worshipped in isolation from other divine figures throughout this period. Inscriptions from sites including Kuntillet Ajrud in the northern Sinai and Khirbet el-Qom in Judah, dated to the ninth and eighth centuries BCE, contain blessing formulas invoking "Yahweh and his Asherah" — a phrase whose precise theological meaning remains debated among scholars, with some interpreting "Asherah" as a reference to a divine consort figure associated with Yahweh and others interpreting it as a reference to a cultic object or symbol rather than an independent goddess, but which in either interpretation suggests a religious landscape more complex than the strict, exclusive monotheism later biblical tradition would associate with faithful worship.

Most historians of Israelite religion today therefore distinguish several stages or currents rather than assuming a single, unchanging religious system: an earlier and probably widespread pattern of monolatry, meaning the worship of Yahweh as the primary or most important god without necessarily denying the existence or occasional veneration of other deities; a movement toward more exclusive Yahwism, associated particularly with prophetic critique and, according to biblical tradition, periodic royal reform efforts; and, only later, associated by most scholars with developments during and after the Babylonian exile examined in Chapter 20, a fully articulated monotheism denying the existence of other gods altogether. Household religion, evidenced archaeologically by figurines, domestic cultic objects, and small local shrines found across numerous excavated sites in both kingdoms, appears to have coexisted with, and sometimes diverged from, religious practice centered on royal sanctuaries and, eventually, the Jerusalem Temple, suggesting that ordinary religious life at the family and village level did not always mirror the more centralized and doctrinally focused religious ideals promoted by royal and prophetic elites.

The biblical narrative describes periodic royal efforts at religious centralization and reform, most notably under Hezekiah in Judah in the late eighth century and, more elaborately, under his descendant Josiah in the seventh century, examined further in Chapter 20, involving the removal of local high places (elevated open-air or built shrines, called bamot in Hebrew) and the concentration of sacrificial worship at the Jerusalem Temple. Archaeological evidence for the actual scope and success of such reforms remains mixed and debated, with some destruction layers at sites associated with local cultic installations offering possible, though not conclusive, support for centralization efforts, while other evidence suggests continued local and household religious diversity persisting alongside official reform programs. This textbook treats these reform narratives as evidence of a real ideological and political struggle over the proper locus and character of Yahweh worship, rather than as straightforward accounts of religious practice as it was actually experienced by the broader population.

Section 18.10

Israel, Judah, and Their Neighbors

Israel and Judah did not develop in isolation. As Chapter 17 established, the Iron Age Levant was a densely interconnected world of neighboring kingdoms and city-states, and Israel and Judah's political, military, economic, and religious history was shaped continuously by relationships with these neighbors. To the east, across the Jordan River and the Dead Sea, lay the Transjordanian kingdoms of Ammon, Moab, and Edom, each sharing broadly similar Northwest Semitic languages, comparable social and religious structures — including, in Moab's case, the worship of a national god, Chemosh, structurally comparable to Israelite and Judahite worship of Yahweh — and a recurring history of warfare, tribute relationships, and periodic domination in one direction or another, as the Mesha Stele examined in Section 18.6 illustrates vividly for Moab's relationship with Israel.

To the north, Aram-Damascus, one of the principal Aramaean kingdoms discussed in Chapter 17, functioned as a recurring rival, and occasionally an ally, of the northern kingdom of Israel, contesting control of border territory in the Golan and northern Transjordan and periodically threatening or besieging Israelite territory, according to both biblical narrative and, more sparingly, Aramaic and Assyrian inscriptional evidence, including the disputed Tel Dan inscription discussed in Section 18.6. Aram-Damascus and Israel also, at times, cooperated militarily against shared threats, as the joint resistance to Shalmaneser III at the Battle of Qarqar in 853 BCE, discussed in Section 18.6, demonstrates.

To the northwest, the Phoenician cities examined in Chapter 17 maintained commercial and, at times, dynastic relationships with the northern kingdom of Israel in particular; the biblical narrative's account of a marriage alliance between King Ahab and Jezebel, daughter of a Phoenician king, reflects this pattern of diplomatic marriage alliance common among Levantine states of the period, though the biblical text's overwhelmingly hostile portrayal of Jezebel's religious influence should be read as reflecting later theological polemic as much as neutral political history. To the southwest, the Philistine city-states examined in Chapter 17 remained a persistent military and political presence along the coastal plain, contesting border territory with both Israel and Judah across the period covered by this chapter, with the biblical narrative's traditions concerning conflict with the Philistines, associated particularly with the era of Saul and David discussed in Section 18.4, reflecting a genuine and long-running pattern of Israelite-Philistine border conflict even where specific narrative episodes cannot be independently verified.

This dense web of regional relationships — alliance, rivalry, tribute, intermarriage, trade, and periodic warfare among Israel, Judah, Moab, Ammon, Edom, Aram-Damascus, the Phoenician cities, and the Philistine states — should be understood as the normal operating environment of Iron Age Levantine political life, directly comparable to the patterns of shifting alliance and rivalry examined among Mesopotamian city-states in Chapters 9 through 12 and among Bronze Age Levantine and Anatolian states in Chapters 15 and 16. Israel and Judah's history cannot be understood as a self-contained biblical narrative; it must be understood as the history of two among several interacting Levantine kingdoms, whose fates, by the eighth century BCE, would become increasingly bound together by a shared and growing external threat: the expanding Neo-Assyrian Empire, examined in the chapter's final major section.

Section 18.11

Assyrian Pressure: The Fall of Israel and the Survival of Judah

The reign of the Neo-Assyrian king Tiglath-pileser III, beginning in 745 BCE, marked a decisive intensification of Assyrian imperial policy toward the Levant, examined in full institutional and administrative detail in Chapter 19 but whose consequences for Israel and Judah must be traced here from the perspective of the two kingdoms themselves. Forced population deportation was not a new Assyrian practice — Mesopotamian rulers had relocated conquered populations for centuries before Tiglath-pileser III's reign — but he and his successors expanded, systematized, and integrated it into a far more intensive and more consistently applied imperial program than earlier kings had pursued. Rather than relying primarily on the extraction of tribute from nominally independent vassal kingdoms, as earlier Assyrian kings had generally preferred, Tiglath-pileser III and his successors increasingly pursued direct annexation, converting conquered territories into Assyrian provinces governed by Assyrian officials, and employing large-scale, more systematic deportation and resettlement, relocating conquered populations to distant parts of the empire and, in many cases, resettling other deported populations in their place. This policy served multiple, overlapping imperial purposes: breaking local political and religious cohesion and reducing the likelihood of rebellion, but also reallocating labor to underpopulated or strategically important regions, integrating conquered territories more fully into the empire's economic and administrative systems, and repopulating annexed provinces with populations more directly dependent on, and loyal to, Assyrian authority.

The northern kingdom of Israel, weakened by internal political instability — the biblical narrative describes a rapid succession of kings, several removed through assassination and usurpation, during its final decades — and caught geographically between Assyrian expansion and its own more distant Levantine alliances, proved unable to withstand this pressure. Tiglath-pileser III's campaigns in the 730s BCE stripped Israel of substantial territory, and after a final Israelite rebellion against Assyrian authority, the Assyrian king Shalmaneser V besieged Samaria; the city fell in either 722 or 721 BCE, with the conquest completed or consolidated under Shalmaneser V's successor, Sargon II, whose own inscriptions claim credit for the city's capture and for the deportation of a substantial portion of its population, a figure Sargon's inscriptions record in specific numerical terms that most historians treat as a genuine, though not necessarily perfectly precise, indication of large-scale forced population removal. The northern kingdom of Israel, as an independent political entity, ceased to exist; its territory was reorganized into Assyrian provinces. Some portion of its population was deported to other parts of the empire, but a substantial local population also remained in place, and Assyrian policy resettled other deported populations from elsewhere in the empire into parts of the former Israelite territory, producing a more layered demographic outcome than simple wholesale replacement. This resettlement became entangled in later, competing narratives about the origins of the population later known as "Samaritans": the biblical account in 2 Kings offers one, polemical, Judahite explanation of that population's descent from Assyrian-resettled peoples, while the Samaritans' own later tradition claimed descent from northern Israelite tribes that had never been deported. Modern scholarship treats Samaritan ethnogenesis as considerably more complicated than either account alone suggests, a subject examined further in later chapters concerning Second Temple Judaism.

Judah's experience under the same imperial pressure diverged sharply, though not without severe crisis of its own. King Hezekiah, ruling Judah in the final decades of the eighth century BCE, undertook the administrative and infrastructural preparations discussed in Section 18.7, including the Siloam Tunnel and the LMLK administrative system, and, following the death of the Assyrian king Sargon II in 705 BCE, joined a wider regional rebellion against Assyrian authority, a decision that provoked a major Assyrian military response under Sargon's successor, Sennacherib, in 701 BCE. Sennacherib's own royal annals, inscribed on clay prisms recovered by archaeologists, describe a campaign in which Assyrian forces captured numerous Judahite fortified cities — the destruction of one of these, Lachish, is vividly depicted in a famous set of stone relief carvings recovered from Sennacherib's palace at Nineveh, showing siege engines, the deportation of captives, and the city's fall in extensive visual detail — and claim that Sennacherib shut Hezekiah up "like a caged bird" within Jerusalem itself, without claiming to have captured the city. The biblical account in the books of Kings and Isaiah likewise describes a siege of Jerusalem that ultimately failed to take the city, attributing its survival to divine intervention resulting in a sudden Assyrian withdrawal, and also records that Hezekiah paid a substantial tribute of silver and gold to Sennacherib, a detail broadly consistent with Sennacherib's own inscription, which likewise claims a very large tribute payment from Hezekiah as part of the settlement that ended the campaign, though, as the Comparative Sources feature below discusses, the two accounts' reported gold totals agree while their reported silver totals differ substantially.

This convergence between an Assyrian royal inscription and biblical narrative, examined further in the Comparative Sources feature below, represents one of the clearest and most historically valuable points of overlap available to historians of this period: both sources, composed independently and for very different purposes — one to glorify an Assyrian king's military achievement, the other to explain Judah's survival within a framework of divine protection — agree that Jerusalem was besieged, that surrounding Judahite territory and cities were devastated, and that the siege ended without the city's capture, with Judah surviving as a diminished, heavily tribute-paying vassal state rather than being annexed outright as Israel had been. Historians have proposed various explanations for why Sennacherib did not press the siege to Jerusalem's capture — including disease within the Assyrian camp, a mentioned but disputed Egyptian military threat drawing Assyrian attention elsewhere, the acceptance of Hezekiah's substantial tribute as a sufficient resolution, or some combination of practical and strategic considerations — but no single explanation commands full scholarly consensus, and this remains, appropriately, an area where the textbook must acknowledge genuine historical uncertainty rather than offer false confidence.

The consequences of this divergence were profound and long-lasting. Israel disappeared as an independent kingdom in 722/721 BCE, its territory absorbed into the Assyrian provincial system and its elite population dispersed, while Judah, though devastated, stripped of much of its territory outside Jerusalem, and reduced to a small, heavily tribute-paying Assyrian vassal, survived as a functioning, if diminished, Davidic kingdom for well over another century — a survival with enormous long-term historical significance, since it was Judah's continued existence, and the traditions preserved and further developed within it, including much of the material later assembled into the Hebrew Bible, that would carry Israelite and Judahite religious and historical memory forward through the further crises examined in Chapter 20 and into the periods covered by later volumes of this textbook.

Comparative Sources

Sennacherib and the Biblical Accounts of 701 BCE

Sources compared: Sennacherib's royal annals (Assyrian, inscribed on clay prisms, composed shortly after the campaign) and the parallel accounts in 2 Kings 18–19 and Isaiah 36–37 (Hebrew, reaching final form considerably later)

Points of agreement: Both sources describe an Assyrian invasion of Judah in 701 BCE; both describe the capture and devastation of numerous Judahite fortified towns; both describe a siege or blockade of Jerusalem under Hezekiah; both describe the siege ending without the city's capture, with Hezekiah remaining on his throne; and both describe Hezekiah paying a very large tribute to Sennacherib, with both sources agreeing on a figure of thirty talents of gold, although their reported silver totals differ substantially — 2 Kings gives three hundred talents of silver, while Sennacherib's annals claim eight hundred — a discrepancy historians have not fully resolved and that itself illustrates the caution required when using royal figures from either tradition as precise statistics.

Points of divergence: Sennacherib's annals present the campaign as an unqualified triumph, attributing Jerusalem's survival to Hezekiah's submission and tribute payment, and make no mention of any military setback. The biblical narrative, by contrast, frames Jerusalem's survival as a divinely wrought deliverance, attributing the Assyrian withdrawal to an act of God rather than to negotiated submission, and includes an account of a sudden, catastrophic loss of Assyrian troops that Sennacherib's own inscription does not mention. The two accounts also disagree on the precise scale of the silver tribute paid, as noted above.

Analysis: Neither source is a neutral chronicle. Sennacherib's annals are royal propaganda designed to present every campaign as a triumph, structurally unable to admit failure to capture a besieged capital; the biblical narrative is a theological account designed to demonstrate divine protection of Jerusalem and the Davidic dynasty. Read together, however, the two sources allow historians to establish a reasonably confident shared chronology and outcome — invasion, devastation, siege, survival without capture, and large tribute payment — while treating each source's explanation for why the siege ended as reflecting that source's own literary and ideological purposes rather than as an objective causal account.

Questions for students:

  1. Why might two sources composed for opposite political and religious purposes still agree on the basic outcome of the same historical event?
  2. What does Sennacherib's inscription's silence about the fate of the siege suggest about the limits of royal Assyrian annals as historical evidence?
  3. Why should historians be cautious about accepting either source's explanation for why Jerusalem survived, even while accepting the shared narrative outline both sources support?
  4. How does this comparison illustrate the broader method for reconstructing Israelite and Judahite history introduced in Section 18.2?
Section 18.12

What Can We Know about Ancient Israel and Judah?

This chapter closes, deliberately, not with a king but with a question, because the most important lesson it can offer is methodological rather than narrative. Two simple positions on ancient Israel and Judah are equally inadequate to the evidence surveyed across this chapter's twelve sections. The claim that "the Bible is history," understood as meaning that the biblical narrative can be read straightforwardly as an accurate, contemporary factual record of events from the highland settlement of Iron Age I through the Assyrian crisis of 701 BCE, fails to reckon with the theological purposes, later editorial shaping, and centuries-long gap between many described events and the texts' surviving form. The opposite claim, that "the Bible has no historical value," understood as meaning the biblical narrative should be set aside entirely in favor of archaeology and external inscriptions alone, fails to reckon with the genuine historical memory, administrative detail, and points of independent confirmation — the Mesha Stele's account of Omride Moab, the Tel Dan inscription's probable reference to a "House of David," Assyrian inscriptions naming "Ahab the Israelite" and describing Sennacherib's siege of Jerusalem — that repeatedly intersect with, and lend historical credibility to portions of, the biblical narrative.

Across the chapter's central historical questions, the appropriate level of historical confidence has varied considerably, and students should carry this variation forward as a model for evaluating historical claims generally. The existence of a population identified as "Israel" by the end of the thirteenth century BCE, attested by the Merneptah Stele, is about as secure as ancient historical evidence permits. The general pattern of highland settlement growth during Iron Age I is well documented archaeologically, even though the precise process of ethnogenesis by which that population came to identify, and be identified, as Israel remains only partially understood. The scale and character of tenth-century political organization under Saul, David, and Solomon remains genuinely and substantially debated among specialists working with the same archaeological evidence, and this textbook has deliberately declined to resolve that debate on the reader's behalf. From the ninth century onward, the existence of a substantial northern kingdom of Israel, centered at Samaria and ruled at points by the Omride dynasty, is well established through multiple independent lines of evidence, as is the slower but eventually substantial development of Judah centered on Jerusalem. The destruction of Israel in 722/721 BCE and the survival of Judah after the crisis of 701 BCE are both about as well documented as any event in this period of ancient Near Eastern history, supported by convergent Assyrian, biblical, and archaeological evidence.

This variation in confidence is not a weakness of the historical method this chapter has modeled; it is the method working correctly. A history of ancient Israel and Judah written with uniform, undifferentiated confidence across every period — whether uniformly credulous toward the biblical narrative or uniformly skeptical toward it — would be a less honest and less useful history than one that distinguishes, as this chapter has attempted to do, between what the evidence supports with confidence, what it supports tentatively, and what remains genuinely open to legitimate scholarly disagreement. Chapter 19 now turns to the Neo-Assyrian Empire itself, examining from the imperial center the institutions, ideology, and administrative practices that brought about Israel's destruction and Judah's subjugation; Chapter 20 will carry Judah's story forward into the further crises of the seventh and sixth centuries BCE, including the Babylonian conquest, the destruction of Jerusalem, and the exile that would do more than any other single event to shape the later editing and transmission of the very texts this chapter has used, and questioned, as historical evidence.

Multiple Perspectives

Living in Israel and Judah

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns historians and archaeologists have identified across many kinds of evidence. They are not quotations from any single named historical actor.

From the perspective of a highland farming household in Iron Age I: Life centers on terraced fields, cisterns, and a small kin-based compound; political authority beyond the household and clan is distant or absent, and identity is shaped far more by lineage, locality, and household religious practice than by any centralized state.
From the perspective of a royal official at Samaria under the Omride dynasty: Service to the crown means managing taxation, corvée labor, and diplomatic and military obligations toward neighboring states such as Moab and Aram-Damascus, within a kingdom whose wealth and administrative sophistication are visible in its monumental capital and fortified provincial centers.
From the perspective of a Judahite farmer facing debt and dispossession in the eighth century BCE: Economic pressure from drought, taxation, or debt threatens the loss of ancestral land to wealthier creditors, a hardship condemned by contemporary prophetic voices even as it appears to have become an increasingly common feature of rural life during a period of overall royal and elite prosperity.
From the perspective of a Judahite resident of Jerusalem during Sennacherib's siege in 701 BCE: Survival depends on the city's water systems, stored grain, and defensive walls, while beyond the walls, refugees from destroyed towns such as Lachish crowd into the city carrying reports of Assyrian conquest and deportation, and the outcome of the siege remains, in the moment, entirely uncertain.
Continuity and Change Over Time

From Highland Village to Monarchic State

What Changed

  • Political organization shifted from dispersed, kinship-based highland villages to centralized monarchic states with royal courts, standing administration, and fortified capitals
  • Religious practice moved, unevenly and incompletely, from household- and locally-centered worship toward greater royal patronage of centralized sanctuaries and, eventually, calls for exclusive Yahwism
  • Social stratification intensified, with growing evidence by the eighth century BCE of indebtedness and land loss among smallholding farmers alongside elite prosperity
  • Israel's political existence ended entirely in 722/721 BCE, while Judah survived in radically reduced and subordinate form after 701 BCE

What Stayed the Same

  • Agriculture based on terraced grain, olive, and grape cultivation combined with sheep and goat pastoralism remained the economic foundation of both kingdoms throughout the period
  • Worship of Yahweh as the principal national deity persisted across the entire period, even as its exclusivity and centralization evolved
  • Both kingdoms remained embedded in a dense regional network of Levantine neighbors — Moab, Ammon, Edom, Aram-Damascus, Phoenician cities, and Philistine states — throughout their history
  • Jerusalem became the enduring dynastic center of Judah; biblical tradition associates that dynasty with David in the tenth century BCE, while independent evidence for a "House of David" appears securely in the ninth-century inscriptional record and the dynasty's rule continued through and beyond the crisis of 701 BCE

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both the profound transformation from highland village to monarchic state and the underlying continuities in economy, religion, and regional relationships helps students avoid treating Israelite and Judahite history as either a story of steady, uninterrupted progress toward later Judaism or a static backdrop to the biblical narrative alone.

Cause and Effect

Assyrian Pressure and Divergent Fates

Long-term causes

Israel's larger territory, greater wealth, and exposed position bordering Aram-Damascus and the coast made it both a more attractive and a more vulnerable target for Assyrian expansion, while Judah's smaller, more marginal, and more geographically defensible territory reduced its strategic priority.

Short-term causes

Tiglath-pileser III's intensified policy of direct annexation and systematic deportation after 745 BCE, Israel's internal political instability and rebellion against Assyrian authority, and Hezekiah's participation in a regional revolt following Sargon II's death in 705 BCE each triggered direct Assyrian military response.

Effect

Israel was annexed outright, with some of its population deported, a substantial local population remaining in place, and other deported populations resettled into parts of its territory, ending its existence as an independent kingdom in 722/721 BCE, while Judah, despite severe devastation, survived the 701 BCE crisis as a diminished, heavily tributary vassal state under continued Davidic rule.

This textbook's response

This textbook treats Judah's survival as a matter of specific historical contingency—geography, timing, tribute payment, and factors that remain only partially understood—rather than as evidence of inherent religious or political superiority, a caution reinforced by the convergent but differently motivated Assyrian and biblical accounts of 701 BCE.

Historical complexity: Judah's survival in 701 BCE was not guaranteed by anything inherent in its political or religious character; it resulted from a specific and only partially understood combination of geography, imperial strategy, and negotiated submission, a reminder that this textbook's broader caution against deterministic historical explanation, introduced in Chapter 2, applies as much to political and religious outcomes as to environmental ones.

Everyday Life and Social History

Households, Fields, and Faith in Israel and Judah

The great majority of people living in Israel and Judah across the period this chapter examines were not kings, officials, or prophets; they were farming and herding households whose lives are visible to historians chiefly through archaeology and through occasional, indirect glimpses in prophetic and legal texts. Household compounds, often built around the four-room house plan discussed in Section 18.3, sheltered extended families across generations, combining living space, storage for grain and oil, and space for domestic animals within a single walled complex. Women's labor within these households included grinding grain, weaving textiles, managing food storage and preparation, and participating directly in agricultural labor during planting and harvest seasons, alongside the bearing and raising of children within a society where high infant and child mortality made large families an economic as well as a social necessity.

Religious practice at the household level, discussed further in Section 18.9, likely combined reverence for Yahweh with a range of other devotional practices attested by archaeologically recovered figurines and domestic cultic objects, suggesting a lived religious world considerably more varied than the centralized, textually idealized religion promoted by royal and prophetic elites. Military service, owed to the crown particularly in times of external threat such as the Assyrian campaigns discussed in Section 18.11, drew on this same rural population, meaning that the wars and sieges narrated in royal and prophetic texts were experienced directly by ordinary farming households whose fields, flocks, and homes bore the practical costs of warfare, taxation, and, in Israel's case, eventual conquest and displacement.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced the history of ancient Israel and Judah from their roots in the transformed Iron Age southern Levant introduced in Chapter 17 through the crisis brought by Neo-Assyrian imperial expansion at the close of the eighth century BCE, while making the process of historical reconstruction itself a central subject of study. Section 18.1 established the archaeological setting of highland village growth during Iron Age I and introduced the Merneptah Stele as the earliest extrabiblical evidence for a people called Israel. Section 18.2 laid out the four major classes of evidence—biblical text, archaeology, inscription, and regional comparison—that historians must weigh against one another throughout the chapter, a methodology this textbook has applied consistently rather than treating any single source as sufficient on its own.

Section 18.3 examined the highland villages of Iron Age I in greater material detail, cautioning against simple equations between artifacts such as the four-room house or the relative absence of pig bones and fixed ethnic identity, while introducing ethnogenesis as the gradual, only partly documented process by which Israelite identity likely emerged. Section 18.4 confronted the United Monarchy debate directly, presenting the biblical narrative of Saul, David, and Solomon alongside the genuinely contested archaeological evidence bearing on tenth-century state formation, and declining to resolve a debate that remains open among specialists.

Section 18.5 explained the emergence of two distinct kingdoms—a larger, wealthier, more internationally exposed northern kingdom of Israel and a smaller, more gradually developing southern kingdom of Judah—as states shaped by real differences in geography and resources rather than as symmetrical halves of an original whole. Section 18.6 examined the Omride dynasty of ninth-century Israel in detail, using the Mesha Stele and Assyrian inscriptions naming "Ahab the Israelite" to establish, through external and independent evidence, the northern kingdom's substantial regional power. Section 18.7 traced Judah's slower but eventually substantial development, centered on the growth of Jerusalem, the Siloam Tunnel, and the LMLK administrative system associated with Hezekiah's reign.

Section 18.8 examined the shared institutional and social structures of Israelite and Judahite kingship—royal administration, taxation, corvée labor, and a social hierarchy that prophetic literature suggests came under growing economic strain by the eighth century BCE. Section 18.9 traced the historical development of Yahweh worship from an early pattern of monolatry, evidenced partly by inscriptions invoking "Yahweh and his Asherah," toward more exclusive Yahwism and eventual centralization efforts, while cautioning against projecting later, fully developed monotheism backward onto earlier centuries. Section 18.10 situated both kingdoms within their dense network of Levantine neighbors—Moab, Ammon, Edom, Aram-Damascus, Phoenician cities, and Philistine states—reinforcing this chapter's insistence that Israel and Judah's history is inseparable from the wider regional history examined in Chapter 17.

Section 18.11 narrated the chapter's climactic turning point: the reorientation of Assyrian imperial policy under Tiglath-pileser III, the fall of Samaria and destruction of the northern kingdom of Israel in 722/721 BCE, and Judah's survival, though severely devastated, after Sennacherib's siege of Jerusalem in 701 BCE—an episode illuminated by a rare and valuable convergence of Assyrian royal annals, biblical narrative, and archaeological evidence. Section 18.12 closed the chapter not with a narrative conclusion but with a historiographical one, reviewing the varying degrees of historical confidence appropriate to different parts of this chapter's reconstruction and insisting that neither uncritical acceptance nor wholesale rejection of the biblical narrative serves sound historical method.

Throughout, this chapter has emphasized that Israel and Judah were two among several interacting Iron Age Levantine kingdoms, whose political, economic, social, and religious history developed through continuous relationships—of alliance, rivalry, trade, intermarriage, and warfare—with Moabite, Ammonite, Edomite, Aramaean, Phoenician, and Philistine neighbors, and whose fates, by the eighth century BCE, became bound up with the expanding Neo-Assyrian Empire examined fully in Chapter 19.

These foundations are essential to the chapters ahead. Chapter 19 turns to the Neo-Assyrian Empire's internal institutions, ideology, and eventual collapse; Chapter 20 carries Judah's story forward into the further crises of the seventh and sixth centuries BCE, including the Babylonian conquest, the destruction of Jerusalem, and the exile that would profoundly shape the editing and transmission of the biblical texts this chapter has used, and questioned, as historical evidence throughout.

Key Terms

Ethnogenesis
The historical process by which a group comes to understand itself, and to be understood by others, as a distinct people, developing gradually rather than existing as a fixed, timeless identity; discussed in Section 18.3.
Merneptah Stele
An Egyptian royal inscription of c. 1208/1207 BCE containing the earliest surviving extrabiblical reference to a population group called Israel, discussed in Section 18.1.
Four-room house
A rectangular dwelling type, divided into three long parallel spaces with a broad room at the rear, common at highland Iron Age sites and debated as a possible ethnic marker; discussed in Section 18.3.
Maximalism
An interpretive approach treating the biblical narrative as substantially reliable unless directly contradicted by other evidence; discussed in Section 18.2.
Minimalism
An interpretive approach treating the biblical narrative as a much later literary construction requiring independent, non-biblical confirmation before acceptance as historical; discussed in Section 18.2.
High chronology / Low chronology
Competing archaeological dating schemes for key tenth- and ninth-century strata in the southern Levant, whose outcome determines whether major monumental structures are attributed to Solomon or to later, ninth-century Israelite kings; discussed in Sections 18.2 and 18.4.
United Monarchy
The biblical tradition of a single kingdom under Saul, David, and Solomon spanning both northern and southern highlands in the eleventh and tenth centuries BCE, whose historical scale remains contested; discussed in Section 18.4.
Divided Monarchy
The period following the biblical account of the kingdom's division into separate Israelite and Judahite states; discussed in Section 18.5.
Omride dynasty
The ruling family of the northern kingdom of Israel founded by Omri in the early ninth century BCE, associated with Samaria and well attested by independent inscriptional evidence; discussed in Section 18.6.
Mesha Stele
A ninth-century Moabite royal inscription describing revolt against Israelite domination begun under Omri, providing external evidence for Omride power; discussed in the Primary Source Window and Section 18.6.
Tel Dan inscription
A fragmentary Aramaic inscription, possibly referring to the "House of David," associated by many scholars with the events surrounding Jehu's coup; discussed in Section 18.6.
LMLK seal impressions
Stamped jar handles bearing the Hebrew phrase "belonging to the king," found across Judah and associated with a centralized late eighth-century royal administrative system; discussed in Section 18.7.
Siloam Tunnel
A rock-cut water tunnel connecting the Gihon Spring to a pool within Jerusalem's walls, associated with Hezekiah's defensive preparations; discussed in Section 18.7.
Ostracon
An inscribed pottery sherd used for everyday administrative or personal record-keeping in the ancient Near East; discussed in Sections 18.2 and 18.7.
Corvée labor
Mandatory, unpaid labor service owed to the crown, often for building and infrastructure projects; discussed in Section 18.8.
Monolatry
The worship of one god, such as Yahweh, as the primary or most important deity without necessarily denying the existence of other gods; discussed in Section 18.9.
Exclusive Yahwism
A religious stance insisting on the worship of Yahweh alone, distinguished by historians from both earlier monolatry and later fully developed monotheism; discussed in Section 18.9.
High place (bamah)
A local, often elevated, open-air or built shrine used for worship, targeted for removal in biblical accounts of religious centralization; discussed in Section 18.9.
Tribute
Payment, often in silver, gold, or goods, rendered by a subordinate ruler or state to a more powerful power to maintain peace or vassal status; discussed in Sections 18.8 and 18.11.
Vassal state
A nominally autonomous state obligated to pay tribute and follow the political direction of a more powerful overlord state or empire; discussed in Section 18.11.
Deportation
The forced relocation of conquered populations, used systematically by the Neo-Assyrian Empire from the mid-eighth century BCE onward, including after the fall of Samaria; discussed in Section 18.11.
Provincialization
The conversion of a conquered kingdom into a directly administered province of an empire, as occurred to Israel after 722/721 BCE; discussed in Section 18.11.
Lachish reliefs
Stone relief carvings from Sennacherib's palace at Nineveh depicting the siege and capture of the Judahite city of Lachish in 701 BCE; discussed in Section 18.11.
Historicity
The quality of being historically factual or verifiable; a central concern throughout this chapter's treatment of biblical narrative alongside archaeological and inscriptional evidence.
Source and redaction criticism
The scholarly discipline of identifying a text's underlying literary sources and tracing its layers of editorial composition and revision over time, distinct from textual criticism proper (which compares surviving manuscript copies to reconstruct a text's earliest recoverable wording); applied throughout this chapter to the books of Samuel and Kings and discussed in Section 18.2.

Select an inscription (such as the Mesha Stele, the Tel Dan inscription, or Sennacherib's annals), an archaeological site (such as Samaria, Jerusalem's City of David, or Lachish), or a historiographical debate (such as the United Monarchy question or the chronology of tenth-century strata) introduced in this chapter.
  2. Research the evidence in detail. Using library resources, academic encyclopedias, and reliable scholarly sources, gather what is known about your chosen source, site, or debate, including its date, discovery or excavation history, and physical condition.
  3. Research the scholarly debate. Identify how historians and archaeologists have interpreted your chosen evidence differently, and why, noting the specific reasoning behind competing interpretations.
  4. Compare interpretations. Where do scholars agree? Where do they disagree, and what kind of additional evidence might resolve that disagreement?
  5. Write your findings. In a 3–5 page essay (or equivalent multimedia presentation), explain your chosen evidence's significance, present differing scholarly interpretations fairly, and use careful language such as "most historians agree" or "this remains debated" rather than overstating certainty.

Library resources to use: Near Eastern archaeology reference collections, biblical studies encyclopedias, academic journal databases, and the MGU History Library's reference materials on ancient Israel and Judah (see also Appendix A, Glossary of Regional and Historical Terminology).

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has asked students to hold two things in mind at once: a genuine, evidence-based historical narrative of ancient Israel and Judah, and a continuous awareness of how uncertain, contested, and methodologically demanding the reconstruction of that narrative actually is. These two commitments are not in tension; they are the same commitment, applied honestly. A history of Israel and Judah that ignored the genuine convergence of the Mesha Stele, Assyrian royal inscriptions, and biblical narrative would be needlessly skeptical. A history that treated the biblical account of Saul, David, and Solomon as settled fact, or that assumed early Israelite religion was always the strict monotheism of later Judaism, would be needlessly credulous. The historian's task, modeled throughout this chapter, is to hold both risks in view simultaneously.

The chapter's central historical argument — that Israel and Judah developed from shared highland origins into two distinct Levantine kingdoms, embedded in a dense regional network of neighbors and, from the ninth century onward, increasingly overshadowed by Mesopotamian imperial power — connects directly to the larger themes of this textbook's second volume. Just as Chapter 17 showed that Phoenician, Aramaean, and Philistine political communities emerged from the same Late Bronze Age collapse, this chapter has shown that Israel and Judah's history is inseparable from that same regional transformation. And just as later chapters in this volume will examine the Neo-Assyrian Empire's rise and eventual collapse, and the further catastrophe Judah would face at Babylonian hands, this chapter's account of Israel's destruction and Judah's precarious survival establishes the essential background against which those later chapters must be read.

Students who leave this chapter understanding that historical confidence is not uniform — that some claims about ancient Israel and Judah rest on strong, convergent evidence while others remain genuinely and legitimately debated among specialists — carry forward a skill that extends well beyond this particular subject. The same disciplined skepticism, applied to later chapters on Assyrian imperial ideology, Babylonian conquest, Second Temple Judaism, and the long history of Jewish, Christian, and Islamic engagement with this same textual and archaeological record across the remainder of this nine-volume work, will serve students far better than either uncritical acceptance or blanket dismissal of any single tradition's account of its own past.

Chapter 19 now turns to the Neo-Assyrian Empire itself — the imperial system whose westward expansion, briefly glimpsed here through Israelite and Judahite eyes, must now be examined from the perspective of its own institutions, ideology, and ultimate collapse. Chapter 20 will then carry Judah's story forward into the further and, this time, final crisis of the late Iron Age political order: the Babylonian conquest, the destruction of Jerusalem, and the exile whose consequences would shape the future editing and transmission of the very texts this chapter has treated, throughout, as valuable but never simple historical evidence.
