Part VI — The Persian Imperial Revolution
24

Society, Religion, Economy, and Identity in Achaemenid Persia

A History of the Middle East: From Ancient Civilizations to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

Across Persis and eastern Elam, local officials recorded transactions of grain, wine, flour, and livestock disbursed to travelers, officials, craftsmen, and laboring groups moving through the heartland of the Achaemenid Empire, with many of these records eventually gathered and stored at Persepolis itself. These records were never meant for historians. They were working documents, drafted in Elamite on clay tablets, meant to track who had received what, in what quantity, on whose authority, and for what purpose, so that royal resources could be managed and accounted for. Thousands of these tablets survived by accident, buried in the ruins of the buildings where they were ultimately stored, and rediscovered by archaeologists in the twentieth century. They say almost nothing about kings, wars, or conquest. They say a great deal about mothers receiving additional rations after childbirth, about work gangs identified by ethnic origin, about couriers carrying messages across the Royal Road, and about the ordinary business of feeding and paying the people who kept a vast empire running.

Chapter 23 examined how Darius and Xerxes governed an empire that stretched, at its height, from the Aegean coast of Anatolia to the Indus Valley and from the Caucasus to the edges of Nubia: satrapies, taxation, roads, royal ideology, and the administrative languages that carried imperial authority across enormous distances. That chapter necessarily concentrated on institutions and on the kings who built them, because the evidence for early Achaemenid government is dominated by royal inscriptions and monumental architecture designed to project an image of ordered, universal kingship. This chapter turns to a different, harder, and in many ways more rewarding question: what did that governmental system mean for the people who actually lived inside it?

The Achaemenid Empire was not a single, culturally uniform society ruled from Persis. It was a vast assemblage of older societies — Babylonian cities with their own deep urban and religious traditions, Egyptian temple institutions with millennia of continuous administrative practice, Phoenician port cities oriented toward Mediterranean trade, Anatolian dynastic households, Central Asian oasis and pastoral communities, and the Persian and Median populations of the Iranian plateau itself — now bound together by shared institutions of taxation, military service, road-based communication, and royal patronage. Understanding this empire as a society, rather than only as a government, means asking how a Babylonian temple administrator, an Egyptian priest, a Persian nobleman, an Aramaic-speaking scribe, a Bactrian soldier, and a female worker at Persepolis each experienced imperial rule differently, according to their status, occupation, region, language, and sex.

This is also a chapter about evidence, and about what kinds of evidence make different kinds of history possible. Royal inscriptions describe an empire of subject peoples bringing tribute to an ordered, divinely sanctioned king; monumental relief sculpture at Persepolis depicts delegations arriving in orderly procession, each rendered in distinctive dress bearing characteristic gifts. These sources tell historians how the Achaemenid court wished the empire to be seen. Administrative archives — above all the Persepolis Fortification tablets, supplemented by Babylonian, Egyptian, and other regional documentation — tell historians something closer to how the empire's economy and labor force actually functioned, at least within the regions and institutions those archives happened to record. Neither source is complete on its own, and the surviving evidence is itself unevenly distributed: it illuminates the Persepolis region, parts of Babylonia, and parts of Egypt far more brightly than it illuminates Bactria, Arachosia, or the Aegean satrapies.

Religion presents a related evidentiary problem. Darius and Xerxes invoked the god Ahura Mazda repeatedly in their inscriptions, describing him as the god who granted kingship and who opposed disorder and falsehood. Later Zoroastrian tradition, codified centuries after the Achaemenid period in texts whose transmission history remains genuinely uncertain, describes an elaborate theological and ritual system attributed to the prophet Zarathustra. The relationship between the religion invoked by Achaemenid kings and the religion later systematized as Zoroastrianism is a subject of real scholarly disagreement, not a settled fact that this chapter can simply assume. Students should expect this chapter to model careful historical reasoning about incomplete and unevenly distributed evidence, rather than offering confident answers to questions the sources do not clearly settle.

Finally, this chapter asks what it meant to belong to an empire that was politically dominated by Persians without requiring cultural or religious uniformity from its subjects. Local languages persisted. Local religious institutions continued to function, often with royal patronage rather than suppression. Local elites frequently retained real authority, provided they cooperated with imperial demands for taxation, labor, and military support. The empire's own visual and ideological program, especially the processional reliefs at Persepolis examined for their political meaning in Chapter 23, will return in this chapter viewed from a different angle: not as a statement of royal ideology alone, but as evidence for how the empire imagined, organized, and represented the diversity of the peoples who composed it.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why the historical study of Achaemenid society depends heavily on administrative archives rather than royal inscriptions alone, and identify the strengths and limitations of each kind of evidence.
  2. Describe the structure of Persian aristocratic society and its relationship to landholding, military service, and court patronage.
  3. Explain how local elites in Babylonia, Egypt, Anatolia, and other regions retained authority and negotiated their position within the imperial system.
  4. Describe the major forms of agricultural production across the empire's diverse ecological zones and their relationship to taxation and landholding institutions.
  5. Analyze the role of trade, transport infrastructure, and long-distance exchange in connecting the empire's regions economically.
  6. Evaluate the evidence for labor organization at Persepolis, including ration systems, work groups, and differentiated compensation, and explain why simple characterizations of that labor as either universal slavery or universal free wage work are historically inadequate.
  7. Describe the evidence for women's economic roles, compensation, and, in some cases, supervisory authority within the Persepolis administrative system and other regional archives.
  8. Distinguish Achaemenid royal invocations of Ahura Mazda from later codified Zoroastrian tradition, and explain why historians treat the relationship between them as an open scholarly question.
  9. Explain how local religious institutions among Babylonian, Egyptian, Jewish, and other subject populations continued to function under Achaemenid rule.
  10. Analyze the empire's ethnic and linguistic diversity and explain what it meant to belong to the empire without becoming culturally or religiously Persian.
  11. Evaluate competing historiographical interpretations of Achaemenid social and economic organization, including debates over religion and labor.
  12. Articulate why this chapter treats the Achaemenid Empire as an interconnected imperial society rather than as a Persian elite standing above passive subject populations.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. What kinds of historical questions can administrative archives answer that royal inscriptions cannot, and what questions remain closed to both kinds of evidence?
  2. How did Persian aristocratic status combine landholding, military obligation, and proximity to the royal court?
  3. In what ways did local elites in Babylonia, Egypt, and Anatolia continue to exercise authority under Achaemenid rule, and what did the empire expect from them in return?
  4. How did differing regional ecologies shape differing patterns of agricultural production and land tenure across the empire?
  5. What does the Persepolis ration evidence reveal about labor organization, and why do historians resist reducing that evidence to a single labor category such as "slavery" or "free wage labor"?
  6. What can administrative texts reveal about women's economic roles that royal inscriptions and Greek literary sources generally do not?
  7. How confidently can historians identify the religion of Darius and Xerxes with the Zoroastrianism described in later religious texts?
  8. How did the empire's ideology of ordered diversity, expressed in monumental art such as the Persepolis reliefs, relate to the empire's actual administrative treatment of its many peoples?

Historical Context

Chapter 21 traced the emergence of Median and Persian political power on the Iranian plateau; Chapter 22 examined Cyrus the Great's conquests and the ideological foundations of Achaemenid kingship; Chapter 23 examined how Darius I and Xerxes I organized the resulting territories into a functioning imperial government, through satrapies, taxation, a system of roads including the Royal Road, a multilingual administrative apparatus, and a program of royal monumental art centered on Persepolis and other imperial sites. That chapter necessarily viewed the empire from the perspective of the institutions the crown built to govern it.

This chapter shifts the angle of vision from institutions to the people living within them. It draws primarily on three overlapping bodies of evidence introduced more fully in Section 24.2: the Persepolis Fortification archive, documenting the Persepolis regional system principally from 509 to 494 BCE under Darius I, and the Persepolis Treasury archive, extending from 492 to 458 BCE across the late reign of Darius, the reign of Xerxes I, and into the opening years of Artaxerxes I; documentary archives from Babylonia, including temple records, private business archives, and legal texts that continued a much older Mesopotamian scribal tradition under Persian rule; and documentary and archaeological evidence from Egypt, the Levant, and other regions, which is less abundant for this period but still valuable. Because these bodies of evidence are concentrated in particular regions and institutions, this chapter's picture of Achaemenid society is necessarily fuller for Persis, Babylonia, and Egypt than for Bactria, Arachosia, or the Aegean satrapies, a limitation the chapter will note explicitly rather than paper over.

The chapter also builds on Chapter 23's introduction of Achaemenid royal ideology, in which Darius and Xerxes presented themselves as agents of the god Ahura Mazda, chosen to maintain cosmic and political order against falsehood and rebellion. This chapter examines that religious language more closely, alongside the religious lives of the empire's many subject populations, most of whom continued to worship their own gods within their own institutions rather than adopting Persian religious practice.

Section 24.1

From Government to Society: Living inside the Achaemenid Empire

Chapter 23 explained how the Achaemenid Empire was governed: satrapies overseen by governors responsible to the crown, a taxation system that extracted grain, livestock, precious metals, and other goods from every province, a network of roads and waystations that allowed messages and officials to move rapidly across enormous distances, and a royal ideology, expressed above all at Persepolis, that presented the empire as an ordered cosmos held together by the king's relationship with Ahura Mazda. That account, like most surviving evidence for the empire's first decades, was written chiefly from the perspective of the crown and its immediate representatives. It answers the question of how the empire was designed to function. It does not, by itself, answer the question of how the empire's institutions were actually experienced by the millions of people living under them.

This chapter asks that second question directly. It treats the Achaemenid Empire not simply as a Persian ruling elite standing above a mass of undifferentiated subject peoples, but as a socially and economically interconnected imperial world, in which royal institutions intersected with much older regional societies — Babylonian cities with millennia of urban and religious tradition, Egyptian temple institutions with their own continuous administrative practices, Phoenician port cities oriented toward Mediterranean commerce, Anatolian dynastic households, and Iranian and Central Asian pastoral and agricultural communities. Persian political dominance did not require, and did not generally attempt to impose, cultural or religious uniformity on these populations.

Making this shift in perspective requires a shift in evidence. Royal inscriptions and monumental relief sculpture remain valuable, but this chapter relies far more heavily on administrative documents never intended for public display or ideological persuasion: ration lists, travel authorizations, temple accounts, private business contracts, and legal records. These sources were produced for practical bookkeeping purposes by scribes working within specific regional institutions, which means they can reveal economic and social detail that royal inscriptions omit entirely, while also being limited to the particular places, institutions, and moments in which they happened to be written, sealed, stored, and eventually preserved by chance.

Section 24.2

Evidence for Achaemenid Social and Economic History

The single most important body of evidence for this chapter is the Persepolis Fortification archive, tens of thousands of clay tablets and fragments, chiefly written in Elamite using the cuneiform script, recovered from storerooms within the Persepolis fortification wall. These tablets record the disbursement of rations — barley, wine, beer, flour, fruit, and livestock — to workers, travelers, messengers, and officials moving through or stationed within the Persepolis administrative region during roughly the last two decades of the sixth century BCE and the early years of the fifth. A smaller, later body of documents, the Persepolis Treasury archive, records silver payments to some categories of workers at Persepolis from 492 to 458 BCE, spanning the later reign of Darius I, the reign of Xerxes I, and the opening years of Artaxerxes I, complementing the ration-based Fortification texts and showing that ration payment and monetary payment could coexist within the same administrative system.

These archives are extraordinarily rich, but they are also geographically and institutionally narrow: they describe the internal economic administration of the Persepolis region specifically, not the empire as a whole. Extrapolating confidently from Persepolis to Bactria or the Aegean satrapies requires caution, since local economic and administrative arrangements plainly varied by region, as later sections of this chapter will show.

Babylonia supplies a second major body of evidence, continuing a much older Mesopotamian tradition of cuneiform record-keeping. Temple archives document the economic activities of major Babylonian temple institutions, including landholding, labor management, and craft production, while private business archives — most famously those of merchant and entrepreneurial households active across multiple generations — document commercial life, credit, contracts, and property transactions among Babylonian urban elites. Legal texts, including marriage contracts, inheritance documents, and court records, further illuminate family and property arrangements.

Egypt contributes a smaller but valuable body of evidence, including continuing temple records, administrative correspondence, and the Aramaic documents of the Jewish garrison community at Elephantine, discussed further in Sections 24.4 and 24.9. Additional evidence for other regions — Anatolia, the Levant, Arabia, and the Iranian plateau beyond Persis — survives more sparsely, through a combination of local documents, archaeology, and references in royal inscriptions and later Greek literary sources.

Greek authors, particularly Herodotus, provide an important but methodologically complicated source of evidence: an outside perspective, often well informed about specific details but shaped by Greek assumptions, political interests connected to the wars discussed in Chapter 25, and reliance on informants whose own biases and access to information cannot always be assessed. This chapter uses Greek evidence carefully, cross-checking it against archaeological and documentary sources wherever possible rather than treating it as an automatically reliable window into Persian society.

Historical Vocabulary
Administrative Archives

An administrative archive, in the sense used throughout this chapter, is a body of documents produced by a governing institution — a royal storehouse, a temple, a private business household — for its own practical record-keeping purposes, rather than for public display, religious commemoration, or persuasion. Because such documents were not designed to shape how outsiders or later generations understood the institution, historians often treat them as offering more direct, if narrower, evidence of everyday economic and social practice than royal inscriptions or monumental art.

Administrative archives nonetheless have real limitations. They record only what the institution that produced them needed to track, in the geographic and institutional context in which that institution operated. A Persepolis ration tablet can tell historians a great deal about workers passing through the Persepolis region, but it cannot by itself tell historians how comparable workers were organized in Bactria or Lydia, where no equivalent archive has survived. Historians must therefore combine evidence from multiple regional archives, read each within its own institutional context, and avoid extrapolating a single administrative practice onto the entire empire without independent confirmation.

Section 24.3

Social Hierarchy and the Persian Elite

At the apex of Achaemenid society stood the royal family and a relatively small circle of great Persian noble households, many of them connected to the royal line by kinship, marriage, or long service, and several of them later remembered by Greek authors as descendants of the seven noblemen said to have supported Darius I's accession. These households held extensive landed estates, particularly in Persis and Media. Elite landholding took several forms, including inherited property, royal grants, estates redistributed after conquest or confiscation, and, in particular regions, service-linked allotments; some of these arrangements carried military or administrative obligations, but there was no single empire-wide system by which all elite estates were held from the crown in exchange for service. Landholding, military obligation, and court proximity were nonetheless closely intertwined for much of this elite: great noble families supplied cavalry and infantry contingents, administered their own estates through subordinate managers and dependent labor, and competed for influence at court through marriage alliances, gift exchange, and personal access to the king.

Below this highest tier, a broader Persian and Median elite held smaller estates and administrative or military positions throughout the empire, serving as garrison commanders, estate managers, tax officials, and satrapal staff. Persian and Median elites were deliberately settled, in some cases, in provincial regions far from the Iranian plateau, holding land grants that supported military obligations while also embedding Persian authority within distant satrapies — a pattern documented, for example, in parts of Asia Minor and the Levant, where garrison colonies and estate holders of Iranian background appear in later documentary and archaeological evidence.

Court culture bound this elite together across enormous distances. Participation in royal ceremonies, access to royal banquets, receipt of royal gifts such as fine textiles, weapons, or drinking vessels, and inclusion in royal hunting expeditions all served as visible markers of status and favor. Chapter 23 examined how the Persepolis reliefs depicted a hierarchy of court officials, guards, and attendants surrounding the king; this chapter adds that such hierarchies were not merely symbolic but reflected genuine competition among elite households for royal favor, offices, and estates, a competition that at times spilled into succession disputes and factional conflict at court, episodes examined further in Chapter 25.

Section 24.4

Local Elites, Temples, and Regional Societies

Achaemenid rule did not erase the social hierarchies of the societies it conquered. In Babylonia, ancient urban elites, priestly households, and temple institutions continued to hold land, manage labor, and conduct business much as they had under earlier Neo-Babylonian rule, subject now to Persian taxation and ultimate political authority. Major Babylonian temples, such as those of Uruk and Sippar, managed substantial agricultural estates, employed dependent and hired labor, and maintained their own internal bureaucracies, continuing an institutional tradition many centuries older than Persian rule itself. Prominent Babylonian business families, best documented through surviving archives of entrepreneurial and commercial households active across several generations, engaged in lending, tax farming, and commodity trade, sometimes acting as intermediaries between local populations and Persian fiscal demands.

In Egypt, the ancient temple institutions of cities such as Memphis and Thebes likewise continued to function under Persian oversight, retaining land, personnel, and ritual practice, though now subject to Persian satrapal administration and, at times, direct royal intervention in temple affairs. Egyptian priestly elites who cooperated with Persian rule could retain considerable local authority and prestige, a pattern of negotiated collaboration that recurs across many regions of the empire and that this chapter treats as a central feature of Achaemenid provincial governance, rather than as an exception to otherwise uniform Persian control.

The Jewish military-colonist community at Elephantine in southern Egypt offers an unusually well-documented example of a local religious and social community operating within the imperial system. Aramaic documents from this garrison community reveal a functioning local temple, family and property law drawing on the community's own legal traditions, correspondence with religious authorities elsewhere, and direct interaction with Persian provincial officials over matters including the community's own temple's fate amid local tensions with neighboring Egyptian religious institutions. This evidence illustrates both the real autonomy such communities could exercise in religious and civil matters and the ultimate authority Persian officials retained over disputes that reached the level of provincial administration.

In Anatolia, local dynastic families in regions such as Lydia and Caria continued to hold regional authority under Persian satrapal oversight, sometimes intermarrying with Persian elite families and adopting elements of Persian court culture while retaining distinct local identities, artistic traditions, and, in some cases, considerable independence in local administration. Phoenician city-states along the Levantine coast similarly retained their own kings, civic institutions, and maritime commercial networks, valued by the Achaemenid crown for their naval capacity and Mediterranean trading connections examined further in Section 24.6.

The recurring pattern across these regions is negotiation rather than simple domination. Local elites who cooperated with Persian fiscal, military, and administrative demands could generally retain substantial authority, wealth, and institutional continuity; the empire, for its part, generally found it more efficient to govern through existing local hierarchies than to replace them wholesale with Persian administrators unfamiliar with local languages, law, and custom.

Section 24.5

Agriculture, Land, and the Rural Economy

The overwhelming majority of the empire's population depended, directly or indirectly, on agricultural production, and agricultural practice varied enormously across the empire's diverse ecological zones, introduced in geographic terms in Chapter 2 and examined here in economic terms. In Babylonia, intensive irrigation agriculture along the Tigris-Euphrates system, examined in Chapter 2's discussion of Mesopotamian hydrology, supported dense cereal cultivation, date palm orchards, and livestock raising, organized through a combination of temple estates, crown land, and private landholding, with labor supplied by a mixture of hired workers, dependent laborers attached to specific estates, and, in some documented cases, enslaved individuals.

In Egypt, the Nile's predictable flood cycle, examined in Chapter 2, continued to support the temple- and crown-centered agricultural economy long characteristic of the Nile Valley, now integrated into the Persian satrapal taxation system. In Persis and Media, agriculture combined dry-farming of cereals in favorable zones with irrigation, including early forms of the qanat technology discussed in Chapter 2's treatment of the Iranian plateau, alongside significant pastoral activity, particularly the raising of horses and sheep central to both the Persian economy and Persian military power.

Royal and elite estates, held variously through inheritance, royal grant, redistribution after conquest, or service-linked allotment, formed an important category of landholding across many regions, particularly in provincial areas where Persian or Median elites had been settled as noted in Section 24.3. These estates typically combined direct cultivation using dependent labor with rents or produce owed to absentee or resident noble landholders, some of whom, though not all, owed military service and loyalty to the crown as a condition of their tenure. Temple estates, especially prominent in Babylonia and Egypt, represented a second major category, with land, labor, and produce organized through religious institutions that predated Persian rule and that continued to manage substantial agricultural resources under it.

Taxation, examined at the level of imperial policy in Chapter 23, translated at the local level into concrete agricultural obligations: grain deliveries, livestock quotas, labor service, and, in some regions, payments in silver. The precise burden and form of taxation varied by region, reflecting both differing local agricultural capacity and differing administrative arrangements negotiated between the crown and local elites.

Section 24.6

Trade, Transport, Markets, and Imperial Connectivity

Chapter 23 examined the Royal Road and the broader imperial road and courier system chiefly as instruments of government: mechanisms for moving officials, messages, and troops rapidly across the empire. This section asks what that same infrastructure meant economically. Roads, waystations, and the relative security the empire provided across much of its territory lowered the costs and risks of long-distance movement, facilitating trade in commodities such as textiles, metals, timber, wine, and luxury goods between regions that might otherwise have been more thoroughly separated by distance and political fragmentation.

This connectivity should not be mistaken for anything resembling a modern integrated market. Most economic activity across the empire remained local or regional: agricultural produce moved short distances from field to local market or estate storehouse, and most people's economic lives were shaped far more by local landholding and labor arrangements than by long-distance commerce. Long-distance trade was nonetheless significant, particularly for specific high-value commodities and for regions with strong maritime or caravan connections. Phoenician and other eastern Mediterranean port cities, discussed in Section 24.4, maintained extensive maritime trade networks connecting the Levant to Egypt, Cyprus, and the wider Mediterranean world, a topic examined further in Chapter 2's discussion of Mediterranean connectivity and in Chapter 51's treatment of medieval Indian Ocean and Mediterranean trade.

Silver played an increasingly important role in facilitating exchange across the empire, particularly as coined money, first developed in Lydia before the Persian conquest of Anatolia, spread gradually through parts of the empire's western regions, even as many transactions elsewhere continued to be conducted through payment in kind, particularly grain, livestock, and other commodities, as the Persepolis ration evidence in Section 24.7 demonstrates. Merchants, including well-documented Babylonian business households, engaged in lending, currency exchange, and commodity trade, sometimes financing agricultural production or acting as intermediaries in tax collection.

Caravan trade connected the empire's eastern and southern regions to markets further afield: Bactria and Sogdiana linked the empire to Central Asian trade routes that would later form part of wider Eurasian exchange networks, while Arabian and southern routes connected the empire's territories to incense-producing regions of South Arabia, examined further in Chapter 2's discussion of Arabian caravan trade and Chapter 34's treatment of pre-Islamic Arabia.

Section 24.7

Labor and the Persepolis Workforce

The Persepolis Fortification archive provides an unusually detailed window into the organization of labor within the Persepolis administrative region, chiefly through the term commonly rendered kurtash, referring to workers organized into groups that received standardized rations of grain, wine, and other commodities in exchange for labor on royal construction projects, agricultural estates, and other tasks. These ration texts record workers identified by occupation, by ethnic or regional origin — including groups identified as Babylonian, Egyptian, Lydian, Carian, Bactrian, and others — and, importantly, by sex, with distinct ration scales recorded for men, women, and children performing different categories of work.

These records reveal a workforce that was neither uniformly enslaved nor uniformly free in any modern sense. Some kurtash groups originated in territories incorporated into the empire and may have reached Persis through relocation, assignment, recruitment, or other forms of labor mobilization; in most cases, however, the tablets do not establish an individual's precise legal status or freedom of movement clearly enough to classify the entire kurtash population within a single legal category. Other workers, including skilled craftsmen, supervisors, and specialists, received substantially higher rations and appear to have held more favorable and more clearly compensated positions, in some cases alongside payments in silver documented in the later Treasury archive. Family units, including women and children, could travel and receive rations together, and mothers of newborn children are specifically documented receiving additional ration allocations after childbirth, indicating a system detailed enough to track individual biological and family circumstances.

Occupational differentiation was considerable: the archive documents specialists in stone-cutting, metalworking, textile production, and other skilled crafts alongside less-skilled laborers, agricultural workers, and support staff such as cooks and porters. Supervisory positions existed at multiple levels, with some supervisors receiving rations calculated to support the maintenance of their own dependents or subordinate work groups, a system that shaded gradually from ordinary compensation into something closer to administrative office.

This evidence resists simple labels. It does not support the older assumption, once common in popular accounts, that Persepolis was built primarily by enslaved captives coerced through violence; the ration and payment evidence indicates organized, administratively tracked compensation across a range of statuses. Nor does it support an equally simple claim that all Persepolis workers were free wage laborers in a modern sense, since some workers appear to have reached their positions through relocation or mobilization rather than free choice, even though the surviving documents rarely specify their exact legal status. The realistic picture, and the one this chapter adopts, is of a differentiated labor system encompassing multiple statuses — including some degree of coerced or dependent labor, some degree of genuinely compensated skilled work, and a great deal of labor whose precise legal status the surviving documents do not allow historians to specify with confidence.

Section 24.8

Women, Work, Property, and Authority

The Persepolis archives provide unusually direct evidence for women's economic roles, precisely because ration and payment records tracked recipients by sex as a matter of routine bookkeeping. Women appear throughout the Fortification archive as members of labor groups, receiving rations calculated according to the same occupational and skill-based scales applied to men, though frequently at somewhat different rates. Some women held supervisory positions over work groups, in a small number of cases receiving among the highest ration allocations recorded in the archive, comparable to or exceeding those of some male supervisors, indicating that skilled and supervisory status, rather than sex alone, substantially determined a worker's standing within this administrative system, even as sex-based ration differentials for comparable categories of labor also appear in the evidence.

Beyond Persepolis, Babylonian legal and business archives document women's participation in property ownership, inheritance, and commercial transactions, particularly among urban elite families, where marriage contracts specified dowry arrangements, inheritance rights, and, in some cases, provisions protecting a wife's property interests within the marriage. These arrangements varied by region, social class, and specific legal tradition, and this chapter does not treat them as representative of a single empire-wide status for women; a Babylonian urban elite woman's legal and economic position differed considerably from that of a female worker in a Persepolis labor gang, which in turn differed from the position of elite Persian women connected to the royal court and to noble estates.

Elite royal and noble women, fewer in number but sometimes unusually well documented in the archival record — figures such as Irdabama appear prominently in the Fortification tablets managing large workforces and resources — are known to have held substantial property, including entire estates and their revenues, managed on their behalf or, in some cases, directly by them, and are documented traveling with significant retinues and receiving royal provisions. Greek sources, read with the same caution urged in Section 24.2, sometimes describe royal women exercising political influence at court, particularly during succession disputes examined further in Chapter 25, though such accounts must be weighed against the polemical and gendered assumptions often present in Greek literary treatment of the Persian court.

Taken together, the evidence indicates that Achaemenid women's economic and social position cannot be reduced to a single universal status. Class, occupation, region, and family position all shaped a woman's access to property, labor, compensation, and authority, a conclusion consistent with this chapter's broader argument that Achaemenid society was internally differentiated rather than uniform.

Section 24.9

Religion in the Empire: Ahura Mazda, Iranian Traditions, and Local Cults

Royal inscriptions of Darius I and Xerxes I, examined for their political function in Chapter 23, repeatedly invoke Ahura Mazda as the god who granted kingship, who favored order and truth over falsehood and rebellion, and who supported the king's authority against his enemies. This royal religious language was clearly important to Achaemenid kingship as an ideological system: it framed royal authority as divinely sanctioned and framed rebellion against the king as a form of cosmic disorder as well as political defiance.

What this royal invocation of Ahura Mazda meant for the broader religious lives of the empire's populations is a more complicated question. There is no clear evidence that Achaemenid kings sought to impose worship of Ahura Mazda on subject populations, and abundant evidence indicates that many local religious institutions persisted under Persian rule, sometimes receiving royal support, though their fiscal privileges and institutional circumstances could also be altered by imperial policy, local conflict, or the decisions of individual rulers. Babylonian temples continued to maintain their traditional cults of Marduk and other Mesopotamian deities, in some cases receiving royal support for temple maintenance and ritual. Egyptian temple institutions likewise continued to function, though not without interruption: Cambyses is reported in Egyptian evidence to have curtailed certain temple revenues, while Darius I subsequently restored and supported Egyptian priestly institutions and temple building projects, illustrating that imperial policy toward local cults could shift significantly from one reign to the next rather than following a single consistent pattern. The Jewish community at Elephantine, discussed in Section 24.4, maintained its own temple and religious practice, and after that temple's destruction around 410 BCE the community petitioned provincial officials, including Bagohi, the governor of Judah, receiving conditional support for its rebuilding — evidence of provincial-imperial adjudication of local religious disputes rather than of straightforward, unconditional royal protection.

This pattern of tolerance for local cults alongside royal invocation of Ahura Mazda should not be mistaken for either strict religious pluralism as a formal state policy or straightforward indifference on the part of the crown. Achaemenid kings clearly considered religious legitimacy important to their own rule, as the persistent inscriptional language shows, while also finding it administratively practical, and perhaps ideologically consistent with their own claims to maintain a divinely sanctioned order, to allow diverse subject populations to continue their traditional worship rather than provoking religious resistance across an already vast and diverse empire.

Section 24.10

Zoroastrian Traditions and the Problem of Reconstruction

The authoritative outline for this textbook specifically directs attention to Zoroastrian traditions, and this section addresses the historical problem directly rather than assuming an answer. Later Zoroastrian religious tradition attributes its origins to a prophet, Zarathustra (known to Greek authors as Zoroaster), who is said to have received revelation from Ahura Mazda and to have taught a religious and ethical system centered on the struggle between truth and falsehood, order and disorder. The core religious texts associated with this tradition, collectively known as the Avesta, were transmitted orally for many centuries before being committed to writing, in a process whose full details remain uncertain, with the surviving manuscript tradition dating from many centuries after the Achaemenid period itself.

This transmission history creates a genuine evidentiary problem for historians of the Achaemenid period. Scholars disagree substantially, and have disagreed for well over a century, about when Zarathustra himself should be dated, with proposals ranging from a period considerably earlier than the Achaemenid Empire to a period much closer to it, based on linguistic analysis of the earliest Avestan texts, comparison with related Indo-Iranian religious traditions, and other indirect evidence. No contemporary sixth- or fifth-century BCE source explicitly identifies Darius or Xerxes as followers of a religious system called Zoroastrianism, and the fully systematized theology, ritual practice, and priestly institutions described in the later Avestan and Middle Persian textual tradition cannot be assumed to have existed in identical form during the Achaemenid period.

What can be said with reasonable confidence is that Achaemenid royal inscriptions consistently invoke Ahura Mazda as the supreme god associated with royal legitimacy, and that the so-called Daiva inscription of Xerxes I (XPh) states that the king suppressed worship of beings called daiva — a term later Zoroastrian tradition associates with demonic or false deities — in a context suggesting religious reform or assertion of orthodoxy of some kind, though the precise historical circumstances and theological content of this episode remain debated among specialists. Some historians treat this evidence as indicating that Achaemenid kings, at least by the time of Xerxes, subscribed to a recognizably Zoroastrian or proto-Zoroastrian religious outlook. Other historians caution that the daiva inscription may reflect a narrower political or ritual dispute rather than evidence of a fully developed Zoroastrian theology, and that projecting the elaborate doctrinal and ritual system of much later Zoroastrian tradition onto Achaemenid royal religion risks anachronism.

Historical Vocabulary
Ahura Mazda and the Daiva Inscription

Ahura Mazda ("wise lord") is the god invoked as the source of royal legitimacy in Achaemenid royal inscriptions from Darius I onward. The so-called daiva inscription, attributed to Xerxes I, refers to the king's suppression of the worship of beings called daiva at an unnamed location where such worship had previously occurred, and to his establishment of proper worship of Ahura Mazda in its place. Later Zoroastrian tradition uses a cognate term for demonic beings opposed to Ahura Mazda, which has led some historians to read the inscription as evidence of religious reform consistent with Zoroastrian theology. Because the inscription does not explain the specific circumstances, location, or theological reasoning behind this episode in detail, historians continue to debate how much weight it can bear as evidence for the content of Achaemenid royal religion.

Historical Debate

Were the Achaemenids Zoroastrian?

The question: How confidently can historians identify the religion practiced and promoted by Darius I and Xerxes I with the religious system later codified as Zoroastrianism? This is not a debate over whether Ahura Mazda appears in the inscriptions — that much is textually certain — but over how much of the later, fully developed Zoroastrian theological and ritual system can be projected back onto Achaemenid royal religion.

Interpretation A — Achaemenid royal religion should be understood as an early or evolving form of Zoroastrianism: Some historians argue that the consistent invocation of Ahura Mazda, the ethical emphasis on truth versus falsehood pervading royal inscriptions, and the daiva inscription's apparent assertion of religious orthodoxy together indicate that Achaemenid kings, at least from Darius onward, adhered to a recognizably Zoroastrian or proto-Zoroastrian worldview, even if later ritual elaborations developed further after the Achaemenid period.
Interpretation B — The evidence supports only a cautious, limited connection: Other historians argue that royal invocation of Ahura Mazda need not imply adherence to the specific theological and ritual system later described in the Avesta, that the Avestan textual tradition's own dating and transmission history remain too uncertain to serve as a reliable retrospective guide to sixth- and fifth-century BCE belief, and that the daiva inscription is too brief and contextually ambiguous to establish a fully Zoroastrian royal religion with confidence.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the same limited body of royal inscriptions, since no securely dated Achaemenid-period Avestan text survives, and on later Zoroastrian tradition whose relationship to Achaemenid-period belief and practice is precisely what is in dispute.

Why it matters: This debate illustrates a recurring methodological challenge in ancient religious history: distinguishing what a much later, fully developed religious tradition claims about its own origins from what contemporary evidence can actually establish about earlier periods.

Section 24.11

Peoples, Languages, and Imperial Identity

The Achaemenid Empire encompassed an extraordinary range of peoples and languages: Persians and Medes on the Iranian plateau; Elamites, whose administrative language served as the primary medium of the Persepolis archives; Babylonians and other Mesopotamian populations; Egyptians; Jews and other Levantine populations; Phoenicians; Lydians, Carians, and Greeks in Anatolia; Armenians and peoples of the Caucasus; and Bactrians, Sogdians, and other Central Asian and eastern Iranian populations, among many others. This diversity was not incidental to the empire's character; it was a defining feature of Achaemenid rule from its inception under Cyrus, examined in Chapter 22, through its administrative elaboration under Darius and Xerxes, examined in Chapter 23.

Chapter 23 examined the Persepolis Apadana reliefs as an expression of royal ideology, depicting delegations from across the empire bringing distinctive goods and wearing distinctive dress in an ordered procession toward the king. Viewed again from the perspective of this chapter, that same visual program can be read as evidence for how the empire itself understood and represented its own diversity: not as a problem to be eliminated through cultural assimilation, but as a source of imperial grandeur, with each people's distinctiveness deliberately preserved and displayed as testimony to the breadth of the king's dominion.

This ideological celebration of diversity coexisted with genuine linguistic pluralism in daily administrative practice. Aramaic functioned as a widely used administrative lingua franca across much of the western and central empire, useful precisely because it was already established as a commercial and diplomatic language before the Persian conquests and could be read by officials across many different linguistic communities. Elamite served as the primary administrative language of the Persepolis region itself, as the Fortification archive demonstrates, while Old Persian cuneiform, a writing system apparently devised or brought into use for royal inscriptional purposes around the beginning of Darius I's reign — Old Persian itself was already a spoken Iranian language — appeared chiefly in royal inscriptions rather than in day-to-day administration. Babylonian, Egyptian, and other regional languages continued in local use for local administrative, legal, and religious purposes.

What did it mean, then, to belong to this empire without becoming Persian? For most subject populations, political membership in the empire — paying taxes, providing military contingents when required, acknowledging the king's authority — did not require adopting Persian language, religion, or customs. Local elites who cooperated with imperial demands, as Section 24.4 described, could retain their own languages, legal traditions, and religious institutions while still participating meaningfully in imperial military and administrative structures, in some cases rising to significant positions of trust and authority. Persian elite identity itself remained meaningful and, to a considerable degree, exclusive: certain court positions, land grants, and honors appear to have been disproportionately concentrated among Persians and, to a lesser extent, Medes, even as the empire's practical administration depended heavily on non-Persian scribes, officials, soldiers, and local elites throughout its territory.

Section 24.12

An Imperial Society without a Single Imperial Culture

This chapter has argued against two tempting but equally inadequate simplifications. The Achaemenid Empire was not a culturally homogenized Persian society imposed uniformly across a conquered world; the evidence for continuing Babylonian, Egyptian, Jewish, Anatolian, and other local religious, legal, and social institutions makes that view untenable. But the empire was also not merely a loose collection of unrelated regional societies sharing nothing beyond a common overlord; the evidence for shared fiscal obligations, military service, court patronage, road-based mobility, and administratively connected labor and provisioning systems documented in particular regions such as Persis demonstrates that imperial rule created real connections among otherwise diverse societies, even if the full extent of such systems elsewhere in the empire cannot be confirmed from the Persepolis evidence alone.

The reality this chapter has traced lies between these extremes: an imperial society built from overlapping systems of connection — taxation, mobility along imperial roads, administrative and military service, royal patronage, and shared participation in an economy that, while never fully integrated in a modern sense, connected agricultural producers, craftsmen, merchants, and officials across enormous distances — layered over, rather than replacing, the deep and continuing diversity of the region's constituent societies. Local languages persisted. Many local religious institutions persisted as well, sometimes with royal support, though their circumstances could also shift with changing imperial policy. Local elites retained real authority, so long as they met the empire's demands for taxation, labor, and military cooperation. Even within the empire's own administrative heartland at Persepolis, the labor force itself was drawn from across this diverse world, organized by occupation, origin, and sex into a system that this chapter has shown to be far more differentiated and far better documented than the empire's royal inscriptions, taken alone, would ever reveal.

This picture of an interconnected but internally diverse imperial society will matter for the chapters that follow. Chapter 25 turns to the empire's confrontation with the Greek world, a conflict shaped in part by the very structures of provincial administration, local elite cooperation, and military mobilization examined in this chapter. Later chapters, including Chapter 26's treatment of Alexander's conquest, will ask how durable these structures of imperial society proved to be when the empire's political leadership changed dramatically, and how much of the social, economic, and administrative world described in this chapter persisted, in modified form, under the successor states examined in Part VII.

Historical Debate

How Should Historians Characterize Labor at Persepolis?

The question: Was the kurtash workforce documented in the Persepolis archives composed chiefly of enslaved or coerced laborers, chiefly of free wage workers, or some more differentiated combination that resists either label?

Interpretation A — The workforce included substantial coerced and dependent labor: Some historians emphasize evidence that certain worker groups had been relocated from conquered territories and assigned to labor at Persepolis on a long-term basis without clear ability to leave, arguing that regardless of specific administrative terminology, such arrangements involved meaningful unfreedom and should not be described using modern language of voluntary employment.
Interpretation B — The workforce was substantially compensated and administratively organized rather than simply enslaved: Other historians emphasize the detailed, individualized ration and payment system, including differentiated compensation by skill, supervisory responsibility, and family circumstance, arguing that this level of administrative attention to workers' welfare and status is difficult to reconcile with a simple model of undifferentiated slave labor, and that many workers likely held positions closer to organized, if not fully voluntary, employment.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the same Fortification and Treasury tablets, differing chiefly in how much weight to place on evidence of relocation and apparent restriction of movement as against evidence of individualized compensation and occupational differentiation.

Why it matters: This debate cautions against importing modern categories of "free" and "unfree" labor too neatly onto an ancient administrative system that likely encompassed a spectrum of statuses not fully captured by either term.

Multiple Perspectives

Living within the Empire

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns historians have identified across administrative, legal, and archaeological evidence. They are not quotations from any single named historical individual.

From the perspective of a Persian noble estate manager in Persis: Status depends on maintaining the estate's productivity, supplying the military contingents owed to the crown, and cultivating favor at court, since land tenure and family standing ultimately rest on continued royal goodwill rather than on any permanent, unconditional right.
From the perspective of a female work-group supervisor at Persepolis: Authority within the ration system depends on demonstrated skill and administrative responsibility for a work group's output, reflected in a higher personal ration allocation, even as most women in comparable but less skilled positions receive rations calculated at a lower rate than men performing similar labor.
From the perspective of a Babylonian temple administrator: Daily responsibilities continue much as they did under earlier Babylonian kings — managing temple land, labor, and produce — but now within a fiscal system that answers ultimately to Persian satrapal authority and its demands for tribute.
From the perspective of an Aramaic-speaking imperial scribe: Professional value rests on literacy in a language understood across many of the empire's provinces, allowing correspondence and administration to move between officials who might otherwise share no common tongue.
From the perspective of a member of the Jewish garrison community at Elephantine: Religious and communal life continues around the community's own temple and legal customs, but the community's security ultimately depends on maintaining good relations with both local Egyptian neighbors and distant Persian provincial authority.
Continuity and Change Over Time

Local Society under Persian Rule

What Changed

  • Ultimate political authority and the direction of tribute shifted to the Persian crown and its satrapal representatives
  • Persian and Median elites acquired estates and administrative positions in many provincial regions, sometimes intermarrying with local elites
  • A new administrative and economic apparatus, documented at Persepolis, organized labor and resources across the Persepolis administrative region within the wider imperial system
  • Aramaic spread further as an administrative language across much of the western and central empire

What Stayed the Same

  • Babylonian and Egyptian temple institutions generally persisted, retaining land, personnel, and ritual practice, though their fiscal privileges could shift with changing imperial policy from one reign to the next
  • Local legal traditions, including Babylonian contract and inheritance law, continued to govern much of everyday civil life
  • Local elites in Babylonia, Egypt, Anatolia, and the Levant generally retained authority and property, provided they met imperial demands
  • Most agricultural and craft production continued to be organized locally, using techniques and institutions that predated Persian rule

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both change and continuity helps students avoid treating Achaemenid rule either as a wholesale cultural transformation of the conquered world or as a purely superficial change of rulers that left underlying society untouched.

Cause and Effect

Taxation, Land, and Social Consequence

Long-term causes

Persian conquest incorporated regions with vastly different pre-existing agricultural, religious, and legal institutions into a single fiscal and military system requiring regular tribute and service.

Immediate mechanism

Elite landholding included inherited property alongside land distributed or confirmed by the crown through grants, post-conquest redistribution, and service-linked allotments; some holdings carried military obligations, while local elites were also confirmed or co-opted in return for tribute and cooperation.

Effect

A layered landholding and social system emerged, combining royal and elite estates, temple estates, and local peasant and dependent labor, all ultimately answerable to imperial fiscal demands, while local religious and legal institutions continued largely intact.

This textbook's response

This chapter treats taxation and landholding as central mechanisms connecting imperial government to local society, while insisting that their specific form and burden varied significantly by region rather than following a single uniform imperial template.

Historical complexity: The empire's fiscal demands interacted differently with Babylonian temple economies, Egyptian priestly institutions, Persian estate systems, and Anatolian dynastic households, producing regionally distinct outcomes even under a single overarching imperial system.

Everyday Life and Social History

Ordinary Lives within an Extraordinary Empire

For most inhabitants of the Achaemenid Empire, daily life was shaped far more directly by local landholding, labor obligations, family structure, and religious practice than by the distant presence of the Persian crown. A Babylonian farming family's year followed the irrigation and harvest rhythms discussed in Chapter 2, now overlaid with tax obligations owed to temple or crown; an Egyptian temple worker's daily routine continued long-established patterns of ritual service and agricultural administration; a worker at Persepolis, whether skilled craftsman or unskilled laborer, experienced the empire chiefly through the ration system that fed, clothed, and, in a real sense, governed daily existence at the site of the crown's most ambitious building projects.

Because most surviving evidence was produced by administrative and religious elites, or, in the case of the Persepolis archives, by an accounting bureaucracy concerned with tracking resources rather than describing lived experience, historians must read this evidence carefully for indirect testimony about the daily lives of the laborers, farmers, soldiers, and family members who composed the overwhelming majority of the empire's population. Archaeology, comparative study of similar agrarian and administrative systems in other periods, and careful attention to the specific, individualized details that do survive — a ration adjusted for childbirth, a work group identified by distant regional origin, a supervisor's elevated allocation — allow historians to reconstruct fragments of this largely undocumented everyday world.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has shifted the study of the Achaemenid Empire from the institutions of government examined in Chapter 23 to the society those institutions governed. Section 24.1 established that the empire should be understood as an interconnected imperial world rather than as a Persian elite standing above passive subject peoples, and Section 24.2 introduced the administrative archives — above all the Persepolis Fortification and Treasury tablets, supplemented by Babylonian, Egyptian, and other regional documentation — that make this social history possible, while noting the geographic and institutional limits of that evidence.

Sections 24.3 and 24.4 examined social hierarchy from two directions: the Persian aristocracy, whose status combined landholding, military obligation, and court patronage, and the local elites of Babylonia, Egypt, Anatolia, and the Levant, who generally retained substantial authority, property, and institutional continuity so long as they cooperated with imperial fiscal and military demands. Sections 24.5 and 24.6 turned to the empire's economic foundations, examining regionally diverse agricultural systems shaped by the ecological zones introduced in Chapter 2, and the trade, transport, and monetary systems that connected the empire's regions without producing anything resembling a modern integrated economy.

Section 24.7 examined the Persepolis labor force in detail, showing through the ration evidence that the kurtash workforce encompassed multiple statuses — including elements of coerced and dependent labor alongside genuinely differentiated, skill-based compensation — that resist simple characterization as either uniform slavery or uniform free wage labor. Section 24.8 extended this evidence to women's economic roles, showing that women's status varied considerably by class, occupation, and region rather than following a single universal pattern.

Sections 24.9 and 24.10 addressed religion, distinguishing the royal invocation of Ahura Mazda documented in Achaemenid inscriptions from the fully developed Zoroastrian theological and ritual system described in much later Avestan tradition, and presenting the genuine scholarly debate over how confidently the two can be identified with one another. These sections also showed that local religious institutions among Babylonian, Egyptian, Jewish, and other populations often continued to function under Persian rule, sometimes receiving imperial or provincial support but also remaining subject to intervention, changing fiscal policy, and local conflict.

Section 24.11 examined the empire's ethnic and linguistic diversity, arguing that political membership in the empire did not require cultural or religious assimilation to Persian norms, even as Persian elite identity itself remained meaningful and, in important respects, exclusive. Section 24.12 synthesized these findings, arguing that the Achaemenid Empire is best understood as an imperial society built from overlapping systems of connection — taxation, mobility, service, and patronage — layered over persistent regional diversity in language, religion, law, and social structure, rather than as either a homogenized Persian society or a loose collection of unconnected regional worlds.

This chapter's recurring methodological lesson — that administrative archives, read carefully and with attention to their institutional and geographic limits, can recover dimensions of ancient social life that royal inscriptions and later literary tradition cannot — will recur throughout this textbook's treatment of subsequent empires and states, including the Hellenistic kingdoms examined in Part VII and the Sasanian Empire examined in Part VIII.

Chapter 25 now turns from the internal society of the empire to its external confrontation with the Greek world, a conflict that drew directly on the provincial administrative structures, military obligations, and local elite relationships examined in this chapter, and that would ultimately expose both the strengths and the vulnerabilities of the imperial system this chapter has described.

King under whom the Persepolis Fortification archive and much of the administrative system discussed in this chapter took shape, discussed throughout.
Xerxes I
King associated with the Treasury archive and the disputed daiva inscription central to Section 24.10.
Persepolis
Administrative and ceremonial center providing the chapter's central body of evidence, discussed throughout.
Babylon and Babylonian Temples
Centers of continuing local elite and religious institutional life under Persian rule, discussed in Section 24.4.
Elephantine
Site of a documented Jewish garrison community illustrating local religious autonomy, discussed in Sections 24.4 and 24.9.
Sardis
Major Anatolian city and terminus of the Royal Road, connected to Lydian dynastic and commercial life, discussed in Sections 24.4 and 24.6.
Ahura Mazda
Central deity of Achaemenid royal religious ideology, discussed in Sections 24.9 and 24.10.
Zarathustra
Traditional founder of Zoroastrianism, whose historical dating and relationship to Achaemenid religion remain disputed, discussed in Section 24.10.

Investigating Society and Evidence in the Achaemenid Empire

This project asks you to investigate one body of evidence or one social group discussed in this chapter in greater depth, comparing scholarly interpretations.

  1. Choose a topic. Select an archive (such as the Persepolis Fortification tablets or the Elephantine papyri) or a social group (such as Persian elites, Babylonian temple administrators, or Persepolis laborers).

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has asked students to look past the monumental image of Achaemenid kingship examined in Chapter 23 and into the working economic and social life of the empire it governed. The habits of mind it has developed — distinguishing ideological sources from administrative sources, resisting simple labels for complex labor systems, refusing to project later religious tradition uncritically onto earlier periods, and recognizing that imperial diversity was managed through negotiation rather than erasure — are methods this textbook will apply repeatedly to later empires, including the Seleucid, Parthian, Sasanian, and Islamic imperial systems examined in later volumes.

The Achaemenid Empire's combination of political unity and social diversity, of centralized ideology and decentralized local authority, of documented labor systems and genuinely uncertain religious history, exemplifies a pattern this textbook will encounter again and again: large multiethnic empires governing through negotiated cooperation with local elites rather than through cultural uniformity, and leaving behind evidentiary records that are rich in some places and silent in others.

Students who understand that Persepolis is not the whole empire, that royal inscriptions are not the whole of Achaemenid religion, and that a labor system can resist simple modern categories are well prepared to read the remainder of this textbook's treatment of ancient and later Middle Eastern societies with the same careful attention to evidence and its limits.

Chapter 25 turns next to the empire's confrontation with the Greek world and its eventual conquest by Alexander of Macedon, developments that will test the durability of the administrative, social, and economic structures this chapter has described.
