Chapter Opening

Military victory created an opening; durable government required fiscal institutions, provincial negotiation, religious legitimacy, and the labor of a wider society.

In 1250, as a French crusading army retreated through the Nile Delta, the political order of Egypt was changing from within. The soldiers who helped defeat the invaders had been acquired as enslaved recruits, educated and trained within royal military households, and subsequently freed into service. Their patrons had depended on them to defend Ayyubid government; they now claimed the power to decide who would rule. The death of al-Salih Ayyub, the murder of his successor Turanshah, and the brief sovereignty of Shajar al-Durr opened a struggle in which familiar forms of dynastic legitimacy could no longer determine outcomes alone.

Ten years later a Mamluk army led by Qutuz and Baybars defeated a Mongol force at Ain Jalut. Within another generation the sultans had expanded their control in Syria and destroyed several of the remaining Latin Christian strongholds. These achievements are prominent in later histories, but they do not by themselves explain how the government endured until 1517. Battlefield triumph could remove a threat; it could not maintain irrigation, collect revenue, train new soldiers, provide law, secure commercial routes, administer a province, or sustain the institutions whose buildings still shape the streets of Cairo and Damascus.

The state that emerged in Egypt therefore requires study as an institutional system. Military households formed its ruling nucleus. Agricultural production on the Nile and in Syrian districts supplied the resources with which soldiers were maintained. Civilian financiers, scribes, governors, judges, and other officials turned claims to authority into daily administrative work. Religious scholars, mosques, schools, charitable endowments, and the Abbasid caliphs maintained forms of legitimacy that differed from command on the battlefield. Commercial networks linked the Mediterranean to the Red Sea, the Indian Ocean, and regions beyond the sultanate’s jurisdiction.

Mamluk political culture was distinctive but never socially self-contained. The majority of inhabitants were not mamluks. Cultivators, artisans, merchants, scholars, women managing family assets, Coptic and Jewish communities, religious specialists, domestic servants, and enslaved laborers lived within structures that military chroniclers rarely described with equal care. Their work supported the ruling order; their experiences of it were neither uniform nor necessarily favorable. New buildings and thriving commerce could coexist with harsh revenue collection, persecution, political violence, and household insecurity.

The history also spans different kinds of time. In the thirteenth century rulers had to establish their authority amid Mongol warfare and the last Crusader states. During the fourteenth century powerful dynasties and commanders attempted to allocate revenues and govern a growing institutional establishment, while the Black Death and subsequent outbreaks transformed population and production. During the fifteenth century Circassian-dominated households preserved the military system, sultans intervened in commerce, Cairo sustained major artistic production, and international rivalries presented new strategic choices.

Chapter 65 therefore asks how a ruling elite recruited through military slavery constructed and maintained a major Egyptian-Syrian state for more than two and a half centuries. Its answer is neither that conquest alone created durable government nor that institutional failures made the Ottoman victory inevitable. By tracing warfare, administration, society, ecology, religion, trade, scholarship, and architecture together, this chapter treats the Mamluk Sultanate as a substantial historical civilization and political system in its own right. The later Ottoman conquest belongs primarily to Chapter 71; the subject here is the state that the Ottomans encountered.

Historical Context

The Mamluk state emerged from the Ayyubid politics developed in Chapter 61. Ayyubid rulers had relied on purchased military retainers, while their disagreements divided Egypt and Syria among related princes. Chapter 62 followed the later Crusades and the increasingly coastal character of mainland Frankish lordships. Chapter 63 explained Mongol expansion and the destruction of Abbasid government in Baghdad; Chapter 64 examined the Ilkhanate in Iran, whose rulers became principal northern rivals of the Mamluks. Those histories explain the conditions of Mamluk state formation without making the Mamluks merely the successor to any one earlier power.

The chapter is organized around three recurring problems. First, a political order rooted in military households had to preserve continuity despite unstable succession. Second, its power in Egypt and Syria depended on agriculture, provincial negotiation, and trade. Third, religious legitimacy and cultural patronage gave military government a social life extending far beyond the palace. The sections move from the mid-thirteenth century through the Ottoman conquest of 1516–1517, but earlier contexts and subsequent legacies are included only to the extent needed to explain the Mamluk period.

Division 1 — The Origins of Mamluk Power and the Defense of Egypt and Syria
Section 65.1

The Ayyubid Crisis and the Mamluk Seizure of Power, 1249–1250

The Mamluk Sultanate did not appear abruptly from an army that conquered an existing government from outside. Its leaders emerged from the military and political institutions of the Ayyubid dynasty, which had ruled Egypt since Saladin ended Fatimid government in 1171. The Ayyubid political world consisted of related princes who governed Egypt and parts of Syria through negotiations, conflict, marriage, and shifting military alliances. They depended on military retainers, including purchased soldiers known as mamluks, alongside free soldiers and other armed groups. The Egyptian ruler al-Salih Ayyub (r. 1240–1249) assembled an especially influential corps of mamluks, many stationed on or near the Nile island of Rawda. Their association with the river, often called al-bahr, contributed to the name Bahriyya. Loyalty to the sultan who purchased and trained them gave this corps a collective interest that could outlast its patron.

The Seventh Crusade brought the tensions in Ayyubid rule into an emergency. King Louis IX of France landed in Egypt in 1249 and seized Damietta, hoping that control of the Nile Valley could force wider political concessions in the eastern Mediterranean. Al-Salih Ayyub was seriously ill and died later that year as the threat advanced. His widow, Shajar al-Durr, and senior officials concealed his death temporarily to preserve military coherence while his heir Turanshah was summoned. The secrecy itself demonstrates the political stakes of succession: a divided command during the invasion could have allowed the Crusader army to gain the initiative. Egyptian defenses, including the mamluks, halted the invaders at al-Mansura; the French army's subsequent retreat ended in defeat at Fariskur in April 1250, and Louis was captured. Victory did not settle who controlled Egypt.

Turanshah returned to claim the throne but alienated important commanders associated with his father's household. In May 1250 he was killed by mamluk conspirators. This act removed the immediate Ayyubid sovereign in Egypt, but it did not automatically make the killers acceptable rulers in the eyes of Ayyubid princes in Syria, religious authorities, or civilians. Shajar al-Durr was proclaimed sovereign and had her title asserted through official political forms, including coinage. Her brief rule raised unusually visible arguments about gender, legitimacy, and the religious-political order. Reports of Abbasid opposition must be read in the context of later chronicles rather than treated as direct transcripts of an unproblematic consensus. The Mamluk leaders nevertheless needed a durable answer to challenges from Syria and Baghdad.

Shajar al-Durr married the commander Aybak, who became sultan, while an Ayyubid child claimant was briefly employed to give continuity to the new regime. Aybak had to balance rival mamluk factions, provincial officials, existing Ayyubid supporters, and the large military households whose officers could make or unmake rulers. Some Bahri commanders, including Baybars, left Egypt during factional struggles and found service with Syrian rulers. Egypt therefore spent the first decade of Mamluk rule defending its independence against fellow Muslim powers as well as preparing for threats from abroad. Shajar al-Durr retained influence behind the throne, but the political relationship deteriorated. Aybak's killing in 1257 and her own death shortly afterward reflected the dangerous mixture of marriage, elite factionalism, and sovereign power.

Aybak's son al-Mansur Ali briefly held the throne while experienced commanders governed, before Qutuz seized effective sovereign authority in 1259. The rapid changes of ruler should not be mistaken for the disappearance of the state. Chanceries still issued orders, taxpayers supplied revenue, religious courts functioned, and soldiers expected payment. The central problem was who could bring these existing institutions together under a recognized command. The Mongol invasion of Syria in 1259–1260 made that question urgent, because a fragmented military regime could not easily defend the routes into Egypt. Later Mamluk chroniclers often interpreted the events of 1250 as the beginning of a heroic new age. For contemporaries, however, the result was uncertain: soldiers created a sovereign order, but institutions, allies, and public legitimacy still had to be won.

Section 65.2

Military Slavery, Recruitment, Training, and the Mamluk Institution

The term mamluk derives from an Arabic word meaning someone who is owned. In the political-military setting of medieval Egypt and Syria it designated men acquired through enslavement, trained for military service, and generally manumitted before entering the full ranks of their patrons' military households. This history cannot be reduced to the claim that the mamluks were simply professional soldiers who rose by merit. Their recruitment depended on human trafficking, forced movement, and purchase, frequently through merchant networks that brought young people from the Qipchaq steppe, the Black Sea region, the Caucasus, and other lands. The market was shaped by wars, intermediaries, transport costs, and diplomatic access to regions from which captives could be obtained. Origins and recruitment patterns changed over time; the early prominence of Turkic speakers did not mean every soldier was Turkic, and Circassian recruits became particularly influential in later centuries.

Boys and young men were typically placed in a ruler's or amir's household, where they underwent education and drilling. The course of instruction could include Arabic, Islamic practice, riding, archery, arms handling, tactics, and the disciplined obligations of service. Conversion to Islam was expected when recruits were not already Muslims, but historians should distinguish an institutional expectation from certainty about each individual's earlier religion. Training did not erase coercion, and accounts written by later officials may idealize the orderliness of barracks life. The process did create shared experiences, however, and successive cohorts acquired bonds with a purchaser or patron and with fellow trainees. Manumission altered legal status and made elite military careers possible, but the continuing prestige of an owner's household remained a political fact long afterward.

Personal ties structured the institution. A soldier might describe himself by an identifying association with the man who owned and trained him. Fellow recruits under the same patron formed relationships referred to as khushdashiyya, a solidarity that could provide protection and advancement in a palace divided among competing households. An officer who became sultan acquired the resources to buy and train new mamluks, who often became his most dependable supporters because their fortunes depended on his favor. Older or rival households continued to possess their own commanders, revenue claims, and loyalties. The result was a governing elite whose cohesion derived in part from institutional repetition, yet whose rival factions could turn violently against each other. Promotion involved ability and patronage, not simply a neutral examination of military talent.

The distinction between purchased military recruits and the children of existing mamluks is fundamental. Sons born to manumitted soldiers, known collectively as awlad al-nas, were freeborn and did not automatically inherit their fathers' status as royal mamluks. Some pursued military or administrative careers, while others became scholars, landholders, or members of the civilian elite. This distinction sometimes complicated hereditary succession, because a ruler's son might lack the household network of a rival commander who had risen through the recruitment system. Yet hereditary dynasties did occur, most notably the line associated with Qalawun. Institutional norms and dynastic claims coexisted rather than operating as exclusive alternatives. Political succession could thus produce child sultans dominated by amirs and powerful sultans who attempted to subordinate amirs to their own households.

Military slavery also formed part of a wider world of enslavement, but the categories must not be collapsed. Women and men were enslaved for domestic, agricultural, courtly, and other forms of labor, and their experiences generally did not resemble the exceptional military careers available to some mamluk recruits. Elite power did not retroactively justify the coercion by which those recruits entered service. The name Mamluk Sultanate refers to a ruling political formation; it does not imply that all inhabitants were enslaved or that slavery alone explains the functioning of society. Mamluks could become patrons of mosques, scholarly institutions, hospitals, and markets while occupying a privileged position above the majority of taxpayers. Understanding both the coercive origins and the institutional achievements of the military elite is necessary to explain the state without romanticizing or flattening it.

Historical Vocabulary: Mamluk, Manumission, and Khushdashiyya

Mamluk refers to a person enslaved and acquired for service; in this context, a trained elite recruit often manumitted before full military office. Manumission is release from legal enslavement and did not erase the patron-client relationship. Khushdashiyya denotes solidarity among soldiers linked through common training or patronage. The three terms identify different legal and political relationships and should not be substituted for one another.

Primary Source Window

Al-Maqrizi on the training of mamluks

Genre: Cairo topographical and historical description; author: Taqi al-Din al-Maqrizi (1364–1442), writing much later than the earliest reigns; text: al-Khitat, passage on the Citadel barracks.

Editorial paraphrase—not a verified verbatim quotation: The historian describes royal attention to the food, instruction, and discipline of enslaved military recruits. Compare its praise of order with the rival-household politics documented elsewhere.

Section 65.3

The Battle of Ain Jalut and the Defense against the Mongols, 1260

By 1260 the Mongol armies had transformed the political map east of Egypt. Baghdad fell in 1258, and Hülegü's forces moved across northern Syria, taking Aleppo and entering Damascus. For the newly founded Mamluk government, the danger was immediate: if the Mongol army mastered Syria, its commanders might threaten Palestine and the approaches to the Nile Delta. The loss of Syria would also deprive Egypt of regional allies, commercial connections, and much of the political prestige that had attached to Ayyubid rulers. The question facing Sultan Qutuz was whether to seek submission, hold Egypt defensively, or take the initiative in the Levant. The Mamluk decision to campaign northward was a calculated political and military choice, not proof that a successful outcome was foreordained.

Hülegü withdrew a large portion of his force eastward after the death of the Great Khan Möngke in 1259. Scholarship also emphasizes the logistical difficulty of supplying mounted armies, the need for pasture, and other strategic commitments, so no single simple explanation should substitute for these intersecting pressures. Hülegü left a substantial but reduced command under Kitbuqa. The Mamluks took advantage of this change. Qutuz and Baybars organized an expedition across southern Palestine and operated amid the complexities of Crusader coastal territories, whose leaders did not act as one unified Christian front. Scouting, intelligence, access to water, and the selection of a battlefield mattered in a region where armies depended on mobile horses and controlled routes.

On 3 September 1260, the opposing forces fought at Ain Jalut in the vicinity of the Jezreel Valley. Sources describe fighting between mounted forces, the use of feigned retreat in Mamluk tactical tradition, and a decisive defeat of Kitbuqa's army; details vary by author and should not be reconstructed as a cinematic sequence without qualification. Kitbuqa was killed, and the Mongol position in Syria collapsed. A victory against an army representing the conquerors of Baghdad carried immense symbolic force. It challenged the aura of Mongol invincibility and supplied Qutuz with an argument for commanding the region's defense. For people in Syria, the immediate issue was whether the Mamluks could bring order after a rapid succession of military occupations and local political realignments.

Military victory was followed by a political struggle among the victors. Qutuz was assassinated shortly afterward and Baybars became sultan. The sources differ on the personal motivations and degree of responsibility of individuals involved in that killing. The transition shows that the commander who defeated an external enemy still had to survive internal rivalries. Baybars soon converted the prestige of the campaign into an enduring governing program, but even he could not eliminate the dangers of competing military households. The memory of Ain Jalut would be shaped by sultanic chronicles as well as by later religious and national narratives. A scholarly textbook should describe those layers without projecting modern states or national identities into a thirteenth-century army.

Most importantly, Ain Jalut did not permanently eliminate the Mongol threat or establish a stable border by itself. Ilkhanid armies returned to Syria, scored important victories, and forced the Mamluks to maintain fortifications, mobile reserves, intelligence networks, and foreign diplomacy for decades. The Mamluk success arose from military organization and political decisions, the reduced Mongol field army, and the requirements of campaigning far from the Iranian center of Ilkhanid power. Its historical significance is best described as a pivotal defense of Egypt and the foundation for renewed Mamluk authority over Syria. The later wars, discussed in §65.5, prove that security had to be maintained and renegotiated rather than inherited permanently from one successful day.

Section 65.4

Baybars and the Consolidation of the Mamluk Sultanate

Baybars, who ruled from 1260 to 1277, inherited a military regime that had survived an existential threat but still required stable institutions. His earlier career linked him to the Bahri corps, factional exile, and the campaigns against the Mongols. After Qutuz's assassination he established his own authority through military patronage, appointments, strategic intimidation, and the demonstration of competence. He could not rule alone: senior amirs commanded armed followers, while civilian secretaries, judges, merchants, and provincial elites controlled forms of expertise and information essential to governing. Baybars's achievement lay less in inventing an entirely new political system than in coordinating these structures around an increasingly authoritative sultanate.

Communications were central to that achievement. A strengthened postal and intelligence network linked Cairo and the Citadel with Damascus and other Syrian centers, allowing orders and information to travel more reliably across the territory. Garrisons, fortifications, and inspections reinforced control over roads and frontiers; some fortresses captured in conflict became nodes within the new regional system. Administrative offices translated royal orders into taxation, recruitment, provincial appointments, and logistics. These measures required money, animals, messengers, and cooperation from local populations. A postal network was therefore a political instrument, but it was also an economic burden and a means of collecting intelligence on the ruler's own officers.

Baybars addressed the legitimacy problem created when Baghdad's Abbasid government ended in 1258. In 1261 a man claiming Abbasid descent was installed as caliph in Cairo, under Mamluk patronage. An attempted caliphal expedition toward Iraq ended disastrously, but subsequent Abbasid claimants continued in Cairo as figures of ceremonial and religious significance. The caliph could participate in investiture and embody continuity with an honored institution; the sultan possessed the effective army, treasury, and power to appoint officials. Presenting the Cairo caliph as the restored ruler of the entire Islamic world would mistake symbolic prestige for practical sovereignty. Yet dismissing the institution as meaningless would ignore the value of public ritual and scholarly recognition to a military regime whose rulers did not inherit traditional dynastic legitimacy.

External diplomacy reinforced internal consolidation. Baybars maintained relations with the Golden Horde, whose rulers had their own reasons to contest the Ilkhanate, and communicated with Byzantine and European powers. He fought Crusader possessions and negotiated truces when politically useful. His campaigns against Latin fortresses and local enemies were designed to remove bases from which future threats might operate, but agreements and exchange remained as important as sieges. The Mamluk ruler could present himself as defender of Islam while using pragmatic diplomacy with Christian and Muslim leaders alike. Neither a fixed religious alliance nor a constant civilizational war describes the complexity of these relationships.

The achievements of Baybars had social costs and beneficiaries. Military households received revenues and prestige, urban scholars gained patronage, and Cairo's importance grew. Farmers and townspeople supplied taxation and provisions, while campaigns exposed frontier populations to destruction, displacement, and demands for labor. Contemporary panegyrics and royal biographies celebrated Baybars's courage and piety, but their function as instruments of legitimation must be part of the analysis. His reign established patterns of military-political authority, caliphal symbolism, communications, and regional defense that successors developed in different ways. The question that remained was whether the new system could survive the death of a ruler whose forceful personality had helped hold it together. Its subsequent history shows both remarkable durability and recurring factional crisis.

Section 65.5

The Mamluk–Ilkhanid Wars and the Defense of Syria

Mamluk control of Syria was never secured once and for all by the victory at Ain Jalut. From 1260 to the peace negotiated in 1323, the rulers of Egypt faced repeated threats from the Ilkhanid Mongol state in Iran and Iraq. The struggle centered on northern Syria, access across the Euphrates, and possession of strategic cities such as Aleppo and Damascus. The conflict was not simply a clash between Muslim and non-Muslim armies. Several Ilkhans eventually embraced Islam, and both sides negotiated with Christian powers and other Mongol dynasties. Territory, revenue, security, dynastic prestige, and the desire to exclude a competing empire from Syria provided enduring causes of war. The Mamluks needed to defend an extensive frontier that could not be guarded by a single fortress or permanent line of soldiers.

The defense of Syria relied on preparedness before an army appeared. Governors in Aleppo and Damascus maintained contacts with scouts and frontier communities; the Cairo government organized mobile field armies and remounted cavalry; military communications supported rapid decisions across hundreds of kilometers. Bedouin and other local groups might provide intelligence, supplies, or assistance, but their relationships with rulers were negotiated and could change. Control over routes and water points limited the ability of an invading force to sustain horses. Frontier cities could suffer requisitions from the very army sent to defend them, while merchants feared interrupted roads and peasants feared the destruction of harvests. What military chroniclers describe as a series of battles was therefore also a prolonged social and economic condition.

Important confrontations punctuated the struggle. In 1281 a Mamluk army defeated an Ilkhanid force near Homs, confirming that the Mongols could be resisted through organized campaigning. The result was not final. In December 1299 Ghazan's army defeated the Mamluks at Wadi al-Khaznadar, sometimes called the Third Battle of Homs, and the Mongols entered Damascus. Their occupation was brief, extending into early 1300, but the episode revealed vulnerabilities that a triumphalist history of Ain Jalut would conceal. Local residents had to confront questions of submission, protection, and collaboration while rival rulers issued proclamations asserting authority. By 1303 the Mamluks again defeated an invading force at Shaqhab, southeast of Damascus. These engagements must be understood as part of a continuing strategic contest, not as interchangeable examples of perpetual victory.

Military rivalry also encompassed diplomacy. The Ilkhans attempted contact with European rulers and the papacy in hopes of cooperation against Egypt and Syria, while Mamluk rulers cultivated relations with the Jochid Mongols of the Golden Horde, who disputed Ilkhanid authority in the Caucasus. Treaties and embassies reflected practical interests more often than permanent religious blocs. Both states also used propaganda, surveillance, and political intervention in neighboring principalities. The Armenian kingdom in Cilicia, for instance, occupied an exposed position in these wider rivalries. Reuven Amitai's research emphasizes the combination of open campaigning and a continuing contest of diplomatic maneuver, raids, and intelligence that characterized the decades after 1260.

As the geopolitical setting changed, the urgency of warfare diminished. Ilkhanid rulers confronted competing pressures, while the Mamluks had reasons to devote resources to administration, commerce, and other frontiers. The 1323 settlement marked the end of the sustained Ilkhanid–Mamluk war, though it did not erase every regional rivalry or produce identical interests on both sides. This extended conflict helps explain the sultanate's organization: it rewarded experienced commanders, justified provincial garrisons, made communication a state priority, and generated repeated demands on civilian revenue. Unlike Chapter 64, which follows war from the Iranian court, this section asks how defending Syria shaped the institutions of a government centered in Cairo.

Section 65.6

The Destruction of the Remaining Crusader States, 1260–1291

By the time the Mamluks consolidated their rule, the remaining Latin Christian territories in the Levant were largely concentrated in coastal cities, fortresses, and connected hinterlands. These were not simply military outposts detached from regional commerce. Italian merchants, local Christians, Muslim traders, military orders, refugees, and aristocratic families interacted within a shifting landscape of diplomacy and competition. Crusader governments negotiated treaties and commercial privileges with Muslim rulers as well as appealing to European patrons. For the Mamluks, fortified coastal positions could facilitate raids, offer potential allies to the Mongols, and obstruct effective control over Syria. Removing the remaining mainland states therefore served strategic and political aims, although particular sieges were shaped by immediate truces, rivalries, and calculations of advantage.

Baybars took an aggressive approach but did not conduct constant indiscriminate warfare. He negotiated truces when convenient and attacked vulnerable positions when opportunity offered. His campaigns included the capture of Caesarea and Arsuf in 1265, the seizure of the inland fortress of Safed in 1266, and the conquest of Antioch in 1268. Antioch's fall was especially consequential because it had been a principal Latin Christian polity since the First Crusade. Contemporary and later accounts describe extensive killing and enslavement following its capture; the precise totals in the chronicles require care. Civilians were not merely spectators to a contest between rulers. The disappearance of political protection, changes in taxation, and forced movements could reshape family fortunes and local religious communities for generations.

The military orders were important participants. Hospitallers and Templars possessed fortifications, military resources, and links to overseas support; they were not, however, capable of acting without constraints imposed by financing, local alliances, and the availability of reinforcements. Control of the coast also connected warfare with Italian maritime economies. Genoese, Venetian, and other merchants might benefit from trading privileges even when the political position of Frankish lords was weakening. This interdependence complicates the image of a simple frontier separating two uniform religious civilizations. Commercial exchange, ransom, diplomacy, and locally negotiated coexistence persisted in the same decades as major sieges.

Qalawun, who became sultan in 1279, continued the campaign to consolidate the coastline, taking Tripoli in 1289. His son al-Ashraf Khalil then besieged Acre, the most significant surviving mainland Latin center, and captured it in May 1291. Other positions were abandoned or taken soon afterward. The loss of Acre was a decisive institutional rupture for the remaining mainland Crusader states, though the Christian inhabitants of the region did not disappear. The fall of a Latin-ruled fortress must not be confused with the ending of local Arab Christian, Syriac, Armenian, Greek, or other Christian communities. Each had distinct political and ecclesiastical histories. The aftermath also brought additional refugees and changing patterns of settlement within the Mediterranean world.

The collapse of mainland Crusader rule did not eliminate Latin Christian power from the eastern Mediterranean. Cyprus remained under Latin Christian kings; the Hospitallers established their base on Rhodes only after taking the island in the early fourteenth century, conventionally dated to 1310, not at the moment Acre fell. Italian commercial establishments and European diplomacy continued to influence Mamluk policy. The sultanate itself was prepared to make commercial agreements with Christian merchants after defeating Christian territorial governments. This history therefore ends not with the closure of exchange but with a reorganization of authority: Mamluk control expanded along the Levantine coast, while networks of shipping, religious pilgrimage, and trade continued across political and confessional borders.

Section 65.7

Syria, Palestine, Cilicia, and the Mamluk Frontier System

The Mamluk government ruled a composite territory. Egypt furnished the principal political center and much of the revenue, but Syria and Palestine were not merely military buffers. Their cities, agricultural districts, religious institutions, and merchant communities formed an integral part of the sultanate. Damascus, Aleppo, Tripoli, and other important centers were administered through provincial offices led by governors, commonly described as na'ibs, supported by military forces and civilian specialists. Revenue collection, legal administration, market regulation, fortification, and relations with local notables were provincial matters whose importance differed from one place to another. Jerusalem and the towns of Palestine possessed religious and commercial significance even where they were not equivalent to major military headquarters.

Provincial authority required regular negotiation. Governors were appointed by the sultan but depended on locally available food, transport, and political intelligence. Merchants relied on protected routes while rural cultivators hoped to avoid repeated troop requisitions. Religious scholars, judges, and endowment managers could seek redress from Cairo or from a provincial official. Control over nomadic and seminomadic groups often involved subsidies, alliances, recognition of local chiefs, and punitive expeditions. Bedouin communities in the deserts adjoining the settled lands were not passive instruments of the state, and neither were Turkmen or Kurdish groups whose military and pastoral activities crossed administrative boundaries. The sultanate's capacity must be measured by these variable relationships, not by coloring a modern map as though every district was controlled identically.

Northern Syria and Cilicia posed particular problems because they joined the Anatolian, Mediterranean, and Mesopotamian political worlds. The Armenian Kingdom of Cilicia had developed close relations with Latin Christian powers and, at times, with the Mongols. Mamluk campaigns, tribute agreements, and diplomatic contacts changed the balance as Mongol protection became less dependable. The conquest of the kingdom's capital Sis in 1375 ended Cilician Armenian royal sovereignty; the last king, Levon V, was taken into captivity. The kingdom's fall did not end Armenian communities, monasteries, merchants, or political activity across the region. Armenian records, including manuscript colophons and royal traditions, offer a different view of these events from official Mamluk narratives and should be compared rather than subordinated automatically to the victors' perspective.

Frontier policies could be harsh. Raids damaged orchards, fields, caravan connections, and local places of worship, and human beings were taken captive or displaced. Yet the same frontier world enabled trade and diplomacy. Armenian, Greek, Muslim, and Latin merchants negotiated across political boundaries; local rulers maintained kinship relationships or informal agreements that did not fit a simple model of perpetual war. The Mamluks could support a local intermediary against a rival and later discipline that same intermediary. The balance between military domination and negotiated accommodation changed with the ruler, the strategic situation, and the resources available to provincial officials.

The history of Syria and Palestine also requires attention to different scales. State capitals like Damascus hosted major scholarly institutions and governorates. Towns such as Safed or Gaza acted as local markets and administrative nodes. Agricultural villages produced grain, olives, fruit, and revenues on which military households depended. Pastoral routes connected settled populations to surrounding ecological zones. The combination of these forms of life made the sultanate economically significant beyond its battlefield reputation. It also helps explain why the defense of Syria remained a persistent political question: its loss would threaten revenue, learning, trade, pilgrimage passage, and the credibility of a Cairo-centered government claiming regional leadership.

Section 65.8

The Hijaz, Mecca, Medina, and the Politics of Pilgrimage

The Mamluk sultans exercised considerable influence over the holy cities of Mecca and Medina, but their role must not be represented as straightforward direct administration of every locality in western Arabia. The sharifs of Mecca retained an important position, claiming prestige through descent from the Prophet Muhammad and exercising local authority through family networks and alliances. Their relations with Cairo involved recognition, financial support, political rivalry, and occasional coercion. The degree of Mamluk intervention varied with local disputes, the security of routes, and the sultan's capacity to send resources. Protecting the Hijaz helped rulers present themselves as guardians of Islamic pilgrimage and worship, a particularly significant claim for a dynasty founded by a military elite rather than by an inherited caliphal lineage.

Pilgrimage required more than ceremonies. Large caravans traveled from Cairo and Damascus, drawing together pilgrims, traders, animals, guards, religious officials, and suppliers. Water access, weather, food provisions, safe passage, and the risk of attack shaped the journey. State officials helped arrange escorts and supplies, while local inhabitants along the route sold goods or demanded payments. Wells, stopping stations, and charitable provisions depended on maintenance. The journey was undertaken by people of varied wealth, age, gender, and place of origin, not only by scholars or officials. Some pilgrims traveled under conditions of considerable hardship; others participated in commercial networks and used the caravan as a means of exchanging goods and information between regions.

The mahmal, an elaborate ceremonial palanquin accompanying the Egyptian pilgrimage procession, became a visible emblem of sultanic prestige. The provision of the kiswa, the textile covering for the Kaaba, similarly linked royal expenditure with sacred space. These objects did not themselves provide security or food, but they made claims of patronage legible to audiences in Cairo and the Hijaz. Public processions, the presentation of gifts, and the announcement of the ruler's name were political acts. Competition over such privileges could express disagreements between Cairo and local rulers or other Muslim powers. Historians should therefore interpret ritual as one part of actual governance rather than as an ornamental addition to military history.

The Hijaz had commercial as well as devotional significance. Pilgrimage brought demand for animals, clothing, food, shelter, manuscripts, and services. Merchants could combine travel for worship with journeys that connected Egypt, Yemen, the Red Sea ports, and Indian Ocean trade. Goods and people moved through systems that included non-Mamluk authorities, maritime connections, and local arrangements. Mamluk policy toward pilgrimage and Red Sea trade thus overlapped with diplomatic and fiscal decisions, even though protection of holy places carried a different kind of legitimacy from revenue collected on imports. Efforts to improve security could benefit pilgrims and traders, while new taxes or disputes might make the journey more costly.

Mamluk authority in the Hijaz illustrates the reach and limits of medieval regional government. Cairo could influence the recognition of local rulers, provide funds and ritual gifts, and direct major caravans without eliminating the autonomy of the sharifian families. Religious commitment, political ambition, local interest, and economic exchange were intertwined. This relationship also helps explain why the sultanate's importance cannot be measured solely through Egypt and Syria: the state participated in an institutional geography of the Islamic world extending toward Arabia and beyond, while always having to cooperate with local powers that possessed histories and interests of their own.

Historical Vocabulary: Mahmal, Kiswa, and Waqf

Mahmal describes a ceremonial palanquin associated with major pilgrimage caravans; kiswa is the covering of the Kaaba, whose provision conveyed prestige; waqf denotes endowed property or revenues assigned under specified conditions. These institutions connected ceremonial religion to real resources but did not give the sultan complete direct control over all local communities.

Section 65.9

Sultans, Amirs, Military Households, and Political Succession

The Mamluk Sultanate was not a hereditary monarchy in the conventional sense, but neither was it a republic governed by an impersonal corps of soldiers. Political authority was distributed through competing military households headed by the sultan and senior amirs. Each important commander cultivated followers whose access to income, offices, military commands, and marriage alliances depended partly on their patron's fortune. The sultan held the highest formal authority and controlled prestigious appointments, but his ability to govern depended on the armed cooperation of other households. A powerful officer could support a claimant and later depose him; a ruler who accumulated loyal troops could diminish the autonomy of rivals. The result was a political order that repeatedly produced intense conflict without losing all capacity for institutional survival.

The careers of Baybars and Qalawun illustrate two routes through these structures. Baybars became sultan following a military crisis and consolidated his position through command, recruitment, and political patronage. Qalawun, who ruled from 1279 to 1290, constructed a more durable family dynasty while continuing to depend on household politics. His descendants dominated the throne for long stretches of the fourteenth century, especially under al-Nasir Muhammad. Yet dynastic prestige never wholly displaced the principle that the most effective ruler needed the support of powerful mamluk networks. Children could receive the title of sultan while senior amirs directed policy. Attempts to govern through a royal family were therefore entangled with the competitive institution that had brought the ruling class to power.

Al-Nasir Muhammad ibn Qalawun held the sultanate three times: 1293–1294, 1299–1309, and 1310–1341. Those dates reveal more than a biographical curiosity. Early in life he operated under the influence of senior commanders, including men who acted as protectors, rivals, or de facto governors. His return to power in 1310 marked a more assertive effort to reduce competing household networks and direct revenue toward the sovereign establishment. His reign witnessed major architectural commissions and fiscal redistribution as well as factional punishment. Scholars often see his third reign as a turning point because the ruler managed, for an extended period, to reshape the relationship between the sultan, influential officers, and the financial system. This did not eliminate the structural tensions of military household government.

Succession after al-Nasir's death in 1341 exposed those tensions. Numerous descendants were raised to the throne amid competition among amirs, and several held power briefly. Political instability could make office-holding dangerous: confiscation, imprisonment, exile, or execution might follow a factional reversal. For civilian officials and merchants, sudden changes at the palace could alter tax expectations and the security of transactions. For soldiers, a ruler's fall might jeopardize the revenues and privileges that supported their households. Chroniclers often presented these conflicts as moral failings of particular men. Historians must also examine how the distribution of military resources made such conflicts recurrent and sometimes rational from the perspective of competing elites.

The endurance of the sultanate was nevertheless remarkable. Judges continued to hear cases, merchants bought and sold goods, provincial governors defended cities, and pilgrimage preparations continued under rulers of very different strength. Institutions were not simply refounded every time the ruler changed. Moreover, ceremonial authority mattered: court processions, investitures, robes of honor, and the participation of Cairo's Abbasid caliph helped mark a new sovereign as legitimate. Political ritual did not neutralize armed competition, but it translated a military result into a recognizable order. Understanding this combination of force, patronage, bureaucracy, and ritual explains why the Mamluk state could be simultaneously unstable at the top and resilient across generations.

Section 65.10

Provincial Administration, Taxation, Landholding, and the Iqta System

The Mamluk military elite could not defend Egypt and Syria without a dependable flow of revenue. The state relied on civilian financial officials, scribes, accountants, provincial administrators, and local intermediaries who converted the resources of farming communities, markets, and international commerce into usable income. The diwans maintained records and issued instructions, while governors and other officials supervised the extraction and distribution of resources. The distinction between governing authority and fiscal entitlement was not always simple: a commander receiving rights to certain revenues might attempt to enlarge those claims, while the central treasury might seek to redirect payments during a crisis. The system rested on a continuous negotiation between the military's needs and the productive capacity of the society that supported it.

The iqta was central to the fiscal-military order. In broad terms it assigned the right to receive specified revenues, usually associated with land, to a military beneficiary or establishment. Such an assignment did not necessarily confer ownership of the soil or complete jurisdiction over its inhabitants. The rights and actual practices varied with time, place, and political power. A soldier or officer depended on revenue to meet the costs of equipment, retainers, horses, and household obligations, while rural cultivators remained responsible for production. Military ranks and the scale of assigned income were connected, making the distribution of fiscal resources inseparable from the hierarchy of armed officeholders. The central government also needed a substantial share to fund the palace, official projects, and public administration.

Cadastral surveys known as rawk sought to assess and reorganize this distribution. Earlier surveys had been conducted under different rulers, and al-Nasir Muhammad pursued a notable series in the 1310s and 1320s. In Egypt the reallocation associated with 1315, often termed the rawk al-Nasiri, redirected portions of revenue and changed the balance among the ruler and military households. Surveys were instruments of political power as well as technical accounting. A register could identify a village's expected output, but it could not guarantee that harvests would match estimates or that a tax collector would follow instructions. Local knowledge, bargaining, and coercion intervened between the official calculation and the burden actually borne by a household.

The consequences for peasants require particular attention. A farmer might cultivate land under customary arrangements while facing demands for grain, cash, labor, transport, or supplies. Collection pressures could intensify when several officials asserted overlapping claims or when a fiscal beneficiary sought immediate profit without regard for future output. Yet it would be misleading to assert that every iqta holder invariably neglected agriculture. Some beneficiaries had reason to maintain waterworks and protect villages because future revenue depended on them. A rigorous history asks how tenure, political security, demographic change, and local environmental conditions affected incentives. Wealthy officials and urban foundations might enjoy exemptions or possess leverage unavailable to poor cultivators.

Provincial administration extended beyond taxation. Governors supervised security, maintained fortifications, responded to unrest, and negotiated with tribal chiefs and religious authorities. Markets were inspected under arrangements associated with the hisba, although actual enforcement varied. Legal institutions addressed property, debt, inheritance, and waqf disputes. The existence of offices should not imply consistent administrative effectiveness: surviving documents are uneven, and the experience of a village is rarely recorded in its own voice. The fiscal system nevertheless reveals a defining feature of Mamluk government. Military power depended ultimately on cultivated land, household labor, and commercial circulation, so the history of taxation is not a technical appendix to military narrative but one of its foundations.

Historical Vocabulary: Iqta, Rawk, and Diwan

Iqta is a military-associated assignment of revenues rather than a simple deed of private land ownership. Rawk refers to fiscal surveying and reassessment; the notable Egyptian survey of al-Nasir Muhammad occurred in 1315. Diwan is an administrative bureau, with functions varying by context. Used together, these concepts show how military ranking, records, and cultivated land supported each other.

Section 65.11

The Ulama, Islamic Law, Abbasid Caliphs, and Religious Institutions

The Mamluk rulers governed a society in which Islamic legal scholars, teachers, preachers, judges, and religious foundations possessed forms of authority different from military command. The ulama did not form a single corporation with one political position. Scholars came from different legal schools and intellectual traditions; their careers depended on learning, reputation, teaching relationships, public employment, and patronage. Sultan Baybars's restoration of an Abbasid caliphal institution in Cairo after 1260 strengthened a claim to continuity with the wider Sunni political order. The Cairo caliphs performed ceremonial functions and provided symbols of investiture, but they did not direct the army or control the Mamluk treasury. Their presence mattered because political power and religious prestige were related without being identical.

In the later thirteenth century, the Mamluk sultanate organized chief judicial offices for the four recognized Sunni legal schools: Hanafi, Maliki, Shafi'i, and Hanbali. These traditions shared major religious commitments but differed in methods of legal reasoning and particular doctrines. Judges administered disputes over marriage, inheritance, property, debt, and endowments, and the application of law depended on the documentary and social circumstances of individual cases. Provincial cities maintained their own scholarly networks and judicial hierarchies. The existence of several chief judges did not guarantee equal access to justice, nor did it mean all inhabitants were governed through one uniform legal system. Christians and Jews had their own communal institutions while also interacting with state courts and legal restrictions.

Religious foundations financed by waqf were crucial to the relationship between rulers and scholars. A donor could dedicate revenue from buildings, shops, estates, or other property to support a school, mosque, hospital, Sufi lodge, or charitable institution. Endowment deeds specified beneficiaries, staffing, income arrangements, and often forms of family interest. Such arrangements served genuine educational and welfare purposes, but they could also enhance a patron's reputation and protect the use of resources over time. A madrasa provided space and salaries for teaching, yet education was not confined to formal buildings. As Jonathan Berkey's work emphasizes, relationships between teachers and students and the circulation of scholarly credentials often mattered more than the physical institution alone.

The career of Ibn Taymiyya, active principally in Damascus and Cairo in the late thirteenth and early fourteenth centuries, demonstrates the complexity of relationships between learned authority and government. He intervened in controversies over doctrine, legal practice, and public morality, and he faced imprisonment as well as influence. It would be inaccurate to imagine a uniform clerical class either permanently endorsing or opposing the military state. Sufi devotional institutions likewise varied widely, ranging from elite-supported khanqahs to popular networks of spiritual instruction. Religious practices were shaped by local communities, pilgrimage, saintly shrines, scholarly debates, and state intervention, not simply by official orders from Cairo.

The sultanate also contained substantial Christian and Jewish populations. Egyptian Copts participated in administration and commercial life while facing episodes of official discrimination, popular violence, and contested conversion. Jewish communities maintained religious, educational, and commercial networks in a changing fiscal and social environment. Syrian Christian communities were internally diverse and cannot be reduced to one collective relationship with the state. Conditions fluctuated across centuries and districts. The historian's task is to connect religious institutions with power, property, inequality, and social practice without claiming that religion alone explains every conflict. The enduring influence of Cairo and Damascus as centers of learning emerged from these multiple interactions between patronage, scholarship, law, and everyday urban society.

Section 65.12

Families, Women, Slavery, Labor, and Everyday Social Life

The phrase “Mamluk society” can easily mislead if it brings only armored horsemen and palace intrigues to mind. Most inhabitants of Egypt and Syria were not members of the military ruling class. They cultivated land, worked in markets, manufactured textiles and metal goods, sold food, served households, maintained water systems, taught children, practiced trades, and negotiated with courts and local officials. The experience of an Egyptian village differed from that of a merchant neighborhood in Cairo or a household in Damascus. These differences were shaped by wealth, gender, religion, occupation, legal status, and access to powerful patrons. The written record is rich in elite chronicles and endowed institutions, but it is uneven in the voices of poorer cultivators and servants.

Families constituted economic as well as domestic units. Marriage contracts, dowers, inheritance claims, property transactions, and endowment records reveal some of the ways households distributed assets and obligations. Women could own property, inherit under applicable legal rules, participate in sales, litigate, and create charitable endowments, though their practical opportunities depended heavily on circumstances. The exceptional political career of Shajar al-Durr should not be used as evidence that ordinary women possessed comparable authority. Elite women associated with military households sometimes managed property, arranged marriages, and sponsored institutions; poorer women might work in family businesses, food production, textile labor, or domestic service. These activities are incompletely recorded because chroniclers generally prioritized public political and scholarly events.

Slavery existed outside the mamluk recruitment system and took many forms. Women and men could be forced into domestic service, skilled labor, court attendance, or other forms of work. The possibility that some purchased boys would later become powerful military officers was exceptional, not typical of the condition of enslavement. The trade in people therefore connected Mamluk elite reproduction with wider systems of coercion and commercial exchange. Free employees and apprentices, meanwhile, worked in craft and service occupations, sometimes under forms of patronage that could offer protection but also constrain autonomy. Students should distinguish an enslaved person, a manumitted military client, a freeborn son of a mamluk, and an ordinary taxpayer rather than treating all as members of one homogeneous class.

Urban life offered opportunities for mobility and association. Merchants accumulated wealth through regional and overseas trade; scholars gained prestige through teaching and transmission; craftsmen worked in metalworking, weaving, woodwork, food production, construction, and other sectors. Neighborhood relationships could provide help in sickness or hardship, while officials, religious courts, and market inspectors affected the conditions of work. People of different religious communities interacted through trade and residence despite social distinctions and periodic persecution. Mamluk rulers' patronage of architecture and learning depended on the labor of stonecutters, builders, copyists, teachers, cleaners, cooks, and many others whose names rarely appear on monumental inscriptions.

Rural households confronted different pressures: changing floods and rainfall, animal disease, tax obligations, irrigation repair, and the dangers created by armies moving through productive districts. Epidemic disease and food shortages could disrupt marriage patterns, labor supply, and family survival. Historical reconstruction must resist two extremes: imagining an unchanging traditional society with no room for individual agency, and treating surviving elite documents as transparent windows into all ordinary lives. The diversity of Mamluk households reveals why the state cannot be evaluated only through victory or defeat. The same government could fund a celebrated school while demanding resources from families who had little influence over its decisions; both experiences form part of its history.

Division 4 — Cities, Economy, Commerce, and Cultural Achievement
Section 65.13

Cairo, Damascus, Aleppo, and the Mamluk Urban World

Cairo was the political center of the Mamluk Sultanate, but its importance was not exhausted by the presence of a palace and a ruling army. The Citadel stood above a dense urban landscape of markets, houses, workshops, mosques, schools, hospitals, and streets linking older quarters with newly developed areas. Its population depended on the movement of food from the Nile Valley, water brought or distributed through different arrangements, and regular flows of migrants, pilgrims, craftsmen, merchants, and scholars. The Ayyubid and earlier Fatimid histories of the city remained visible in its built environment. Mamluk rulers did not establish Cairo from nothing; they enlarged and reconfigured a metropolis whose institutions already possessed long histories.

Royal and elite patronage left a material record that can be studied beyond courtly praise. A new school or mosque required construction workers, skilled decorators, land or shops to supply endowed revenue, supervisors, religious staff, and future maintenance. Streetscapes changed as patrons erected complexes along important processional or commercial routes. Markets clustered in districts where particular goods could be found; warehouses, lodging places, and caravanserais connected local work to long-distance trade. Urban administration also required mechanisms to manage disputes over building, water access, public space, and prices. A monument could be a devotional act, a political memorial, an investment in public services, and a strategy for sustaining a patron's family's influence simultaneously.

Damascus occupied a different but equally essential place in the sultanate. It was a provincial administrative headquarters, a center of Sunni learning, and a nexus connecting Palestine, the Syrian interior, pilgrimage routes, and trade toward the north and east. Its scholars traveled, taught, corresponded, and contested ideas with counterparts in Cairo. Aleppo's position near the Anatolian and Euphrates frontiers made it a significant commercial and military center exposed to recurrent strategic threats. Both cities contained diverse neighborhoods, craft traditions, religious establishments, and commercial institutions that cannot be understood solely through Egyptian government orders. Their prosperity and vulnerability depended on regional geography and on the peace or insecurity of the surrounding countryside.

Cities functioned through labor that sources do not always name. Bakers, porters, water suppliers, muleteers, artisans, market sellers, scribes, healers, servants, and builders made urban life possible. Women participated in households and some economic activities; religious minorities traded and worked within systems of differentiated legal status. A hospital might treat patients from several backgrounds under specified endowment arrangements, while the operation of a madrasa depended on professional scholarly relationships. Institutions could generate wages and provide public benefits without removing broader inequality. The existence of spectacular buildings should therefore not be accepted as a direct measure of every inhabitant's welfare.

Urban development was subject to crisis as well as growth. Epidemics, high grain prices, military factional conflict, drought or low Nile floods, and disruption of caravan routes could reduce employment, create shortages, and disturb neighborhoods. Cairo, Damascus, and Aleppo were not identical in their exposure to these pressures. Their histories demonstrate both the power of metropolitan institutions and their reliance on agriculture, commerce, transport, and human labor elsewhere. A comparative approach also guards against the assumption that Cairo's political centrality erased Syrian intellectual and economic agency. The sultanate's urban world was a network of interdependent places, not a single capital surrounded by insignificant provincial settlements.

Section 65.14

The Nile Economy, Agriculture, Irrigation, and Rural Society

The spectacular architecture of Cairo and the maintenance of elite cavalry depended fundamentally on agriculture. Egypt's Nile Valley and Delta supported cultivation through annual flood patterns and irrigation systems constructed, maintained, and altered over many generations. Water was distributed through embankments, basins, canals, drains, and locally organized labor. The pattern differed from rainfall-dependent agricultural districts of Syria and Palestine, where local streams, springs, and other irrigation arrangements could play major roles. These environmental contrasts shaped harvest risk, settlement, crop selection, and the state's ability to estimate revenue. They should be explained as ongoing ecological processes rather than as a timeless description of a fertile Nile that automatically yielded wealth.

Egyptian cultivators produced grain and other foodstuffs, as well as crops supporting textiles and trade, including flax and cotton. Animal husbandry, gardens, orchards, and local craft production supplemented cultivation. Households needed seed, tools, animals, accessible markets, and workers capable of maintaining irrigation channels. A successful flood could improve prospects but did not guarantee security if taxation, grain storage failures, or political disorder deprived families of resources. Conversely, low water levels could reduce yields and produce price increases even without an invasion. Rural life involved constant coordination among neighbors, landowners, village headmen, fiscal agents, and local religious establishments.

The relationship between agriculture and iqta revenue was structurally important. Military beneficiaries received income claims tied to agricultural assessments; the government needed their cavalry service and loyalty. But revenues recorded in registers did not equal an unlimited ability of peasants to pay. If extraction exceeded productive capacity, livestock could be sold, cultivation curtailed, or households forced into debt or movement. If a district's irrigation deteriorated, revenue might decline for many years. Evidence is uneven, however, and interpretations of systematic neglect should be assessed rather than assumed. Some officials sought to protect productive resources, while others prioritized immediate demands in a politically insecure environment. Village populations were active participants in negotiating and sometimes resisting local conditions, although direct testimony from cultivators is scarce.

Agriculture in Syria and Palestine reflected a different mosaic of rainfall, irrigated gardens, orchards, vineyards, pasture, and commercial crops. Northern Syria's cities depended on surrounding grain supply and routes into the Anatolian and Mesopotamian worlds. Frontier campaigns could damage farms or remove workers, while new security arrangements sometimes permitted markets and cultivation to recover. Coastal and inland districts did not respond uniformly to the Mamluk conquest of Crusader territories. Students should avoid applying conditions from the Egyptian Nile floodplain to every field in the wider sultanate. A regional history requires explanations of local water sources, crop systems, tax expectations, and the varying distance between cultivators and centers of military power.

After the Black Death, repeated mortality complicated these relationships further. Fewer workers could mean difficulties in maintaining canals and embankments; neglected maintenance could reduce cultivable area and revenue, which in turn could diminish resources for repair. Stuart Borsch has developed this argument using fiscal and environmental evidence, while other historians stress the uncertainty of demographic baselines and the importance of government policy, regional variation, and longer economic trends. The causal relationship should not be simplified into a single disease event mechanically producing centuries of decline. Nonetheless, the interdependence of population, water infrastructure, cultivation, and military finance makes agricultural history indispensable to explaining both Mamluk resilience and vulnerability.

Section 65.15

Mediterranean, Red Sea, and Indian Ocean Commerce

Mamluk Egypt occupied a strategically important position between the Red Sea and the Mediterranean. Commercial networks carried spices, textiles, and other commodities through Indian Ocean connections to Yemen and ports on the Red Sea, across Egyptian transport routes, and onward to Alexandria and Mediterranean merchants. This geography did not make Egypt a passive toll station. Merchants and rulers invested in storage, credit, transport, commercial agreements, and relationships with suppliers and purchasers abroad. Syrian routes through Damascus and Aleppo linked the region with Anatolia, Iran, and Mediterranean ports as well. The resulting system joined maritime and overland travel, and its operation depended on political security across territories that no single ruler controlled entirely.

Spices, especially pepper, formed an important part of high-value commerce, but they were far from the only goods involved. Egyptian and Syrian producers supplied textiles, sugar, foodstuffs, and craft products, while metal goods, glass, luxury fabrics, horses, and other commodities circulated in multiple directions. Commercial activity also involved pilgrims and small traders who did not appear in the accounts of the most influential merchant houses. Karimi merchants were important figures in Red Sea trade and finance, though the organization and influence of particular partnerships changed over time. A market operating in Alexandria depended on a chain of agreements and labor stretching far beyond a single port: ship crews, caravan operators, warehouse owners, money changers, translators, customs officers, and regional brokers all participated.

Venetian, Genoese, and other European merchants negotiated privileges, trading quarters, and customs arrangements with Mamluk rulers. Diplomatic and commercial agreements could persist even while Muslim and Christian armies fought elsewhere. The government valued customs revenue and could use access to markets as a diplomatic instrument, but attempts to raise state income might discourage trade if merchants judged the risks too high. Italian commercial records and Arabic chronicles offer different perspectives on these relationships. The former are often attentive to prices, cargoes, and contracts; the latter may foreground royal policy and political conflicts. Together they show how economic history can challenge overly rigid narratives of religious confrontation.

State intervention in commerce became especially significant during the fifteenth century. The sultans faced fiscal demands associated with military households and changing agricultural revenues, and rulers such as Barsbay attempted to capture more of the profit from pepper and other goods. Policies described as monopolies need close explanation: they could compel sales through state channels, regulate quantities or prices, or alter commercial privileges in ways that shifted risk onto merchants. These interventions did not occur all at once, and their effectiveness changed. Some merchants adapted by negotiating anew or redirecting transactions; others lost established opportunities. The state itself remained dependent on commercial expertise and overseas suppliers, making coercive regulation potentially costly for the government as well as for the merchant community.

The emergence of Portuguese naval expansion in the Indian Ocean after 1498 challenged established networks but did not instantaneously end Mamluk commerce. Regional actors, including Indian, Arabian, Egyptian, and other merchants, continued to pursue strategies of resistance, accommodation, or alternative routing. Mamluk fleets and allies engaged Portuguese forces in the early sixteenth century, with limited resources and mixed results. Equating the Ottoman conquest of 1517 solely with a Portuguese diversion of spices would ignore Ottoman military strategy, frontier conflicts, fiscal pressures, and the continuing importance of the Red Sea. The Mamluk commercial system should be understood as dynamic and contested, shaped by policies and choices made within the region as well as by foreign competition.

Section 65.16

Scholarship, Architecture, Hospitals, and Artistic Patronage

Cairo and Damascus were among the great scholarly centers of the late medieval Islamic world. Teaching took place in mosques, madrasas, homes, libraries, and the circles of individual scholars. Legal instruction, Qur'anic studies, hadith, history, biography, medicine, language, and other pursuits overlapped within careers that were shaped by travel and patronage. A student might study with several teachers across cities, collecting credentials through relationships rather than completing a modern institutional degree. Scholars such as Ibn Taymiyya, al-Maqrizi, Ibn Hajar al-Asqalani, and Ibn Taghribirdi worked in different periods and genres, but together their surviving writings demonstrate the extraordinary scale of religious and historical scholarship in Mamluk territories. Their views must be differentiated: a jurist's controversial theological argument is not the same type of evidence as a historian's retrospective account of a sultan's reign.

Endowed architecture made such activities materially possible. The complex of Sultan Qalawun in Cairo included a major hospital, a madrasa, and a mausoleum, reflecting the interconnected purposes of charity, learning, dynastic remembrance, and political prestige. The hospital, or bimaristan, required personnel, supplies, and endowed revenue; its reputation should be assessed through institutional evidence, not imagined as identical to a modern public health system. The monumental complex of Sultan Hasan, begun in 1356, joined a mosque-madrasa arrangement associated with the four Sunni legal schools to a funerary program of extraordinary scale. These institutions differed in their intended services, legal endowments, architectural forms, and audiences, even though both displayed royal patronage.

Later patrons continued to reshape Cairo. Barquq's constructions, the foundations associated with Sultan Faraj, and the fifteenth-century programs of Qaytbay established religious, funerary, and commercial buildings in different districts. Qaytbay's complex in Cairo's Northern Cemetery, built in the 1470s, demonstrates the refinement of carved stone decoration, domes, minarets, and coordinated architectural space. Building inscriptions and waqf deeds identify patrons and beneficiaries and sometimes record revenue sources and staff responsibilities. They are not transparent records of actual long-term service; an endowed institution might later face shortages or managerial changes. Nevertheless, material remains combined with documents offer evidence unavailable from chronicles of rulers and battles.

Mamluk artistic production was diverse. Metalworkers produced objects with elaborate inlay, calligraphic inscriptions, and intricate forms; glassmakers made enameled and gilded vessels; artisans worked in wood, stone, ceramics, and textiles. Manuscripts required scribes, binders, painters where illustration was used, and patrons capable of paying for skilled labor. Artistic styles developed through earlier Ayyubid traditions and exchanges with neighboring societies. The survival of a lamp or basin in a museum can reveal techniques and networks of circulation, but its presence in a wealthy patron's collection tells less about the everyday possessions of ordinary households. Students should compare the evidence of luxury objects with documents of production, wages, and trade where available.

Cultural achievement should not function as an apology for political coercion, nor should military conflict erase the history of scholarship and art. The same military households that fought for power could commission structures providing education, worship, water, medical treatment, and public prestige. Scholarly institutions could depend on patrons while preserving forms of intellectual authority that were not simply extensions of the court. The material legacy of Mamluk cities therefore opens a broader historical problem: how societies under hierarchical and sometimes violent government create enduring forms of knowledge and beauty, and how those achievements are financed through the work and resources of people whose names often remain unknown.

Section 65.17

The Black Death, Recurrent Plague, and the Transformation of Society

The Black Death reached Egypt and Syria in 1347–1349 during a wider pandemic that crossed Eurasian trading and movement networks. Its immediate effects were catastrophic in many places: chroniclers describe heavy mortality, disrupted trade, reduced agricultural labor, and anxious religious and social responses. The disease spread through communities whose members lived in different environments and possessed unequal opportunities for protection or flight. Elite officials could sometimes leave a stricken city, but such movement did not assure safety; poorer households might lack the means to move at all. The chronology is reconstructed from multiple accounts, including writers whose works record the original epidemic retrospectively. Al-Maqrizi, born in 1364, was not an eyewitness to the first outbreak, even though his historical narrative is an important source for its later memory and documentation.

Mortality figures in medieval sources require interpretation. Chroniclers recorded daily funeral totals, deserted villages, and extraordinary levels of suffering, but their figures cannot always be checked against reliable census data. It is reasonable to conclude that mortality was severe without asserting one exact empire-wide percentage. The effects could differ between Cairo, Alexandria, Damascus, smaller towns, and agricultural districts, and later epidemics did not necessarily strike every population identically. Households lost workers and dependents, religious institutions lost teachers or students, and commercial relationships were disrupted. Survivors faced mourning and practical difficulties as well as shifting opportunities in labor and property markets.

The effects extended well beyond the initial epidemic. Plague recurred across the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, making demographic recovery uncertain. A disease episode could remove skilled workers needed to maintain canals, bridges, transport systems, and urban services, while repeated outbreaks affected the training and replacement of elite soldiers and the collection of taxes. Stuart Borsch has argued that Egyptian rural depopulation contributed strongly to irrigation decay: reduced labor made canal upkeep more difficult, deteriorating irrigation reduced cultivation, and lower production weakened the resources for future repair. This argument offers a mechanism, not merely a correlation, connecting epidemic disease and agrarian change. It should be evaluated alongside the possible effects of fiscal policy, warfare, environmental variation, and preexisting conditions.

Economic consequences were not necessarily the same as those sometimes described in histories of western Europe. The labor institutions, property relations, flood-dependent ecology, and tax systems of Egypt produced distinctive responses. Fewer cultivators did not automatically mean durable wage gains for every surviving worker; the ability to bargain depended on local power and resources. A village whose irrigation channel was neglected might offer fewer opportunities even with reduced population pressure. In cities, prices, rents, employment, and endowment revenues could shift in complicated ways. Historians must not turn a regional comparison into a universal claim that one society recovered and another only declined. Distinct outcomes require explanation from institutions and evidence.

Plague also generated religious interpretation and public responses. Preachers, physicians, rulers, and ordinary families confronted disease using the knowledge and practices available to them. Chronicles describe prayers, charitable activities, medical advice, fear, and debates about proper responses. These accounts reveal the moral and intellectual worlds of their authors but cannot alone determine the microbiological details of transmission. The repeated epidemics altered the balance between rulers, taxpayers, endowment managers, and laborers; they contributed to the pressures facing the later Mamluk state without making its eventual defeat inevitable. Plague history is therefore not an isolated episode between political reigns. It is a continuing thread in the history of population, revenue, built infrastructure, and household survival.

Section 65.18

The Burji Mamluks, Barquq, and the Transformation of Military Politics

The conventional division between Bahri and Burji Mamluks marks important changes within a continuing sultanate. By the later fourteenth century, mamluks of Circassian and other Caucasian origins had become increasingly prominent in military recruitment and senior command, while many earlier military households remained politically influential. The term Burji derives from the association of recruits with towers of the Cairo Citadel, but it should not be treated as a precise ethnic or institutional boundary applying to every soldier. In 1382 Barquq assumed the sultanate, generally taken as the beginning of the Burji period. He was briefly displaced in 1389 and restored in 1390, demonstrating that a new dominant household did not immediately silence rival factions.

Barquq's rise followed decades in which descendants of al-Nasir Muhammad held the title of sultan while powerful commanders exercised substantial authority. Shifting coalitions among officers, control over the royal mamluks, appointments to revenue-bearing positions, and influence at court shaped the political environment. Barquq attempted to establish a dependable household and direct the distribution of resources, but he governed amid military revolts and a wider climate of fiscal and demographic pressure. His career shows that dynastic legitimacy could be replaced by a different political coalition without replacing the state's fundamental institutions. The sultan, amirs, provincial governors, diwans, and waqf system continued to operate through the transition.

Barquq's son Faraj inherited a troubled political situation in 1399. His reign was marked by factional turmoil and the challenge created by Timur's campaigns toward Syria. Aleppo and Damascus experienced major violence and disruption in 1400–1401, including the movement of captives and skilled workers. Those events belong partly to Chapter 66's history of Timur, but the Mamluk consequences must be understood here: weakened provincial security, contested command, local suffering, and the difficulty of recovering revenue. The fact that Mamluk government endured after such shocks is as historically important as the shock itself. A simple description of permanent collapse would not explain later building, commerce, or long reigns.

Political history in the fifteenth century cannot be reconstructed exclusively from short-lived rulers and palace conspiracies. Several sultans held power for substantial periods, and some developed lasting patronage networks. New military recruits continued to arrive through Black Sea and Caucasian connections; court households competed for prestige and fiscal privileges; provincial offices remained essential. Waqf foundations could stabilize religious and social services while reducing resources available to ordinary fiscal administration. These dynamics created incentives for rulers to seek new revenue from commerce or exceptional taxes. The state's problems were therefore institutional and socioeconomic as well as personal. A factional struggle mattered because it could alter taxation, army provisioning, diplomatic strategy, or the security of an entire province.

The Burji era is best approached as an evolving military state within the same Egyptian-Syrian political geography. Circassian prominence did not render the majority of subjects Circassian, nor did it erase the Turkic, Arab, Kurdish, Armenian, Coptic, Jewish, and other communities in the sultanate. Royal architectural patronage and the scholarship of Cairo and Damascus continued. Meanwhile, recurrent plague, competition over revenues, Ottoman and Anatolian developments, and changing trade made effective government increasingly demanding. The historical question is not why the state failed immediately after 1382—it did not—but how its rulers adapted an inherited system to new circumstances while retaining the dependencies and vulnerabilities built into military household politics.

Section 65.19

Barsbay, Qaytbay, Commercial Policy, and the Later Mamluk State

The fifteenth-century Mamluk Sultanate remained a substantial regional power rather than an inert remnant awaiting Ottoman conquest. The reign of Barsbay (1422–1438) illustrates the interaction of fiscal pressure, commercial policy, and military ambition. His government sought to strengthen control over lucrative spice commerce, particularly pepper, through interventions that could compel merchants to deal with state agents or accept regulated terms. These policies have often been called monopolies, but their mechanisms and consequences varied over time. Barsbay's measures were designed to increase royal resources at a time when military households and government expenditures placed heavy demands on revenue. The policies also risked disrupting established merchant networks, an important example of the limits of extracting income from markets that the ruler did not control from production to final sale.

Barsbay's forces conquered Cyprus in 1426 and took the island's king captive. Cyprus became tributary rather than a province administered in precisely the same fashion as Egypt. The distinction matters: a victory could increase prestige and bring revenue while leaving local rulers and institutions in place under obligations to Cairo. The campaign also revealed the continuing importance of eastern Mediterranean maritime power after the end of the mainland Crusader states. The sultanate maintained relationships with Christian maritime governments through warfare, diplomacy, ransom, and commerce. A historian should examine the practical purposes of each relationship instead of assigning states permanently to hostile religious camps.

The later fifteenth century saw notable long reigns and continued state activity, especially under Qaytbay (1468–1496). His government pursued military defense, diplomacy with neighboring powers, administration of court households, and an extensive program of architectural patronage. His foundations in Cairo and other cities served religious, funerary, and charitable purposes while projecting royal prestige. Trade and urban craft production continued, and scholarly and artistic institutions remained vigorous. This is not evidence that the treasury was free from difficulty or that rural inhabitants enjoyed uniform prosperity. It shows why “decline” needs to be tested against several indicators, rather than inferred solely from chroniclers' complaints about corruption or from the eventual outcome in 1517.

Qaytbay's reign also involved conflict with the expanding Ottoman state, particularly wars and frontier disputes between the Mamluks and Ottomans in 1485–1491. These disputes were shaped by southeastern Anatolian politics, influence over frontier principalities, and changing strategic capacities. Neither side was yet fated to conquer or be conquered. After Qaytbay, political succession and the burden of maintaining the military establishment became more difficult, while outside threats multiplied. Qansuh al-Ghawri, who became sultan in 1501, attempted to govern amid rising Ottoman power and changes in Indian Ocean commerce. His architectural programs and diplomatic initiatives show continuing capacity for action even under strain.

Portuguese expansion after Vasco da Gama's voyage around southern Africa in 1497–1499 introduced new armed competitors into the Indian Ocean. The Mamluks participated in naval efforts, including cooperation with regional forces, to defend commerce and challenge Portuguese attacks. Defeat at Diu in 1509 was important, but it did not instantly shut down the Red Sea trade or alone determine the fate of the sultanate. Port cities, merchants, and regional rulers made adjustments, and old routes retained value. The late Mamluk story is therefore one of competing attempts to preserve revenue, security, and status under changing conditions. Its final phase must be explained through the convergence of military rivalries, institutional pressures, fiscal decisions, and maritime change rather than through an inevitable moral or technological failure.

Section 65.20

The End of Mamluk Sovereignty and Its Enduring Regional Legacy

The Ottoman conquest of 1516–1517 ended the Mamluk Sultanate's independent sovereign government, but the explanation requires more than a comparison of armored cavalry with firearms. Ottoman expansion under Selim I followed major developments in Anatolia and Iran, including conflict with the Safavid state. The Mamluks had their own interests in frontier territories and regional balances, and diplomatic disputes accumulated alongside military competition. Differences in command coordination, military organization, artillery deployment, manpower, financing, and political alliances all contributed to the outcome. Gunpowder weapons were important, but the Mamluks were not wholly unfamiliar with firearms and the decisive issue was not a simple technological opposition between modern and medieval armies.

In August 1516 the Ottoman army defeated the Mamluks at Marj Dabiq north of Aleppo; Sultan Qansuh al-Ghawri died during the campaign. Ottoman forces gained control over Syria as the Mamluk political order confronted a crisis of leadership and military recovery. A new ruler, Tumanbay II, attempted to defend Egypt. The Ottoman victory at al-Raydaniyya near Cairo in January 1517 and subsequent fighting resulted in Ottoman control of the capital. Tumanbay was captured and executed. The detailed chronology of campaigns, annexation, and the Hijaz belongs to Chapter 71, so this chapter's task is to explain the institutional significance of the end of independent rule rather than repeat the entire military narrative.

What ended was the authority of a Cairo-centered sultan who ruled Egypt and Syria as an independent sovereign. That rupture affected diplomacy, military command, provincial administration, and claims to the caliphate and guardianship of the holy cities. It did not eliminate the population, architecture, scholarly networks, economic systems, or households formed during the preceding centuries. Ottoman officials inherited cities whose major monuments, endowments, markets, and judicial institutions remained consequential. Some Mamluk-origin households retained influence within the new provincial environment, adapting patronage networks to Ottoman authority. Continuity should not be exaggerated into the claim that nothing changed, but neither should conquest be described as the replacement of one society by another.

The sultanate's history reveals several durable achievements. A military elite initially rooted in enslavement constructed institutions capable of governing a large composite territory over more than two and a half centuries. It defended Egypt against Mongol invasion, eliminated the remaining major mainland Crusader states, and made Cairo a leading center of Islamic scholarship, architecture, and regional commerce. The state supported pilgrimage networks in the Hijaz and maintained political connections reaching toward the Mediterranean, the Black Sea, and the Indian Ocean. These achievements were inseparable from taxation, military coercion, hierarchical social relations, and the labor of cultivators and urban workers. A balanced account places the accomplishments and human costs within the same explanatory framework.

The Mamluk experience also complicates conventional narratives of rise and fall. Military household rivalry did not always paralyze government; epidemics damaged society without ending cultural production; commercial intervention could both enrich and burden the state; religious patronage could provide lasting benefits while securing elite fortunes. By studying these tensions, the reader sees the sultanate as an historical system rather than a heroic sequence of warriors or a doomed prelude to Ottoman rule. The subsequent Ottoman chapters will examine the imperial order that absorbed its territories, but the Mamluk period retains a coherent identity of its own: a centuries-long experiment in military sovereignty, regional administration, institutional legitimacy, and the creation of a remarkable urban and intellectual world.

Chapter Summary

The Mamluk Sultanate arose amid the Ayyubid succession crisis of 1249–1250. Mamluks were acquired through enslavement, trained in military households, and commonly manumitted before elite military office. The political strength of their households made them capable of assuming sovereignty, but patronage rivalries meant political succession remained contested. Shajar al-Durr, Aybak, Qutuz, and Baybars reveal how the new regime emerged through both victory and factional violence.

The defeat of Kitbuqa’s Mongol force at Ain Jalut in 1260 gave the Mamluks the opportunity to secure Egypt and Syria, but war with the Ilkhanate persisted into the fourteenth century. Baybars developed communications, fortifications, provincial appointments, and a symbolic Abbasid caliphal institution in Cairo. Subsequent rulers eliminated major mainland Crusader strongholds, culminating in the capture of Acre in 1291, and extended influence through negotiations and campaigns in Cilicia and the Hijaz.

The state was sustained by agricultural taxation, military revenue assignments, commercial duties, civilian administration, and local intermediaries. Al-Nasir Muhammad’s reign illustrates the efforts of a strong sultan to reorganize fiscal claims and subordinate competing military households. Judges, ulama, Sufi organizations, and charitable endowments served important social and religious functions without simply obeying every governmental preference. The civilian majority—farmers, merchants, artisans, scholars, women, and religious minorities—experienced the same government in unequal ways.

Cairo, Damascus, and Aleppo were centers of commerce, education, and artistic production. Their wealth depended on rural cultivation, transport, and far-reaching Mediterranean and Red Sea networks. Important buildings such as Qalawun’s hospital complex and the mosque-madrasa of Sultan Hasan combined royal commemoration with charitable and educational institutions. Luxury objects, manuscripts, historical chronicles, and building inscriptions are indispensable forms of evidence, although each reflects particular patrons and modes of preservation.

The Black Death and recurrent epidemics produced severe human loss and long-term strains on labor, agriculture, and fiscal resources. The transition conventionally dated to Barquq’s accession in 1382 changed the leading military households without founding an entirely different state. Fifteenth-century sultans pursued commercial interventions, military diplomacy, maritime campaigns, and patronage even as economic and geopolitical challenges increased. The sultanate’s late history must be assessed through both resilience and vulnerability.

Ottoman victories in 1516–1517 ended independent Mamluk rule. The conquest did not erase its institutions, communities, or artistic and intellectual traditions; many continued in altered forms within an Ottoman provincial order. The Mamluk period demonstrates how an elite originating in slavery could maintain a long-lived imperial system through military organization, revenue extraction, local negotiation, legal scholarship, international commerce, and cultural patronage—and why its achievements and coercive foundations belong to the same historical account.

Conventional designation for the earlier Mamluk period, linked to the Bahriyya military establishment.
Burji
Conventional name for the later, increasingly Circassian-dominated phase of the sultanate.
Amir
A senior military officeholder commanding men and often receiving revenue assignments.
Sultan
Sovereign exercising effective political and military authority in Egypt and Syria.

Mamluk sultan who led the army against Kitbuqa at Ain Jalut in 1260.
Khushdashiyya
Solidarity and political association among mamluks linked through a shared patron or training household.

Freeborn descendants of mamluks, whose status differed from newly purchased military recruits.

An assignment of specified revenue, frequently supporting military service, not necessarily ownership of the underlying land.

An administrative bureau, often handling state accounts, correspondence, or other official business.
Na’ib
A deputy or governor acting with delegated authority from the ruler.
Waqf
A legally organized endowment assigning property income to specified religious, charitable, or other purposes.
Ulama
Religious scholars and learned specialists with diverse legal and political positions.
Qadi
A judge exercising authority within Islamic legal institutions.

Institution supporting religious and legal education, often through endowment.

An endowed residential or devotional institution associated with Sufi practice.
Bimaristan
A hospital or organized medical establishment often funded through waqf.

Normative framework of public-market and moral oversight associated with officials such as the muhtasib.
Mahmal
Ceremonial palanquin sent with major pilgrimage caravans, especially from Cairo.
Kiswa
Textile covering of the Kaaba, whose provision carried religious and political prestige.
Sharif
A title associated with descent from the Prophet and, in the Hijaz, leading local ruling families.

Prominent commercial networks active in Red Sea and Indian Ocean-linked trade.

Ruler at the moment of transition from Ayyubid to Mamluk authority in 1250.

Battle in September 1260 in which Mamluk forces defeated Kitbuqa’s Mongol command.
Acre
Major mainland Latin Christian stronghold captured by Mamluks in May 1291.

Mid-fourteenth-century plague pandemic that caused severe mortality and recurring demographic disruption.
Barquq
Sultan whose accession in 1382 conventionally marks the Burji period.

Fifteenth-century sultan known for commercial intervention and the conquest of Cyprus in 1426.
Qaytbay
Sultan from 1468 to 1496 associated with military diplomacy and notable architectural patronage.

Battle in August 1516 where Ottoman forces defeated the Mamluks in northern Syria.

Battle near Cairo in January 1517 associated with Ottoman conquest of Egypt.

Chapter Reflection

The Mamluk Sultanate emerged from military slavery but cannot be reduced to that institution alone. For 267 years its rulers maintained a political order across Egypt and Syria using military households, fiscal administration, religious legitimacy, and provincial networks. This political system could be coercive and unstable at the center while supporting sophisticated scholarship, art, commerce, and charitable institutions.

The historical challenge is to evaluate all those dimensions at once. Triumph at Ain Jalut did not ensure permanent military security; spectacular buildings did not prove universal prosperity; epidemic mortality did not stop every form of cultural achievement. The Ottoman conquest in 1517 ended independent Mamluk sovereignty without eliminating the institutions and communities whose history the chapter has traced. Chapter 66 turns to Timur and the post-Mongol order, while Chapter 71 treats Ottoman conquest in full.
