New Kingdom Egypt and the Age of Empire

A History of the Middle East: From Ancient Civilizations to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

In the eastern Nile Delta, at a site the Egyptians called Avaris and modern archaeologists call Tell el-Dab'a, excavators have uncovered a townscape unlike the Nile Valley heartland to the south: houses built in a style associated with the Levant, burials accompanied by donkeys and weapons of a kind familiar from Syria-Palestine, and, layered above them, the palatial remains of rulers who eventually took full pharaonic titles while governing from this delta city rather than from Thebes or Memphis. Two centuries later, the same region tells a very different story. Egyptian soldiers under Thutmose III made camp outside the Levantine city of Megiddo, having marched their army single-file through a dangerous mountain pass that Thutmose's own officers had counseled against, in order to surprise a coalition of Canaanite rulers who did not expect an Egyptian army to arrive by that route at all. Between these two moments—a delta capital ruled by kings of western Asian background, and an Egyptian pharaoh personally leading a Levantine campaign that Egyptian scribes would later celebrate as one of history's first fully narrated battles—lies the transformation this chapter sets out to explain.

Chapter 13 left Egypt in fragments. The centralized authority of the Twelfth Dynasty had weakened during the Thirteenth Dynasty, and by the era historians call the Second Intermediate Period, no single royal house controlled the entire Nile Valley. Rulers based in the eastern Delta, at Avaris, governed a kingdom with deep commercial and demographic connections to the Levant; a rival Theban kingship controlled Upper Egypt; and, to the south, the kingdom of Kush at Kerma exercised its own considerable power in Nubia. This chapter does not treat that fragmentation as a simple story of Egyptian weakness followed by heroic recovery, although Egyptian sources themselves, composed generations after the fact, often told the story that way. It instead treats fragmentation as a genuine historical condition with its own internal logic, out of which a new and different kind of Egyptian state eventually emerged.

That new state is what modern historians call the New Kingdom, beginning with the reunification of Egypt under Ahmose I and continuing through the Eighteenth, Nineteenth, and Twentieth Dynasties. The New Kingdom was not simply the Middle Kingdom restored to its earlier borders. It was a substantially different kind of monarchy: more militarized, more habitually engaged beyond the Nile Valley, more dependent on standing armies, professional officer corps, and an administrative apparatus built to manage garrisons, tribute, and foreign vassals as well as domestic taxation. Egyptian rulers of this era campaigned deep into the Levant and far south into Nubia, exchanged letters and daughters in marriage with the kings of Babylon, Mitanni, and the Hittites, and built temples on a scale that still dominates the modern landscape of Luxor and Karnak. Egypt, in short, became an imperial power fully embedded in the wider political world of the eastern Mediterranean and Near East—the same world this textbook will examine from other vantage points in Chapters 15 and 16.

This chapter traces that transformation from the fall of the Middle Kingdom through the later Twentieth Dynasty, when Egypt's imperial reach and state institutions were under increasing strain. Along the way it will introduce rulers whose reigns illuminate different dimensions of New Kingdom power: Ahmose I, who completed reunification and expulsion of the Hyksos; Hatshepsut, whose reign shows that royal authority in this period rested on trade, temple-building, and ideological construction as much as on warfare; Thutmose III, whose Levantine campaigns created Egypt's most extensive sphere of imperial influence and hegemony in the Levant; Amenhotep III, whose reign shows imperial wealth converted into diplomacy, marriage alliance, and monumental building; Akhenaten, whose religious reforms remain one of the most debated episodes in ancient historiography; and Ramesses II, whose long reign and enormous monuments made him, in the eyes of many later observers, the archetypal pharaoh of empire, even though his most famous battle, at Kadesh, ended in something considerably less clear-cut than his own inscriptions proclaimed.

Several cautions will recur throughout this chapter. The peoples Egypt fought, ruled, married, and traded with—the Hyksos rulers of the Second Intermediate Period, the Nubian populations of Kush, and the many local rulers of Canaan, Amurru, and other Levantine polities—were not merely obstacles or resources in an Egyptian story. They possessed their own institutions, interests, and strategies, even though the surviving evidence is overwhelmingly Egyptian in origin and therefore tells this history disproportionately from Egypt's point of view. This chapter will note that imbalance repeatedly rather than allowing it to pass unremarked. It will also resist several tempting but misleading narratives: that the Hyksos were a foreign horde who conquered Egypt by force and introduced the horse and chariot in one dramatic stroke; that Hatshepsut's reign is chiefly interesting as a puzzle about gender; that Thutmose III's Levant was a modern-style province; that Akhenaten invented monotheism; and that Ramesses II won an unambiguous victory at Kadesh. Genuine historical uncertainty, rather than confident simplification, will guide the treatment of each of these questions.

Finally, this chapter is deliberately Egypt-centered. It examines the Hyksos, Nubians, and Levantine peoples chiefly insofar as they intersect with Egyptian history in this period. Chapter 15 will take up the Hittites, Hurrians, and Canaanite societies of the Bronze Age Levant on their own terms, examining their institutions and histories independent of Egyptian campaigns against them, and Chapter 16 will assemble Egypt, the Hittite Empire, Mitanni, Babylon, Assyria, and the Aegean world into the single interconnected diplomatic and commercial system that characterized the Late Bronze Age—and will examine why that system came apart around 1200 BCE. This chapter's task is narrower and more specific: to explain how and why Egypt itself was transformed by the experience of reunification, conquest, and sustained international engagement, and what that transformation meant for Egyptian kingship, society, and religion across roughly five centuries.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain the political fragmentation of the Second Intermediate Period and identify the major power centers that divided Egypt and Nubia during this era.
  2. Describe the Hyksos as rulers of the eastern Delta with strong western Asian connections, avoiding outdated narratives of a single overwhelming foreign invasion.
  3. Trace the process of reunification and the expulsion of Hyksos rule under Seqenenre Tao, Kamose, and Ahmose I.
  4. Analyze how the experience of fragmentation and warfare reshaped Egyptian military institutions, technology, and frontier policy in the early New Kingdom.
  5. Evaluate Hatshepsut's reign as an expression of royal power through trade, temple-building, and ideology as well as through military authority.
  6. Explain how Thutmose III's campaigns, particularly the Battle of Megiddo, created Egypt's most extensive zone of influence in the Levant, and distinguish direct administration from hegemonic or tributary control.
  7. Compare Egyptian imperial methods in Nubia with Egyptian methods of control in the Levant.
  8. Describe Amenhotep III's reign as a period in which imperial wealth was converted into diplomacy, marriage alliance, and monumental construction.
  9. Analyze Akhenaten's religious reforms and evaluate the ongoing scholarly debate over whether Atenism should be described as monotheism.
  10. Explain the restoration of traditional religious and political order under Tutankhamun, Ay, and Horemheb.
  11. Describe Ramesside military and diplomatic history, including the Battle of Kadesh and the Egyptian-Hittite peace treaty, without treating Egyptian royal inscriptions as neutral historical accounts.
  12. Identify the economic, administrative, and military strains that limited New Kingdom imperial power by the later Twentieth Dynasty.

  2. How should historians describe the Hyksos, given that later Egyptian sources present them as hated foreign occupiers while archaeological evidence suggests a more gradual process of migration, settlement, and political consolidation?

Historical Context

Chapter 13 traced Egyptian state formation from unification around 3100 BCE through the Old Kingdom's pyramid age, the First Intermediate Period's fragmentation, and the Middle Kingdom's reconsolidation under the Eleventh and Twelfth Dynasties. That chapter ended with the weakening of centralized Twelfth Dynasty authority during the Thirteenth Dynasty, a gradual process in which regional power centers reasserted themselves and no single royal line could sustain the level of control exercised by rulers such as Senusret III. This chapter begins precisely where that account left off, in the political landscape historians call the Second Intermediate Period, and follows Egyptian history forward through the reunification, imperial expansion, religious upheaval, and eventual strain of the New Kingdom, ending on the threshold of the wider Bronze Age crisis examined fully in Chapter 16.

Two development processes distinguish this period from earlier Egyptian history and deserve emphasis before the narrative sections begin. First, the eastern Nile Delta had, for generations before the Second Intermediate Period, attracted population movement and settlement from the Levant, driven by trade, seasonal labor, and, at times, deliberate royal policy that used Levantine communities as a source of skilled and unskilled labor. This gradual, long-term process of contact and settlement is essential background for understanding the Hyksos, who should not be imagined as a sudden invading horde but as the eventual political consolidation of communities whose presence in the Delta long predated their assumption of kingship. Second, Nubia to the south had, by this period, developed its own substantial state at Kerma, capable of contesting Egyptian control of the southern frontier and, during the Second Intermediate Period, entering into diplomatic contact with the Hyksos kings of Avaris against Thebes—a reminder that Second Intermediate Period Egypt existed within a triangular political landscape rather than a simple two-sided contest between "Egyptians" and "foreigners."

The chapters that follow this one depend on understanding the New Kingdom as the era in which Egypt became durably entangled with the broader Near Eastern and eastern Mediterranean political system. Chapter 15 will introduce the Hittites, Hurrians, and Canaanite city-states as historical actors in their own right, and Chapter 16 will explain how Egypt, the Hittite Empire, Mitanni, Babylon, and Assyria together constituted an interconnected diplomatic order—sometimes called by modern historians a "club of great powers"—whose correspondence, treaties, and royal marriages this chapter will begin to introduce through Egyptian evidence such as the Amarna Letters.

Section 14.1

Egypt after the Middle Kingdom: The Second Intermediate Period

The Middle Kingdom's later strength rested heavily on the personal authority of individual Twelfth Dynasty rulers, particularly Senusret III and Amenemhat III, examined in Chapter 13. By the Thirteenth Dynasty, royal succession became increasingly rapid, with numerous kings occupying the throne for short periods. The causes of this rapid succession are difficult to reconstruct with confidence, and Egyptologists caution against treating it, by itself, as proof of administrative collapse: functioning bureaucracies and building programs persisted under at least some Thirteenth Dynasty rulers even as central royal authority became less commanding, over time, than it had been under the Twelfth Dynasty's strongest kings.

What is clear is that by the middle of the seventeenth century BCE, Egypt no longer functioned as a single unified kingdom. Three distinct power centers had emerged, each worth understanding in its own terms rather than merely as a foil to the others. In the eastern Nile Delta, a population with strong Levantine cultural and material connections, present in the region for generations through trade and settlement, had by this time developed independent political authority, eventually consolidating into the kingship historians call the Fifteenth Dynasty, ruling from the city of Avaris. In Upper Egypt, a Theban-based Seventeenth Dynasty maintained a more traditionally Egyptian royal ideology while controlling a reduced southern kingdom. And in Nubia, south of Egypt's traditional frontier at the First Cataract, the kingdom of Kush, centered on the city of Kerma, had developed into a powerful and sophisticated state in its own right, with monumental architecture, extensive trade networks reaching into sub-Saharan Africa, and its own political institutions independent of Egyptian models.

These three powers did not exist in simple isolation from one another. Kamose's own Second Stela, a Dynasty Seventeen royal commemoration composed during the conflict itself, claims that his forces intercepted a written message from the Hyksos king Apepi to the ruler of Kush, seeking cooperation against Thebes from both north and south simultaneously. As a contemporary Theban royal monument, the stela is valuable evidence, but it is also partisan: it obviously serves Theban interests by depicting the Hyksos as conspiring with a hostile foreign power, and the alleged letter survives only through Kamose's own account of intercepting it, not independently. Even read cautiously, however, the episode suggests that Second Intermediate Period Egypt existed within a genuinely triangular political landscape rather than a straightforward story of a unified "Egypt" resisting "foreign" occupation.

Section 14.2

Avaris, the Hyksos, and the Fifteenth Dynasty

Excavations at Tell el-Dab'a, the site of ancient Avaris, have transformed modern understanding of the Hyksos far beyond the hostile caricature preserved in later Egyptian tradition, most famously in the much later account of the Egyptian priest Manetho, writing in the third century BCE, who described a sudden and violent foreign conquest that overthrew Egyptian rule by force. Archaeological stratigraphy at Avaris instead reveals a long, gradual process: houses, burial customs, and material culture showing clear Levantine characteristics appear in the site's lower, earlier strata, well before any Hyksos king ruled Egypt, indicating substantial and sustained population presence and cultural continuity between the eastern Delta and Syria-Palestine that predates the Fifteenth Dynasty's political emergence by generations.

This evidence supports a model in which communities of Levantine background settled in the eastern Delta over an extended period, likely drawn by trade opportunities, agricultural land, and, in some periods, active Egyptian recruitment of foreign labor, and gradually built up local political influence until, amid the weakening of Thirteenth Dynasty central authority described in Section 14.1, a Hyksos ruling family was able to establish independent kingship at Avaris. The rulers of this Fifteenth Dynasty took full Egyptian royal titulary, adopted Egyptian administrative practices and religious forms alongside their own traditions, worshipped the Egyptian god Seth in a form associated with the Levantine storm god Baal, and issued royal monuments in an Egyptian style—evidence that Hyksos kingship represented a genuine, if distinctive, participation in Egyptian political culture rather than a simple foreign military occupation imposing an alien order on a passive population.

The most durable popular misconception about the Hyksos holds that they introduced the horse, war chariot, and composite bow to Egypt in one sudden technological transfer accompanying a military conquest. The archaeological and textual evidence is considerably more ambiguous. Horse remains and chariot-related equipment appear in the archaeological record of the wider eastern Mediterranean and Near East gradually over this period rather than arriving as a single package, and it remains uncertain precisely when and through what channels these technologies reached the Nile Valley. What is reasonably well supported is that these technologies became significant in Egyptian warfare during the era of Hyksos rule and the subsequent wars of reunification, and that Theban forces adopted and adapted them in the course of the conflict examined in Section 14.3—but historians should resist the older narrative in which the Hyksos are credited, almost as if by a single dramatic act, with modernizing an otherwise technologically static Egypt.

Historical Vocabulary
Hegemonic versus Territorial Empire

Historians of the ancient Near East distinguish between two broad models of imperial control that will recur throughout this chapter. A territorial empire directly incorporates conquered land into the ruling state's administrative structure, typically through provincial governors, garrisons, taxation systems, and settlers answerable to the central government. A hegemonic or tributary empire, by contrast, leaves local rulers in place and allows considerable local autonomy in exchange for tribute payments, military cooperation, loyalty oaths, and symbolic acknowledgment of the dominant power's superiority, intervening directly only when local rulers fail to comply or when rival powers threaten the arrangement.

As Sections 14.6 and 14.7 will show, Egyptian control during the New Kingdom combined elements of both models, and did so differently in Nubia than in the Levant. Understanding this distinction helps prevent students from imagining ancient Egyptian "empire" as a uniform system resembling a modern colonial administration, when in practice it varied considerably by region, period, and local circumstance.

Section 14.3

Thebes, Kamose, Ahmose, and the Expulsion of the Hyksos

The war that ended Hyksos rule was not a single battle but a sustained, multi-generational campaign whose surviving evidence—largely composed by the eventual Theban victors—must be read with some caution regarding both chronology and motive. The Theban ruler Seqenenre Tao, whose mummy displays severe head wounds consistent with battle injuries, appears to have initiated open conflict with Avaris, although the precise circumstances of his death and the sequence of early campaigns remain incompletely documented. His successor, Kamose, left two inscribed stelae at Karnak recording his own account of renewed war against the Hyksos king Apepi, including the intercepted-message episode discussed in Section 14.1, and describing raids northward along the Nile and asserting Theban determination to reunify Egypt under a single Egyptian kingship—texts that, like most Theban sources for this conflict, present a one-sided view of a war whose Hyksos perspective does not survive independently.

Reunification was completed under Kamose's successor, Ahmose I, whose reign traditionally marks the beginning of the Eighteenth Dynasty and the New Kingdom. Egyptian sources describe Ahmose's forces besieging and eventually capturing Avaris itself, followed by campaigns pursuing Hyksos remnants into southern Canaan, and separate campaigns securing Egypt's southern frontier against Kush. These campaigns should be understood as a sustained process of consolidation extending over multiple years rather than a single decisive battle, and Ahmose's reign also involved substantial internal reorganization, including the reassertion of royal authority over provincial elites and renewed royal building projects signaling restored centralized kingship.

The political and psychological legacy of this period mattered enormously for what followed. Having experienced both internal fragmentation and, in Theban ideology, a war against foreign rule, the rulers of the early Eighteenth Dynasty inherited a political culture newly attentive to frontier security, military capability, and the dangers of allowing rival power centers to develop unchecked—concerns that, as Section 14.4 explains, would shape New Kingdom state institutions for generations to come.

Section 14.4

Why the New Kingdom Became Expansionist

Historians generally agree that the New Kingdom state differed from its Middle Kingdom predecessor in its degree of militarization and its sustained attention to territory and influence beyond Egypt's traditional borders, but they are more cautious about attributing this shift to any single cause. The experience of the Second Intermediate Period certainly mattered: a ruling elite that had fought for generations to reunify the country, and that had directly experienced the vulnerability created by a divided Egypt and an independent, potentially hostile Nubian kingdom to the south, had strong incentives to invest in military institutions capable of preventing similar vulnerabilities in the future. This does not mean that fragmentation mechanically "caused" imperialism in any deterministic sense—human decisions, court politics, and specific strategic calculations mattered enormously—but the historical sequence is a genuine and important part of the explanation.

The early Eighteenth Dynasty built or expanded a number of military institutions that distinguish this period from earlier Egyptian history. Chariot forces, organized into units with their own officers and support personnel, became a prestigious and increasingly central component of the Egyptian army, complementing rather than simply replacing infantry organized into regional and later more standardized formations. The composite bow, constructed from laminated layers of wood, horn, and sinew, offered greater range and power than earlier simple bows and became strongly associated with chariot warfare and with royal imagery of the pharaoh as a skilled archer. Military logistics also developed considerably: campaigns into the Levant required organized supply lines, coastal and inland fortresses, and careful management of seasonal campaigning windows, all of which required administrative capacity beyond what earlier Egyptian warfare, generally more defensive and more locally focused, had required.

It is important not to overstate the novelty of these developments or to attribute them entirely to Hyksos influence, as older scholarship sometimes did. Egyptian society had long possessed organized military forces, and many elements of New Kingdom military culture developed through Egypt's own experience of the reunification wars and subsequent campaigning rather than through direct technological transfer from a single external source. What changed most significantly was not any single piece of equipment but the scale, permanence, and centrality of military institutions within the state, and the willingness of Eighteenth Dynasty kings to use those institutions for sustained campaigning far beyond Egypt's traditional frontiers rather than primarily for frontier defense.

Section 14.5

Hatshepsut and the Many Forms of Royal Power

Hatshepsut, daughter of Thutmose I and widow of Thutmose II, initially governed as regent for her young stepson and nephew, Thutmose III, before assuming the full royal titulary of a pharaoh in her own right, a step for which precedents existed but which remained unusual. Egyptian royal ideology was built around the office of kingship as a male role linked to the god Horus, and Hatshepsut's monuments respond to this tension in varied ways: some depict her in traditionally male royal costume, including the false beard associated with kingship, while other texts and images acknowledge her identity more directly. Egyptologists caution against reducing her reign to a simple story of "a woman pretending to be a man," since Egyptian kingship was understood as an office and a set of divine functions that could, in unusual circumstances, be occupied by a woman without requiring her to erase her identity entirely; the visual conventions she employed reflect the demands of royal ideology and iconography more than any straightforward personal claim to masculinity.

Hatshepsut's reign is unusually well documented for its non-military achievements, and this chapter treats those achievements as substantive expressions of royal power rather than as a lesser alternative to warfare. Her mortuary temple at Deir el-Bahari, built into the cliffs of western Thebes, is among the most architecturally distinctive monuments of ancient Egypt, and its reliefs record in detail a long-distance trading expedition to the land of Punt, likely located along the Red Sea coast of the Horn of Africa, which brought back incense, exotic woods, ivory, gold, and living myrrh trees—a voyage celebrated in text and image as a triumph of royal administration and divine favor. She also sponsored extensive construction and restoration at Karnak and elsewhere, asserting continuity with earlier kingship while developing her own distinctive building program.

Some later New Kingdom monuments show evidence of deliberate efforts to remove or alter Hatshepsut's name and image from public record, a phenomenon scholars once attributed simply to hostility from Thutmose III seeking revenge for having been overshadowed during her reign. More recent scholarship treats this erasure with greater caution, noting that it appears to have occurred later in Thutmose III's reign rather than immediately after Hatshepsut's death, and suggesting that concerns about orderly succession, precedent, and the proper ideological ordering of king lists may have mattered as much as personal animosity. The precise motivations remain a matter of ongoing scholarly discussion rather than settled fact.

Historical Vocabulary
Royal Titulary and Divine Kingship

Egyptian royal titulary developed gradually over many centuries. By the Middle Kingdom, the full titulary had become a formal set of five names and epithets, each linking the king to a different divine or cosmic role: the Horus name, the Nebty ("Two Ladies") name, the Golden Horus name, the throne name (or prenomen), and the birth name (or nomen), the latter two typically enclosed in the oval cartouche familiar from Egyptian monuments. Assuming the full royal titulary was the formal marker of kingship itself, distinct from lesser titles such as regent or God's Wife of Amun.

Hatshepsut's assumption of the full royal titulary, discussed above, is therefore significant not merely as a personal or biographical fact but as a formal ideological claim to the office of kingship in its own right, distinct from and beyond the regency she had exercised earlier on behalf of Thutmose III. Because Egyptian kingship was conceived as fundamentally linked to the god Horus and expressed through titulary, iconography, and ritual roles understood in male terms, a woman holding the full titulary required the adaptation, rather than the abandonment, of established royal conventions—a pattern this chapter treats as evidence of ideological flexibility within Egyptian kingship rather than as an anomaly to be explained away.

Section 14.6

Thutmose III and the Creation of an Egyptian Empire

Following Hatshepsut's death, Thutmose III ruled alone for over three decades and conducted, according to Egyptian records, at least seventeen military campaigns into the Levant, more than any other New Kingdom pharaoh. His first and most celebrated campaign, against a coalition of Canaanite rulers gathered at Megiddo, is recorded in unusually detailed narrative form on the walls of the temple of Amun at Karnak, in what Egyptologists consider one of the earliest surviving detailed battle narratives from any ancient civilization, even though the account was composed to celebrate royal achievement and should be read with appropriate attention to its propagandistic purpose.

According to this account, Thutmose III's war council debated three possible routes to reach Megiddo, two considered safer but longer, and one—a narrow mountain pass—considered faster but dangerous, since an army marching through it would be strung out and vulnerable to ambush. Thutmose reportedly chose the dangerous route himself, over his officers' objections, and the gamble succeeded: the coalition forces, apparently not expecting an Egyptian approach from that direction, were caught unprepared, and after an initial battle outside the city, Egyptian forces settled into a siege that eventually forced Megiddo's surrender. The text emphasizes Thutmose's personal courage and strategic judgment, a literary convention typical of Egyptian royal inscriptions that historians must weigh against the possibility that actual battlefield decision-making involved considerably more collective input from experienced officers than the pharaoh-centered narrative suggests.

Subsequent campaigns extended Egyptian influence through Canaan and into Syria, bringing Thutmose III's forces into contact and eventual conflict with the kingdom of Mitanni, a major Hurrian-ruled power in northern Syria and upper Mesopotamia examined more fully in Chapters 15 and 16. Rather than annexing conquered Levantine territory as directly administered Egyptian provinces, Egyptian policy generally left local rulers in place as vassals bound by loyalty oaths, required to pay tribute, provide military support when called upon, and, in a notable and revealing practice, send sons of the ruling family to be raised at the Egyptian court—a policy that served simultaneously as a guarantee of loyalty, a means of Egyptianizing future rulers, and an opportunity for those same individuals to later return home with intimate knowledge of Egyptian court politics that could be leveraged in either direction. This overall pattern corresponds to the hegemonic model of empire introduced in Section 14.2's Historical Vocabulary feature, distinguished from more direct territorial administration.

Section 14.7

Empire in Nubia and the Levant

Egyptian control took distinctly different institutional forms in its two major spheres of imperial activity, and this section treats each on its own terms rather than assuming a single uniform "Egyptian empire." In Nubia, Egyptian kings from the early Eighteenth Dynasty onward pursued a policy considerably closer to direct territorial administration than anything attempted in the Levant. Following the conquest of Kerma and the extension of Egyptian control deep into Nubian territory, the New Kingdom state established an administrative office, the Viceroy of Kush, typically held by a senior Egyptian official who governed Nubia on the king's behalf, supported by Egyptian garrisons, fortified towns, and temples built in Egyptian style that served simultaneously as religious institutions and instruments of ideological incorporation. Nubian gold, extracted under the oversight of Egyptian imperial administration, funded much of the New Kingdom's monumental building and international diplomacy. Mining, processing, and tribute operated within Egyptian-controlled structures, and compulsory or coerced labor may have played an important role in extraction, although the actual labor regimes are incompletely documented and should not be treated as uniform across the period; historians should not lose sight of the likely human cost this extraction imposed on Nubian populations even where the specific evidence remains limited.

Nubian populations were not, however, simply passive subjects. Egyptianized Nubian elites appear in the archaeological and textual record occupying positions of local authority under overall Egyptian supervision, and Nubian cultural traditions persisted and interacted with imported Egyptian religious and artistic forms in ways that produced distinctive local variations rather than uniform replication of Nilotic Egyptian culture. Nubian soldiers, notably archers, also served within Egyptian military forces, complicating any simple division between Egyptian rulers and Nubian subjects.

In the Levant, by contrast, Egyptian control operated mostly through the hegemonic mechanisms introduced in Section 14.6: vassal treaties, tribute, occasional punitive campaigns against rebellious rulers, garrisons at a smaller number of strategically important sites, and the practice of raising the sons of local rulers at the Egyptian court. Direct Egyptian administrative presence was considerably thinner on the ground than in Nubia, reflecting both the greater distance and logistical difficulty of sustained occupation and the political reality that Canaan and Syria were contested among multiple regional powers—Egypt, Mitanni, and later the Hittite Empire—in a way that made outright annexation considerably riskier and more costly than maintaining a looser hegemonic arrangement. Local Canaanite and Syrian rulers retained meaningful autonomy in internal affairs even as they acknowledged Egyptian overlordship, and, as the Amarna Letters discussed in Section 14.8 will show, those rulers actively used correspondence with the Egyptian court to pursue their own local rivalries and interests rather than functioning merely as passive Egyptian instruments.

Section 14.8

Amenhotep III: Wealth, Diplomacy, and Monumental Kingship

The reign of Amenhotep III, spanning roughly four decades in the mid-fourteenth century BCE, is often described by historians as a high point of New Kingdom stability and prosperity, during which Egypt's accumulated imperial wealth—drawn from Nubian gold, Levantine tribute, and expanding international trade—was converted into diplomacy, marriage alliance, and building on an extraordinary scale rather than into further large-scale military campaigning. Amenhotep III conducted relatively few major military campaigns compared with Thutmose III, relying instead on Egypt's established reputation for power and wealth to maintain its position among the major courts of the Late Bronze Age Near East.

Diplomatic marriage became a particularly important instrument of this policy. Amenhotep III received princesses from Mitanni and from Babylon as wives, cementing alliances through kinship in a diplomatic practice common among the major powers of the period, while notably declining, according to surviving correspondence, similar requests from foreign rulers seeking Egyptian princesses in marriage—a one-directional pattern that reflects Egyptian rulers' particular ideological reluctance to send royal Egyptian women to foreign courts, even as they readily received foreign women into the Egyptian royal household. Egypt's construction projects during this reign were similarly vast: the temple complex at Luxor, extensive additions at Karnak, and Amenhotep III's own mortuary temple, of which the massive Colossi of Memnon are the most visible surviving remnant, all attest to the scale of resources available to the Egyptian crown during this period.

The beginning of the Amarna Letters archive, a collection of clay tablets written in Akkadian cuneiform—the diplomatic language of the Late Bronze Age Near East—and discovered at the later site of Akhetaten, dates from the later years of Amenhotep III's reign and continues into the reign of his son Akhenaten. These letters, comprising correspondence between the Egyptian court and the rulers of Babylon, Mitanni, Assyria, and the Hittites, as well as numerous letters from Egyptian vassal rulers in Canaan and Syria, provide an invaluable and unusually direct window into Late Bronze Age diplomacy. This chapter introduces the archive primarily as evidence for Egyptian foreign relations under Amenhotep III and Akhenaten; Chapter 16 will examine the archive more fully as evidence for the broader international system linking Egypt, Mitanni, Babylon, Assyria, and the Hittite Empire.

Section 14.9

Akhenaten and the Aten Revolution

Amenhotep IV, who would take the name Akhenaten early in his reign, initiated one of the most striking and most debated transformations in ancient Egyptian religious and political history. Building on religious currents already visible under his father, which had increasingly emphasized the sun disk, or Aten, as a supreme divine force, Akhenaten elevated Aten worship to a uniquely privileged position, progressively restricting the traditional cults of other Egyptian deities, most consequentially the powerful cult of Amun centered at Karnak, whose priesthood had accumulated substantial wealth and influence over preceding generations. Akhenaten relocated the royal capital from Thebes to a newly constructed city, Akhetaten, on a previously undeveloped site in Middle Egypt, physically and symbolically distancing the new religious order from the established religious and political center of Amun worship.

Royal art during this period underwent a dramatic stylistic transformation, moving away from the idealized, timeless conventions of earlier Egyptian royal representation toward a more distinctive, sometimes exaggerated naturalism, depicting Akhenaten, his chief royal wife Nefertiti, and their daughters in informal domestic scenes and with unusual physical characteristics whose interpretation—whether reflecting an actual physical condition, evolving artistic convention, religious symbolism, or some combination of these—remains debated among Egyptologists. Religious worship also became increasingly centered on the royal family itself, with the king and queen represented as the primary, and in some contexts the sole, intermediaries between the Aten and humanity, a concentration of religious authority around kingship that had significant implications for the traditional priestly establishment's role and status.

Historical Debate

Was Atenism Monotheism?

The question: Should Akhenaten's promotion of the Aten be described as history's first monotheism, or does that label overstate or mischaracterize what actually occurred?

Interpretation A — Atenism represented a genuine monotheistic revolution: Some historians and Egyptologists, echoing an interpretive tradition that gained particular prominence in the twentieth century, argue that Akhenaten's exclusive elevation of the Aten, his active suppression of other cults, and his removal of the traditional plural word for "gods" from some inscriptions together constitute a genuine, if short-lived, monotheistic reform—one that some have speculatively linked to later developments in the history of monotheistic religion in the region, examined beginning in Chapter 18, though such connections remain highly uncertain and are not treated as established fact in this textbook.
Interpretation B — Atenism is better described as monolatry or exclusive royal solar theology: Other historians argue that the Aten was elevated to unique prominence primarily as an instrument of royal and political power rather than as an abstract theological monotheism, that other deities and their symbolism were never entirely denied existence even if their worship was restricted, and that describing the reform as "monotheism" imports a later theological category not fully appropriate to Akhenaten's own religious and political context. On this view, terms such as monolatry, exclusive royal solar theology, or henotheism describe the phenomenon more accurately.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the same body of temple inscriptions, royal hymns to the Aten, the archaeological remains of Akhetaten, and the pattern of restrictions on other cults, but differ in how they weigh the theological content of these sources against their evident political function.

Why it matters: This textbook uses the term "Atenism" and describes the reform as a distinctive royal religious program without asserting that it should be classified definitively as monotheism, reflecting the genuine and unresolved character of this scholarly debate.

Student task: Using the evidence described above, construct an argument for each interpretation and explain which you find more persuasive, being explicit about what evidence would be needed to resolve the debate more conclusively.

Section 14.10

Tutankhamun, Restoration, and the End of Dynasty 18

Akhenaten's religious program did not survive him for long. The succession immediately following his death remains difficult to reconstruct with confidence, involving one or more short-reigned figures—conventionally associated with the names Smenkhkare and/or Neferneferuaten, whose precise identities and order of rule remain debated among Egyptologists—before the young Tutankhaten came to the throne. Egypt's kingdom faced a court in which the traditional priesthoods, particularly that of Amun, had been displaced from their accustomed position for over a decade and in which administrative and diplomatic attention to the Levant, as some Amarna Letters from besieged or threatened vassal rulers suggest, may have suffered from reduced royal engagement during the Amarna period—though the extent and causes of any such neglect remain debated among historians, who caution against assuming that Akhenaten simply ignored foreign affairs.

Early in his reign, the young king changed his name from Tutankhaten to Tutankhamun, signaling the restoration of Amun to religious primacy, and a surviving monument known as the Restoration Stela explicitly describes the neglect of the traditional temples during the preceding period and announces the king's program of restoration, including renewed royal patronage of Amun and other traditional cults and reopening of temples that had fallen into disuse. Akhetaten was abandoned as the royal capital, and the traditional centers of Thebes and Memphis resumed their former prominence. Tutankhamun's own historical importance considerably exceeds the fame brought to him by the modern discovery of his largely intact tomb in 1922; his reign marks the deliberate, carefully managed reconstruction of religious and political normalcy after a genuinely disruptive religious experiment, a process his advisors—including the influential courtier and later king Ay, and the military commander and later king Horemheb—appear to have guided closely given the king's youth.

Following Tutankhamun's early death without a surviving heir, Ay briefly held the throne, followed by Horemheb, a career military official whose reign completed the restoration process and worked to erase remaining traces of the Amarna period from official monuments and king lists—a program that intensified during his own reign and continued under his Nineteenth Dynasty successors. Later king lists omitted Akhenaten and his immediate successors, including Tutankhamun and Ay, from official royal sequences, and Horemheb's monuments in some cases directly usurped or overwrote those of Tutankhamun. Akhenaten himself was subjected to particularly hostile treatment in later Egyptian historical memory, sometimes referred to in later documents simply as "the enemy," though this harshest language was not applied uniformly to every Amarna-era ruler. This erasure explains why the Amarna period remained comparatively obscure in ancient Egyptian historical memory until modern archaeological rediscovery, and it should be read as a deliberate act of political and religious reconstruction rather than as evidence that the episode was historically unimportant.

Section 14.11

Ramesside Egypt: Seti I, Ramesses II, and Imperial Competition

Horemheb's death without a surviving royal heir brought the throne to Ramesses I, a former military official whose brief reign inaugurated the Nineteenth Dynasty and the era historians call Ramesside Egypt. His son, Seti I, and grandson, Ramesses II, pursued renewed and vigorous military campaigning in the Levant, responding in part to the growing power of the Hittite Empire, which had absorbed Mitanni's former sphere of influence in northern Syria and increasingly contested Egyptian claims over the region, a rivalry examined further from the Hittite side in Chapter 15.

Ramesses II, whose reign of some sixty-six years made him one of the longest-reigning and most extensively commemorated of all Egyptian kings, is most famous in military terms for the Battle of Kadesh, fought around 1274 BCE against the Hittite king Muwatalli II near the Orontes River in Syria. Egyptian inscriptions and reliefs, carved at multiple temple sites including Abu Simbel and the Ramesseum, present an elaborate narrative in which Ramesses, having been misled by captured spies into believing the Hittite army was still far away, found his forces divided and vulnerable to a surprise Hittite chariot attack, and personally rallied his own division to avoid catastrophic defeat before the timely arrival of reinforcements secured the field.

Historians treat this account with considerable caution. It survives only through Egyptian royal inscriptions explicitly designed to celebrate Ramesses's personal courage and divine favor, no comparable Hittite narrative account of the battle survives to provide an independent perspective, and the actual strategic outcome appears considerably less favorable to Egypt than the inscriptions suggest: Ramesses's forces withdrew from the field without capturing Kadesh, the city and the broader region of Amurru remained within the Hittite sphere of influence in the years that followed, and subsequent Egyptian campaigns failed to reverse this outcome. Most historians therefore describe Kadesh as a battle whose immediate tactical events remain genuinely contested and whose strategic consequences favored the Hittites more than Ramesses's own monuments acknowledge, even though outright Egyptian defeat would also overstate the case, since Egyptian forces avoided the destruction that the initial surprise attack threatened.

The eventual resolution of this prolonged rivalry came not through further warfare but through diplomacy: around 1259 BCE, Ramesses II and the Hittite king Hattusili III concluded a peace treaty, preserved in both Egyptian hieroglyphic and Hittite cuneiform versions, that formalized peace and mutual nonaggression, pledged mutual military assistance against external threats and internal rebellion, and provided for the extradition of fugitives, while effectively accepting the existing balance of power in Syria rather than defining a precise formal boundary line—one of the earliest surviving international peace treaties in world history and a document this textbook will examine further, from the Hittite perspective and within the wider context of Late Bronze Age diplomacy, in Chapters 15 and 16. The treaty was later reinforced by Ramesses's marriage to a Hittite princess, illustrating how diplomatic marriage, introduced in Section 14.8 as an instrument of Amenhotep III's foreign policy, remained a central diplomatic tool throughout this period.

Section 14.12

The Later New Kingdom and the Limits of Empire

The decades following Ramesses II's long reign reveal an Egyptian state increasingly strained by the costs of sustaining its imperial position. Ramesses III, ruling in the early Twentieth Dynasty, faced significant military challenges, including conflicts with groups Egyptian inscriptions describe using terms modern scholars conventionally translate as the "Sea Peoples"—a label that should be used cautiously, since it refers to a loosely defined and possibly heterogeneous set of populations known chiefly through hostile Egyptian sources, and whose relationship to the wider pattern of instability affecting the eastern Mediterranean during this period is examined fully, and with appropriate attention to ongoing scholarly debate over causation, in Chapter 16. This chapter introduces these conflicts only insofar as they affected Egypt directly, without attempting to resolve the larger question of what caused the broader Late Bronze Age crisis.

Domestic evidence from this period points to genuine institutional and economic strain. At Deir el-Medina, the specialized workers' village housing the artisans who built royal tombs in the Valley of the Kings, the Turin Strike Papyrus and related administrative records document a strike in Year 29 of Ramesses III, often placed around 1157–1155 BCE, in which workers, having not received their customary grain rations on schedule, staged organized work stoppages and sit-ins, in what is among the earliest documented labor actions in recorded history. The episode illustrates both effective worker organization and, more importantly for this chapter's argument, direct evidence of serious provisioning and administrative difficulties within the royal necropolis workforce, consistent with broader late-New-Kingdom economic and political strain, even though the strike papyrus itself documents a specific provisioning failure rather than proving a single, general "crisis" affecting the whole of the New Kingdom state.

Several factors likely combined to produce this strain: the cumulative cost of sustained military campaigning and garrison maintenance across Nubia and the Levant; disruptions to long-distance trade networks affecting the flow of resources into Egypt; possible climatic pressures affecting agricultural yields, a subject of ongoing environmental-historical research; and the sheer administrative burden of sustaining a large standing military and a sprawling temple economy over multiple centuries. Historians resist attributing this strain to any single cause, and this chapter deliberately stops short of narrating Egypt's involvement in the fuller Bronze Age collapse, since that wider international story—spanning the Hittite Empire's fragmentation, upheaval in the Aegean world, and the interconnected trade and diplomatic system introduced in Section 14.8—belongs properly to Chapter 16, which will draw together the evidence from Egypt, Anatolia, the Levant, and the Aegean to examine why the Late Bronze Age international order came apart around 1200 BCE.

Living within and beside Egyptian Power

From the perspective of an Egyptian soldier or chariot officer: Service in a Levantine campaign offers prestige, plunder, and possible advancement, but also requires enduring long marches, unfamiliar terrain, and the genuine danger of battle far from home, in a military institution increasingly central to royal ideology and personal opportunity alike.
From the perspective of a Canaanite or Syrian vassal ruler: Survival and local authority depend on carefully balancing nominal loyalty to a distant Egyptian king against the immediate pressures of local rivals, occasional threats from Hittite or Mitannian influence, and the practical need to send tribute and, at times, one's own sons to the Egyptian court as a guarantee of continued goodwill.
From the perspective of a Nubian worker in an Egyptian-controlled gold-producing district: Egyptian control means labor and resources organized under close administrative supervision, quite possibly involving compulsory service, in conditions that leave little room for the kind of local autonomy that Levantine vassal rulers retained, even as some Nubian elites accommodate and adapt to Egyptian religious and administrative forms.
From the perspective of a priest of Amun during the Amarna period: The sudden restriction of the traditional cult, loss of royal patronage, and relocation of the court away from Thebes threaten both religious life and the economic and institutional position of the temple, creating strong incentive to support restoration once the opportunity arises.
From the perspective of a royal necropolis worker at Deir el-Medina: Skilled, specialized labor on royal tombs depends on a reliable flow of grain rations and supplies from the state; when that flow is disrupted, organized collective action becomes a reasonable and effective response rather than a spontaneous outburst.
Continuity and Change Over Time

Middle Kingdom and New Kingdom Kingship

What Changed

  • Standing military institutions, including chariot forces and a more professionalized officer corps, became far more central to royal power and royal ideology than they had been under Middle Kingdom rulers
  • Egyptian kingship became habitually engaged in sustained campaigning and diplomacy far beyond the Nile Valley, in the Levant, in Nubia, and among the major courts of the Late Bronze Age Near East
  • Royal ideology absorbed new imagery emphasizing the king as a victorious warrior and skilled archer, alongside older ideals of the king as guarantor of cosmic order
  • Administrative institutions expanded to manage garrisons, tribute, foreign vassals, and, in Nubia, direct territorial governance under the Viceroy of Kush

What Stayed the Same

  • Kingship remained framed in traditional Egyptian religious terms, linking the king to Horus and to the maintenance of cosmic and social order (maat)
  • Temple building and patronage remained a central expression of royal legitimacy, even as the scale and international context of that patronage changed considerably
  • Royal monuments and inscriptions continued to present idealized, propagandistic narratives of royal achievement rather than neutral historical accounts
  • The traditional priestly establishment, particularly the cult of Amun, remained a powerful institutional force whose interests rulers needed to manage, as the Amarna episode and its reversal illustrate

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both continuity and change helps students avoid treating the New Kingdom as a wholly new form of Egyptian civilization detached from earlier tradition, while still appreciating the genuine institutional transformation that fragmentation, reunification, and sustained international engagement produced.

Cause and Effect

How Fragmentation Reshaped Egyptian Military Policy

Long-term causes

Gradual Levantine settlement in the eastern Delta over generations created conditions from which independent Hyksos kingship eventually emerged, while weakening Thirteenth Dynasty central authority allowed Theban and Nubian power centers to consolidate independently.

Short-term causes

The Theban wars of reunification under Seqenenre Tao, Kamose, and Ahmose I required Egypt to develop more effective military organization, including chariot forces and composite bow technology, in order to defeat Hyksos and, separately, Nubian forces.

Effect

The early Eighteenth Dynasty inherited both the military capability developed during the reunification wars and a strong political incentive to prevent renewed fragmentation, producing a state considerably more inclined toward sustained frontier campaigning and international engagement than its Middle Kingdom predecessor.

This textbook's response

This textbook treats the connection between Second Intermediate Period fragmentation and New Kingdom militarism as a significant contributing factor rather than a simple mechanical cause, insisting that court politics, individual rulers' decisions, and specific strategic circumstances also shaped the pace and direction of Egyptian expansion.

Historical complexity: No single cause explains why Egypt became an expansionist imperial power in this period; the experience of fragmentation and reunification interacted with technological change, court politics, and specific opportunities and threats presented by neighboring powers such as Mitanni, Kush, and later the Hittite Empire.

Everyday Life and Social History

Deir el-Medina: A Community of Royal Tomb Builders

The village of Deir el-Medina, established to house the specialized workforce responsible for excavating and decorating royal tombs in the Valley of the Kings, provides an unusually rich body of evidence for the daily lives of skilled, literate, state-employed workers in New Kingdom Egypt—evidence considerably more detailed than what survives for most other segments of Egyptian society. Surviving administrative records document work schedules, absences, rations of grain, fish, vegetables, and other supplies distributed to workers and their families, and disputes brought before local courts concerning debts, property, and family matters, offering historians a rare window into the practical texture of skilled laborers' lives rather than only the idealized world of royal inscriptions.

The community's relatively high literacy rate, a product of the specialized and often hereditary nature of tomb-building and decoration work, produced an unusually large body of personal letters, literary texts, and informal notes on pottery shards known as ostraca, through which historians can trace individual relationships, grievances, and everyday concerns across multiple generations of the same families. The workers' strike documented in the Turin Strike Papyrus, Year 29 of Ramesses III (often placed around 1157–1155 BCE) and discussed in Section 14.12, stands as the best-documented labor action from this community and from ancient Egypt more broadly, illustrating both the practical dependence of specialized royal workers on a functioning state supply system and their capacity for organized collective response when that system failed to meet its obligations.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced Egypt's transformation from the political fragmentation of the Second Intermediate Period into the militarized, internationally engaged imperial state historians call the New Kingdom. Section 14.1 established the triangular political landscape of the Second Intermediate Period, in which a Hyksos kingdom at Avaris, a Theban kingdom in Upper Egypt, and the Nubian kingdom of Kush at Kerma coexisted and interacted, sometimes in direct communication with one another, rather than presenting a simple two-sided story of Egyptian resistance to foreign occupation.

Section 14.2 examined the Hyksos themselves, showing through archaeological evidence at Avaris that their rise to kingship reflected a long, gradual process of Levantine settlement in the eastern Delta rather than a single dramatic invasion, and cautioning against attributing the introduction of chariot warfare and the composite bow to Hyksos rule alone. Section 14.3 traced the Theban wars of reunification under Seqenenre Tao, Kamose, and Ahmose I, drawing on Theban sources that, while informative, present a necessarily one-sided account of the conflict.

Section 14.4 argued that the experience of fragmentation and reunification helped produce, without simply determining, a New Kingdom state considerably more attentive to military institutions, frontier security, and sustained engagement beyond Egypt's traditional borders than its Middle Kingdom predecessor had been. Sections 14.5 through 14.8 then examined the varied expressions of New Kingdom royal power across the fifteenth and fourteenth centuries BCE: Hatshepsut's reign, emphasizing trade, temple-building, and ideological presentation of female kingship; Thutmose III's extensive Levantine campaigns, beginning with the Battle of Megiddo, which created Egypt's most extensive zone of hegemonic influence abroad; the distinctive, more territorially direct character of Egyptian administration in Nubia under the Viceroy of Kush, contrasted with the looser hegemonic arrangements governing Egyptian relations with Levantine vassal rulers; and Amenhotep III's conversion of accumulated imperial wealth into diplomacy, marriage alliance, and monumental construction, alongside the beginning of the invaluable Amarna Letters archive.

Section 14.9 examined Akhenaten's promotion of Aten worship and relocation of the capital to Akhetaten as one of the most striking and most debated religious transformations in ancient history, presenting the ongoing scholarly disagreement over whether this reform constituted genuine monotheism or a more narrowly political and theological exclusivism centered on royal authority. Section 14.10 then traced the deliberate restoration of traditional religious and political order under Tutankhamun, Ay, and Horemheb, and the subsequent effort to erase the Amarna period from official historical memory.

Section 14.11 examined Ramesside military and diplomatic history, emphasizing that Ramesses II's celebrated account of the Battle of Kadesh should be read as royal ideology rather than neutral history, and that the subsequent Egyptian-Hittite peace treaty, rather than the battle itself, marks the more historically significant resolution of decades of Egyptian-Hittite rivalry over control of the Levant. Finally, Section 14.12 showed that by the reign of Ramesses III, the New Kingdom's imperial apparatus faced genuine and well-documented strain, evidenced directly by the Deir el-Medina workers' strike, foreshadowing the wider regional crisis that Chapter 16 will examine in full.

Throughout, this chapter has emphasized that Egypt's imperial subjects and neighbors—Hyksos rulers, Nubian populations, and Levantine vassal rulers alike—were active historical participants whose interests and strategies shaped the course of events, even though the surviving evidence overwhelmingly reflects Egyptian perspectives. It has also resisted several common oversimplifications: treating the Hyksos as a sudden foreign conquest, reducing Hatshepsut's reign to a question of gender, describing Egyptian control of the Levant as uniform territorial annexation, calling Atenism straightforwardly the first monotheism, and accepting Ramesses II's own account of Kadesh as an uncomplicated victory.

These developments prepare students directly for the two chapters that follow. Chapter 15 will examine the Hittites, Hurrians, and Canaanite societies of the Bronze Age Levant as historical actors in their own right, moving beyond the Egypt-centered perspective necessarily adopted in this chapter. Chapter 16 will then assemble Egypt, the Hittite Empire, Mitanni, Babylon, and Assyria into the single interconnected Late Bronze Age international system whose correspondence, treaties, and trade this chapter has only begun to introduce, and will examine the environmental, political, and military factors that brought that system to crisis around 1200 BCE.

Key Terms

Second Intermediate Period
The era of Egyptian political fragmentation between the Middle and New Kingdoms, characterized by Hyksos, Theban, and Nubian power centers, discussed in Section 14.1.
Hyksos
Rulers of western Asian cultural background who governed the eastern Nile Delta as the Fifteenth Dynasty, discussed in Section 14.2.
Fifteenth Dynasty
The Hyksos ruling line based at Avaris during the Second Intermediate Period, discussed in Section 14.2.
Avaris
The eastern Delta capital of the Hyksos Fifteenth Dynasty, identified with the archaeological site of Tell el-Dab'a, discussed in Section 14.2.
Kerma
The capital of the Nubian kingdom of Kush during the Second Intermediate Period, later brought under Egyptian control, discussed in Sections 14.1 and 14.7.
Composite bow
A bow constructed from laminated layers of wood, horn, and sinew, offering greater range and power than simple wooden bows, associated with New Kingdom chariot warfare, discussed in Section 14.4.
Chariot warfare
Military tactics employing horse-drawn, two-wheeled chariots, which became central to New Kingdom Egyptian and broader Near Eastern military organization, discussed in Section 14.4.
Royal titulary
The formal set of names and epithets adopted by an Egyptian king to express divine kingship and legitimacy, relevant to Hatshepsut's assumption of full pharaonic titles, discussed in Section 14.5.
Co-regency
A period in which two rulers, typically an older and younger monarch, shared or jointly claimed kingship, relevant to the debated relationship between Hatshepsut and Thutmose III, discussed in Section 14.5.
Deir el-Bahari
The site of Hatshepsut's mortuary temple on the west bank at Thebes, notable for its Punt expedition reliefs, discussed in Section 14.5.
Punt
A trading partner region, likely located along the Red Sea coast of the Horn of Africa, the destination of Hatshepsut's celebrated trading expedition, discussed in Section 14.5.
Hegemonic empire
A system of imperial control in which local rulers retain autonomy in exchange for tribute and loyalty, contrasted with direct territorial administration, discussed in Section 14.2 and applied to the Levant in Sections 14.6 and 14.7.
Territorial empire
A system of imperial control in which conquered land is directly incorporated into the ruling state's administration, applied to Egyptian Nubia in Section 14.7.
Vassal ruler
A local ruler who retains internal authority while acknowledging the overlordship of a more powerful state, central to Egyptian control of the Levant, discussed in Sections 14.6 and 14.7.
Viceroy of Kush
The senior Egyptian official responsible for administering Egyptian-controlled Nubia, discussed in Section 14.7.
Tribute
Goods, resources, or labor rendered by a subordinate ruler or population to a dominant power, central to both Nubian and Levantine relations with Egypt, discussed throughout Sections 14.6 and 14.7.
Amarna Letters
A diplomatic archive of cuneiform correspondence between Egypt, other major Near Eastern powers, and Levantine vassal rulers, discussed in Sections 14.8 and 14.9 and examined further in Chapter 16.
Atenism
The religious program promoted by Akhenaten centered on exclusive worship of the sun disk, the Aten, discussed and debated in Section 14.9.
Monolatry
The worship of one deity as supreme without necessarily denying the existence of other deities, offered as an alternative description to monotheism for Atenism, discussed in Section 14.9.
Damnatio memoriae
A modern Latin historiographical label, not an ancient Egyptian term, applied retrospectively to describe the deliberate erasure or suppression of a historical figure's memory from official records; applied by modern historians to the treatment of Akhenaten and, to varying degrees, his immediate successors, discussed in Section 14.10.
Restoration Stela
A monument of Tutankhamun describing the neglect of traditional temples under Akhenaten and announcing their restoration, discussed in Section 14.10.
Kadesh inscriptions
Ramesses II's monumental royal account of the Battle of Kadesh, carved at multiple temple sites, discussed critically in Section 14.11.
Egyptian-Hittite peace treaty
The agreement concluded around 1259 BCE between Ramesses II and Hattusili III, among the earliest surviving international peace treaties, discussed in Section 14.11.
Deir el-Medina
The specialized workers' village housing artisans who built royal tombs in the Valley of the Kings, notable for its documented labor strike, discussed in Section 14.12.
Sea Peoples
A loosely defined set of populations described in hostile Egyptian sources as attacking Egypt and other eastern Mediterranean states around 1200 BCE, discussed cautiously in Section 14.12 and examined further in Chapter 16.
Ostraca
Inscribed pottery or stone fragments used for informal writing, an important source of evidence for daily life at Deir el-Medina.
Manetho
A third-century BCE Egyptian priest whose later account of Egyptian history, including a hostile description of the Hyksos, shaped much subsequent tradition, discussed in Section 14.2.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has traced one of the most consequential transformations in ancient Egyptian history: the movement from a fragmented, triangular political landscape to a militarized imperial state whose kings campaigned in Nubia and the Levant, corresponded and intermarried with the great powers of the Late Bronze Age Near East, and built monuments whose scale still shapes the modern landscape of Luxor and Karnak. The habits of mind this chapter has emphasized—distinguishing gradual processes from sudden invasions, recognizing that hegemonic and territorial control are different things requiring different explanations, reading royal inscriptions as ideology rather than neutral record, and taking seriously the perspectives and agency of Hyksos, Nubian, and Levantine populations even where the surviving evidence speaks mostly in Egyptian voices—should inform the study of every subsequent chapter in this textbook.

New Kingdom Egypt's imperial reach, this chapter has shown, was neither uniform nor permanent. It combined direct territorial administration in Nubia with looser hegemonic arrangements in the Levant, it converted military success into diplomacy and monumental building under some rulers and renewed campaigning under others, and it proved capable of both dramatic religious experimentation under Akhenaten and equally dramatic religious and political restoration under his immediate successors. By the reign of Ramesses III, the same imperial apparatus that had built Karnak and campaigned at Megiddo and Kadesh faced genuine strain, documented directly in the grievances of royal tomb workers at Deir el-Medina—a reminder that even the most seemingly permanent ancient power was sustained by ordinary human labor and administrative systems capable of failure.

Understanding New Kingdom Egypt as one great power among several, rather than as an isolated civilization occasionally interacting with foreign "others," prepares students directly for the two chapters that follow. Chapter 15 will introduce the Hittites, Hurrians, and Canaanite societies as historical actors whose institutions, interests, and perspectives deserve treatment on their own terms, moving beyond the necessarily Egypt-centered vantage point this chapter has adopted. Chapter 16 will then draw Egypt together with the Hittite Empire, Mitanni, Babylon, and Assyria into a single interconnected diplomatic and commercial system, and will examine the political, environmental, and military factors that brought that system to a still-debated crisis around 1200 BCE—a crisis in which the Egypt examined in this chapter, already showing signs of strain, would play its own significant part.
