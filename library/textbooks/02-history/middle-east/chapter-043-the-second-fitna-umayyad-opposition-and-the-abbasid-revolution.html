The Second Fitna, Umayyad Opposition, and the Abbasid Revolution

A History of the Middle East: From Ancient Civilizations to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

On the tenth of Muḥarram, 61 AH (about 10 October 680), a small party led by al-Ḥusayn, grandson of the Prophet Muḥammad, was killed by an Umayyad force on the plain of Karbalāʾ in Iraq. The fact of his death is secure. Almost everything else about it reached us through narrators writing generations later.

This chapter follows seventy years of contest over a single question: who has the right to lead the Muslim community? Between 680 and 750 that question produced a rival caliphate in the Hijaz, revolts in Iraq, Khārijite insurgencies, a second and third civil war, and finally a revolutionary army from Khurāsān that destroyed the Umayyad dynasty.

The Umayyads were not doomed. They survived the Second Fitna and under ʿAbd al-Malik and his sons ruled a stronger state than before. They fell when dynastic quarrels after 743 fragmented their army and an organized, religiously framed movement exploited the opening. The chapter ends in 750; Chapter 44 treats the Abbasid state that followed.

The sequence begins with a succession. Muʿāwiya's rule had held the community together largely through his own management, and when he arranged for his son Yazīd to follow him, he left open every question his settlement had not answered: the place of the Prophet's family, the weight of Qurashī precedence, and the limits of obedience. The first part of this chapter explains why those questions could not stay quiet.

Karbalāʾ is the chapter's emotional and historical center, and also its sharpest test of method. Its outline is secure, but its details reached us through narrators writing later, and its meanings continued to develop. The Second Fitna that followed involved at least three distinct movements, each with its own constituency and aims, and the Umayyads' recovery under Marwān I and ʿAbd al-Malik rested on Syrian armies and administrative change.

The final part follows a different kind of conflict. After 743 a dynasty that had survived civil war split from within, while in Khurāsān a patient clandestine organization used a deliberately open slogan to gather support from Arabs and non-Arabs alike. The result in 750 was the destruction of one dynasty and the proclamation of another, and students should hold in view both the planning behind the revolution and the contingency of its success.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why Muʿāwiya's settlement left legitimacy unresolved.
  2. Reconstruct the events of Karbalāʾ while distinguishing early reports from later elaboration.
  3. Distinguish Ibn al-Zubayr, the Tawwābūn, and al-Mukhtār as separate movements.
  4. Compare Khārijite, Alid, Iraqi, and Amazigh opposition.
  5. Analyze the Third Fitna and its military consequences.
  6. Describe the Hāshimiyya network and its slogan al-riḍā min āl Muḥammad.
  7. Trace the campaigns of 747–750 with accurate dates.
  8. Evaluate explanations for Umayyad collapse without assuming inevitability.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did the Umayyads survive the Second Fitna but not the Third?
  2. What can and cannot be known about Karbalāʾ?
  3. Why did Iraq repeatedly support, then abandon, claimants to leadership?
  4. Was the Abbasid Revolution an ethnic uprising?
  5. How explicitly Abbasid was the revolutionary movement before 749?
  6. What role did contingency play in Marwān II's defeat?

Division I

The Crisis of Caliphal Legitimacy after Muʿāwiya

43.1 Reconstructing Civil War: Sources and Memory

Our narrative of 680–750 comes mainly from Arabic chronicles compiled after the Umayyads fell. Al-Ṭabarī (d. 923) and al-Balādhurī (d. c. 892) preserve earlier material, including reports from Abū Mikhnaf (d. c. 774) on Iraq, and from other transmitters on Syria and Khurāsān. Most of these earlier works survive only as quotations inside later books, so we usually see them through the choices of a compiler writing generations after the events.

Those compilers wrote under the Abbasids, whose rule rested on the overthrow of the Umayyads, and in a scholarly world where Sunni, Shiʿi, and Khārijite communities were defining themselves. Each tradition had reasons to remember Yazīd, al-Ḥusayn, Ibn al-Zubayr, or the revolutionaries in a particular way. Speeches and dialogues are especially likely to be literary composition rather than record.

Other evidence is thinner but valuable. Coins and papyri show how the state functioned; Syriac and Armenian chronicles give non-Muslim views, though their coverage is uneven; poetry preserves contemporary sentiment but was edited in transmission. Historians therefore separate layers: what several independent lines agree on, what one partisan narrator reports, and what later devotion or polemic added. Modern scholars still debate how early particular narratives were fixed, which is why this chapter attributes contested details to their sources.

The principal narratives have different strengths. Al-Ṭabarī arranges material by year and often places several versions side by side, each with a chain of narrators, which lets modern readers see disagreement. Al-Balādhurī's genealogical history, the Ansāb al-ashrāf, preserves material on prominent families. Al-Yaʿqūbī, writing in the ninth century, gives a more Shiʿi-leaning account. For the revolution itself, an anonymous Abbasid-period text, the Akhbār al-dawla al-ʿabbāsiyya, and other sources preserve eastern traditions. Each reflects the interests of its time.

Because so many of these works rely on earlier narrators, historians examine who transmitted a report, whether he had a regional or partisan stake, and whether similar accounts appear independently. Abū Mikhnaf's Kūfan reports are valuable for the Iraq of 680 but come from a Kūfan perspective. Reports from Syria, where fewer early traditions survive, are thinner and mainly preserved by writers outside it. This chapter flags such differences and does not treat transmitted speeches as transcripts.

43.2 The Unsettled Legacy of 661–680

When Muʿāwiya became sole ruler in 661, he did so after ʿAlī's death and after ʿAlī's son al-Ḥasan, according to the tradition, gave up his claim by agreement. The sources remember the year as the year of the community's unity. Chapter 40 narrated that war; here the point is what its ending left open.

Several questions were unresolved. What rights did the Prophet's family have to leadership? How far did precedence among the Quraysh and the senior Companions, such as ʿAbd Allāh ibn al-Zubayr and ʿAbd Allāh ibn ʿUmar, limit a dynasty's claim? Did a Muslim owe obedience to a ruler regarded as unjust? Khārijite groups, who had broken with ʿAlī, kept a militant answer in the field, and Iraq contained many people who had supported ʿAlī.

Muʿāwiya governed by patronage, a loyal Syrian army, and capable governors, notably Ziyād ibn Abīhi in Iraq until his death in 673. Stability rested heavily on personal management of tribal leaders and on payment of stipends. That made succession the dynasty's greatest weakness, because a settlement built on one man's skill did not obviously pass to his son. The settlement after the First Fitna is remembered as the 'year of the community' (ʿām al-jamāʿa), when al-Ḥasan ibn ʿAlī gave up his claim and Muʿāwiya was acknowledged. It ended the fighting between Syria and Iraq, but the settlement was personal, and its terms were differently remembered by Umayyad and Alid supporters.

Muʿāwiya's methods of control were sometimes harsh. Through Ziyād ibn Abīhi and later governors, he suppressed Kūfa's supporters of ʿAlī. The execution of Ḥujr ibn ʿAdī and his companions around 671 outraged many Muslims and showed that dissent could be punished by death. Khārijites, defeated at Nahrawān in 658, continued to attract those who rejected both camps. Thus the dynasty's inheritance included a legacy of coercion as well as order.

Muʿāwiya's reign also left a contested memory about ʿAlī. Later traditions report that Umayyad governors had ʿAlī cursed from the pulpit at Friday prayers, a practice said to have lasted until the reign of ʿUmar II. Whether the practice was as systematic as the reports suggest is uncertain, but the tradition was believed, and it fed the sense in Iraq and the Hijaz that the dynasty disrespected the Prophet's family.

43.3 Yazīd's Designation and Accession, 680

In the later years of his reign, Muʿāwiya sought pledges of allegiance for his son Yazīd as heir. The reports differ on dates and details, but agree that the step was unusual: earlier caliphs had come to office through consultation or nomination of a respected Companion, not through an apparent hereditary succession. Pressure on the Hijaz included a visit in which, according to the accounts, he pressed leaders there to accept.

Several prominent figures withheld allegiance, among them al-Ḥusayn, Ibn al-Zubayr, and Ibn ʿUmar, though Ibn ʿUmar did not rebel. The designation and the accession are separate events: Yazīd became caliph on Muʿāwiya's death in Rajab 60 AH (spring 680), when his governors demanded pledges, particularly in Medina. Those who had refused earlier now faced a direct demand with a ruler in office.

Later sources, written by his enemies, portray Yazīd as impious and frivolous. These portraits cannot be taken at face value. What can be shown is that he had Syrian support, continued his father's administration, and was willing to use force against challengers in 680–683. His legitimacy was disputed because of how he came to power as much as because of his personal reputation.

Hereditary succession had no clear precedent. Abū Bakr had nominated ʿUmar, and ʿUmar had appointed a council. Muʿāwiya's argument, as reported, was that nominating his son would prevent a fresh civil war. The sources say that governors such as al-Mughīra ibn Shuʿba in Kūfa and Marwān ibn al-Ḥakam in Medina promoted the idea, though the details of who first suggested it are uncertain.

Muʿāwiya obtained pledges through delegations to Damascus and, reportedly, through pressure in the Hijaz, where he made a visit in his last years. Those who refused included Ibn ʿUmar, Ibn al-Zubayr, al-Ḥusayn, Ibn ʿAbbās, and others. Yazīd himself is reported to have taken part in campaigns against Byzantium and to have had a Syrian base through his mother, Maysūn of Kalb. These reports show a prince prepared for rule, though hostile sources emphasize his flaws.

43.4 The Political Geography of Opposition

The empire's regions gave opposition different resources. Damascus, with its Syrian army organized in districts and paid by the state, was the dynasty's reliable base. Kūfa and Baṣra, founded as garrison cities, held large and competitive Arab populations whose leaders depended on stipends and tribal standing, and Iraq had long memories of ʿAlī and of Umayyad governors.

Medina and Mecca were religiously prestigious but militarily weak. Their leaders were descendants and Companions of the Prophet, and Ibn al-Zubayr and al-Ḥusayn could draw on that standing without having troops. Khurāsān, far to the east, was settled by garrisons drawn from Iraq and governed with considerable independence. Distance mattered everywhere: messages and armies took weeks, so a governor's local control often decided events before the caliph could react.

These differences explain why a succession dispute became an empire-wide war. A claimant could speak in the Hijaz, but only a base in Iraq or Syria could raise an army. Almost every challenger of this chapter tried to join prestige to a regional force, and the Umayyads' ability to defeat them depended on keeping Syrian loyalty and controlling Iraq. Yazīd's Syrian base was tied by marriage and patronage. His mother Maysūn belonged to the Kalb tribal group, and the Syrian army was loyal to the dynasty that paid it. Elsewhere, loyalty cost money. Stipends (ʿaṭāʾ) from state revenue went to soldiers and to the descendants of early Muslims, and any change in them could create resentment. The Hijaz's leaders received subsidies but had no army.

The empire was linked by the post road and by letters, but messages and troops took weeks to move. A governor who controlled a garrison city and its stipend register could make or unmake movements before Damascus responded. The Umayyad system of governors, from Ibn Ziyād in Iraq to the governor of Khurāsān, worked when the caliph was strong and gave much freedom when he was not.

Baṣra, founded in the 630s as a military camp, and Kūfa, founded soon after, were rivals as well as partners. Their Arab populations came from different tribal mixtures, and their leaders competed for stipends, governorships, and the assignment to eastern campaigns. Governors who held both cities, as Ibn Ziyād did in 680, could play one off against the other, but the unity was fragile, as the revolts of the next decades would show.

Division II

Al-Ḥusayn, Karbalāʾ, and the Political Consequences of 680

43.5 Al-Ḥusayn and the Refusal of Allegiance

Al-Ḥusayn, son of ʿAlī and Fāṭima and grandson of the Prophet, held a position of unusual honor in the community. When Yazīd demanded allegiance after Muʿāwiya's death, the governor of Medina pressed him to swear. He declined and, according to the reports, left Medina for Mecca around the end of Rajab (spring 680) rather than submit.

He remained in Mecca for several months, where Ibn al-Zubayr was also sheltering. The two men's aims differed: the sources suggest that Ibn al-Zubayr hoped to build his own position, while al-Ḥusayn's claim rested on his descent. Letters began arriving from Kūfa, urging him to come and promising support.

Opinion was divided on what he should do. Reports credit several prominent Muslims, including Ibn ʿAbbās and Ibn ʿUmar, with advising against going to Iraq, citing its record of failing his father and brother. Whether al-Ḥusayn aimed at winning rule, at taking a stand against an illegitimate caliph, or at both is unclear, since later accounts put elaborated speeches in his mouth. What the sources show is a refusal with political consequences that his contemporaries understood.

The reports describe the first confrontation in Medina. The governor summoned al-Ḥusayn, informed him of Muʿāwiya's death, and asked for his pledge. According to the narratives, al-Ḥusayn asked for time, and the powerful Umayyad figure Marwān ibn al-Ḥakam urged the governor to compel or kill him. Details of the conversation are almost certainly reconstructed, but the broad pattern, of a demand for allegiance met by delay and departure, fits the other evidence for 680.

The sources also report that while in Mecca al-Ḥusayn wrote to leaders in Baṣra as well as receiving letters from Kūfa. A messenger carrying such a letter was reportedly intercepted and executed by Ibn Ziyād. His half-brother Muḥammad ibn al-Ḥanafiyya is said to have advised against going and stayed behind. These episodes show a leader weighing options and counsel, and they matter for later debates about whether he sought to rule or to resist. The arguments about obligation divided the Prophet's community: some believed obedience to an established ruler was required, others that a ruler seen as illegitimate could be refused.

43.6 Kūfan Appeals and Muslim ibn ʿAqīl

The Kūfan letters presented al-Ḥusayn as the leader they wanted. He sent his cousin Muslim ibn ʿAqīl to judge the situation. Muslim reached Kūfa, was sheltered by supporters, and received, according to reports, large numbers of pledges, although the figures in the sources cannot be trusted.

The governor, al-Nuʿmān ibn Bashīr, was regarded in Damascus as too lenient. Yazīd gave authority over Kūfa to ʿUbayd Allāh ibn Ziyād, already governor of Baṣra. Ibn Ziyād moved quickly, using threats against tribal leaders, offers of reward, and the arrest and execution of Hāniʾ ibn ʿUrwa, who had sheltered Muslim. When Muslim rose, the reports say his support dissolved, and he was captured and killed about the same time al-Ḥusayn set out from Mecca.

Historians draw a structural lesson. Kūfa's tribal leaders held their standing through the state and had much to lose. Sympathy for the Prophet's family was real, but it did not overcome a governor who controlled security and stipends. The speeches reported in these accounts are later reconstructions, but the sequence of events is well attested.

The sources name Kūfan tribal leaders among those who wrote to al-Ḥusayn, including Shabath ibn Ribʿī and Ḥajjār ibn Abjar. If the reports are accurate, the same men who invited him later joined or served Ibn Ziyād's side, which made the Kūfan betrayal a central theme of later memory. Historians caution that the names and the number of letters may have been shaped by that theme.

Muslim ibn ʿAqīl was sheltered in Kūfa, and a spy for Ibn Ziyād reportedly located his host Hāniʾ. When Hāniʾ was arrested and killed, Muslim rose, but the accounts say his followers dispersed as tribal chiefs told their kin to withdraw and as Ibn Ziyād's threats spread. Some reports give moving detail about women urging sons home, which may be literary elaboration. What is clear is that a promising movement collapsed within a day or two, and that al-Ḥusayn, already traveling, entered a situation that had been decided before he arrived.

43.7 The Journey and Confrontation

Al-Ḥusayn left Mecca in early Dhū l-Ḥijja 60 (September 680) with his family and a small band of followers. News of Muslim's death reached him on the way, according to the reports, and the sources record that he nevertheless continued, with his companions deciding whether to go on. Ibn Ziyād deployed troops to intercept him. A commander, al-Ḥurr ibn Yazīd, stopped the party and, in several accounts, later joined it. The party was diverted to Karbalāʾ, near the Euphrates, and arrived on 2 Muḥarram 61 (early October 680). ʿUmar ibn Saʿd then commanded a larger Umayyad-aligned force.

The next days saw negotiations whose terms are disputed. Different reports say al-Ḥusayn asked to return, to go to Yazīd, or to go to a frontier; others say Ibn Ziyād demanded surrender and allegiance. Reports also say access to the river was restricted. Because the narrators had strong commitments, historians treat the stated terms cautiously while accepting that a settlement was sought and refused.

The route ran along the road from the Hijaz toward Iraq. The sources place news of Muslim's death at a stage of the journey, along with news of the death of another messenger, and say al-Ḥusayn told his companions that those who wished to leave could do so. Some did, and the party that continued consisted largely of kin and close supporters. These reports, like many of the speeches attributed to him, express the later understanding of his choice.

ʿUmar ibn Saʿd, the commander Ibn Ziyād chose, was reportedly assigned an expedition toward Rayy and accepted the Karbalāʾ command reluctantly, with the prospect of a governorship as an incentive. This detail, if reliable, suggests how the confrontation was handled by officials with career interests rather than ideological hostility. It also illustrates how Umayyad governors used appointments to secure military cooperation in Iraq.

The journey itself says something about al-Ḥusayn's intentions. He traveled with women and children, which suggests that he expected to settle in Kūfa and not to raid or to fight a battle, though that is an inference and not a statement in the sources. The distance from Mecca to Iraq took weeks to cover, and the changing news along the way, of Muslim's death and Ibn Ziyād's control of Kūfa, left him with few options and little time.

43.8 The Battle, 10 Muḥarram 61 / October 680

On 10 Muḥarram 61 (about 10 October 680), the two sides fought. The sources describe a force of a few thousand and a party of al-Ḥusayn's supporters numbering from several dozen to around a hundred. The imbalance was great, and the outcome was not in doubt for long. Al-Ḥusayn and most of his male companions and relatives were killed, among them members of the families of ʿAlī, Jaʿfar, and ʿAqīl. Reports name Shimr ibn Dhī l-Jawshan among those who pressed the final attack. Individual combats, last speeches, and the deaths of particular family members are told in many versions, and historians cannot confirm their details.

The killing of a grandson of the Prophet by the army of a caliph was an act that horrified contemporaries, and the sources record that people at the time recognized it as a grave offense. The secure outline is small: a confrontation, a battle in which al-Ḥusayn was killed, and an Umayyad force that included Iraqi Muslims. Everything further depends on a reading of the sources.

The sources describe the night before the battle and the morning of the tenth, with al-Ḥusayn's party arranged in a defensive camp, volleys of arrows, and a series of individual combats before a general assault. Al-ʿAbbās ibn ʿAlī, al-Ḥusayn's half-brother, is named in the traditions among the dead. The detailed order in which individuals fell differs between versions and cannot be reconstructed with confidence.

After the killing, the Umayyad side buried its own dead, and the sources say local people buried the bodies of al-Ḥusayn's party. The descriptions of mutilation and of what was done with the head are reported in conflicting ways. The disagreement over responsibility, how much blame lay with Ibn Ziyād, with ʿUmar ibn Saʿd, with Shimr, or with Yazīd, is a political question as well as a historical one, and sources from different traditions assign it differently.

According to the reports, the opposing force was largely drawn from Kūfan tribal contingents under Ibn Ziyād's orders, not from the Syrian army. If so, many of those who fought al-Ḥusayn were Iraqis of the same kind as those who had invited him, which helped make Kūfan guilt a lasting theme and explains why the Tawwābūn arose from Kūfa and not from the Hijaz.

43.9 Survivors and Reaction

The survivors were mostly women and children, together with ʿAlī ibn al-Ḥusayn, who reportedly survived because he was ill. They were taken to Ibn Ziyād in Kūfa and then sent to Damascus. The sources preserve scenes at both courts, including words attributed to the women, to Ibn Ziyād, and to Yazīd, but the versions contradict one another and are colored by later controversy.

Accounts of the treatment of al-Ḥusayn's head also vary in places and details. Some traditions have Yazīd expressing regret and blaming Ibn Ziyād; hostile traditions have him mocking the victims. Both versions served later interests, and historians cannot decide between them from this evidence alone. The political reaction is clearer. Reports describe revulsion in Iraq, where those who had failed al-Ḥusayn felt shame, and in the Hijaz, where Ibn al-Zubayr turned opposition to Yazīd into a public cause. The survivors returned to Medina, and ʿAlī ibn al-Ḥusayn became a respected figure who avoided open rebellion.

One reported episode has ʿAlī ibn al-Ḥusayn, who was ill, nearly killed on the order of Ibn Ziyād before being spared, and the women, led by Zaynab, speaking to the governor. These scenes are not independently confirmed. They became important in later literature because they made the survivors witnesses of what had happened, and the woman's voice a vehicle for memory.

In Damascus the reports diverge about Yazīd's conduct, as noted. Some versions say he blamed Ibn Ziyād and treated the survivors honorably before sending them back to Medina under escort. Others emphasize his indifference or hostility. Whatever Yazīd's real stance, the political effect was that the dynasty could not escape responsibility, and both its opponents and its later Abbasid successors found it useful to keep the memory alive.

ʿAlī ibn al-Ḥusayn, the survivor, remained in Medina and did not join Ibn al-Zubayr, the Tawwābūn, or al-Mukhtār. He is reported to have been respected as a source of religious knowledge, and later Shiʿi tradition counts him among the imams. His quietism shows that loyalty to al-Ḥusayn's family did not require armed rebellion, and that members of the family could take different paths after Karbalāʾ.

43.10 Karbalāʾ as Crisis and Memory

Karbalāʾ damaged the Umayyads' claim to be the rightful leaders of the Muslim community. An army serving the caliph had killed the Prophet's grandson, and many Muslims saw it as proof that Yazīd's rule was unjust. It fed Iraqi guilt, which found expression five years later in the Tawwābūn, and it provided later opponents a vocabulary of martyrdom, justice, and loyalty to the Prophet's family.

The memory developed over time. Laments and poetry appeared early, and the narrative was repeatedly retold, with speeches, miracles, and details added. Annual commemorative rituals and a developed Shiʿi theology of the imams belong to later centuries and are treated in Chapter 47. Muslims of many traditions, not only Shiʿis, revere al-Ḥusayn, though they interpret his death differently. For the historian, two tasks must be kept apart. One is reconstructing what happened in 680 from critically read sources. The other is tracing how communities remembered it, which is a historical subject in its own right even where the memory goes beyond the evidence.

Early expressions of memory are visible in the sources. Laments circulated in verse, and the Tawwābūn are reported to have visited al-Ḥusayn's grave on their march in 684–685. Al-Mukhtār's followers are said to have used the cry of vengeance for al-Ḥusayn, though how far this was an early slogan and how far a later attribution is debated. These uses show that the death became a rallying point within a few years.

The Umayyads and their allies did not ignore the issue. Some accounts show them presenting al-Ḥusayn's death as the consequence of rebellion against legitimate authority, a view that found sympathy among those who valued unity after the first civil war. Yet the broader view of him as an innocent victim grew stronger over time among many Muslims. The interpretation as a foundational martyrdom, with an elaborate commemoration and theology, took shape over later centuries and is the subject of Chapter 47.

The politics of memory did not stand still. The shrine at Karbalāʾ became a place of visits, and the later Abbasid caliph al-Mutawakkil reportedly ordered it demolished in 236 AH (850–851), which shows that the commemoration of al-Ḥusayn had by then become politically sensitive for the same dynasty that had once used Alid martyrs against the Umayyads. These later developments lie outside this chapter, but they warn against treating the early memory as fixed.

Division III

The Second Fitna and the Struggle among Rival Caliphates

43.11 Ibn al-Zubayr in the Hijaz

ʿAbd Allāh ibn al-Zubayr was the son of al-Zubayr ibn al-ʿAwwām, a Companion of the Prophet, and of Asmāʾ, daughter of Abū Bakr. He belonged to the Qurashī elite that had opposed ʿAlī, and he had fought at the Battle of the Camel. His family connections gave him standing with those who resented both ʿAlī's descendants and the Umayyads.

After refusing Yazīd's demand for allegiance, he took refuge in Mecca, where, according to the reports, he presented himself as one seeking sanctuary at the Kaʿba. Opposing a caliph is different from claiming the office, and the sources suggest his claim to the caliphate itself developed after Yazīd's death rather than before. His strength was prestige and sanctuary, not an army. He drew on Hijazi and Iraqi sympathies and on Qurashī hostility to Syrian dominance. Whether he was a principled opponent, an ambitious claimant, or both is argued from sources that were mostly written by his Marwānid and Abbasid-era opponents.

After Yazīd's death, support for Ibn al-Zubayr widened. The sources report recognition in the Hijaz, in Egypt, and in much of Iraq, and some Syrian governors, among them al-Ḍaḥḥāk ibn Qays at Damascus, Zufar ibn al-Ḥārith at Qinnasrīn, and al-Nuʿmān ibn Bashīr at Ḥimṣ, are reported to have leaned toward him. In Khurāsān, ʿAbd Allāh ibn Khāzim al-Sulamī recognized him. This breadth shows that his claim answered a real need, a leader acceptable to those who rejected both the Umayyads and ʿAlī's family.

His appeal rested on piety, descent from early Companions, and control of the holy cities, and his brother Muṣʿab served as governor in Baṣra. Critics in the sources describe him as stingy and rigid, qualities that may have weakened him as a ruler. He is also reported to have rebuilt the Kaʿba after its damage, a project later reversed by al-Ḥajjāj. The caliphate he claimed was real in the sense that many provinces obeyed it for years, and historians therefore treat 683–692 as a period of two rival caliphates.

Ibn al-Zubayr's family background deserves attention. His father al-Zubayr ibn al-ʿAwwām was killed after the Battle of the Camel in 656, and his aunt ʿĀʾisha, the Prophet's widow, had led the opposition to ʿAlī there. The family therefore carried a record of opposition to ʿAlī's line and of prestige among the early Muslims, which explains why Ibn al-Zubayr could oppose both the Umayyads and ʿAlī's descendants without appearing to contradict himself.

43.12 Al-Ḥarra, 683

Medina's anger had two sources. One was Karbalāʾ and the standing of the Prophet's family. The other was the conduct of Umayyad governors and the presence in the city of Umayyad relatives. Al-Ṭabarī reports that a Medinan delegation visited Damascus, was received with gifts, and returned with accounts of Yazīd's court that portrayed him as impious. This report is hostile and cannot be verified, but it explains how the Medinans justified their action: in 63 AH they withdrew their pledge to Yazīd and expelled the Umayyads living in the city, who gathered in Marwān ibn al-Ḥakam's household. Marwān is reported to have asked ʿAlī ibn al-Ḥusayn to shelter his family.

Yazīd answered by sending a Syrian army under the veteran commander Muslim ibn ʿUqba. The reports say he offered the Medinans a short period to submit and that Ibn ʿUmar and ʿAlī ibn al-Ḥusayn, who kept out of the rebellion, were to be spared. The battle at al-Ḥarra, in August 683, was fought east of the city. The Medinan side, led by ʿAbd Allāh ibn Ḥanẓala for the Anṣār and ʿAbd Allāh ibn Muṭīʿ for the Quraysh, was outnumbered and untrained for set battle, and some accounts say Syrian troops entered through a quarter whose inhabitants did not resist.

The aftermath is the least secure part of the story. Hostile sources claim that Medinans were compelled to pledge as slaves of Yazīd, that the city was plundered for days, and that the killing was widespread. The figures differ, and the vividness of later tradition suggests embellishment. Even so, the sources agree that an Umayyad army killed members of Companion families in the Prophet's city. That fact was enough to make the day of al-Ḥarra a long-lived symbol.

Its significance was political. It was the first time a caliph used Syrian forces to crush the Hijaz, and it removed any plausible claim that the Umayyads had the consent of the Prophet's own community. It also helped Ibn al-Zubayr, who could now present himself as defender of a violated sanctuary. Muslim ibn ʿUqba died on the road to Mecca, and command passed to Ḥuṣayn ibn Numayr.

43.13 The Siege of Mecca and Yazīd's Death

Ḥuṣayn ibn Numayr's army reached Mecca in autumn 683. Ibn al-Zubayr had gathered supporters from Medina's refugees and from others who opposed the Umayyads, among them Khārijites, and, according to reports, al-Mukhtār, who left after a dispute. Syrian forces placed siege engines on the hills overlooking the sanctuary and bombarded defenders around the Kaʿba. The reports put the siege at about two months.

The fire that damaged the Kaʿba is told in several versions. In one, a torch or burning projectile ignited the coverings. Zubayrid narrators blamed the besiegers, while Umayyad narrators said the defenders caused it. Other reports say the Black Stone was cracked. No version can be confirmed, but the damage mattered politically because it connected the Umayyads with an attack on the holiest shrine.

News of Yazīd's death reached the siege lines in November 683. Ḥuṣayn then reportedly offered to recognize Ibn al-Zubayr as caliph if he would accompany the army to Syria. Ibn al-Zubayr declined to leave the Hijaz, and the Syrians withdrew. The reports add that Ibn al-Zubayr expelled the Umayyads still in the Hijaz, and that Marwān went to Syria, where he soon became central to the dynasty's survival.

The sequence shows how much depended on contingency. Had Yazīd lived, the Syrian army might have broken Ibn al-Zubayr. His death left it without a caliph and, as the next section shows, without a plan, and sparked a crisis of succession that nearly ended the Umayyad dynasty.

Both attacks on Mecca, in 683 and in 692, became part of how later writers judged the Umayyads. Zubayrid and later Alid and Abbasid traditions stressed the sacrilege of bombarding the sanctuary, while Umayyad traditions presented the sieges as suppression of a rebel who had refused obedience to the caliph. Because writers placed the damage to the Kaʿba differently, responsibility for it is one of the standard examples historians use to show how partisan memory shaped the record of the Fitna.

43.14 Collapse of Sufyānid Authority

Muʿāwiya II's reign is reported as lasting from a few weeks to about three months, and the sources disagree on whether he was a weak figure, a pious one who renounced rule, or a nominal successor. He left no agreed heir. The Sufyānid line descended from Abū Sufyān had run out of adult men, and the elders of the wider Umayyad family, among them Marwān ibn al-Ḥakam, the young Khālid ibn Yazīd, and ʿAmr ibn Saʿīd al-Ashdaq, now had to decide whether any Umayyad would rule.

The provinces made their own choices. In Baṣra, the notables reportedly chose ʿUbayd Allāh ibn Ziyād as an interim leader and then turned on him, and he left for Syria. Kūfa accepted a Zubayrid governor. Egypt and parts of Khurāsān, where ʿAbd Allāh ibn Khāzim controlled the garrison, acknowledged Ibn al-Zubayr. These shifts show that Umayyad rule had depended on governors whose loyalty followed the person of the caliph.

In Syria, al-Ḍaḥḥāk ibn Qays at Damascus, Zufar ibn al-Ḥārith at Qinnasrīn, and al-Nuʿmān ibn Bashīr at Ḥimṣ are reported to have leaned toward Ibn al-Zubayr, while Ḥassān ibn Mālik ibn Baḥdal of Kalb, governor in Palestine and Yazīd's maternal kinsman, backed an Umayyad successor. The divisions reflected patronage and local rivalries more than principle.

The dynasty survived partly because Ibn al-Zubayr could not project power beyond the Hijaz and Iraq. He did not go to Syria and did not use the opportunity to build an army there. Meanwhile Syrian leaders with a stake in the Umayyad system, including the army's pay and the tribal patronage of Kalb, had reason to preserve it. The outcome was far from certain.

Egypt mattered because of its grain, revenue, and large Arab military population. According to the reports, its governor ʿAbd al-Raḥmān ibn ʿUtba al-Fihrī (known as Ibn Jaḥdam) acknowledged Ibn al-Zubayr, and Egyptian recognition deprived the Umayyads of a major source of money while the Zubayrids lacked the means to exploit it. The control of Egypt later became one of Marwān's first objectives, which shows that the contest was fought for resources as well as for legitimacy.

43.15 Marwān I and Marj Rāhiṭ, 684

The leaders met at al-Jābiya, in the Golan region. The candidates included the young Khālid ibn Yazīd, ʿAmr ibn Saʿīd, and the older Marwān. Ibn Baḥdal of Kalb was the key organizer, and the reports say Marwān was chosen with an arrangement that Khālid and ʿAmr would follow him. That arrangement was not respected, which created resentments within the family.

Marwān's forces met those of al-Ḍaḥḥāk at Marj Rāhiṭ in August 684. The Umayyad side included Kalb, Ghassān, and other groups, and the reports say ʿUbayd Allāh ibn Ziyād, arriving from Iraq, helped organize them. The battle lasted many days. Al-Ḍaḥḥāk was killed, and Qaysī leaders such as Zufar ibn al-Ḥārith withdrew to Qarqīsiyā in the Jazīra, where they held out until about 691.

Marwān then recovered Egypt and appointed his son ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz as governor there. He sent ʿUbayd Allāh ibn Ziyād toward Iraq, which led to the confrontation with the Tawwābūn at ʿAyn al-Warda. Marwān died in spring 685. Later stories attribute his death to violence within his household, but they are hostile and unverifiable. Marj Rāhiṭ shifted the dynasty from the Sufyānid line to the Marwānid, which would rule until 750. It also made Syrian tribal alliances central to Umayyad politics for the rest of the dynasty's existence. ʿAbd al-Malik, Marwān's son, inherited a Syria reunited in practice but still divided in memory.

Marwān's succession arrangements did not hold. The sources say he named ʿAbd al-Malik and then ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz as his heirs, setting aside the agreement made at al-Jābiya. ʿAmr ibn Saʿīd al-Ashdaq, who had been promised the succession, rebelled in Damascus in 689–690 (69–70 AH) and was killed by ʿAbd al-Malik, reportedly after being lured to a meeting. The episode shows that unity within the Marwānid family was achieved by force as well as by agreement.

Poetry is one of the few contemporary sources for this period. Al-Akhṭal, a Christian poet of the Taghlib tribe, wrote panegyrics for the Marwānid caliphs, and his verse shows how the dynasty presented itself to Syrian tribes and notables. Such poems were commissioned by patrons and preserved by later anthologists, so they cannot be taken as neutral, but they are valuable because they were composed when the events were recent.

43.16 The Tawwābūn

The Tawwābūn, or Penitents, were Kūfans moved by guilt over their failure to support al-Ḥusayn. Sulaymān ibn Ṣurad al-Khuzāʿī, a Companion, led them, together with figures such as al-Musayyab ibn Najaba. Their call, the sources report, was to atone through death in battle against the killers of al-Ḥusayn.

They gathered in Kūfa in late 684, and the reports say far fewer appeared than had pledged. They set out toward Syria, reportedly stopping at al-Ḥusayn's grave, and fought a Syrian army under Ḥuṣayn ibn Numayr at ʿAyn al-Warda in early 685. Sulaymān was killed, and survivors returned. Their goal was repentance, not rule, and they did not seek a caliphate. The detailed speeches reported in the accounts are probably later composition, and recent scholarship questions whether all narrative details derive from one early source.

The movement was organized by letters and meetings in Kūfa and drew supporters from Kūfa, Madāʾin, and Baṣra. Besides Sulaymān ibn Ṣurad and al-Musayyab ibn Najaba, other leaders such as Rifāʿa ibn Shaddād are named. The sources give figures such as sixteen thousand enrolled and four thousand mustered, which probably express the gap between enthusiasm and action rather than exact numbers.

The Tawwābūn did not attack the Zubayrid governors in Kūfa, whom they did not see as their enemy. They marched against Syria, the source of the army that had killed al-Ḥusayn, and fought at ʿAyn al-Warda in the Jazīra. Their defeat left Kūfa's Alid sympathizers without leadership, which created the opening al-Mukhtār used a few months later.

The Tawwābūn's movement was an unusual form of political action, built around repentance (tawba) and the wish to die in a just cause. The reports say Kūfa's Zubayrid governor allowed them to leave, and that the Syrian army they met was far larger than their force, which numbered only a few thousand. After the defeat, survivors led by Rifāʿa ibn Shaddād returned to Kūfa. Later Shiʿi piety gave prominence to penitence and mourning for al-Ḥusayn, and historians see the Tawwābūn as an early expression of that tradition, though its links to later practice cannot be traced precisely.

43.17 Al-Mukhtār, 685–687

Al-Mukhtār belonged to the Thaqīf tribe of al-Ṭāʾif. He rose in Kūfa in autumn 685 with the military support of Ibrāhīm ibn al-Ashtar, expelling the Zubayrid governor ʿAbd Allāh ibn Muṭīʿ. The timing followed the Tawwābūn's defeat, which had left Kūfa's Alid sympathizers leaderless.

He claimed to act on behalf of Muḥammad ibn al-Ḥanafiyya, ʿAlī's son by a woman of the Banū Ḥanīfa, and called for vengeance on those who had killed al-Ḥusayn. Hostile sources say he claimed revelation and used a chair attributed to ʿAlī as a sacred object, and that he applied the title Mahdī to Ibn al-Ḥanafiyya. These charges reflect polemic as well as tradition, and the authenticity of the chair and its meaning are disputed. His movement drew heavily on mawālī, who received pay and status.

His forces hunted down participants in Karbalāʾ, among them ʿUmar ibn Saʿd and Shimr. At the Khāzir River in August 686 Ibn al-Ashtar's army defeated the Syrian expedition that Marwān I had launched toward Iraq and that ʿAbd al-Malik continued after his father's death, and ʿUbayd Allāh ibn Ziyād and Ḥuṣayn ibn Numayr were killed. Ibn al-Ḥanafiyya, living in the Hijaz, gave no clear endorsement, and is reported to have been held for a time by Ibn al-Zubayr.

The sources are divided between those who saw al-Mukhtār as an avenger and those who saw him as a deceiver. What is clear is that his movement showed that Alid sympathy could mobilize large numbers of people, and that mawālī could be political actors. Both points matter for the revolution of 747. After the Khāzir victory, Ibn al-Ashtar is reported to have taken control of Mosul and the northern region, extending al-Mukhtār's reach beyond Kūfa. Al-Mukhtār's stipend policy, which paid mawālī alongside Arabs, imposed costs on Kūfa's finances and on the tribal leaders who had controlled its distribution. His government thus faced enemies from within the city before Muṣʿab's army arrived.

The length of al-Mukhtār's rule is worth noting. He took Kūfa in October 685, won at the Khāzir in August 686, and fell in April 687, so his government lasted about eighteen months. In that time he fought a Syrian army, hunted down Karbalāʾ participants, and reshaped Kūfa's social order. That so much happened so quickly explains both his appeal and the intensity of the hostility he attracted.

43.18 Zubayrid Iraq

After Khāzir, al-Mukhtār's position was strong in Kūfa but weakened elsewhere. His reliance on mawālī, who received pay and status, angered Kūfa's tribal leaders, the ashrāf, who saw their authority threatened. Many left for Baṣra, where Muṣʿab ibn al-Zubayr was governor for his brother and where the commander al-Muhallab ibn Abī Ṣufra served him. Muṣʿab's army marched on Kūfa and defeated al-Mukhtār's forces at Madhār in 687, according to the reports, in an engagement in which one of al-Mukhtār's commanders was killed. Al-Mukhtār fought again near Kūfa and then withdrew into the governor's residence, where he was besieged for some months. He was killed in 687.

Accounts of the end vary. Some say supporters surrendered on a promise of safety and were executed, with numbers reaching thousands. The figures are not reliable, but the reports agree that Muṣʿab killed many of al-Mukhtār's followers and that the act was criticized. The suppression showed that Kūfa's Arab elite preferred a Zubayrid governor who restored their standing to a leader who elevated mawālī.

Muṣʿab governed Iraq from 687 to 691 but lacked the funds to satisfy his allies, and the sources say that ʿAbd al-Malik's agents corresponded with Iraqi leaders. Ibrāhīm ibn al-Ashtar, who had been al-Mukhtār's commander, is reported to have joined Muṣʿab. Zubayrid Iraq was therefore a coalition of former enemies held together by their opposition to Syrian rule.

Baṣra's role was shaped by the Khārijite war on its edge. Al-Muhallab ibn Abī Ṣufra's fighting against the Azāriqa absorbed Baṣran troops and attention during these years, and Baṣra's tribal composition, with Tamīm, Bakr, and Azd groups, differed from Kūfa's. Muṣʿab's position therefore depended on balancing a divided garrison city while fighting a second front, which helps explain why his regime lacked the strength to withstand the Syrian army in 691.

Coins offer a check on the narratives. Arab-Sasanian silver coins struck in Iraq and Iran in the name of Zubayrid governors, including Muṣʿab, survive and show that Zubayrid authority was exercised through the established mint system, using older Iranian designs with additions in Arabic. They confirm that Ibn al-Zubayr's rule was more than a literary construction, and that Iraq's administration continued while the caliphate was divided.

43.19 ʿAbd al-Malik's Reconquest, 691–692

ʿAbd al-Malik spent the years after 685 securing his base. He settled with Byzantium by a truce that reportedly involved payments, dealt with Zufar ibn al-Ḥārith at Qarqīsiyā, and consolidated Syria and the Jazīra. Only then did he turn to Iraq. Zubayrid weakness there encouraged him. At Maskin in October 691, the Zubayrid army broke when many Iraqi commanders deserted or held back. Ibrāhīm ibn al-Ashtar was killed. Muṣʿab, according to the reports, refused offers of safe conduct and died fighting. His head was reportedly brought to ʿAbd al-Malik at Kūfa. Iraq was again under Marwānid rule.

Al-Ḥajjāj led the force sent to the Hijaz, based himself at al-Ṭāʾif, and besieged Mecca for months in 692. Ibn al-Zubayr's supporters deserted as supplies dwindled. He died fighting near the Kaʿba in October 692, and the reports say his body was displayed. The Hijaz returned to Umayyad rule.

The reunification brought a more centralized government. ʿAbd al-Malik and al-Ḥajjāj reinforced Syrian garrisons in Iraq, and the administrative and monetary changes are discussed in Chapter 41. Mecca and Medina, which had been political centers during the Fitna, lost that role. Al-Ḥajjāj became governor of Iraq in 694. The victory ended the rival caliphate, but it did not answer the questions of legitimacy that had produced it. Some scholars connect the Dome of the Rock to the competition with Mecca, though that is debated.

The Zubayrid family's influence did not end with Ibn al-Zubayr's death. His brother ʿUrwa ibn al-Zubayr (d. about 712) was among the most important early transmitters of the biography of the Prophet, and later historians drew on his reports. The family thus helped shape how the early community was remembered, even after its political cause failed. This is a reminder that the Umayyad victory did not control the writing of history.

Al-Ḥajjāj governed the Hijaz for a time after the siege, and is reported to have reversed Ibn al-Zubayr's changes to the Kaʿba and to have treated the region's leaders firmly. The settlement of the Hijaz under Umayyad governors ended its role as a site of political rivalry, though Medina and Mecca remained centers of religious learning. The shift matters for later chapters, because scholarship in the holy cities developed largely apart from the court.

Division IV

Opposition Movements and the Limits of Marwānid Authority

43.20 Khārijite Opposition

Khārijite groups held that leadership belonged to the most pious Muslim, regardless of lineage, and that those who committed grave sins or accepted unjust rulers had forfeited legitimacy. The Azāriqa, associated with Nāfiʿ ibn al-Azraq, fought in southern Iraq and Ahwāz and took the most militant positions.

Umayyad commanders, notably al-Muhallab ibn Abī Ṣufra, fought them for years. The Najdāt, led by Najda ibn ʿĀmir, controlled parts of central and eastern Arabia. Other Khārijite currents were less radical, and the community from which the Ibāḍī tradition later developed took shape in Baṣra. Doctrinal boundaries were still fluid in the 680s, and later sectarian classifications should not be projected backward. Khārijites opposed both Umayyads and Zubayrids and sometimes fought each other. They offered an alternative to lineage-based claims.

The Azāriqa emerged in Baṣra in the 680s, reportedly after some Khārijites who had helped defend Mecca left Ibn al-Zubayr. Nāfiʿ ibn al-Azraq led them until his death, and Qaṭarī ibn al-Fujāʾa later commanded them. They controlled parts of Ahwāz and southwestern Iran for years. Their opponents report that they declared other Muslims unbelievers and killed indiscriminately, a charge that may exaggerate their practice but reflects how radical they seemed. Al-Muhallab's long campaign against them, in the period of ʿAbd al-Malik and al-Ḥajjāj, became a defining achievement of his family.

Other currents differed. The Najdāt in central Arabia and Bahrayn, led by Najda ibn ʿĀmir, ruled territory for several years before his followers turned on him. Ṣufrī groups and the quietist community around Jābir ibn Zayd and Abū ʿUbayda in Baṣra, from which Ibāḍī doctrine emerged, rejected the Azāriqa's extremes. Later heresiographers sorted these groups into neat categories, but contemporary distinctions were less clear, and the groups' shared positions on leadership and sin were worked out through debate and conflict.

Qaṭarī ibn al-Fujāʾa led the Azāriqa for years after Nāfiʿ's death and was killed, about 698, by an army under an Umayyad commander. Their campaigns reached Fars and Kirmān. Al-Muhallab's long war against them was an important part of the career that made his family powerful in Khurāsān, as Chapter 42 shows through the later role of his descendants.

43.21 Ibn al-Ashʿath, 700–701

ʿAbd al-Raḥmān ibn al-Ashʿath, a Kindite noble, commanded Iraqi troops sent by al-Ḥajjāj to Sijistān against a local ruler. When al-Ḥajjāj ordered a harder campaign, the troops refused and rebelled in 700. Baṣran and Kūfan soldiers and religious scholars, including Qurʾān reciters, joined the revolt, directing their anger at al-Ḥajjāj and at Syrian dominance. They took Baṣra and Kūfa, but Syrian troops defeated them at Dayr al-Jamājim and Maskin in 701.

The result was greater Syrian control of Iraq. Al-Ḥajjāj founded Wāsiṭ as a garrison city manned by Syrians. The revolt showed Iraqi resistance to Damascus, and that grievances over service and pay could unite groups with different religious views. Al-Ḥajjāj became governor of Iraq in 694 and imposed strict control over the garrison cities, including the return of people who had left for the countryside. The expedition to Sijistān was an expensive campaign against a local ruler, and the reports say troops were lavishly equipped, leading to its nickname, the Army of Peacocks. When al-Ḥajjāj insisted on continuing after setbacks, the troops under Ibn al-Ashʿath refused and returned toward Iraq.

Baṣra and Kūfa joined the revolt. The reports describe a pledge to follow the Book of God and the deposition of ʿAbd al-Malik, with Qurʾān reciters (qurrāʾ) among the participants, which gave the rebellion a religious character alongside its grievances over pay and service. According to the chronicles, ʿAbd al-Malik offered concessions, including the removal of al-Ḥajjāj, but the rebels refused. The Syrian forces' victory at Dayr al-Jamājim in 701 left Iraq under tighter control.

After the defeat at Dayr al-Jamājim in 701, Ibn al-Ashʿath fled eastward and died in exile, reportedly around 704. Al-Ḥajjāj then punished participants and strengthened control. Many Iraqi religious scholars are reported to have turned from armed opposition toward quietism and the work of law and teaching, a change that helped shape later legal and religious communities and reaches into Chapter 47.

The revolt also shows how Iraqi soldiers viewed their service. Troops sent to distant Sijistān resented being used as expendable forces in costly campaigns, and al-Ḥajjāj's insistence on discipline and payment of their obligations made enemies of men who might have followed him. The sources report that many of the rebels had been among his own supporters. The rebellion thus reflected the strain of imperial warfare on provincial armies.

43.22 Zayd and Yaḥyā

Zayd ibn ʿAlī, a grandson of al-Ḥusayn, rose in Kūfa in 740 against the Umayyad governor of Iraq. The reports say many Kūfans who promised support abandoned him, and he was killed in the fighting. His death gave Alid sympathizers another martyr.

His son Yaḥyā fled to Khurāsān, where some local Muslims supported him, and he was killed in Jūzjān in 743 by forces linked to Naṣr ibn Sayyār. His death was remembered in the east, and later Abbasid revolutionaries used the memory of Alid martyrs. These events must be separated from the later Zaydī communities that took Zayd's name, since political loyalty to ʿAlī's descendants took several forms. Zayd and Yaḥyā show that open revolt by Alids repeatedly failed before 750.

Zayd ibn ʿAlī was a respected scholar, and a tradition holds that when Kūfan supporters asked him to denounce Abū Bakr and ʿUmar, he refused, and those who left him were called Rāfiḍa, the 'rejectors'. The story, preserved in later sources, is used to explain how divisions in Shiʿi thought began. It cannot be taken as a precise description of 740, but it shows that Zayd's followers disagreed about leadership and the early community.

Yaḥyā ibn Zayd went to Khurāsān, was sheltered in Balkh, and was reportedly killed in Jūzjān in 743 by Salm ibn Aḥwaz, acting for Naṣr ibn Sayyār's government. Later accounts say Abū Muslim honored his memory, which fit the revolution's appeal to the Prophet's family. Their rebellions' failure showed that open Alid revolt could not overcome Umayyad forces and left a legacy of grievance that others later exploited.

Zayd's revolt began in Kūfa in the winter of 739–740 against the governor Yūsuf ibn ʿUmar al-Thaqafī. The reports say he was struck by an arrow in the fighting and died, and that his body was exhumed and displayed. The account varies in detail, and it was retold often by later Zaydī and other writers. Its main point for this chapter is that a revolt led by a respected Alid scholar attracted Kūfan promises of support and failed to hold them.

43.23 The Amazigh Revolt

The Amazigh uprising grew from grievances against officials in Ifrīqiya. Reports say the governor's demands for tax, tribute, and slaves provoked anger, and that Muslim Amazigh were still subject to burdens. In 740 an Amazigh leader named Maysara led a rising beginning near Tangier and, drawing on Khārijite ideas of righteous leadership, he was acclaimed as caliph by his followers. He was later killed by his own supporters, and Khālid ibn Ḥamīd al-Zanātī took command.

The first major engagement was the battle of the nobles in 740, in which the Amazigh defeated an Arab force whose commanders included many leading men of the province. Hishām then sent a Syrian army under Kulthūm ibn ʿIyāḍ, and in 741 it was defeated at Bagdoura, on the Sebou river, and Kulthūm was killed. These were two separate engagements, and the sources sometimes blur them.

Survivors under Balj ibn Bishr took refuge at Ceuta and then crossed to Spain. A new governor, Ḥanẓala ibn Ṣafwān, defended Qayrawān in 742 against rebel armies, including Khārijite forces, and defeated them. His victory saved the Arab-Muslim hold on Ifrīqiya but not the western Maghrib, which passed beyond Damascus's control. Khārijite communities there later built regional states, including at Sijilmāsa and Tāhart, which lie beyond this chapter. The revolt's primary significance for 750 is that it drew Syrian troops away at a time when the army's cohesion was already under strain, and showed that grievance over taxation and status was not confined to the east.

Khārijite ideas reached the Maghrib through missionaries from the east, reportedly associated with Baṣra, who taught that leadership belonged to the most pious Muslim and that unjust rulers could be resisted. For Amazigh communities who felt overtaxed and treated as inferior, this message offered a religious language for protest. The movement should not be reduced to doctrine, though: grievances over taxes and slaving were its material cause, and the Khārijite teaching gave it a form and a leadership.

The consequences reached al-Andalus. Syrian troops who escaped from the Maghrib crossed the strait in 742, initially becoming embroiled in conflicts with the existing Andalusian Arab forces. Their organized assignment to provincial military districts followed principally under the governorship of Abū al-Khaṭṭār in 743–745. This brought a significant group of Syrian soldiers into Iberia, linking the Maghrib revolt to later developments in al-Andalus that lie beyond this chapter.

43.24 What Did Opponents Want?

Opponents of the Umayyads wanted different things. Alid supporters sought rule for the Prophet's descendants. Khārijites wanted a pious leader of any lineage. Iraqi soldiers and notables sought pay, status, and relief from Syrian governors. Mawālī wanted equal standing and fair taxation. Amazigh rebels wanted an end to discriminatory demands.

These groups used religious language, but their aims were also political and fiscal. They often fought each other: Khārijites opposed Zubayrids and Umayyads, al-Mukhtār's mawālī were crushed by Kūfa's Arab elite, and the Tawwābūn did not seek rule. Historians should therefore avoid treating anti-Umayyad movements as a single tradition leading toward the Abbasids. The later revolution drew on some of these currents, but it did not inherit a united movement.

The movements can be compared by constituency. The Tawwābūn and al-Mukhtār drew on Kūfan supporters of ʿAlī's family and, in al-Mukhtār's case, on mawālī. The Khārijites drew on settled and tribal Arabs and some non-Arabs who rejected Qurashī leadership. The Iraqi revolt of 700 brought together soldiers and reciters angered by service and pay. The Amazigh rebels were provincial populations resisting taxation and status. Alid risings in Kūfa and Khurāsān relied on loyalty to a particular family.

Umayyad governments responded in different ways: military force against rebels, fiscal and administrative changes under ʿAbd al-Malik and later rulers, and occasional concessions. None of these dealt fully with the problem of leadership. Opposition thus left the dynasty with unresolved political questions and a record of violence, but not with a single enemy. When the Hāshimiyya appeared, they drew on this inheritance by offering a message broad enough to appeal to several groups.

The reforms discussed in Chapter 42, including attempts around 717–720 to treat converts more equally, did not remove these grievances. Governors and caliphs continued to face the pressure of revenue demands and military needs. Different oppositional movements picked at different parts of the problem, but none had both the organization and the broad appeal to solve it until the Hāshimiyya.

Division V

The Third Fitna and the Disintegration of Umayyad Political Unity

43.25 Al-Walīd II, 743–744

Hishām died in February 743 after a long reign that had restored fiscal order but strained relations within the dynasty. His nephew al-Walīd II succeeded him, having been designated heir earlier. Reports say Hishām had tried to displace him, and the new caliph began his reign in conflict with Hishām's family and officials.

The sources, written under the Abbasids, portray al-Walīd II as a poet, hunter, and drinker who disregarded religion. These accounts are hostile and often anecdotal. More reliably, he acted against members of Hishām's circle and executed Khālid al-Qasrī, former governor of Iraq, who was linked to Yamanī interests. His designation of his own young sons as heirs and his harsh treatment of relatives antagonized other Umayyad princes. The crisis arose from dynastic rivalry among a large family with competing claims to the throne, not merely from one ruler's character.

Hishām's reign had left a treasury in good order and a record of firm control, but also the grievances of those he had disciplined. Al-Walīd II, who had spent part of Hishām's reign in the desert outside Syria's cities, inherited both. The sources say he raised stipends modestly, which won him some favor in the army, and he named his own young sons as heirs, a move that angered other branches of the family. He also had Sulaymān ibn Hishām, Hishām's son, flogged and imprisoned, and the reports say he mistreated other members of Hishām's circle.

His execution of Khālid al-Qasrī, former governor of Iraq, who was handed to Iraq's governor Yūsuf ibn ʿUmar, offended Yamanī leaders who had been Khālid's allies. His preference for living away from Damascus, in desert residences, reinforced the impression of a ruler detached from the Syrian elite. These grievances are known mainly from hostile narrators, but they point to a political problem: a caliph who offended relatives, officers, and tribal leaders at the same time had left himself without defenders when a challenge appeared.

Desert palaces such as Mshatta in Jordan, commonly attributed to al-Walīd II, provide material evidence of elite Umayyad investment outside the major cities. Mshatta remained unfinished, and its attribution, while widely accepted, is not equivalent to a contemporary account of the caliph’s personal habits. Such monuments illuminate courtly patronage and architectural culture, but cannot by themselves confirm hostile literary portraits of a ruler supposedly detached from Damascus.

43.26 Yazīd III and Ibrāhīm

In April 744 a coalition of Umayyad princes, officers, and Syrian notables rebelled in Damascus in support of Yazīd III ibn al-Walīd. Al-Walīd II was caught away from the capital and killed at al-Bakhrāʾ, east of Damascus. It was the first killing of a ruling caliph by Umayyad rivals.

Yazīd III is reported to have presented himself as a reformer and to have had Qadarī sympathies. He cut the stipends that al-Walīd II had raised, earning the nickname al-Nāqiṣ, 'the reducer'. His reign lasted about six months before he died in autumn 744. His brother Ibrāhīm was briefly acknowledged but lacked wide support. Provinces and army commanders did not accept the new order uniformly, and the way was open for a commander with a military base outside Damascus.

The rising began in the villages around Damascus in April 744. Yazīd III ibn al-Walīd, a nephew of Hishām, found support among Syrian soldiers and tribal leaders, including the Kalb leader Manṣūr ibn Jumhūr, according to the sources. Al-Walīd II, who was away from the capital, was caught at al-Bakhrāʾ and killed. His head was brought to Damascus, and he had no loyal army to resist.

Yazīd III's reported promises on taking power were to avoid lavish building, to keep wealth in the provinces where it was raised, to pay stipends fairly, and to give up office if he failed. His reputation as sympathetic to Qadarī ideas on free will is reported but disputed. Ḥimṣ and other places refused to accept the coup against a ruling caliph, and Yazīd had to send troops to enforce his authority. After his death in autumn 744, his brother Ibrāhīm held office for only a couple of months before Marwān II arrived.

The killing of a reigning caliph by his own relatives recalled the assassination of ʿUthmān in 656 and shocked many Muslims. Marwān II is reported to have presented himself as avenger of al-Walīd II against those who had overthrown him. Whether that claim was sincere or tactical, it gave him a justification to oppose Yazīd III's supporters and to keep rival princes in check.

43.27 Marwān II

Marwān ibn Muḥammad had governed Armenia and Azerbaijan and had fought Byzantines and Khazars. Commanding troops in the north, he refused to recognize Yazīd III's coup and marched south. He defeated forces loyal to the new regime and entered Damascus, taking power as caliph in late 744.

Rather than rule from Damascus, he established his base in Ḥarrān in the Jazīra, nearer his troops and the frontier. Syrian notables resented the shift, and Ḥimṣ and other cities rose against him. The sources report that he besieged rebellious cities and had walls demolished. Marwān II was an experienced soldier who spent his reign campaigning against rivals, including Sulaymān ibn Hishām and a Khārijite revolt in Iraq and the Jazīra. His efforts were substantial but costly, and they left him tied to the west when the east began to slip away.

Marwān ibn Muḥammad had spent years on the northern frontier, where he governed Armenia and Azerbaijan and, according to the reports, led campaigns against the Khazars in the Caucasus. He brought experienced frontier troops and a reputation for tenacity, and was nicknamed 'the Donkey' for his stubbornness in war. His power base was among the garrisons of the Jazīra, a region closer to the frontier than to Damascus's court.

After defeating a force of Yazīd's supporters at ʿAyn al-Jarr in the Biqāʿ, he took Damascus and assumed the caliphate. He then moved to Ḥarrān, which reflected both his military base and mistrust of Damascus. In 745 Ḥimṣ rebelled, and the reports say he besieged it and had the walls demolished, along with those of other rebellious towns. Sulaymān ibn Hishām and a Khārijite leader, al-Ḍaḥḥāk al-Shaybānī, led further revolts. Marwān defeated them, but each campaign took years from the time he might have used to stabilize the east.

Marwān II is credited with introducing a more flexible tactical formation, the kurdūs, a small cavalry unit, which gave his frontier troops greater maneuver than the older long lines. Whether this innovation was decisive is hard to judge from the sources, but they portray his army as better trained than many opponents, which makes his defeat in 750 more notable.

43.28 Syrian Fragmentation

The Third Fitna split Syria. Princes, regional commanders, and tribal groups competed, and the army that had given the Umayyads their strength fought itself. Reports describe killings and reprisals in Syrian cities and the destruction of fortifications. Later writers explained the conflict through Qays and Yaman, rival tribal groupings. Marwān's base was among northern, Qaysī troops, and Kalb and other Yamanī groups were reportedly suspicious of him. But alliances shifted with interests and patronage, and tribal labels were not permanent parties. The consequence was military exhaustion. Troops available to confront a new threat in the east had to be drawn from an army fighting wars at home, and local loyalty could not be assumed.

Syria was divided among its districts: Damascus, Palestine, Jordan, Ḥimṣ, and Qinnasrīn each had its own troops and notables. The killing of al-Walīd II turned these districts against each other. Some sided with Yazīd III's coalition, which included Kalb and other groups, and others with Marwān's frontier troops or the Hishāmī princes. Armies that had once campaigned together now fought each other.

Older scholarship described this as a struggle between Qays and Yaman parties. More recent historians stress that alliances were political and local, shifting as individuals sought patronage and safety. The point is not that tribal identity was irrelevant, but that it did not act like a fixed party. The practical result was that no single Syrian force could be assumed loyal to any caliph, and the dynasty's reserves of manpower were consumed in internal fighting.

Environmental and economic hardship deepened the crisis. A major earthquake in January 749 damaged cities in Palestine and the Jordan valley, and years of fighting disrupted harvests and taxes in many districts. The political effect of these hardships is hard to measure, but they added to the strain on a population already burdened by civil war.

Non-Muslim communities lived through the same disorder. Syriac and Greek Christians in Syria and the Jazīra, along with Jewish communities, faced requisitions and the risk of violence during the fighting. Few non-Muslim writings from the 740s survive in detail, but the later Syriac historical tradition remembered the Umayyad civil wars as a time of hardship, and these sources offer a useful, if limited, outside perspective.

43.29 Rival Revolts

ʿAbd Allāh ibn Muʿāwiya, a descendant of Jaʿfar ibn Abī Ṭālib, rose in Kūfa in 744 and was driven out the next year. He moved east, taking Iṣfahān, Rayy, and Hamadhān, and attracted Iraqi and Iranian supporters including some Hāshimī sympathizers. He was an Alid-related claimant with his own network, not an Abbasid.

The reports say he was defeated in 746–747 by Umayyad commanders and fled to Khurāsān, where he was killed, reportedly on Abū Muslim's orders. His rebellion shows that the collapse of Umayyad control created room for movements other than the Abbasid organization. Khārijite leaders also took advantage, and western Iran became a patchwork of rival forces. The Abbasid organization was one among several challengers that benefited from the disorder.

ʿAbd Allāh ibn Muʿāwiya, a descendant of ʿAlī's brother Jaʿfar, rose in Kūfa in late 744 against the Umayyad governor. He attracted Kūfan Alid sympathizers, but after he was forced out, he moved to western Iran and took control of Iṣfahān, Rayy, and other towns. The sources say members of the Abbasid family, including the future caliph al-Manṣūr and ʿAbd Allāh ibn ʿAlī, were with him at some point, though accounts differ on their role and motives.

Marwān II appointed Yazīd ibn ʿUmar ibn Hubayra to restore order in Iraq. Ibn Hubayra defeated Khārijite forces led by Shaybān al-Yashkurī and drove Ibn Muʿāwiya from the region. Ibn Muʿāwiya fled to Khurāsān, where he was killed, reportedly on Abū Muslim's orders. His movement was Alid-oriented and separate from the Abbasid organization. Its existence showed that the revolution of 747 grew in a field of competing movements, and that the Abbasid network's later victory was not the only possible outcome of Umayyad weakness.

Marwān II dealt with these revolts one after another, which kept him in the west. A single decisive victory in the east might have changed matters, but his forces were occupied in Syria, the Jazīra, and Iraq. The Abbasid leadership benefited from the delay, since it could build its organization while the Umayyads fought each other.

Division VI

Khurāsān, the Hāshimiyya, and the Organization of the Abbasid Movement

43.30 Khurāsān before the Revolution

Khurāsān was settled by Arab garrisons sent from Baṣra and Kūfa in the later seventh century, who lived alongside local Iranian, Sogdian, and Hephthalite populations. The principal garrison was at Marw. Local landowners, the dihqāns, collected taxes and negotiated with Arab governors. Disputes over taxation and status were common. Chapter 42 described conversion and the situation of mawālī; in Khurāsān, governors' decisions about whether converts still paid taxes and how Arab soldiers were compensated created grievances among Muslims of different origins.

The province was far from Damascus, and its governors, often appointed from among powerful Arab families, relied on the local garrison. Tribal rivalries, especially between Mudar and Yaman groupings, shaped military politics and meant that armies could be mobilized against other Arabs.

Arab settlement in Khurāsān grew in the second half of the seventh century as Baṣran and Kūfan families were sent to Marw and other towns. Over time the settlers intermarried, took up land, and acquired local interests, and Marw became a major garrison city. The frontier conquests of Qutayba ibn Muslim reached into Transoxiana by 715, and his death at the hands of Arab troops shortly after showed how readily tribal rivalries could turn on commanders. The soldiers in these garrisons were paid from state revenue and organized by tribal group, with Mudar, Rabīʿa, Azd, and Tamīm among the main blocs.

Local elites, including landowners (dihqāns) and Sogdian merchants, collected taxes and negotiated with Arab governors. The reports say that under the governor Ashras al-Sulamī in the 720s, converts were promised exemption from the poll tax and then required to pay it again when revenues fell, which led to unrest. Asad ibn ʿAbd Allāh al-Qasrī, governor in the 730s, ran expensive campaigns beyond the frontier. These fiscal and military pressures left Muslims and non-Muslims, Arabs and non-Arabs, with grievances that could be turned against the governor's authority.

Beyond the frontier, the principalities of Tokhāristān and Transoxiana, ruled by local princes, complicated Umayyad control. Their rulers sometimes accepted Arab overlordship and sometimes sheltered rebels or allied with Turkic powers, as al-Ḥārith ibn Surayj's alliance showed. A governor of Khurāsān therefore managed three problems at once: a divided Arab garrison, local landowners and Muslims of non-Arab origin with their own tax grievances, and an unstable frontier. Any of these could draw attention and troops away from the others, and none was easy to resolve from Damascus.

43.31 Al-Ḥārith ibn Surayj and Naṣr ibn Sayyār

Al-Ḥārith ibn Surayj, a Tamīmī leader, rebelled in the 730s against what he described as unjust taxation and neglect of Islamic principles, according to the reports. He allied with Turkic forces beyond the frontier and spent years in exile before returning in 745 under an amnesty.

Naṣr ibn Sayyār, a veteran commander, had been governor since 738. He tried to restore order, resettle Arabs, and reform taxation so that converts were not charged. His success was limited, and Syrian politics distracted the caliphs from the east. Al-Ḥārith reportedly rebelled again after returning and was killed in 746. Naṣr's authority weakened as the political crisis in Syria deepened and as rivals in Khurāsān challenged him.

Al-Ḥārith ibn Surayj, from Tamīm, rebelled around 734 in the Balkh-Marw region, with support from Arabs and some local notables. According to the reports, he demanded governance by the Book and the Sunna and relief from unjust taxation. He allied with Turkic forces in Tokhāristān, and after Asad al-Qasrī's forces defeated him he spent years in exile beyond the frontier.

Naṣr ibn Sayyār, from Kināna, was appointed in 738 and worked to repair the province: reportedly he conducted a census of who owed taxes, attempted to equalize payment, and tried to settle Arab soldiers. Al-Ḥārith came back in 745 under an amnesty and proposed that Naṣr step down in favor of a consultation, which Naṣr refused. Al-Ḥārith then joined Naṣr's rivals. His defeat and death in 746 removed one challenger but did nothing to unify the province.

Al-Ḥārith's revolt has often been read as a forerunner of the revolution. The reports say he used black banners and called for rule by the Book and the Sunna, language close to the later revolutionary message, and he drew support from Arabs, local notables, and Muslims who resented tax practices. But his movement was an Arab leader's rebellion against a governor, not a clandestine organization, and he accepted Turkic help. The comparison shows the grievances available to a later movement without proving a direct connection.

43.32 Factions in Marw

In 745 al-Kirmānī, leader of Azd and other Yamanī groups, joined with al-Ḥārith ibn Surayj against Naṣr at Marw. Fighting in the city was among Arab factions, with each side holding quarters and gathering allies from the villages of the oasis. Al-Ḥārith was killed in 746, according to the transmitted accounts.

After al-Ḥārith's death, al-Kirmānī remained Naṣr's rival. The sources place his death in 747, killed by Naṣr's partisans, and his son ʿAlī ibn al-Kirmānī took command of the Yamanī group. The exact sequence of events in these years is uncertain, since our sources record the struggle in anecdotes and Naṣr's own account is preserved by hostile narrators.

The reports say Naṣr warned Marwān II and the governor of Iraq about the revolutionary movement and received no effective help, since the caliph was fighting in Syria and Iraq. The prolonged struggle exhausted the Arab garrison, divided it along tribal lines, and left unpaid and displaced soldiers. Abū Muslim moved into this opening. He allied with ʿAlī ibn al-Kirmānī, whose followers had a grievance against Naṣr, and presented a message that did not belong to any of the old factions. The struggle for Marw is therefore best understood as the condition that allowed a clandestine movement to become a military one.

Marw itself was divided into quarters held by rival groups, with the city's canals and villages supplying fighters to each side. Many local Muslims of non-Arab origin and villagers appear to have sided with whoever offered protection and tax relief, so the fighting did not follow a simple line between Arabs and others. Abū Muslim's camp, reportedly at a village outside Marw, lay in the oasis where this population lived, which meant he could recruit among those who had suffered during the faction fighting.

The chronology of these years is the least secure part of the story. Our principal source, al-Ṭabarī, and the other Khurāsānī traditions give dates that differ by a year or so, and the sequence of alliances and betrayals among Naṣr, al-Ḥārith, and al-Kirmānī is reconstructed from anecdotes preserved by narrators with local loyalties. Historians therefore treat the main outline, a province tearing itself apart in 745–747, as secure, and the details of who allied with whom and when as provisional.

43.33 The Hāshimiyya and Kaysāniyya

The Hāshimiyya were followers of Abū Hāshim ʿAbd Allāh, son of Muḥammad ibn al-Ḥanafiyya. Heresiographers later grouped some of them as the Kaysāniyya. They held that leadership belonged to the family of Hāshim, the Prophet's clan, and to a designated imam. According to Abbasid tradition, Abū Hāshim died in about 716 in al-Ḥumayma in Syria and passed his claim to Muḥammad ibn ʿAlī of the Abbasid family. This account supports Abbasid legitimacy and is not independently confirmed, and the earlier history of Hāshimī movements is not well documented. Some followers did not accept the transfer and supported other leaders. Historians therefore distinguish the Abbasid claim as later presented from the earlier organizational history, which remains uncertain.

The name Hāshimiyya refers to the clan of Hāshim, to which both the Prophet and ʿAlī's family and the Abbasids belonged. The label Kaysāniyya, used by later heresiographers, has a disputed origin. The followers of Abū Hāshim looked to the family of Muḥammad ibn al-Ḥanafiyya, who died around 700, and the movement was shaped by messianic expectations that a descendant of the Prophet would come to rule justly.

The Abbasid claim of inheritance from Abū Hāshim rests on the report that he visited al-Ḥumayma and designated Muḥammad ibn ʿAlī. Some modern historians, including Patricia Crone and Andrew Marsham, doubt that the revolutionary movement was explicitly Abbasid from the start, and see the later narrative as Abbasid legitimation. Competing Hāshimī claimants existed, such as ʿAbd Allāh ibn Muʿāwiya, who also gained followers. The historical question is how an unnamed call for a leader from the Prophet's house became an Abbasid caliphate.

A later tradition, preserved in Abbasid-era sources, says that the Kūfan organizer Abū Salama offered leadership to Alid figures, including Jaʿfar al-Ṣādiq, who declined. The story, if it has any basis, shows how open the question of leadership remained. It also served later Abbasid interests by presenting the Alids as having refused and the Abbasids as accepting a task others declined. Historians therefore treat it as a legitimizing narrative, not as established fact.

The Abbasid tradition says that Abū Hāshim died in al-Ḥumayma around 716 after being poisoned on the orders of the Umayyad caliph Sulaymān. The story makes his death an Umayyad crime and his bequest an Abbasid inheritance. Whether it is accurate is uncertain, and historians treat it as part of the process by which the Abbasid family established its claim to leadership.

43.34 The Underground Daʿwa

Muḥammad ibn ʿAlī, a great-grandson of the Prophet's uncle al-ʿAbbās, lived at al-Ḥumayma in Syria. According to tradition, he sent agents from about 718 to Kūfa and Khurāsān. A Kūfan organizer, Bukayr ibn Māhān, was prominent, and traditions list twelve local leaders (naqībs) in Khurāsān.

After Muḥammad's death in about 743, his son Ibrāhīm, called al-Imām, led the movement. Agents moved secretly, collected funds, and recruited, often disguised as merchants or pilgrims. Kūfa served as the link between the leaders in Syria and the field in Khurāsān. The organization was clandestine and decentralized, a strength against Umayyad suppression. Its Abbasid leadership was accepted by many, but the sources, written afterward, may exaggerate its coherence from the start.

Al-Ḥumayma, in the Balqāʾ region of Jordan, was an Abbasid family estate. Muḥammad ibn ʿAlī's father, ʿAlī ibn ʿAbd Allāh, was a grandson of al-ʿAbbās, and Muḥammad lived there quietly. The sources name organizers in Kūfa, including Maysara and Bukayr ibn Māhān, and describe agents who traveled to Khurāsān around 718. One of them, Khidāsh, was reportedly killed under Asad al-Qasrī in the 730s after being disowned by the leadership for unacceptable teachings.

After Muḥammad's death in about 743, his son Ibrāhīm led the movement. The reports say that Sulaymān ibn Kathīr al-Khuzāʿī, Lāhiz ibn Qurayẓ, and Qaḥṭaba ibn Shabīb were among the principal leaders in Khurāsān, and that the organization worked through local cells, collecting a share of income for the cause. Secrecy protected the network, and its distance from Syria meant that the Umayyad government did not recognize the danger until the revolt was public.

Tradition says the organization had twelve leaders (naqībs) in Khurāsān and seventy subordinate agents (duʿāt), numbers that evoke early Islamic precedents and may be schematic. The agents carried letters and funds, reportedly used the pilgrimage season to meet the leadership, and chose cover occupations. The sources do not allow us to judge how many members the network had before 747, but its success in recruiting across the province without discovery indicates sustained, disciplined work.

43.35 The Message: al-Riḍā min Āl Muḥammad

The movement's message called for al-riḍā min āl Muḥammad, an acceptable leader from the Prophet's family. The formula did not specify a name, which allowed supporters of ʿAlī's descendants, followers of other Hāshimī claimants, and those with practical grievances all to find a place in it.

The call also criticized Umayyad rule as unjust and promised justice, a share of revenue, and an end to oppression. Black banners, which the sources describe as the movement's emblem, gave a visible identity; their precise meaning, whether mourning, eschatological expectation, or simply distinctiveness, is uncertain. Arab soldiers, non-Arab Muslims, and Alid sympathizers expected different things from victory. That diversity made the movement strong in 747 and would pose problems for the new rulers after 750.

The slogan al-riḍā min āl Muḥammad drew on the expectation that a righteous member of the Prophet's family would restore justice. Traditions about black banners coming from Khurāsān, preserved in apocalyptic reports of uncertain date, may have encouraged supporters to see the revolution as part of a sacred plan. Such reports were widely circulated, but their authenticity and date cannot be established.

Supporters interpreted the message in different ways. Khurāsānī Arabs hoped for fair pay and an end to factional rule. Non-Arab Muslims hoped for equality in tax and status. Alids hoped the leader would be one of ʿAlī's descendants. Umayyad officials were condemned as usurpers who had killed the Prophet's kin. The word dawla, meaning a turn of fortune, was used to describe the coming change. The movement's flexibility was a source of strength before victory and a source of tension afterward.

The message also drew on a record of Umayyad violence. The memory of al-Ḥusayn's death, of Zayd's and Yaḥyā's killings, and of al-Ḥarra gave the movement a catalog of wrongs to cite. Abbasid propaganda reportedly used the Alid martyrs to condemn the Umayyads while leaving open who would lead afterward. This borrowing was effective, and it later caused difficulty, since Alid supporters expected the leader to come from their line.

Division VII

Abū Muslim and the Military Revolution, 747–749

43.36 Abū Muslim al-Khurāsānī

Abū Muslim became the organization's leader in Khurāsān. The sources say Ibrāhīm al-Imām, the Abbasid head of the movement, sent him there, and that he worked with local chiefs (naqībs) such as Sulaymān ibn Kathīr and Qaḥṭaba ibn Shabīb. His youth, his political skill, and his ability to coordinate Arab and non-Arab supporters were remembered by all accounts.

His origins are uncertain. Later traditions gave him Arab, Persian, and slave ancestry, and several accounts, including legends that grew after his death, served competing interests. Historians therefore treat his early life as unknown and know him chiefly through his role in the revolution. His authority rested on appointment by the imam, success in the field, and the loyalty of his commanders. His later fate, treated in Chapter 44, shows that his position was never secure.

Abū Muslim's name in the sources is ʿAbd al-Raḥmān ibn Muslim, and his origins are told in conflicting versions. Some traditions make him a client (mawlā) of an Arab family, others a man of Iranian descent from the Iṣfahān region, and later legend claimed Abbasid or other high descent for him. Which of these has any basis cannot be established, and the more elaborate stories seem to have grown from his later fame and his killing in 755.

What the sources agree on is that Ibrāhīm al-Imām trusted him and that the leaders in Khurāsān were instructed to follow him. Traditions say Qaḥṭaba brought the Imām's instructions from a meeting in the Hijaz in 747. Abū Muslim was a young man who nevertheless proved able to manage rival Arab leaders, local village notables, and the demands of an army. His power rested on delegated authority from the Imām and on military success, not on tribal standing, which later made him both indispensable and vulnerable.

In the sources Abū Muslim is reportedly styled Amīr Āl Muḥammad, 'commander of the family of Muḥammad,' a title that expressed his delegated authority rather than any claim of his own. Qaḥṭaba, an older and respected figure, commanded the main army in the west, while Abū Muslim remained in Khurāsān directing the province. This division of tasks worked while the campaign succeeded, but it left a powerful figure at the movement's base whom the new rulers would later regard with suspicion.

43.37 Open Revolt and the Capture of Marw

The movement came into the open in 747 (Ramaḍān 129 AH), when, according to the sources, black banners were raised in a village near Marw and supporters from nearby villages assembled. Abū Muslim allied with ʿAlī ibn al-Kirmānī, son of Naṣr's rival, which brought Yamanī Arab support.

Naṣr ibn Sayyār, unable to unite the Arab garrison, called for help from Syria without result. As Abū Muslim's forces gained, some of his own supporters changed sides, and he withdrew westward to Nīshāpūr. Abū Muslim occupied Marw in early 748. Al-Ṭabarī preserves different reports, corresponding to late January and mid-February, so the exact day is uncertain. Naṣr died soon afterward while still trying to organize resistance.

The sources date the first public declaration to Ramaḍān 129 AH (June 747), when the black banners were raised in a village near Marw and supporters gathered from the surrounding oasis. Abū Muslim built a fortified camp and gathered recruits while Naṣr, preoccupied with al-Kirmānī, was slow to respond. The alliance with ʿAlī ibn al-Kirmānī gave him Yamanī support in the Marw area, though that alliance was a convenience, not an agreement of principle.

After engagements in which Naṣr's forces failed to dislodge the revolutionaries, Naṣr left for Nīshāpūr. Abū Muslim entered Marw in early 748, with sources giving dates that correspond to late January and mid-February. He organized rule there: appointing administrators, collecting taxes, and arranging pay for his troops. The occupation turned a clandestine movement into the government of a major city, and gave it revenue and a base for further campaigns.

Once Marw was taken, Abū Muslim began to govern. He sent officers to other towns, reportedly including Abū Dāwūd Khālid ibn Ibrāhīm to Balkh, and used the province's revenues to pay his troops. Local leaders and princes in Tokhāristān, who had their own reasons to oppose Naṣr's government, began to recognize him. The occupation of Marw therefore turned a movement into the government of a province, which could supply armies as well as banners.

43.38 The Revolutionary Armies

The revolutionary army drew on Arab settlers, local Iranian villagers, and mawālī, along with Yamanī groups. Recruits came from villages and garrisons across Khurāsān and Tokhāristān. The sources say they wore black and received pay and provisions through captured revenues and local support. Motives varied. Some sought revenge on Naṣr's faction, some relief from taxes, some religious reform, and some the prestige of the Prophet's family. Treating the army as a Persian force against Arabs misses the Arab leaders such as Qaḥṭaba and Abū Dāwūd, and the Arab troops who joined. Discipline was a problem, and the sources report violence against opponents and civilians. Holding the coalition together depended on shared victory and Abū Muslim's control of rewards.

The call spread through the villages of the Marw oasis and beyond, where the revolutionary leaders had local followers. Recruits included Arab settlers, local villagers, and mawālī, and the chronicles describe large numbers gathering under black banners. Commanders such as Abū Dāwūd Khālid ibn Ibrāhīm in Tokhāristān and others led their own contingents, and the army that moved west was organized under Qaḥṭaba with Abū Muslim remaining in Khurāsān.

Cohesion was difficult. Yamanī and Muḍarī Arabs, local landowners, and village recruits had different interests, and Abū Muslim used pay, promises, and punishment to maintain discipline. The sources, written by the victors and by their critics, attribute large-scale killings to the army, and these reports vary in reliability. The revolution should be seen as a coalition held together by a message, an organization, and early success.

The sources say little about logistics, but the army's ability to move for a year suggests that it relied on captured stores in Marw, local taxes, and requisition from towns along its route. Cavalry from the Arab garrison and infantry from the villages seem to have formed its core. Winning towns quickly mattered because each brought food, money, and recruits, and left Umayyad commanders with fewer places to gather forces.

The movement's program spoke to the garrison grievances described in §43.30. The sources say Abū Muslim promised to end unjust exactions and to pay stipends to those who joined, and that he treated recruits in a way designed to attract them from both factions of the Arab garrison and from local populations. The promise of fair treatment was a practical recruiting tool as well as an ideal.

43.39 Qaḥṭaba and the Westward Campaign

Abū Muslim sent Qaḥṭaba ibn Shabīb westward in 748. The revolutionary army defeated Umayyad forces under Nubāta ibn Ḥanẓala at Jurjān, killing him, and moved on through northern and western Iran. Reports describe the capture of Iṣfahān and operations around Nihāwand and the Zagros passes.

The campaign succeeded in part because there was no unified Umayyad field army. Marwān II was occupied in Syria and the Jazīra, local governors were isolated, and Khārijite and other rebels kept the western forces busy. By controlling routes and garrison towns, the revolutionary forces cut Iraq off from the east. The sequence of engagements in 748–749 is known only from narratives that differ in detail.

From Marw, Qaḥṭaba's army moved through Ṭūs and Nīshāpūr, where Naṣr's remaining forces were scattered. At Jurjān the army defeated Nubāta ibn Ḥanẓala, who was killed, and gained control of the road to the west. Reports then place his son al-Ḥasan at the siege of Nihāwand, which fell after a prolonged siege, while an Umayyad commander named ʿĀmir ibn Dubāra is said to have been defeated in the Jibāl.

The campaign was not a single march but a sequence of engagements in which the revolutionary forces took cities, garrison towns, and roads. Each victory attracted more recruits and discouraged supporters of the Umayyads. The Umayyad governor of Iraq, Ibn Hubayra, could not rely on reinforcements from a Syria that was occupied by Marwān II's wars. The sources are not consistent about the order and date of every battle, and the sequence should be read as an outline.

The route followed the great highway from Khurāsān across northern Iran toward Rayy and Hamadān, then over the Zagros at Ḥulwān into Iraq. Control of that road and its cities mattered more than any single battle, because it joined the east to the Iraqi heartland. Ibn Hubayra, in Iraq, faced the problem of defending several possible approaches while Syria was preoccupied, and he did not manage to stop the advance before the Zagros.

43.40 The Campaign for Iraq and Kūfa

Qaḥṭaba's army crossed into Iraq, where the Umayyad governor Yazīd ibn ʿUmar ibn Hubayra commanded forces based at Wāsiṭ. The Umayyad army was defeated in fighting near the Euphrates in the summer of 749. Qaḥṭaba died during the fighting at the river crossing, but accounts differ on how.

His son al-Ḥasan ibn Qaḥṭaba took command and led the army to Kūfa, where the Abbasid organizers had long worked. The city was occupied in late summer 749 without major resistance. Control of Kūfa gave the revolution a major Arab city with a history of loyalty to the Prophet's family, and cut Marwān II off from Iraq's revenue and manpower.

Qaḥṭaba advanced toward Iraq through Ḥulwān and the Zagros, and the sources report fighting with Ibn Hubayra's forces on the Euphrates in Muḥarram 132 (August 749). Qaḥṭaba died during the fighting. Some accounts say he drowned or was killed in the battle, and later stories developed around his death, so the circumstances cannot be fixed. The Umayyad force was defeated, and Ibn Hubayra withdrew to Wāsiṭ, where he held out into 750.

Al-Ḥasan ibn Qaḥṭaba took command and moved on Kūfa, which he entered around the end of summer 749. The Abbasid organizers in the city, including Abū Salama, welcomed him. The occupation of Kūfa was decisive because it placed the revolutionaries in a major garrison city with sympathy for the Prophet's family. It also meant the new movement had to decide whom it was fighting for.

The occupation of Kūfa was an unusual event: a revolutionary army arrived in a city that had long been a center of Alid sympathy and where an Abbasid organizer held the local leadership. The sources say the army encamped outside the city, at a place called Ḥammām Aʿyun, and that Abū Salama, whom later sources call the 'vizier of the family of Muḥammad,' had authority in the city. How power was divided between him and the army's commanders is not clear from the sources, and later narratives shaped that story.

43.41 Ibrāhīm, Abū Salama, and Abū al-ʿAbbās

Ibrāhīm al-Imām was seized by Marwān II's agents and imprisoned at Ḥarrān. Accounts of his death differ. Before dying, he reportedly named his brother Abū al-ʿAbbās as successor, and the Abbasid family moved to Kūfa.

Abū Salama, a Kūfan organizer who had acted as the movement's representative, hid the family. Some reports say he hesitated and may have favored an Alid candidate, and that the revolutionary commanders pressed him to reveal them. These accounts reflect Abbasid concerns, and Abū Salama was killed in 750. Abū al-ʿAbbās was proclaimed caliph in the mosque of Kūfa in late 749. Modern reconstruction favors October, though transmitted dates differ. His proclamation turned a revolution into a rival caliphate.

Ibrāhīm al-Imām was arrested in Syria, probably in 749, and taken to Ḥarrān, where he died in prison. Reports say he was killed by suffocation or poison, and others that he died of illness. Before his arrest he reportedly named his brother Abū al-ʿAbbās as successor and told the family to go to Kūfa, so that the revolutionary army arriving there would find the Abbasids waiting.

Abū Salama, the Kūfan organizer, reportedly kept the family hidden for some weeks. The sources, in their later Abbasid form, accuse him of favoring an Alid candidate, but they may be justifying his later killing. When the commanders learned where the family was, Abū al-ʿAbbās was presented to the army and proclaimed caliph in the mosque. The title al-Saffāḥ, whose meaning is debated, was attached to him later. His rule belongs to Chapter 44. Abū Salama was killed, reportedly on Abū Muslim's orders, early in the new reign.

The Abbasid family included Ibrāhīm's brothers Abū al-ʿAbbās and Abū Jaʿfar, and their uncles ʿAbd Allāh, Ṣāliḥ, Dāwūd, ʿĪsā, and Sulaymān, sons of ʿAlī ibn ʿAbd Allāh. After the proclamation, family members took military and administrative commands, and ʿAbd Allāh ibn ʿAlī led the force that faced Marwān II. The prominence of the family in the army shows that the leadership was not merely symbolic, though the sources' detail on who held which command should be read with caution.

Division VIII

The Fall of the Umayyads and the Revolutionary Transfer of Power

43.42 The Battle of the Great Zāb, January 750

Marwān II marched from Ḥarrān to the Tigris with an army drawn from Syria and the Jazīra and camped on the Great Zāb, south of Mosul. The Abbasid army, under ʿAbd Allāh ibn ʿAlī, an uncle of Abū al-ʿAbbās, with Abū ʿAwn named in the reports as vanguard commander, faced him across the river. The Zāb protected Mosul and the road to the Jazīra, which was Marwān's base.

The sources describe several days of skirmishing and negotiation before the main engagement. Whether any real chance of a settlement existed cannot be judged from partisan accounts. Marwān's army was large, but the loyalty of his commanders was uneven after years of civil war.

The decisive battle took place on 25 January 750 in rain and cold, according to the accounts. The Umayyad lines broke under the Abbasid attack and retreated in disorder, with many reportedly drowned in the river. The numbers given, in the tens of thousands, are probably exaggerated, and details of formation and the moment the line gave way cannot be reconstructed with confidence.

The defeat cost Marwān his main field army and opened Mosul and the Jazīra to the Abbasids. He retreated westward without a reserve capable of confronting a second army. Historians agree that the battle was a clear military defeat. Why the army broke is harder to say. Some narratives stress poor morale and defections, others the Abbasids' discipline, and all come from writers close to the victors. The result shows that the outcome rested on a battle, not only on long-term decline.

The Great Zāb mattered strategically because it lay on the road between the Jazīra and Iraq. Winning there let the Abbasid army move into northern Iraq and on to Syria without a second major battle. It also showed that a force drawn largely from Khurāsān could defeat a Syrian-Jazīran army in open combat, a result that would have seemed unlikely a decade earlier. Chroniclers who wrote under the Abbasids stressed the victory's decisive nature, and modern historians have generally accepted that judgment while noting how little we know about the battle itself.

43.43 The Fall of Damascus and Marwān's Death

After the Great Zāb, Marwān II fell back through Mosul to Ḥarrān and then into Syria. The reports describe a withdrawal through the Jazīra, with towns and garrisons changing sides as the news of the defeat spread. ʿAbd Allāh ibn ʿAlī followed with the Abbasid army, and Marwān could not assemble enough force to stand between him and Damascus.

Damascus was left under al-Walīd ibn Muʿāwiya ibn Marwān, Marwān's son-in-law. According to al-Ṭabarī (History, vol. XXVII, trans. J. A. Williams, printed pp. 169–172), the city was torn by factional fighting among its defenders, al-Walīd was killed in that violence, and the Abbasids breached the walls on 2 May 750 (20 Ramaḍān 132). The sources are not fully consistent, and the date should be read as al-Ṭabarī's, late April or early May. It should not be assumed that al-Walīd died resisting the Abbasid attack.

Marwān continued south through Palestine and into Egypt, pursued by an Abbasid detachment under Ṣāliḥ ibn ʿAlī. He was killed at Būṣīr in Egypt in August 750, and his head was reportedly sent to Abū al-ʿAbbās. With his death, no claimant had a force able to challenge the new regime in the central lands.

The Abbasid family took direct charge. ʿAbd Allāh ibn ʿAlī became governor of Syria and Ṣāliḥ ibn ʿAlī was later associated with Egypt. The reports suggest the new rulers placed trusted relatives over the former Umayyad heartland, where their legitimacy was weakest.

The fall of Damascus ended its role as capital. Later chapters trace how the Abbasids reoriented the caliphate toward Iraq and Iran, but the loss of the city that had been the center of imperial government for ninety years was the visible sign of the dynasty's end. Details of the siege, such as the strength of the defense and the extent of local resistance, rest on a handful of reports that cannot be tested against independent evidence.

43.44 Victory, Reprisals, and Survivals

The Abbasid victors targeted Umayyad family members. Reports describe a banquet organized by ʿAbd Allāh ibn ʿAlī at Nahr Abī Futrus in Palestine, to which Umayyad notables were invited and then killed, and the desecration of Umayyad graves. These accounts come from sources that wished either to show the revolution's severity or to criticize its violence, and numbers and details vary. Historians accept that a purge of the dynasty occurred, because Umayyad princes could be put forward as claimants.

Some survived. ʿAbd al-Raḥmān, an Umayyad prince, escaped to Iberia and later founded a state there, a development outside this chapter. Sulaymān ibn Hishām reportedly joined the Abbasids and later fell out with them. Ibn Hubayra held out at Wāsiṭ before surrendering under a promise of safety and was reportedly killed, an act criticized by contemporaries.

Syrian opposition continued in 750. Revolts broke out in several places, including one led by a claimant from the Sufyānid line, which showed that Umayyad networks remained. The new rulers handled them with force and with concessions to local leaders, and the Syrian army that had once ruled the empire was gradually replaced by troops from Khurāsān.

Much of the Umayyad administration continued. Scribes, fiscal officials, and local notables who understood the empire's records and revenues were needed, and many were kept. This continuity connects to the institutional history in Chapter 41 and helps explain why the change of dynasty did not transform governing practice overnight.

The reprisals also raise a problem of evidence. Almost everything we know about them was written under the Abbasids or by those with reason to resent them, and the stories of banquets, desecration, and reckless killing may be shaped by those motives. The safest conclusion is that the purge was real, extensive, and politically purposeful, and that its details are uncertain. Its consequences, including the problem of controlling the victors' own coalition, are the subject of Chapter 44.

43.45 Why Did the Umayyads Fall?

Scholars have offered several explanations. One emphasizes structural weakness: the dynasty's reliance on Syrian armies, unresolved legitimacy, and tensions over taxation and status. A second emphasizes the Third Fitna, which divided the family and army and killed a caliph. A third emphasizes the revolutionary organization: its secrecy, its appeal to many groups, and the leadership of commanders like Abū Muslim. A fourth emphasizes geography: Khurāsān was distant, and Marwān's best troops were fighting in the west.

These are not alternatives. A strong dynasty might have survived a clandestine movement, and a divided dynasty might have defeated a weaker one. The Third Fitna mattered because it coincided with a province in disorder and a movement ready to act. Each factor made the others more consequential. Contingency also mattered. Marwān II was an experienced ruler who had won several campaigns, and a different sequence of events, such as an earlier settlement in Syria or a different result at the Great Zāb, could have produced a different outcome. Counterfactuals cannot be proved, but they warn against treating the dynasty's end as inevitable.

Historians have also revised the older view of the revolution as a Persian uprising against Arabs. Current scholarship stresses the mixed coalition, the religious message of the Prophet's family, and the role of Arab leaders, while debating how Abbasid the movement was before 749. The Abbasid-era sources that narrate it were written to legitimate the winners.

The consequences were large. A dynasty based in Syria was replaced by one that would rule from Iraq, drawing on Khurāsānī support and a claim of kinship with the Prophet. The revolution did not resolve the questions of legitimacy that had driven seventy years of conflict. Chapter 44 shows how the Abbasids tried to answer them.

The end of Umayyad rule closes Part X, but not the problems it raised. Questions about who could lead the community, how revenue was shared, and what role the Prophet's family would play continued under the Abbasids and in later communities, which is why the study of the civil wars matters beyond the political history of one dynasty.

Chapter Summary

The Umayyads inherited an unsettled question of legitimacy. Yazīd I's succession provoked al-Ḥusayn's refusal and death at Karbalāʾ in 680, which damaged the dynasty and became a lasting focus of memory. In the Second Fitna Ibn al-Zubayr set up a rival caliphate, Syrian armies fought in the Hijaz, the Tawwābūn and al-Mukhtār mobilized Iraqis, and Marwān I and ʿAbd al-Malik rebuilt the dynasty and reunited the caliphate by 692.

Later opposition (Khārijite, Iraqi, Alid, Amazigh) had varied aims and did not by itself threaten the dynasty. After 743 the Third Fitna split the ruling family and the Syrian army. In Khurāsān, factional conflict and long organization by the Hāshimiyya produced an open revolt in 747, the fall of Marw in 748, Kūfa in 749, and Abū al-ʿAbbās's proclamation in October 749. Marwān II's defeat at the Great Zāb in January 750 and death in August ended Umayyad rule in the central lands. His fall was a consequence of particular developments, not of inevitable decline.

Several themes link these developments. Each major challenger combined a claim to leadership with a regional base: Ibn al-Zubayr in the Hijaz, al-Mukhtār in Kūfa, the Khārijites in the garrison and desert margins, and the Hāshimiyya in Khurāsān. Muṣʿab ibn al-Zubayr, with Kūfa's Arab notables, destroyed al-Mukhtār, and ʿAbd al-Malik's Syrian armies and administrative reforms then overcame the Zubayrids and contained the Khārijites. The fourth succeeded because the Syrian army was divided, the east was disordered, and the movement offered an inclusive message.

Historians continue to debate how far the revolution was planned by the Abbasid family, how far Alid hopes were used, and how much weight to give ethnicity and tribe. What is not in dispute is that the process was political and military, and that its outcome was not fixed in advance. The new dynasty's task of governing a diverse coalition is the subject of Chapter 44.

The pattern of regional bases explains much of the period. Hijazi prestige, Kūfan Alid sympathy, Iraqi garrison grievances, Khārijite desert and marsh strongholds, and Khurāsānī distance each gave opposition a place to form. The Umayyads, with Syria behind them, were repeatedly able to defeat movements that lacked a comparable army, and the revolution of 747 succeeded because it combined an organization with a province in disorder.

Opposition to the Umayyads was never unified. Khārijites rejected both Umayyads and Zubayrids. The Tawwābūn sought atonement, not office. Al-Mukhtār drew mawālī into politics and was destroyed by Kūfa's Arab elite. Zayd and Yaḥyā died in failed Alid risings. These differences make it misleading to read every revolt as a step toward the Abbasids.

The Abbasid movement itself should be seen as a coalition. Its leaders included Arabs and Khurāsānī recruits of many origins, and its message promised justice and leadership from the Prophet's family without naming a candidate. That ambiguity brought supporters but also created demands that the victors could not all satisfy.

Violence and displacement ran through the whole period. Medina and Mecca were attacked, Kūfa and Baṣra changed hands repeatedly, and civilians in Syria, Iraq, and Khurāsān bore the costs of requisitioning, plunder, and reprisals. Source evidence on these experiences is uneven and often partisan.

Finally, the chapter shows why historians separate event, narrative, and memory. The Abbasid-era sources that tell this story were written by or for the victors, and later Sunni, Shiʿi, and Khārijite traditions each drew on it. Chapter 44 begins with the new dynasty facing the task of turning revolutionary victory into stable government.

Chapter Reflection

The struggles of 680–750 changed the character of the caliphate. A community that had been held together by Arab conquest and the authority of the Prophet's Companions now argued openly about who should rule and on what basis. Karbalāʾ remained a lasting reference point, and the Second Fitna showed that rival caliphs could command real obedience in different provinces.

The chapter also shows how provincial resources shaped politics. The Syrian army, Iraqi garrisons, Hijazi prestige, and Khurāsānī distance each gave different movements different chances. The Umayyads managed the first challenges, and fell when their own army divided and a province slipped from control.

The revolution was a coalition, and its success rested on a message that could be read in several ways. That is why the victory raised new questions as it settled old ones: whom the Abbasids represented, how Alid supporters would respond, and how Arab and non-Arab Muslims would be treated.

Chapter 44 follows the Abbasids as they tried to turn revolutionary victory into stable government, moving the center of the caliphate toward Iraq and Iran. Later volumes will return to the religious and legal communities whose boundaries began to form during these seventy years, especially in Chapter 47.
