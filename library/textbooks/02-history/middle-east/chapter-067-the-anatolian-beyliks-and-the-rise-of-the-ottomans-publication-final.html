Chapter Opening

A small frontier principality became a powerful state through contingent opportunities, political adaptation, and institutions that survived defeat.

In the opening decades of the fourteenth century, the northwestern Anatolian frontier contained many powers but no obvious future empire. The Sultanate of Rum had been weakened by Mongol supremacy, competing Turkish beyliks were asserting authority across the peninsula, and Byzantine rulers struggled to defend their remaining Asian provinces. In this setting a leader associated with the name Osman acquired a following. His descendants would eventually govern lands stretching across continents, but that outcome was far from predetermined. Explaining the Ottoman rise requires beginning with the instability and opportunity of this particular world rather than with the grandeur of the later empire.

Anatolia was not empty territory waiting for a new dynasty. Greek, Armenian, Turkic, Kurdish, Syriac Christian, and other communities maintained villages, towns, religious institutions, agricultural systems, and regional trading networks. Pastoralist migration and frontier raiding interacted with established landholding and urban life. Rival beyliks built courts, supported religious foundations, and exercised military power. In some respects the early Ottomans were less prominent than their neighbors. Their eventual success must be assessed through comparison with these alternatives, not treated as the product of an inherited destiny.

The first surviving Ottoman narratives of the dynasty’s earliest years were written after the events they describe. Later authors presented Osman and his followers through stories of religious warfare, dynastic genealogy, spiritual blessings, and exemplary leadership. Some preserve memories of a distant frontier society; others express the political needs of a more mature ruling house. Byzantine observers, travel writers, coins, inscriptions, and documents sometimes provide nearer evidence, but none answers every question. This chapter makes source criticism integral to the explanation of Ottoman origins.

The transition from frontier following to regional state becomes clearer under Orhan. The capture of Bursa, İznik, and İzmit enlarged the dynasty’s access to urban revenues, agricultural districts, literate officials, and religious institutions. Entry into Europe developed partly through Ottoman participation in Byzantine dynastic struggles before military consolidation produced durable footholds in Thrace. Under Murad I and Bayezid I the dynasty acquired extensive Balkan alliances and dependencies while expanding against Anatolian rivals. Frontier opportunity gradually became the problem of governing a diverse interregional political order.

Such expansion produced institutional changes. Cavalry linked to revenue assignments, military households, frontier commanders, judicial and fiscal officers, religious endowments, and eventually a growing central infantry establishment sustained the dynasty. These institutions did not appear simultaneously or in the complete forms familiar from later centuries. The history of captive recruitment and the devşirme requires particular care, since it involved coercion and conversion and developed through phases. Equally important were the farmers, artisans, merchants, clerics, and households whose labor and decisions allowed state government to continue.

The Ottoman defeat by Timur at Ankara in 1402 nearly destroyed the political achievements of Bayezid I. Dependent beyliks reemerged and the ruler’s sons fought a prolonged succession war. Yet the dynasty survived. Mehmed I restored a central government, and Murad II combined military recovery, diplomacy, and provincial administration to rebuild Ottoman strength. The Interregnum therefore provides a test of what the state had become: could institutions and political relationships outlive a catastrophic defeat and the disappearance of the victorious sultan?

The chapter ends in 1451 with the accession of Mehmed II. The conquest of Constantinople in 1453 and the subsequent imperial transformation belong to Chapter 70. Here the aim is to explain the historical foundations of that later event: Seljuk and Byzantine inheritances, plural frontier politics, the agency of Balkan communities, the cumulative development of military and administrative institutions, and the resilience of a dynasty that was neither inevitable nor invulnerable. By holding origins, expansion, crisis, and recovery together, the chapter addresses why this particular beylik became a lasting state.

Division 1 — The Fragmentation of Seljuk Anatolia and the Rise of the Beyliks
Section 67.1

The Sultanate of Rum after Köse Dağ: Mongol Overlordship and Political Fragmentation

The Ottoman story begins in a landscape shaped by an older state. In the early thirteenth century the Seljuk Sultanate of Rum controlled extensive portions of Anatolia through a court at Konya, provincial governors, fiscal offices, and networks of roads, market towns, madrasas, and caravanserais. Its power was neither uniform nor uncontested. Byzantine polities remained important in western and northern Anatolia; Armenian political centers, Turkmen pastoral populations, and local military lords also exercised authority. The Mongol victory at Köse Dağ in 1243 profoundly changed the balance. The Seljuk court continued, but its rulers acknowledged Mongol supremacy and faced outside intervention in succession and taxation. Describing the result as instantaneous collapse misses the survival of people, institutions, and commercial links that subsequent rulers would inherit.

Mongol overlordship involved much more than nominal recognition of a distant sovereign. Tributary demands, the movement of armies, political representatives, and disputes over who could authorize taxation altered the relationship between the Seljuk capital and its provinces. Rival Seljuk princes sought support from Mongol commanders; local magnates sought opportunities to protect their jurisdictions or enlarge them. These relationships shifted as Mongol authority in western Asia passed through commanders associated with the empire and later the Ilkhanid political system in Iran. For taxpayers, the distinction between demands made by an Anatolian ruler and those extracted on behalf of Mongol superiors could be less important than the cumulative burdens. Yet the state retained officials and procedures capable of collecting revenues and dispensing justice. Political subordination did not erase administrative competence.

The Seljuk cities of Konya, Kayseri, and Sivas illustrate the combination of continuity and instability. They remained places of scholarship, craftsmanship, trade, and government even when dynastic conflict undermined the court. Religious schools and foundations held property; caravan routes connected Anatolian markets with Iran, the Black Sea, and the Mediterranean; merchants did not cease traveling simply because princes quarreled. Urban populations nevertheless confronted requisition, insecurity, and rival claims on their resources. Some prominent families could negotiate with more than one sovereign. Frontier districts offered different possibilities. Where the sultan's capacity to control armed men diminished, military patrons, tribal leaders, and local commanders could attract followers without entirely abandoning Seljuk political titles or practices.

The fragmentation of Rum was gradual. The careers of provincial rulers, the changing strength of the Mongol presence, and the expansion of Turkish principalities cannot be reduced to a single date. The disappearance of the effective Seljuk sultanate around 1308 is a useful chronological marker, not proof that all its territorial institutions vanished in that year. By then, competing rulers already exercised substantial control in many regions. They used titles, patronized religious foundations, issued coinage in some settings, and employed clerks familiar with Persianate traditions. Rivalry with the Karamanids, Germiyanids, and other beyliks developed within this mixed inheritance. Ottoman growth occurred on the western edge of this world, where the Byzantine frontier presented military opportunities unavailable to many rivals farther inland.

The relationship between Mongol domination and beylik independence also raises a methodological question. Chronicles often frame the period as a sequence of rulers and battles, whereas inscriptions, buildings, coins, and administrative language show continuities crossing political upheaval. A madrasa built under one dynasty might operate under another; a road or commercial partnership might outlast several campaigns. The careful historical explanation is therefore neither that the Seljuks disappeared and the Ottomans simply replaced them, nor that the Mongols wholly determined Anatolia's future. Fragmentation weakened central authority and multiplied political entrepreneurs while leaving administrative, religious, economic, and social practices that made later state formation possible.

Section 67.2

Anatolian Society: Migration, Pastoralism, Agriculture, and Cultural Transformation

Political maps cannot explain the Ottoman rise without the people inhabiting them. Anatolia in the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries contained agricultural villages, market towns, maritime ports, transhumant grazing regions, fortified settlements, and routes connecting different ecological zones. Turkoman groups associated with Oghuz linguistic and social traditions had entered over generations, and disruptions connected with the Mongol expansion encouraged further movements. Some newcomers joined mobile pastoral communities; others settled in villages, cultivated land, entered urban crafts, or became retainers of rulers. These categories overlapped. A household might maintain animals in upland pasture while growing crops elsewhere; soldiers could also farm; merchants could invest in land. Describing the entire movement as a single invasion obscures its scale, duration, and uneven consequences.

Anatolia's existing populations were no less diverse. Greek-speaking Orthodox Christians predominated in important western and northern districts, while Armenian communities and ecclesiastical institutions were especially significant in eastern areas and elsewhere. Syriac Christians, Kurdish-speaking groups, Muslims of different backgrounds, and residents whose languages or affiliations are poorly recorded participated in the same regional economy. Some lived under rulers who shared their religion; others did not. A monastery might retain buildings and lands under one political arrangement, then face additional taxes or confiscation after a conquest. Local notables sometimes negotiated collective terms with incoming commanders. The experience of a village under new rule depended on the specifics of ownership, taxation, security, religious patronage, and relations with armed groups, rather than on a single uniform frontier policy.

Pastoral mobility made the frontier dynamic. Herds required seasonal access to water and grazing; settled cultivation required predictable boundaries and protection of harvests. Competition could produce raids and retaliation, but it could also generate arrangements for seasonal use of pasture and exchange in markets. Frontier rulers able to mediate such interests might accumulate authority by distributing booty, protecting communities, and controlling routes. They could also generate chronic insecurity when military followers depended heavily on raiding. The Anatolian landscape encouraged mobility between plains, mountains, and river valleys. Military campaigns, livestock movement, and commercial traffic shared some corridors, which gave passes and bridgeheads strategic importance. Economic geography helps explain why small communities could be politically valuable.

Religious and linguistic transformation proceeded over generations. Conversion to Islam might arise from family ties, patronage relationships, migration, economic choices, Sufi influence, or the political advantages of association with Muslim institutions; the relative significance of each differed. Changes of language could accompany settlement, intermarriage, and the growth of new towns without involving immediate changes of religion. Christian institutions remained active under many Turkish rulers, while Muslim lodges, madrasas, and mosques expanded in other places. Some regions became predominantly Turkish-speaking relatively early; others retained substantial Christian populations much longer. Nor were all Muslim communities alike: legal scholars, wandering dervishes, Sufi orders, pastoral warriors, and urban householders participated in distinct though interacting institutions.

The evidence requires caution. Later narratives sometimes present the past as an inevitable struggle of two fixed peoples. Documents from individual religious houses, inscriptions, architecture, coins, geographical writings, and the accounts of travelers suggest more fluid local arrangements. Such evidence is unevenly distributed: the possessions of wealthy foundations are easier to recover than the experiences of tenant farmers or mobile laborers. Historical reconstruction should recognize coercion and displacement where attested without assuming that everyone in conquered territory was killed, expelled, or converted. This social setting matters for Ottoman origins because the earliest dynasty drew manpower, supplies, allies, and legitimacy from a frontier society whose households and institutions already crossed linguistic, political, and religious boundaries.

Section 67.3

The Anatolian Beyliks: Karaman, Germiyan, Aydın, Menteşe, Candar, and Other Principalities

The phrase Anatolian beyliks describes a landscape of competing principalities, not a single shared political system. As Seljuk central authority weakened, regional households consolidated control of territory and military followings. The Karamanids, centered in the south-central Anatolian world and associated with Konya, could invoke Seljuk political inheritance and contested authority with rivals. Germiyan controlled lands around Kütahya, linking western Anatolia's inland districts to commercial and military routes. Candar or the Candarids ruled in the north, with access toward the Black Sea. Smaller powers differed in their resources, strategic prospects, and relationships with remaining Mongol authorities. Their histories demonstrate why Ottoman primacy was uncertain around 1300: several possessed more established courts or more extensive territories than the Osmanlı following.

Western Anatolia's principalities acquired opportunities from the contraction of Byzantine mainland power and from access to the Aegean. Aydın, associated with Birgi and coastal activity around Ayasuluk, became known for maritime warfare as well as exchange. Menteşe, Saruhan around Manisa, and Karasi near the Marmara participated in overlapping rivalries involving Byzantine towns, island communities, and Italian maritime interests. It is misleading to classify every ship that sailed from a beylik port as either a pirate or a merchant vessel without context. Raiding, negotiated ransom, provision of maritime military service, commercial agreements, and trading ventures could coexist. Coastal actors also depended on hinterland agriculture and access to inland roads. Aegean activity connected Anatolian dynastic competition to the eastern Mediterranean.

The beyliks varied in their modes of legitimacy. A bey could cite descent from a military household, service to Seljuk rulers, defense of Islam, successful distribution of land and plunder, or patronage of a saintly figure. Courts attracted poets, scholars, artisans, and jurists; buildings and inscriptions made political claims visible in urban space. Some rulers issued coins to display authority, although coinage alone does not establish fully independent sovereignty. The right to appoint officials, demand taxation, make peace, and command armed forces could be divided or contested. The language of the gaza frontier appeared in some settings, but campaigns also targeted neighboring Muslim principalities. Diplomacy, intermarriage, alliance, tribute, and the transfer of towns sometimes mattered as much as battlefield victories.

Consider the strategic differences among these states. Karaman's position near central Anatolian routes gave its rulers reasons to challenge successors claiming Seljuk prestige. Germiyan's inland position made relations with both western beyliks and the emerging Ottoman polity important. Karasi occupied terrain from which the Straits could eventually be reached, which later increased its value to Orhan. Coastal beyliks could leverage sea power but faced competition from maritime cities with considerable resources. Northern principalities confronted Black Sea connections and different patterns of Mongol influence. No general rule dictated which beylik would dominate. Geography provided opportunities; leadership, alliances, access to revenue, military organization, and a series of contingent crises determined how they were used.

The Ottomans' rise should therefore be framed comparatively rather than as the automatic fulfillment of an Ottoman destiny. Early Ottoman expansion into the weakened Byzantine frontier offered territory where victories against one political opponent could attract diverse new followers. Yet Ottoman rulers also engaged other Turkish principalities through marriage, purchase or transfer of lands, alliance, coercion, and later annexation. Resistance persisted, and some beyliks regained independence after the Ottoman defeat in 1402. The persistence of rival courts and their cultural activities prevents a story in which every principality served only as a preliminary step toward Ottoman unification. Chapter 68 will examine the eastern Turkoman confederations in their own right; here the beyliks establish the plural Anatolian background against which an initially modest dynasty acquired regional significance.

Historical Vocabulary: Beylik, Rum, and Frontier

Beylik means a principality or domain associated with a bey. Its institutions, territories, and claims could vary greatly.

Rum means lands associated with the Roman or Byzantine past in several medieval usages, including the Seljuk Sultanate of Rum. Frontier identifies a zone of changing political authority and social interaction, not necessarily a fixed border line.

Interpretive value: these words help explain why early Ottoman political status cannot be measured by a single modern definition of sovereign state.

Section 67.4

The Byzantine Frontier, Borderland Communities, and the Conditions of Political Expansion

Northwestern Anatolia occupied a distinctive position at the end of the thirteenth century. The Byzantine Empire had recovered Constantinople in 1261, but the resources required to defend its European and Asian territories were stretched by dynastic disputes, fiscal needs, and rival commitments. Bithynia contained important Byzantine fortified towns, agricultural districts, roads, and older ecclesiastical institutions. Settlements around the Sakarya or Sangarios basin, Söğüt, Eskişehir, Bursa, İznik, and İzmit participated in different military and commercial networks. A frontier leader able to gather mobile fighters could threaten villages and communications even when strong walls remained beyond his capacity. The weakness of local defense did not mean the absence of Byzantine communities or institutions.

The term frontier must not be mistaken for a fixed line on a modern map. Political control could vary between a town and its countryside, between a road and the hills surrounding it, or between the summer and winter seasons. Fortified settlements might acknowledge imperial authority while neighboring villages paid tribute to other armed groups. The movement of pastoral households complicated boundaries, as did the ambitions of Byzantine landowners and military officers. A frontier chief could obtain revenues through warfare or protection agreements and then use these resources to attract more followers. Yet such arrangements were fragile. Repeated raids could drive cultivators away and undermine the productive base from which rulers hoped to draw income.

Byzantine civil and military society also contributed to changes in the balance. Local officials might defend a position independently, seek aid from the emperor, accommodate a stronger frontier force, or shift loyalties during dynastic struggles. Some alliances between Muslims and Christians grew from shared political objectives rather than religious conversion. Marriage, service in another ruler's army, ransom, prisoner exchange, and commercial negotiation brought people across cultural boundaries. Religious identities were significant, and religious warfare language existed, but they did not dictate every political choice. Nor should the frontier be romanticized as a zone of harmonious coexistence: coercion, enslavement, looting, and displacement were real possibilities for populations exposed to poorly controlled warfare.

Geography shaped military possibilities. The Sakarya watershed and paths toward Bithynian towns allowed mounted groups to move rapidly around strongpoints. Forested and broken terrain could favor raiding or ambush; routes connecting river valleys carried supplies and information. Bursa's hinterland provided valuable farmland and markets, while İznik retained symbolic weight as a historic Byzantine and Christian center. İzmit controlled connections toward the eastern approaches of Constantinople. To seize the countryside surrounding these cities was to put pressure on the roads, villages, and economic relationships that sustained their defenders. The Ottomans could therefore advance territorially before possessing sophisticated siege technology sufficient to capture every fortress.

The strongest explanation of early Ottoman opportunity integrates these political and social conditions. A weakened Byzantine regional defense made expansion possible, but the outcome also depended on Ottoman coalition-building, access to soldiers, the geography of Bithynia, and the willingness of local groups to negotiate. Byzantine narrative evidence, particularly George Pachymeres, provides an indispensable contemporary counterweight to Ottoman dynastic histories compiled much later. Even Pachymeres was not an omniscient witness to every village or campaign. The chapter must use his testimony to reconstruct changing frontier conditions without converting one observer's anxieties into a complete account of all communities. The next division follows how Osman and Orhan exploited, and gradually institutionalized, these possibilities.

Division 2 — Osman, Orhan, and the Formation of the Ottoman Principality
Section 67.5

Osman and the Problem of Ottoman Dynastic Origins

Few problems in medieval Middle Eastern history illustrate the difference between evidence and retrospective legend more clearly than the early life of Osman. Later Ottoman chronicles identify his father as Ertuğrul, locate the family in the northwestern Anatolian frontier, and recount dreams, prophecies, and genealogies through which divine approval was supposedly revealed. Some narrative elements may preserve earlier memories, but most surviving Ottoman accounts were composed long after the events they describe, often in a mature empire anxious to explain its origins. An affiliation with the Kayı branch of the Oghuz is prominent in some later traditions, yet it is not a securely contemporary genealogical fact. Rather than treating the stories as useless, historians ask what they reveal about subsequent Ottoman ideals of legitimate kingship and how they should be weighed against nearer evidence.

Osman emerged among Turkoman and frontier military groups in northwestern Anatolia around the end of the thirteenth century. His followers likely included people linked by kinship, service, patronage, and the expectation of reward; the precise composition of the early community is disputed. Osman was not yet the head of the great centralized empire described by later chroniclers. His power depended on alliances and the ability to lead successful operations, defend or redistribute resources, and maintain relations with rival chiefs. Frontiersmen could join a more successful leader, while local Byzantine authorities faced choices about resistance and accommodation. These conditions make the formation of an Ottoman political identity a process rather than an event fixed by an official ceremony.

The year 1299 is conventionally used as the Ottoman founding date. It remains useful as a chronological signpost, but available evidence does not establish it as a single moment at which a constitution, sovereign state, or empire was proclaimed. Some historians emphasize the change in Osman's military prominence around 1302, others stress institutional consolidation under Orhan. The problem is partly definitional. A leader might exercise de facto control over raids and tribute while continuing to operate within larger, ambiguous structures of Seljuk and Mongol prestige. Political sovereignty was not necessarily expressed through one unambiguous declaration. Coinage, inscriptions, accounts of military encounters, and chronicles each illuminate a different dimension of power and must be dated and interpreted separately.

An important part of later Ottoman memory was the linking of Osman's fortunes to religious men and to the language of gaza. Stories about Shaykh Edebali, marriage, spiritual blessing, and a dream that foretold a vast tree of empire connect the dynasty with sacred approval and a destiny of universal rule. Their literary power is unmistakable, but their historical use is primarily as evidence for how later authors wished to represent the founding generation. This does not prove that religious language was absent from frontier warfare. Rather, it prevents a retrospective origin story from becoming a verified account of one ruler's intentions. Material incentives, religious commitments, personal loyalty, and opportunities for accommodation may all have mattered without being assigned identical weight in every encounter.

The historian's task is to explain how a modest political following became durable enough to survive its founder. Osman could build upon preexisting patterns of Turkish frontier lordship, a changing Byzantine border, local religious patronage, and a community increasingly associated with his name. Yet claims about precise dates, genealogies, and exemplary speeches must be scrutinized. The contrast between later dynastic chronicles and contemporary Byzantine writings is particularly important; neither is neutral, but their distance from events differs. The next section examines Bapheus and the campaigns of Bithynia, where a better-documented military encounter offers firmer ground for analyzing early Ottoman expansion.

Section 67.6

Osman, Bapheus, and the Expansion into Byzantine Bithynia

The Battle of Bapheus, usually placed in 1302 though a minority chronology favors 1301, represents one of the earliest relatively well-attested military episodes associated with Osman. The Byzantine historian George Pachymeres describes a defeat of imperial forces by warriors under the leadership of Atman or Osman in the vicinity of Nicomedia. The account must be read in the political context of the empire's mounting difficulty protecting its Asian provinces. Its importance does not rest on a claim that Osman suddenly conquered all Bithynia. Rather, it shows that a frontier commander could defeat a Byzantine force sent to restore order and attract attention beyond his immediate following. Success in the field enlarged his reputation, encouraged recruitment, and exposed the limits of imperial control over the countryside.

Several dimensions of the battle matter. Mounted raiders could move rapidly across districts where imperial troops needed to defend scattered settlements. Local knowledge, timing, and the possibility of reinforcing a successful band shaped operations. Byzantine military difficulties reflected political choices as well as simple military weakness: reliable pay, leadership, and coordination were essential when multiple frontiers demanded attention. Pachymeres writes from within a Byzantine world alarmed by displacement and insecurity, and his narrative is not a comprehensive Ottoman campaign diary. The exact composition and size of Osman's force are not available in the precision implied by later military descriptions. Historical reconstruction therefore places the battle within a sequence of raiding, siege pressure, and migration, rather than inventing tactical details unsupported by testimony.

The immediate effects fell heavily upon inhabitants of the countryside. Farmers whose crops or animals were threatened could move toward walled towns, seek protection from local lords, pay tribute to a new armed authority, or abandon vulnerable settlements. Each choice affected future revenue and military logistics. Fortified cities might remain Byzantine while the nearby fields became dangerous for their residents. Such conditions made it harder to maintain supplies and communications, increasing pressure on urban defenders over time. Conversely, raids also risked destroying the wealth an emerging Ottoman ruler hoped eventually to tax. The gradual movement from predatory campaigns toward regular claims over land and population was a central institutional challenge of frontier state formation.

Bithynia's geography deepened the significance of this pressure. Bursa, İznik, and İzmit occupied different strategic positions and possessed distinct local political traditions. Roads linking them to the imperial capital and nearby agricultural zones were vulnerable to mounted forces even where urban walls resisted. Osman and his followers did not possess the consistent siege machinery or fiscal apparatus of a mature state. Operations against surrounding settlements and lines of communication could nevertheless weaken a city's prospects. Changes in allegiance were sometimes negotiated, and a broadening frontier following could include allies whose backgrounds did not conform to later standardized stories about Turkish Muslim warriors. Success in one valley did not guarantee control of the next; persistence and accumulation mattered.

Bapheus illustrates how to use historical sources. A Byzantine account near the events can establish that a leader named Osman achieved military prominence and that Byzantine communities faced serious insecurity. It does not establish every later story about his ancestry, every motivation of his followers, or an uncontested founding date. Nor should the experience of defeat be extrapolated into immediate universal conversion or displacement. The decisive long-term issue was whether the new frontier rulers could convert battlefield advantage into enduring relationships with towns, cultivators, merchants, and religious institutions. That transition is much more visible under Osman's successor Orhan and the conquest of the urban centers of Bithynia.

Section 67.7

Orhan, Bursa, İznik, and the Development of Ottoman Urban Government

The capture of Bursa in 1326 was a major change in the resources and responsibilities of the Ottoman dynasty. The chronology of Osman's final years and Orhan's early accession is not perfectly documented, but by the time Bursa came under Ottoman control the leading figure was Orhan. A fortified city brought a treasury of possibilities beyond plunder: markets could generate regular dues, adjacent farmland supplied food and revenue, properties needed adjudication, and people sought protection against soldiers and tax collectors. Capturing Bursa did not make every institution suddenly Ottoman. It compelled rulers and followers accustomed to frontier campaigning to take responsibility for an existing urban community with material assets, social hierarchies, and commercial relationships.

The subsequent capture of İznik in 1331 and İzmit in 1337 extended control over Bithynia. The Battle of Pelekanon in 1329, in which a Byzantine attempt to challenge Orhan's forces failed, helped expose the difficulty of imperial intervention before the final fall of these cities. İznik possessed exceptional religious and historical significance for Byzantine Christians. İzmit occupied a strategic position near eastern approaches to the Sea of Marmara. Each city required arrangements governing land, buildings, worship, markets, and relations between old residents and incoming elites. Different towns were incorporated through different episodes of siege, surrender, negotiation, and settlement. The mere listing of conquest dates cannot capture the institutional work that followed.

Orhan's government increasingly employed officials, religious foundations, and material signs of rulership. Coinage issued under his authority is one form of evidence, though historians debate the earliest attributions and how particular coins should be interpreted. The development of mosques, madrasas, charitable works, and endowed properties demonstrates investment in urban order and prestige. Bursa became a key political and commercial center; its markets connected the emerging Ottoman court with Anatolian and wider trade. Judges and literate administrators assisted with disputes over contracts, inheritance, land, and taxation. Such developments drew upon institutions already familiar in Seljuk and other Islamic governments, adapted to a frontier society that now included substantial populations previously subject to Byzantine rule.

The experience of Christian inhabitants requires a differentiated account. Some communities retained churches and clergy; others encountered confiscation, conversion of particular buildings, new taxes, or shifts in local authority. A conquest might lead to emigration by some families and accommodation by others. The extent of continuity depended on negotiated arrangements and the choices of powerful groups, not on a fixed uniform procedure. Muslim settlers, soldiers, and religious figures also altered the social landscape over time. Work in fields, workshops, and markets continued because the new rulers required grain, craft production, and exchange. For those inhabitants, a change of sovereign could be both disruptive and a reorganization of familiar obligations.

Historians should resist attributing to Orhan the complete bureaucratic system of the sixteenth-century empire. Early institutions were developing and often remain visible only through scattered documentary and material evidence. Their importance is precisely that they mark a change of scale: the dynasty increasingly had to manage garrisons, urban economies, and social relationships over a defined territory. Bursa, İznik, and İzmit made it possible to support larger forces and participate in diplomacy with greater resources. They also brought new vulnerabilities, including the need to defend fixed centers and regulate increasingly diverse subjects. The Ottoman movement into Europe built upon this new territorial base as much as upon the older logic of frontier raiding.

Section 67.8

Karasi, the Straits, Byzantine Civil Wars, and the Ottoman Entry into Europe

The incorporation of Karasi was an important step in the Ottoman expansion beyond the Bithynian interior. The beylik controlled territory near the Sea of Marmara and the Dardanelles, and its absorption in the mid-fourteenth century expanded Ottoman access to the coastline and to experienced military commanders. The precise chronology and mechanisms of incorporation remain debated, and a single definitive annexation date can mislead. The political effect is clearer: the Ottomans acquired positions from which a movement across the Straits was increasingly feasible. Maritime transport, coastal fortifications, supplies, and contacts with people accustomed to operations near European shores became more central to their strategy.

Byzantine civil wars provided further opportunity. John VI Kantakouzenos entered arrangements with Orhan during struggles for imperial authority; dynastic marriage reinforced the alliance, and Turkish forces intervened in Balkan conflicts. These contingents were not simply invaders appearing in Europe without prior political connection. They were also participants in wars among Byzantine claimants and their regional allies. Such intervention illustrates the porousness of political boundaries: an emperor could employ soldiers from a Muslim frontier ruler against Christian opponents, and the soldiers themselves could gain familiarity with European geography and vulnerabilities. The relationship also created suspicion and grievances among populations who experienced these troops as raiders or occupiers rather than as legitimate allies.

The early Ottoman foothold at Tzympe, or Çimpe, belongs to the contested history of these arrangements. Accounts differ over how the position was obtained and retained, and simplistic stories of formal invitation followed by uninterrupted betrayal fail to capture the uncertainty. More decisive was Gallipoli in 1354. An earthquake severely damaged defenses in the region, and Ottoman forces under Süleyman Pasha exploited the resulting vulnerability. Holding Gallipoli gave a substantial gateway into Thrace. Yet fortifications alone did not guarantee lasting possession: populations, garrisons, shipping, agricultural support, and political backing were required. Early European expansion combined opportunistic seizure with a growing commitment to maintain a permanent foothold.

The Straits were strategically valuable because they linked an Anatolian territorial base with the politically fragmented Balkan peninsula. Coastal access made movement of troops and horses possible, while fortified ports and nearby villages helped supply campaigns. Byzantine rulers continued to negotiate, contest, or cooperate with Ottoman leaders as their own political circumstances changed. Genoese and other maritime actors also influenced the practical conditions of cross-water transport. Political expansion cannot be explained by an earthquake alone; the event created an opening that a dynasty with coastal positions, troops, and leadership could exploit. Nor should the conquest of Gallipoli be conflated with immediate command of all Thrace. Consolidation required further decades of campaigning and agreement.

The passage into Europe changed what the Ottoman political enterprise could become. It opened new agricultural districts, urban markets, military recruiting opportunities, and diplomatic relationships while exposing the state to Byzantine, Serbian, Bulgarian, Venetian, and other interests. A following that had developed on an Anatolian frontier was becoming an interregional power. But this expansion remained contingent on the loyalty of commanders, the capacity to provision forces, and the integration of local communities. The transition also exposed a problem that would recur: rulers who controlled territories on both shores needed mechanisms for distributing revenue and authority across distance. The next division follows the transformation of these opportunities under Murad I and the growing importance of Rumelia.

Division 3 — Balkan Expansion and the Construction of Ottoman Political Institutions
Section 67.9

Murad I, Edirne, and Ottoman Expansion into Thrace

The consolidation of Ottoman rule in Thrace during the reign of Murad I, conventionally dated 1362–1389, transformed the dynasty's geographical position. A foothold on the Gallipoli peninsula became the base for control over routes extending inland toward important towns and river valleys. Edirne, or Adrianople, was a prize of particular significance because it connected the road system of southeastern Europe with approaches to Constantinople and the Balkan interior. The dating of its capture is disputed: sources and historical reconstructions place it at different points in the 1360s. It is better to acknowledge that disagreement than to assign false precision. By the later fourteenth century Edirne had become a major Ottoman administrative and military center, though the development of its institutions took time.

Campaigns in Thrace were conducted by several commanders, not solely through a ruler's personal presence at each engagement. Frontier military leaders possessed followers, experience, and interests of their own. They could move along roads and river crossings, seize fortifications, negotiate local surrender, and establish bases for further operations. The crown benefited from their successes while facing the problem of keeping provincial commanders within a dynastic political system. The attraction of revenue assignments, opportunities for promotion, and shared objectives helped to align interests. Yet as the Ottoman state expanded, the tension between decentralized frontier initiative and the ruler's effort to centralize authority became more visible. This institutional relationship was as important to expansion as individual battlefield victories.

Controlling Thrace required more than the occupation of Edirne. Agricultural villages supplied grain, livestock, and manpower; fortresses helped regulate movement; towns contained craft workers, merchants, and religious institutions whose continued activity sustained the region. Existing Byzantine systems of landholding and ecclesiastical patronage did not disappear uniformly. Some elite families left, others remained under arrangements with the new authorities. Revenue and military service could be assigned to commanders, but the exact forms changed across time and place. The sultan's ability to draw regular support from newly acquired territory depended upon predictable administration as well as coercion. Communities were not merely passive scenery: their decisions about flight, cooperation, and resistance affected the practical reach of Ottoman control.

The widening frontier also altered regional diplomacy. The Ottomans interacted with Byzantine rulers, Serbian and Bulgarian lords, maritime powers, and competing Balkan factions. A principality facing several divided neighbors could exploit alliances without defeating all opponents at once. Local rulers sometimes calculated that submission to a growing Ottoman power was less dangerous than rivalry with other Christian or Muslim powers. Conversely, Ottoman expansion could trigger coalitions and resistance. Religious identity mattered to ideology and community life, yet it did not prevent cross-confessional political arrangements. Murad's European government increasingly involved a mixture of direct military authority, dependent rulers, provincial commanders, and institutions adapted from conquered lands.

The history of Edirne illustrates a larger change of political scale. The Ottoman rulers were no longer merely acquiring towns in Bithynia or operating coastal bridgeheads. They possessed territories and interests on both sides of the Straits, with communication and defense requirements extending into southeastern Europe. This increased the strategic value of the European provinces and enabled the dynasty to recruit and finance larger forces. The shift also raised new dangers: long supply lines, hostility among neighboring rulers, and competition between military households. Later Ottoman historians often presented Murad's successes as a seamless sequence; a more careful account emphasizes a process of consolidation through conflict, negotiated rule, and gradual institutional growth.

Section 67.10

The Maritsa, Kosovo, and the Transformation of Balkan Political Authority

The battle commonly known as the Maritsa or Chernomen battle in 1371 marked a major Ottoman success against forces led by important Balkan nobles. The defeat contributed to a new balance of power in Macedonia and adjacent regions, but it did not immediately transform every Balkan principality into an Ottoman province. In subsequent decades rulers made distinct choices about submission, resistance, dynastic alliance, and tribute. Some accepted forms of Ottoman suzerainty while retaining control over local government; others fought and faced further military intervention. The political consequences of Maritsa therefore unfolded over time. Battle narratives alone are inadequate without attention to the agreements, landholding arrangements, and military obligations that followed them.

Balkan political diversity was fundamental to these developments. Serbian lands were divided among competing rulers after the weakening of earlier central authority, and Bulgarian territories contained their own dynastic rivalries. Byzantium, though still symbolically significant, often lacked the resources to coordinate effective defense across the region. Hungary and maritime powers became more involved where their own strategic interests were threatened. Ottoman commanders could operate in this environment by seeking allies, offering terms to dependent lords, and exploiting rivalries. Yet such arrangements remained reversible: a vassal might switch allegiance, a defeated ruler might regroup, and a change of sultan could expose the fragility of earlier commitments. The distinction between vassalage and direct administration is essential for understanding a state growing through varied forms of control.

The Battle of Kosovo in 1389 occupies an exceptional place in later Serbian and Ottoman memories. A coalition confronting the forces of Murad I suffered a major military reversal, while Murad himself died in connection with the campaign and Bayezid I succeeded him. The exact course of the battle, the timing and circumstances of individual deaths, and the meaning of its immediate outcome have been retold in different traditions. Later heroic poetry and dynastic historiography shaped popular understandings of the event. Historians can identify Kosovo's importance without turning these traditions into fully verified battlefield reports. Nor did Kosovo eliminate all independent Serbian authority or produce permanent stability. Subsequent relations involved vassalage, campaigns, negotiation, and renewed resistance.

Military service under Ottoman suzerainty complicates any simple Muslim-versus-Christian account. Balkan rulers and their retainers could provide troops to Ottoman campaigns, including conflicts against other Christian powers or rival Muslim principalities. The Ottoman state thus increased its capacity by using local military resources while allowing dependent elites some room for maneuver. Such relationships required a balance of incentives and threats. The rulers who cooperated could retain estates or political standing; communities exposed to punitive campaigns might suffer displacement and loss. The character of incorporation varied among regions and across successive decades. To speak of conquest alone is to ignore the political negotiations through which an expanding power became an accepted, contested, or feared superior.

These developments brought the Ottomans considerable revenues and strategic depth, especially as more of Rumelia became tied to their military and fiscal networks. But they also created a political system with multiple levels of allegiance. An Ottoman sultan had to maintain the cooperation of frontier commanders, provincial officials, and Christian vassals while securing communications between Anatolia and Europe. Victories at Maritsa and Kosovo were important episodes within this wider process. The enduring historical significance lies not only in the defeated armies but in the gradual restructuring of sovereignty across southeastern Europe, where local political institutions survived in some places even as the balance of military power shifted decisively.

Section 67.11

Balkan Communities, Christian Institutions, and the Negotiation of Ottoman Authority

The incorporation of Balkan territory must be studied through the people living there. Rural cultivators, pastoral households, merchants, urban artisans, monastic communities, nobles, and clerics encountered Ottoman authority in very different circumstances. A provincial elite might negotiate the retention of property or military office; a town might surrender and obtain terms intended to protect markets and religious establishments; another settlement might endure siege, raiding, or forced displacement. The political and religious map of southeastern Europe was complex before Ottoman expansion, including Orthodox Christian institutions, Latin Christian influences in some regions, and populations with varied linguistic and social affiliations. These divisions did not disappear when a new ruler asserted sovereignty.

Orthodox monasteries and churches are especially important evidence of institutional continuity. Religious houses owned land, received donations, maintained written records, and participated in village economies. Their survival or loss depended on local circumstances and relations with rulers and officials, rather than a universal rule applied identically everywhere. Some establishments benefited from protection or confirmation of property; others faced confiscation or increased fiscal burdens. The position of bishops and monastic communities could change during warfare and succession crises. Documentary evidence is often selective because wealthy foundations had greater opportunities to preserve charters than ordinary households did. A historian should therefore avoid confusing the survival of a monastery's archives with comprehensive evidence of security for the surrounding population.

Ottoman authority was frequently negotiated through local elites. Christian lords might serve as vassals, provide troops, give hostages, enter dynastic marriage arrangements, or retain estates under new obligations. Military revenue assignments to Ottoman servants became more prominent, but change in village-level control did not always follow one model. A peasant community might experience a change in the identity of the person entitled to collect dues before other social practices shifted. In times of unstable conquest, overlapping demands could be onerous. The growing Ottoman government also had incentives to prevent unnecessary destruction of fields, mills, roads, and markets because these produced the resources needed by the army. Extraction and protection were inseparable aspects of rule, and rulers did not always achieve the balance they desired.

Religious and demographic change is likewise a long-term historical problem. Muslim soldiers and settlers established new households and institutions; some Christians converted to Islam; other Christian populations remained numerous for centuries. Migration could occur through military settlement, economic opportunity, flight from insecurity, or coercive removal. It is misleading to read the religious configuration of later Ottoman provinces back into the fourteenth century. Nor should military incorporation be confused with the much later fully developed legal and communal arrangements of the empire. Differences between a coastal city, inland market town, mountain community, and agricultural plain were often as consequential as membership in a broadly defined religious group.

The histories of Thrace, Macedonia, Serbia, and Bulgaria demonstrate that Ottoman rule took shape within existing local worlds. Communities adapted through calculation as well as through compulsion, and some resisted over long periods. Their stories can be approached through later tax surveys used cautiously, surviving foundations, travel accounts, Byzantine and South Slavic narratives, and architectural remains. These sources illuminate institutions more readily than private feelings. The task is to reconstruct what people were required to pay, whom they could appeal to, which religious places endured, and how military conflict altered family strategies. Such evidence helps explain why Ottoman conquest evolved into governance and why its consequences varied among populations even as a recognizable dynastic political order became established.

Section 67.12

Frontier Warriors, Cavalry, the Timar System, and the Beginnings of a Standing Army

Early Ottoman military power did not spring fully formed from an imperial recruitment system. Frontier raiders, Turkmen cavalry, retinues attached to local commanders, and troops supplied by vassals all contributed to expansion. Their incentives varied: some sought booty, others access to revenue, political protection, prestige, or the advancement of a patron. As Ottoman territory grew, the ruler needed forces whose loyalty was more directly attached to the dynasty and whose mobilization could be coordinated across provinces. The military institutions familiar from the later empire therefore developed through experimentation and changing political circumstances. The historian must distinguish an early practice, its later regularization, and the much more elaborate form it eventually acquired.

The timar system linked the assignment of certain revenues to the obligation to render military service. A timar was not simply a private estate owned without conditions by a cavalryman. It was a claim to specified revenues and obligations within a hierarchy of authority, capable of being reassigned and regulated by the ruler. Earlier Islamic and Seljuk revenue-assignment practices provided precedents, and existing Balkan arrangements could also influence local implementation. The relationship between a cavalry servant and the cultivators who produced revenue was therefore political and fiscal as well as military. Predictable assessment could support an army without requiring every soldier to collect pay from the treasury, but abuses and disputes were possible. The institution's documentary visibility increases over time; its mature rules should not be assumed to have governed Osman's followers.

Another development was the growth of central or household forces more dependent on the sultan. Early Ottoman traditions describe the allocation of a share of war captives to the ruler and the training of some captives for military service. Enslavement and coerced incorporation were integral to such arrangements, whatever opportunities later service might offer to individual recruits. The Janissary corps emerged as a distinctive infantry establishment associated with the dynasty, but its earliest chronology is not reducible to one universally accepted founding act. Narratives attributed to particular reigns are often retrospective. The corps' later structure, ceremonial practices, and political influence evolved through generations. Explaining these processes requires separation of central household troops from provincial cavalry and locally mobilized frontier fighters.

The devşirme, a levy of boys from Christian subject communities for conversion and service in Ottoman institutions, must also be placed accurately in time. Evidence points toward origins around the late fourteenth or early fifteenth century, with increasingly regular use during the fifteenth century, notably under Murad II. It was not identical to the seizure or purchase of captives in wartime. For families compelled to surrender boys, the institution was coercive; for some recruits, subsequent training led to careers of great status. Those possible outcomes do not make the initial process voluntary. The levy also created legal and religious tensions because of the position of Christian subjects under Islamic legal norms. Chapter 72 will examine the mature imperial system; Chapter 67 should explain only the stages relevant to its early development.

Civil and military administration expanded alongside recruitment. Members of the Çandarlı family held prominent judicial and ministerial positions, showing the increasing importance of literate administrators who could organize revenue, diplomacy, and appointments. Commanders received posts and influence, but the ruler's household sought to coordinate them. Across Anatolia and Rumelia, military necessity created incentives for census-like fiscal knowledge, regular provisioning, and protection of productive communities. This system was not yet a flawless centralized machine; recurrent succession crises and battlefield defeats revealed its limits. Its development nevertheless helped the Ottomans convert geographically scattered conquests into a politically recognizable state. The central interpretive point is cumulative institutional change: frontier warfare supplied opportunities, while administrative and military organization made it possible to preserve and exploit them.

Historical Vocabulary: Timar, Kul, Pençik, and Devşirme

Timar is a conditional revenue assignment associated with military obligations; the land itself did not automatically become the holder’s free property.

Kul identifies a servant or dependent, often a person enslaved in the ruler’s household. Pençik refers to an allotted share of war captives. Devşirme is the compulsory levy of Christian boys, involving conversion and training for state service. These practices were not one timeless institution and require distinct dating.

Division 4 — Bayezid I, Anatolian Consolidation, Timur, and the Ottoman Interregnum
Section 67.13

Bayezid I, Balkan Vassalage, Nicopolis, and the Expansion of Ottoman Power

Bayezid I inherited the Ottoman leadership after Murad I's death in 1389 and pursued a more assertive program of consolidation on both sides of the Straits. His reign illustrates the growing advantages and risks of dynastic centralization. The sultan possessed Balkan territories and dependent rulers whose resources could support further campaigns, but maintaining their obedience required political skill and the capacity to punish rebellion. Christian vassal contingents sometimes fought in Ottoman armies, demonstrating that military coalitions did not follow a simple religious boundary. Other Christian rulers opposed Ottoman expansion and sought outside assistance. Bayezid's government thus combined direct conquest, threat, diplomacy, and obligations imposed on subordinate princes. The rapid enlargement of his responsibilities made the state stronger in the short term while increasing the costs of defending its various frontiers.

The pressure on Constantinople exemplified the dynasty's growing strategic ambition. From the 1390s Bayezid attempted to constrain Byzantine movement and supplies, and the blockade or siege conventionally dated from 1394 became a central concern for the imperial government. Constantinople remained a symbolically and strategically important city with resilient defenses and access to maritime assistance. Bayezid could menace it without possessing every resource necessary to conquer it. Byzantine diplomacy sought allies among European courts and in the wider region, while Ottoman pressure also affected nearby towns and the movement of people. The survival of the city before 1453 reveals the limits of early Ottoman siege and naval capabilities as well as the persistence of Byzantine political agency.

The Battle of Nicopolis in 1396 became a highly visible demonstration of Ottoman power. A crusading force associated with Hungary and western European knights moved against Ottoman positions, but Bayezid's army defeated it near the Danube. The engagement involved allied forces and tactical circumstances that cannot be reduced to a morality play about European cavalry or Ottoman genius. Ottoman troops included men with different military backgrounds, and the battlefield was only part of a wider war over political influence in the Balkans. The defeat affected the prospects of the coalition and strengthened Bayezid's prestige, yet it did not mean that Hungary or the wider region ceased to contest Ottoman authority. Long-term border competition persisted, with local rulers responding to changing opportunities.

Bayezid's authority in southeastern Europe rested on more than victory. Provincial commanders needed access to revenue; garrisons required provisions; roads and passes needed protection; and vassals had to be persuaded or compelled to honor obligations. The monarchy could use offices and fiscal assignments to reward service, while dynastic marriages or diplomatic guarantees could structure relations with subordinate Christian rulers. Some arrangements were stable enough to provide troops for campaigns elsewhere. Others unraveled when a ruler died, an opponent intervened, or demands became unsustainable. This mixture helps explain the significant military power Bayezid could assemble and also why forces could be difficult to coordinate across such a wide political landscape.

The sultan's ambitions in Europe occurred alongside campaigns against Anatolian beyliks. An expanded Balkan military base offered troops and revenues useful for western Anatolian consolidation, but pursuing both theaters multiplied rivals. Opposition did not arise simply from religious difference: Muslim beylik rulers feared loss of autonomy, while Christian rulers might be Ottoman allies. The same interconnected political system that produced Bayezid's success exposed him to external intervention when displaced lords sought a stronger protector. By the end of the 1390s the Ottoman dynasty was a formidable regional state, but its territorial reach was built upon unequal layers of direct administration, vassalage, and recently imposed submission. The next sections show how those layers became vulnerable when Timur entered Anatolian politics.

Section 67.14

The Ottoman Incorporation of the Anatolian Beyliks and Competition for Regional Supremacy

The drive to incorporate Anatolian principalities was not an inevitable gathering of Turkish lands under a natural national sovereign. The beyliks were functioning political entities whose rulers commanded troops, held courts, supported religious institutions, and controlled valuable fiscal resources. Bayezid sought to make them subordinate through varied methods. In western Anatolia the Germiyan, Aydın, Menteşe, and Saruhan spheres became targets of expansion; further east, Karaman remained a determined competitor. In some cases a territory passed through marriage negotiations or political agreement before later conquest or consolidation. Elsewhere military operations displaced a ruling family or forced tribute. The chronological details are important because relationships changed over successive campaigns and were sometimes reversed after 1402.

Central Anatolia had exceptional strategic value. Roads linked the Aegean hinterland, Konya, Ankara, and routes toward eastern Anatolia and Iran. Fortified towns provided bases from which competing rulers could threaten communications. Cultivated districts, markets, and caravan traffic supplied revenue. Command of these networks could help an Ottoman ruler mobilize forces between western Anatolia and Rumelia, but it could also bring conflict with elites who had administered them for generations. Karaman's invocation of Seljuk prestige gave its rulers a political language distinct from Ottoman dynastic claims. A beylik ruler who resisted might do so not because he opposed political unity in the abstract, but because he wished to preserve his household's revenue, alliances, and authority.

Bayezid's incorporation policies also tested the institutional capacity of the Ottoman court. Conquering a town required assigning military protection and establishing fiscal relations; governing several principalities required coordination among provincial officers, judges, military households, and retained local notables. Officials drawn from the older administration could be useful because they understood lands, markets, and customary obligations. Yet displaced ruling families remained politically significant. A ruler could lose a capital and retain supporters who hoped for restoration. The Ottoman government therefore operated in an environment of incomplete consolidation, where military possession and durable allegiance were not identical. The consequences would become unmistakable after the defeat at Ankara.

The interaction with eastern powers intensified the problem. Refugees and displaced beylik rulers might seek support from actors operating within the post-Mongol Iranian and Anatolian world. Timur's intervention arose amid claims by multiple political figures regarding rightful rule and the treatment of dependent lords. The Qara Qoyunlu and Aq Qoyunlu had separate histories and interests, to be examined in Chapter 68, but their broader frontier environment mattered to Ottoman diplomacy. No simple division between a cohesive Ottoman west and a foreign east explains the rivalries. Turkoman, Persianate, and Mongol-derived political traditions circulated across the same routes, and competing rulers invoked overlapping claims to tribute, protection, and dynastic legitimacy.

The comparison with the beyliks is fundamental to understanding early Ottoman success. Some had naval resources, others stronger positions in Seljuk ceremonial tradition, and still others favorable agricultural lands. The Ottomans benefited from their Bithynian and Balkan expansion but still faced substantial Anatolian opposition. The terms annexation, tribute, and vassalage should be used precisely rather than interchangeably. A principality compelled to provide soldiers was not necessarily administered like a directly held Ottoman district; a treaty might be abandoned when military circumstances shifted. Bayezid's gains represented a dramatic enlargement of dynastic power, not the final settlement of Anatolia. Their reversibility is part of the historical explanation for the crisis of 1402.

Section 67.15

Timur, the Battle of Ankara, and the Collapse of Bayezid's Political Order, 1402

The confrontation between Bayezid I and Timur arose from overlapping expansionist ambitions in Anatolia and the post-Mongol political sphere. Timur had established a vast military dominance across Iran and Central Asia and intervened in neighboring regions through campaigns that combined claims to overlordship, punishment of opponents, and the distribution of conquered resources. Bayezid had absorbed Anatolian beyliks and pressured rulers who could appeal to Timur for restoration. Diplomatic exchanges escalated competing demands. Neither sovereign faced an entirely alien world: both operated among military patrons, displaced dynasts, and regional elites who understood power through tribute, service, and recognized status. Their conflict was therefore an international contest over political hierarchy as well as a struggle between two formidable armies.

The Battle of Ankara occurred in July 1402; published reconstructions differ over the precise day, so a general month-and-year date is preferable unless the disagreement is explicitly discussed. Timur assembled forces shaped by campaigns across several regions, while Bayezid drew on Ottoman troops and allied contingents. The Ottoman army included elements whose loyalty had been tested by recent annexations, as well as Balkan soldiers bound by vassalage. Explanations of the defeat stress strategic positioning, the stresses of campaigning, the comparative resources of the armies, and defections or unreliable service among some Anatolian groups. The exact tactical sequence cannot be recreated without considering the purposes and limitations of competing narratives. Bayezid was defeated and captured, a catastrophic reversal for a ruler whose prestige had risen through victory and consolidation.

Bayezid died in captivity in 1403. Timur did not simply replace him as the permanent governing monarch of the Ottoman territories. Instead, the defeat opened the way for renewed autonomy among Anatolian principalities and intensified struggles within the Ottoman dynasty. Some deposed beylik rulers recovered positions with Timurid approval or under the general conditions produced by the collapse of Bayezid's authority. Byzantine rulers and Balkan elites gained opportunities to revise agreements or recover room for maneuver. The geopolitical environment was transformed without being permanently organized under a single Timurid administration. This difference between battlefield victory and sustainable state-building is central to the chapter's interpretation of the event.

Ankara also exposed structural vulnerabilities within the Ottoman state. The monarchy controlled a large territory but had incorporated many of its Anatolian districts only recently. Provincial loyalties, military assignments, and tribute arrangements did not automatically remain intact after a ruler's defeat. Sons of Bayezid could claim portions of the inheritance, and military commanders had to choose among them. Yet the disaster did not erase the dynasty's achievements. Bursa, Edirne, and other centers contained administrative networks and populations invested in political stability; Balkan troops and elites could still support Ottoman princes. The existence of these institutions and relationships helps explain why a dynastic struggle followed rather than complete dissolution into unrelated local sovereignties.

Chapter 66 has already treated Timur's broader campaign sequence and the place of Ankara within Timurid history. Here the battle matters for what it reveals about the Ottoman project. Rapid territorial enlargement had created military capacity without eliminating the particular interests of recently subordinated rulers. A defeat at the top could reopen those political claims. Conversely, the government's survival depended on resources and loyalties capable of being mobilized after the founder of its recent expansion was removed. The Ottoman Interregnum therefore becomes a test case in institutional resilience, provincial politics, and the difference between a dynasty that loses one ruler and a state that disappears entirely.

Section 67.16

The Ottoman Interregnum, 1402–1413: Dynastic War and the Survival of the State

Between the defeat at Ankara and Mehmed I's victory in 1413, Bayezid's surviving sons fought over an inheritance divided between Anatolia and Rumelia. The major contenders included Süleyman, İsa, Musa, and Mehmed. Their positions changed as alliances shifted, battles were lost, and commanders revised their calculations. It is misleading to depict the period as a simple orderly competition governed by a recognized electoral rule. Each prince sought military support, fiscal resources, towns, and recognition from groups able to confer practical authority. The dynastic name mattered, but the value of a princely claim depended upon the soldiers and communities prepared to support it. The Interregnum was simultaneously a family war and a struggle over the territorial organization of the Ottoman state.

The distinction between European and Anatolian holdings was particularly important. Rumelia contained established military networks and revenue resources that could sustain a claimant independently of his brothers. Anatolian districts remained politically unsettled after the restoration of beylik rulers and the disruption of Bayezid's annexations. The struggle could move from one theater to another as a prince gained or lost access to forces and routes. Towns such as Edirne and Bursa mattered because they were administrative and economic centers, not merely names on a dynastic list. The Straits created opportunities for movement and obstacles to coordination, while maritime actors and neighboring states could influence who gained access to crossing points.

Byzantine diplomacy played an active role. The imperial government and other Balkan powers could negotiate with competing Ottoman princes, exchange assistance for territorial or financial concessions, and attempt to create a less threatening balance. Christian rulers were neither uniformly passive Ottoman dependents nor members of one permanent anti-Ottoman alliance. Their choices reflected immediate survival, dynastic rivalries, and regional opportunities. Ottoman commanders also calculated which prince could best maintain their privileges and protect their territories. A soldier's loyalty to a household might conflict with the interests of a particular province, while a ruler attempting to centralize authority could alienate commanders whose support he needed. These tensions prevented a quick reunification.

Mehmed's eventual triumph in 1413 was built through successive political and military contests. His victory over Musa brought a relatively unified dynastic government back into being, but the restoration was incomplete: different regions had suffered varying degrees of destruction and dislocation, and rivals could still appeal to disgruntled groups. The existence of later claimants and revolts warns against portraying 1413 as a magical return to the conditions before Ankara. Mehmed needed to reestablish agreements, appoint officers, secure borders, and restore flows of revenue. He also had to decide when accommodation would be more useful than punitive reconquest. The continuity of Ottoman political identity through these struggles was a significant achievement precisely because it had not been guaranteed.

Why did the dynasty survive? One explanation lies in the institutional accumulation of the previous century. Administrative centers, commercial networks, military followings, and established relationships between rulers and local elites did not vanish with Bayezid. Many groups benefited from some form of Ottoman government and could support a prince likely to maintain it. A second explanation lies in contingency: no rival external power succeeded in permanently reorganizing all Ottoman territories after Timur's departure. A third lies in Mehmed's capacity to build coalitions. These explanations are compatible rather than mutually exclusive. The Interregnum demonstrates that early Ottoman rule had become more than Osman's personal frontier following while remaining vulnerable to dynastic war and the political demands of provincial society.

Division 5 — Ottoman Recovery, Institutional Development, and the Debate over State Formation
Section 67.17

Mehmed I and the Restoration of Ottoman Authority, 1413–1421

Mehmed I's victory in 1413 ended the most acute phase of the Ottoman dynastic civil war, but restoring rule proved different from winning the last decisive contest. Commanders had become accustomed to choosing sides, towns had experienced multiple claims to revenue, and neighboring rulers had secured concessions during the struggle. Mehmed needed to reestablish the relationship between the central household and provincial officials while avoiding a succession of new rebellions. The surviving state still controlled substantial resources and military followings, but its political legitimacy required renewal through appointments, negotiation, and the performance of sovereignty. Measured against Bayezid's rapid conquests, the achievement of Mehmed's reign lay especially in recovery and the preservation of the dynasty as a viable ruling institution.

The sultan operated within a region in which many of the earlier Anatolian principalities had recovered room for maneuver. He could not necessarily impose immediate uniform administration everywhere. Diplomacy and selective campaigns were therefore essential tools, as were agreements with Byzantium and other Balkan neighbors. The crown sought to secure roads, markets, and garrisons while restoring claims to taxation and service. Military patronage remained central to the retention of followers, yet government also relied on literate administrators, judges, and locally influential families. The distinction between consolidation and outright territorial reconquest helps explain why Mehmed's reign was not a simple replay of his father's policies. A sultan who could reduce internal conflict might increase state strength even without restoring every lost frontier.

The movements associated with Şeyh Bedreddin, Börklüce Mustafa, and Torlak Kemal reveal tensions within the post-Interregnum political landscape. Later Ottoman chronicles sometimes link these figures within a single rebellion, but their relationships, chronology, teachings, and objectives are not equally established. Bedreddin was a learned jurist and religious figure whose career intersected with the dynastic struggle; movements linked to Börklüce in the Aegean and Torlak Kemal in western Anatolia were described by different and sometimes hostile writers. Some narratives emphasize unconventional religious teachings or communal practices; others frame the unrest as political disobedience. Modern interpretation must distinguish what sources say from what can be independently demonstrated. A claim that all participants followed one coherent revolutionary program would go beyond the evidence.

The revolts also expose the varied interests of provincial society. War, fiscal pressure, disruption of established patronage, and competition among religious authorities could create dissatisfaction, but one should not infer identical motives for every follower. Rural people, frontier warriors, religious disciples, and local notables inhabited different settings. State suppression demonstrates the crown's resolve to contain challenges, while the persistence of disputes indicates that military recovery did not eliminate questions about authority or economic obligation. The events are also a useful lesson in source criticism: hostile histories may combine genuine memories of conflict with theological accusations designed to discredit opponents. A scholarly textbook should present competing interpretations rather than invent a single settled doctrine.

By Mehmed's death in 1421, Ottoman government had recovered its center sufficiently to pass to Murad II. This does not mean that the polity was free of succession challenges or that provincial interests had ceased to matter. On the contrary, the first years of Murad's reign show how dangerous rival claimants remained. Mehmed's importance lies in demonstrating that Ottoman political restoration depended on the sustained work of coalition-building and administration after civil war. The state that survived into the 1420s had learned from a crisis that exposed its vulnerability, and its institutions now provided a stronger platform for continued Balkan and Anatolian expansion.

Everyday Life: A Frontier Society Becomes a Regional State

The experience of a cultivator differed from that of a cavalry commander. A village household faced harvest demands, livestock movements, market access, military passage, and disputes over who had the right to collect dues. An urban craft worker relied on supplies, customers, protection, and contracts; religious foundations employed teachers, servants, and administrators.

Christian clergy, Muslim jurists, merchants, migrants, enslaved recruits, and women who held or endowed property entered Ottoman institutions under different conditions. No single source speaks for all. A dated foundation document illuminates property relationships; a traveler describes public hospitality; a chronicler emphasizes war. Read together, these sources suggest why institutions mattered to civilians without proving that every community experienced the same terms.

Section 67.18

Murad II, Renewed Expansion, Varna, Kosovo, and the Consolidation of Ottoman Power

Murad II's reign, spanning 1421–1444 and a renewed period of rule from 1446 to 1451, demonstrated the resilience and limits of Ottoman recovery. The succession initially confronted the challenges of rival claimants, including Mustafa, whose position involved Byzantine diplomacy, and further disputes within the dynasty. Murad's government had to maintain authority in Anatolia while managing a complex Balkan frontier where Hungary, Serbia, Byzantium, Venice, and other powers held conflicting interests. Campaigns and diplomacy were interdependent. A settlement with one neighbor could make operations against another possible; a rival prince supported from abroad could threaten domestic cohesion. Statecraft involved shifting alliances and negotiation alongside military force.

The Ottoman capture of Thessaloniki in 1430 illustrates the importance of established urban and maritime centers. The city had been a place of prolonged struggle involving Byzantine and Venetian claims, and its conquest altered regional trade and strategic access. Control of a major port required continued management of inhabitants, religious institutions, markets, and defenses. It was not merely an additional victory on a military chronology. Elsewhere Murad faced campaigns connected with Serbia, Hungary, and the northern Balkans. John Hunyadi emerged as a formidable opponent whose operations challenged Ottoman military positions and encouraged coalitions. The sultan could suffer reverses as well as achieve victories, and the frontier balance remained fluid throughout the 1440s.

The crisis of 1444 reveals both dynastic calculation and military necessity. After a period of warfare and negotiation, Murad withdrew from active sovereignty in favor of his young son Mehmed II. Yet a renewed crusading campaign under the leadership of King Władysław and associated Balkan and Hungarian forces brought Murad back into military command. At the Battle of Varna in November 1444, the Ottoman army defeated the coalition and Władysław was killed. Later accounts offer vivid stories about treaty-breaking and battlefield heroism, but their details must be tested against differing narratives. The victory was politically consequential because it checked a major effort to capitalize on apparent Ottoman vulnerability and helped sustain the government's Balkan position.

Murad resumed the sultanate in 1446 after tensions involving the young Mehmed's government and military factions. The Second Battle of Kosovo in 1448, where forces associated with John Hunyadi were defeated, further strengthened the Ottoman position. Nevertheless opposition persisted. Skanderbeg's resistance in Albania presented difficult terrain, resilient local alliances, and sustained challenges to Ottoman control. Venetian and other maritime interests also remained relevant. The army's capacity to fight on several fronts depended upon revenue, provincial administration, troop recruitment, and the maintenance of communications between Europe and Anatolia. Victories were made possible by institutions that had been developing over the preceding century, but those institutions did not eliminate the political significance of local resistance.

The government Murad left at his death in 1451 differed markedly from Osman's frontier enterprise. It possessed established urban centers, military hierarchies, fiscal arrangements, provincial officials, diplomatic relationships, and a dynasty that had survived defeat and civil conflict. Yet major ambitions remained unfinished: Constantinople retained Byzantine government, Anatolian rivals continued to matter, and European powers contested Ottoman expansion. Mehmed II's accession marks a suitable conclusion for Chapter 67 because it allows historians to assess Ottoman state formation before the conquest of 1453. Chapter 70 will analyze that conquest and the wider imperial transformation. The question answered here is how the dynasty acquired the resources and resilience that made a new phase of expansion possible.

Section 67.19

Towns, Trade, Religious Foundations, Fiscal Institutions, and Everyday Ottoman Society

The early Ottoman state can be understood only partly from its campaign history. Bursa, İznik, Edirne, and other towns connected military government to markets, workshops, religious life, and the countryside. Bursa became important for trade linking Anatolia with routes toward Iran and the Mediterranean; Edirne provided a political and commercial center in Rumelia; İznik retained significance as a center of craft production and religious learning. Merchants carried textiles, livestock products, grain, metal goods, and other commodities through networks that crossed political frontiers. Such activity depended on access to credit, protection of roads, markets, and some capacity to resolve disputes. Towns created regular revenue and provided the administrative environment for an expanding court, even as political upheaval could interrupt exchange.

Agriculture and pastoralism remained foundations of everyday life. Villagers cultivated grain, tended orchards, kept animals, and maintained local water systems where necessary. Pastoral households moved herds between seasonal grazing zones, sometimes entering negotiations or conflicts with settled cultivators. Tax obligations could include payments in kind, monetary dues, labor demands, and various locally specific assessments. The person entitled to collect revenue might change with military reassignment or political conquest. Such adjustments mattered to the survival strategies of families whose labor supported both urban consumption and cavalry service. The emergence of the timar system helps explain the transfer of resources from countryside to army, but precise legal arrangements differed across time; later standardized categories cannot simply be read backward.

Religious foundations offer evidence of social investment and political legitimacy. A waqf, often termed vakıf in Ottoman Turkish, dedicated property income to designated purposes such as a mosque, madrasa, hospice, public kitchen, or other charitable and religious activity. Rulers, commanders, wealthy townspeople, and women could participate in endowment-making. These institutions provided places for worship, education, hospitality, and the distribution of assistance while visibly connecting patrons with neighborhoods and families. Endowments also organized property, employment, and maintenance, creating networks of staff, beneficiaries, and supervisors. A foundation charter may preserve valuable information about land and administration, but its ideal statement of obligations is not itself proof that every benefit was delivered exactly as planned.

Social diversity must be kept in focus. Muslim and Christian inhabitants shared commercial spaces and some obligations while belonging to different religious institutions. Christian communities could retain churches and clergy under negotiated conditions; converts developed new relationships with Muslim religious and legal frameworks. Women participated in household property, inheritance, trade, and patronage, although surviving evidence often favors elite families and charitable foundations. Artisans, market inspectors, local judges, students, servants, and enslaved persons occupied social positions rarely visible in royal military stories. Coercion remained part of the economy: warfare created captives, and household slavery existed alongside other labor arrangements. A balanced account neither overlooks these relations nor assumes that all people experienced Ottoman rule in one way.

The state itself was continually being made through such activities. Regular markets required security; garrisons required provisions; religious institutions required maintenance; and agricultural production required cultivators willing or compelled to remain. Conquest could disrupt each of these, while stable rule could support their continuation or expansion. Documentary records, inscriptions, coins, foundations, and urban structures are indispensable for recovering this history, though they favor institutions wealthy enough to leave traces. The deeper reason to include everyday life is explanatory: Ottoman political resilience rested partly on a society and economy that could sustain government beyond the personal fortunes of a single ruler. This provides the basis for evaluating why the Ottoman dynasty ultimately prevailed among its many Anatolian rivals.

Why Did the Ottomans Succeed? Historiography, State Formation, and the World of 1451

The success of the early Ottoman dynasty has generated one of the most influential debates in Ottoman historiography. Its starting problem is deceptively simple: around 1300 the Ottomans were not obviously the strongest of the Anatolian beyliks, yet by 1451 their rulers governed a major interregional state. Earlier historical writing sometimes explained this rise through an uninterrupted religious mission, exceptional leadership, or the inevitable weakening of Byzantium. Modern scholarship has challenged each exclusive explanation. Available sources are uneven, especially for Osman, and many detailed Ottoman narratives were composed long after the events they describe. Explaining Ottoman success therefore requires both historical argument and sustained inquiry into what can actually be known.

Mehmet Fuat Köprülü emphasized the social and cultural contexts of Turkish Anatolia, seeking to connect Ottoman formation with earlier institutions rather than treating it as a spontaneous creation. Paul Wittek advanced a famous interpretation stressing the role of gaza, frontier struggle understood in religious terms, in attracting warriors and legitimating expansion. The thesis directed attention to ideological language in chronicles and inscriptions, but it became controversial when interpreted as a claim that all early Ottoman actors pursued holy war as their exclusive motive. Actual alliances crossed religious boundaries, and Ottoman rulers fought Muslim rivals as well as Byzantine and Balkan opponents. These facts challenge a rigid religious explanation without proving that religious commitment or political rhetoric had no historical importance.

Rudi Paul Lindner drew attention to tribal organization, social alliances, and the changing circumstances of nomadic and settled communities. Colin Imber emphasized difficulties posed by late and legendary accounts. Cemal Kafadar, in turn, interpreted the frontier as a flexible cultural environment in which gaza language, pragmatic accommodation, and social networks could coexist. Heath Lowry explored the importance of political incorporation and the role of Byzantine and Balkan elites. These scholars do not share one identical theory, and their positions should not be collapsed into a simplistic opposition between religion and economics. A strong historiographical account asks what each interpretation explains, which evidence it emphasizes, and how arguments have evolved in response to criticism.

Comparative history supplies additional tests. Many Anatolian principalities could claim military achievement or religious patronage; only some occupied a frontier that opened extensive new territories in the Balkans. Ottoman location in Bithynia created opportunities against a weakened Byzantine regional defense, while Ottoman relations with Byzantine factions and Christian vassals enlarged military and diplomatic options. Urban conquests under Orhan supplied revenues and institutions; Rumelian expansion offered further manpower and strategic depth; the timar and developing household forces strengthened dynastic organization. Bayezid's expansion showed the power of these resources, and the Interregnum exposed their fragility. The recovery under Mehmed I and Murad II suggests that administrative networks, regional elites, and commercial centers could outlast the defeat of an individual sultan.

Sources must be assessed for their proximity and function. Pachymeres supplies an early Byzantine viewpoint on Osman, while Ibn Battuta offers a traveler's perspective on Orhan's Anatolia. Ahmedi's early Ottoman dynastic writing constructs moral and political meaning around a ruling family, and later chronicles amplify or revise that memory. Coins, inscriptions, and foundation deeds can test aspects of political authority and patronage but rarely reveal private motives directly. The best conclusion is therefore multicausal and provisional: geography, military enterprise, local accommodation, dynastic endurance, revenue, institutions, and legitimizing ideas reinforced each other in changing combinations. By 1451 the Ottomans possessed a state capable of major expansion, not because their empire had been destined since 1299, but because generations of contingent decisions and accumulated institutions had made it possible.

Historical Vocabulary: Gaza, Gazi, and Dynastic Memory

Gaza and gazi carried military and religious associations, but their specific uses changed across settings and texts. Later Ottoman historical writers deployed them to interpret early expansion and legitimate the ruling family.

Dynastic memory is the selective historical narrative through which a ruling house represents its origins and character. Such narratives are evidence of later political culture even when they cannot verify every event they describe.

Historical Debate: Gaza, Frontier Society, or Institutions?

Interpretation stressing religious frontier warfare: gaza language and the prestige of campaigning may have attracted followers and offered a moral explanation of expansion. Paul Wittek made this argument influential, but later readers sometimes treated it as more exclusive than the evidence permits.

Interpretations stressing tribal and political relations: Rudi Paul Lindner and others emphasized changing social organization; Heath Lowry highlighted the incorporation of regional and Christian elites; Colin Imber stressed the limits of late traditions.

Integrative interpretation: Cemal Kafadar investigated the religious and political fluidity of frontier society without erasing either ideology or pragmatism. His approach does not eliminate debate over specific sources or motivations.

Chapter Summary

After the Mongol victory at Köse Dağ in 1243, the Sultanate of Rum survived under external supremacy while the balance of authority shifted toward regional households and principalities. Persianate and Seljuk administrative practices, caravan routes, religious institutions, and urban economies persisted even as central political power diminished. Migration, pastoral life, agricultural continuity, and the participation of Greek, Armenian, Turkic, Kurdish, and other communities made Anatolia a socially complex setting for new states.

The Karamanids, Germiyanids, Aydınids, Menteşe, Candarids, Karasi, and other beyliks pursued distinct political and military strategies. Their existence demonstrates that Ottoman primacy was uncertain around 1300. Osman’s own origins are difficult to establish because later chronicles contain legends and retrospective dynastic claims. The battle at Bapheus, conventionally dated 1302 but disputed in detail, supplies relatively near-contemporary evidence of his military prominence along the Byzantine frontier.

Under Orhan the capture of Bursa, İznik, and İzmit provided access to established towns, markets, agricultural revenues, and new possibilities of administration. The 1324 Mekece foundation document is an important corrective to later narratives. The incorporation of Karasi and the exploitation of Byzantine civil conflicts facilitated Ottoman movement into Europe. Gallipoli in 1354 became an essential foothold, while Murad I expanded Ottoman authority in Thrace and the Balkans through military campaigns and negotiated political relations.

The victories at the Maritsa in 1371 and Kosovo in 1389 altered Balkan politics, but local rulers and communities were incorporated through several forms of submission, vassalage, service, and provincial government. Ottoman military power developed gradually through frontier bands, cavalry connected to revenue assignments, and growing dynastic household forces. The Janissaries and compulsory devşirme cannot be projected backward in their later mature forms or separated from the coercion of recruitment and conversion.

Bayezid I expanded rapidly in the Balkans and against Anatolian beyliks, defeating a crusading coalition at Nicopolis in 1396. The defeat and capture of Bayezid by Timur at Ankara in 1402 opened the way for revived beylik power and a severe Ottoman dynastic struggle. The Interregnum lasted to 1413, when Mehmed I prevailed. The dynasty survived because administrative centers, military households, regional alliances, and populations with interests in continuity continued to exist after the catastrophic defeat.

Mehmed I restored government amid continued revolts and competition, while Murad II defended and expanded Ottoman power through diplomacy and campaigns, including Varna in 1444 and the Second Battle of Kosovo in 1448. By 1451 the dynasty possessed substantial resources but had not yet conquered Constantinople. Historians explain the Ottoman rise through a changing combination of geography, political opportunity, religious language, alliances, institutions, and dynastic resilience, rather than one inevitable or exclusive cause.

Chapter Reflection

The Ottoman rise demonstrates that the formation of a durable state was neither a sudden foundation in 1299 nor a continuous series of victories. A fragmenting Seljuk order, Byzantine frontier insecurity, competition among Anatolian beyliks, Balkan alliances, military recruitment, fiscal institutions, and the agency of regional communities all shaped the dynasty’s possibilities. Ankara in 1402 revealed the fragility of rapid conquest, while the recovery through 1451 demonstrated the significance of institutions capable of surviving a ruler’s defeat.

Later Ottoman governments would expand the dynasty’s reach, elaborate its institutions, and construct stories about its origins. Chapter 67 stops before the conquest of Constantinople in 1453, allowing the formative phase to be understood on its own terms. Chapters 68–70 examine eastern Turkoman polities, local frontier communities, Constantinople, and the new imperial rivalries that emerged from this late medieval transition.
