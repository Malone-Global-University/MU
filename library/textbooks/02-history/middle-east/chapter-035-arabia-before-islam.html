Part IX — Arabia and the Emergence of Islam

Arabia before Islam

A History of the Middle East: From Ancient Civilizations to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

Across the black lava fields and gravel plains of the northern Arabian desert, thousands upon thousands of short texts were scratched into basalt boulders by people who left no books, no royal archives, and no names that any chronicler thought worth recording. Many of them record little more than a name and a genealogy, a season spent pasturing animals at a particular place, grief for a dead relative, or an appeal to a deity for safety or rain. Read together, they are among the most direct voices to survive from the pastoral world of ancient Arabia. Yet those voices fall silent well before the year 600 CE, and the major narrative sources that describe the Arabia of that later moment were written down only generations afterward, by authors who already knew how the story ended. This chapter begins with that difficulty, because it defines the whole task: to reconstruct a society as it was, rather than as later memory needed it to have been.

The Arabian Peninsula on the eve of the seventh century was not a blank space waiting for a prophet. It was a large, ecologically varied, and densely connected region. Camel herders and sheep-and-goat pastoralists moved seasonally between wells and pastures. Date-growing oases such as Yathrib, Khaybar, and Tayma supported farmers, artisans, and fortified households. Western Arabian towns such as Mecca and Ṭāʾif supplied pilgrims, markets, and caravans. The highlands of Yemen carried the legacy of long-established literate kingdoms. The coasts of the Persian Gulf and Oman looked toward Iran and the Indian Ocean. In the north, communities along the Syrian and Mesopotamian frontiers lived beside the Byzantine and Sasanian empires and had long been drawn into their strategic rivalry, the subject of Chapter 34. To speak of “Arabia” in the singular is therefore a convenience. The peninsula contained many societies whose political forms ran from households and tribal coalitions to kingdoms and imperial clients.

The religious landscape was just as varied. Local and regional deities were venerated at shrines, sacred stones, springs, and trees. Jewish communities lived in Yemen, the Hijaz, and elsewhere. Christianity had taken root in Najran, along the northern frontier, and on the shores of the Gulf, through several rival ecclesiastical traditions. In South Arabia, inscriptions from the fifth and sixth centuries address a single God in monotheistic terms. Different groups worshiped, interpreted, and organized their religious lives in different ways, and the idea that “pre-Islamic religion” meant a single, simple idol worship is a later simplification that this chapter will not adopt.

Arabic literary culture was already sophisticated. Poets composed elaborate odes whose conventions imply long training and wide audiences, though the poems we possess were largely collected and transmitted in the Islamic period. Arabic was beginning to be written, in scripts descended from Nabataean Aramaic, in a handful of surviving inscriptions. Genealogies, stories of battles, and the memory of tribal alliances formed a rich oral tradition, whose later written form is indispensable and yet cannot be treated as a transparent window on the sixth century. A responsible account must therefore use inscriptions, archaeology, foreign Greek and Syriac writers, poetry, and later Arabic histories together, and be candid about where each one fails.

This chapter accordingly has three aims. The first is to describe the social worlds of Arabia, at several scales: household, tribe, oasis, town, regional kingdom, and imperial frontier. The second is to show how those worlds were connected through trade, kinship, religion, language, and politics. The third is to teach the habit of source criticism that this period demands. Throughout, the chapter resists three older textbook shortcuts: that “Bedouin” means all Arabs, that the Arabic term jāhiliyya (often translated “ignorance”) describes a culturally barbarous age, and that pre-Islamic religion was merely the worship of idols. Each of these shortcuts owes more to later polemic than to the surviving evidence.

The chapter also has firm boundaries. Chapter 36 turns to Muhammad, the first revelations, the Qurʾan as historical evidence, and opposition in Mecca; Chapter 37 treats Medina after the Hijra. Chapter 35 builds the world in which those events occurred and stops at its threshold. Nothing in the Arabia of about 600 made Muhammad’s career or the rise of Islam inevitable, and describing the earlier society as though it were a preparation for what followed would distort it. Arabia before Islam deserves to be understood on its own terms, and the later transformation is more interesting, not less, for being understood against that background.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Evaluate the principal kinds of evidence for pre-Islamic Arabia (inscriptions, archaeology, external Greek and Syriac sources, poetry, and later Arabic historical tradition) and explain the strengths and limits of each.
  2. Describe the major ecological and regional zones of the peninsula and explain why they supported different economies and political forms.
  3. Explain how pastoral, oasis, and town-based ways of life were interdependent rather than separate “worlds.”
  4. Analyze kinship, lineage, tribe, clan, and clientage as flexible social and political categories rather than fixed biological units.
  5. Compare forms of political authority in Arabia, from household and tribal leadership to confederations, Kindite kingship, South Arabian monarchy, and imperial client arrangements.
  6. Assess the evidence for Mecca, Quraysh, and the Kaʿba, distinguishing later Islamic tradition from independently supported claims.
  7. Describe Yathrib as an oasis society before the Hijra, including its agriculture, clans, and fractures.
  8. Explain the diversity of religious life in Arabia, including polytheistic cults, Jewish and Christian communities, and South Arabian monotheism.
  9. Interpret pre-Islamic poetry and inscriptions as evidence while identifying problems of authenticity and transmission.
  10. Argue why Arabia around 600 CE should be understood as a diverse and connected society rather than a primitive prelude to Islam.

  2. In what ways did Arabia’s varied environments produce different economies, and how were those economies linked?

Historical Context

Chapter 2 introduced the physical geography of the peninsula, its deserts, oases, highlands, and coasts, and showed that Arabia’s apparent isolation was always permeable. Chapter 16 described the end of the Bronze Age order, and Chapters 28 through 34 traced the Hellenistic, Parthian, Roman, and Sasanian worlds that surrounded Arabia and the Christianization of the Middle East. Chapter 34 in particular examined Arabia, Yemen, caravan and maritime networks, Jewish and Christian communities, the Ghassanids, the Lakhmids, Aksumite involvement, and the Byzantine-Sasanian wars of the early seventh century. This chapter does not retell that material. It turns inward, asking what Arabian society looked like from within.

A few background facts are needed. By late antiquity, the Roman and Persian empires had both built client relationships with Arab groups on their desert frontiers. The Ghassanids (also called the Jafnids) served the Byzantines, and the Lakhmids of al-Ḥīra served the Sasanians. In the south, the kingdom of Ḥimyar had unified most of South Arabia by about 300 CE and adopted monotheism before suffering Aksumite and then Sasanian intervention in the sixth century. In the center and west of the peninsula, by contrast, there was no durable state. Authority was dispersed among kin groups, local leaders, and religious and commercial institutions. Understanding how such a society functioned, and how it connected to its neighbors, is the central problem of this chapter.

Historians also need a terminological caution. The word “Arab” appears in Assyrian texts from the first millennium BCE, in Greek and Latin sources, in South Arabian inscriptions, and later in Arabic, but it did not always mean the same thing. In some contexts it meant mobile pastoralists, in others speakers of a language, and in others a political or ethnic claim. This chapter uses “Arab” cautiously, following the usage of the sources in each case, and does not assume that all inhabitants of the peninsula shared a single self-identification. It likewise does not assume that “Arab” and “Muslim” are interchangeable. They were not, then or later.

Section 35.1

Reconstructing Arabia before Islam: Sources, Silences, and Later Memory

The evidence for pre-Islamic Arabia is abundant in some respects and thin in others, and what survives is unevenly distributed across regions, periods, and social classes. The best way to begin is to sort it by kind. Inscriptions are the most direct contemporary evidence. South Arabia produced thousands of texts in the Ancient South Arabian scripts (often called Musnad), recording royal building projects, irrigation works, dedications, legal arrangements, and, in the fifth and sixth centuries, military campaigns and religious statements. Northern Arabia produced a very different corpus: the Safaitic inscriptions of the basalt desert, the Dadanitic and Lihyanite texts of the al-ʿUlā region, the Nabataean inscriptions of Hegra, and later Arabic texts in Nabataean-derived script. Many ordinary individuals wrote these northern texts, and they are therefore valuable for social history. The Namāra inscription of 328 CE, a funerary text for a ruler named Imruʾ al-Qays, shows an Arab ruler presented with a grand title, but its interpretation continues to be debated.

Archaeology adds settlement patterns, irrigation systems, tombs, shrines, and trade goods. It is, however, uneven. Excavation at sites such as Tayma, Qaryat al-Fāw, Thaj, and Najrān has produced significant results, while the sanctuary area of Mecca and the old core of Medina are essentially closed to systematic archaeological investigation. This produces a paradox. The two towns that matter most to later history are among the least archaeologically known for the period before Islam. External sources in Greek, Latin, Syriac, Ethiopic, and Persian provide outside views: Roman and Byzantine historians, ecclesiastical writers, accounts of Christian martyrdom, and travelers’ notes. They are contemporary but they are also partial, written by outsiders with their own purposes, and they concentrate on regions near imperial frontiers.

The great narrative source is the later Arabic tradition. Biographies of Muhammad (such as the work of Ibn Isḥāq, known through the recension of Ibn Hishām), histories such as al-Ṭabarī’s, genealogical works, anthologies like the Kitāb al-Aghānī, and Ibn al-Kalbī’s Book of Idols all preserve a wealth of detail on pre-Islamic tribes, rulers, shrines, and battles. They were composed from the eighth century onward, within Islamic communities that had their own questions: how to understand the age before revelation, how to trace genealogies of rulers and companions, and how to describe the “days of ignorance.” These works drew on older oral and written material that cannot always be reconstructed. Historians therefore treat them as indispensable but filtered. They may preserve genuine memory, but they also reflect retrospective schemes, partisan interests, and literary shaping.

Poetry is a fourth category, and a difficult one. Poems attributed to pre-Islamic poets were collected and edited in the eighth and ninth centuries. Section 35.21 explains why this raises hard questions of authenticity. Finally, the Qurʾan contains references to the religious and social world of its audience. Chapter 36 will treat the Qurʾan as historical evidence in detail; here it is enough to say that it must be used carefully, with attention to what it can and cannot tell us about the society it addresses.

Good practice means triangulating. Where an inscription, an external source, and the later tradition agree, confidence is high. Where only a later tradition speaks, as for the internal history of Mecca, claims must be flagged as tradition. Where the evidence is silent, the silence should be acknowledged and not filled with plausible narrative. The honest summary is that we can describe a great deal about the structures, institutions, and ways of life of pre-Islamic Arabia, and much less about specific events, dates, and individuals before the later sixth century. Dates such as that of the Battle of Dhū Qār, or the date of the expedition against Mecca, are traditional and uncertain.

Section 35.2

Arabia’s Regional Worlds: Desert, Highlands, Oases, Coasts, and Steppe

Chapter 2 introduced the peninsula’s geography; this section summarizes only what is needed for social history. The western edge, the Hijaz, consists of a narrow coastal plain (the Tihāma), a mountain escarpment, and an interior of lava fields and wadis. Mecca lies in a narrow dry valley within these hills, while Ṭāʾif, at higher elevation, has gardens and fruit. Further north, oases such as Yathrib, Khaybar, Tayma, Wādī al-Qurā, and al-ʿUlā lie along the routes between Yemen and Syria. East of the Hijaz is Najd, a high plateau of pastures and seasonal wells, and further east and south the Yamāma, with its own oasis settlements and powerful groups.

In the south, the Yemeni highlands receive monsoon rains and supported terraced agriculture, dense settlement, and the old kingdoms of Saba, Qataban, Maʿīn, Ḥaḍramawt, and Ḥimyar. The coastal and desert margins of Ḥaḍramawt and Oman produced frankincense and myrrh, and linked Arabia to the Indian Ocean. Eastern Arabia, then often called al-Baḥrayn, ran along the Gulf from the head of the Gulf to the base of Qatar, with oasis agriculture at al-Aḥsāʾ and Qaṭīf, and maritime communities along the coast. Oman, as Chapter 2 noted, faced the Indian Ocean and had its own agriculture and irrigation traditions. In the north, the Syrian steppe, the Wādī Sirḥān, and the lava fields supported pastoral communities and linked Arabia to Syria and Mesopotamia.

The implication is that “Arabia” did not have a single economy or society. Yemen’s kingdoms resembled the older states of the Near East more than they resembled the herding societies of the Najdi steppe. The oases of the Hijaz were closer to Mediterranean-style agricultural communities than to nomadic encampments. The Gulf coast had its own links to Iran and the Indian Ocean. Treating the peninsula as uniformly “tribal” or “Bedouin” collapses all of this variety, and the remaining sections therefore proceed region by region as well as theme by theme.

Section 35.3

Pastoralism, Herding, and Seasonal Mobility

Pastoralism in Arabia took several forms. Camel herders could range widely across arid zones, relying on camels for milk, transport, and meat, and on specialized knowledge of watering points. Sheep-and-goat pastoralists moved over shorter distances and stayed closer to wells and settled areas. Many groups practiced a mixed economy, keeping herds while also cultivating small plots, trading animal products, or working as guides and guards. As Chapter 2 noted, camel-based transport expanded the range of mobility in the later second and first millennia BCE, and pastoral groups were integral to the caravan routes described in Section 35.10.

Mobility was seasonal rather than random. Herds followed rainfall and grazing, moving toward desert pastures after winter rains and returning in summer to wells and oases. Groups also had customary rights to particular pastures and wells, and disputes over these resources were a frequent source of conflict, as Section 35.9 describes. Specialized knowledge of the landscape, of rainfall patterns, of poisonous plants, and of how long a given well would last, was a crucial resource. The Safaitic inscriptions repeatedly mention seasons spent in particular places and waiting for the rains, which shows how central this rhythm was to the lives of their authors.

It is important not to treat “Bedouin” (from Arabic badw, desert-dwellers) as a timeless type, or as a synonym for “Arab.” Pastoralists were one segment of a society that also included farmers, townspeople, and craftsmen, and kin ties often crossed these lines. Many Arabic speakers lived in settlements, and many mobile groups had permanent connections to oases. Descriptions of nomadic life written in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries by travelers and ethnographers are useful comparisons, but they describe different periods, with different technologies and political contexts, and must be used with caution when applied to the sixth century.

Pastoralists were not isolated from imperial politics. Byzantine and Sasanian authorities recruited mobile groups as scouts, auxiliaries, and border guards, paid subsidies to chiefs, and relied on them to control movement along desert frontiers. Inscriptions and Greek sources attest to such relationships. Pastoral groups were thus neither “outside” history nor merely its victims. They were participants in regional economies and polities, and sometimes decisive ones.

Section 35.4

Oases, Agriculture, and Settled Communities

Oases were the agricultural heart of much of Arabia. Fed by springs, wells, wadi floods, and groundwater, they supported date palms, grain, vegetables, fodder crops, and, in cooler highlands, fruit and vines. The date palm was especially important: it tolerates salty soil, produces a storable and nutritious food, and provides fibers, wood, and leaves. Oasis farming required labor-intensive work such as digging and maintaining wells and channels, pollinating palms, guarding crops, and organizing the distribution of water. These tasks were organized by households, clans, and sometimes larger bodies, and they generated property claims, inheritances, and disputes.

Oasis societies were also socially diverse. Alongside landholding families there were tenants, laborers, enslaved persons, craftspeople, and traders. Fortified residences and small towers, which later sources call ʾuṭum or āṭām, are reported for settlements in the Hijaz, indicating that security was a concern and that wealthier families invested in defensible houses. Relations between oasis dwellers and nearby pastoral groups were complex. Herders provided animals, wool, and transport, and received dates, grain, and goods in return, but they could also pose a threat, and oasis communities often paid for protection or made alliances.

Oases of northern and western Arabia illustrate the variety. Tayma, in the northwest, had been important since at least the sixth century BCE, when, as later Mesopotamian sources record, the Babylonian king Nabonidus resided there for several years. Khaybar and Wādī al-Qurā were agricultural centers with substantial Jewish populations according to the tradition. Dūmat al-Jandal controlled routes between Syria, Iraq, and Arabia. Yathrib, discussed in Section 35.13, was among the largest. Each shows that settled agriculture was an established, not marginal, element of Arabian life. The assumption that Arabia belonged to nomads and that settled life was exceptional arises from a focus on the desert at the expense of the oases.

Section 35.5

Towns, Crafts, Markets, and Local Exchange

Settlement in Arabia did not take a single “urban” form. Some towns, such as the cities of South Arabia, had temples, walls, and monumental buildings and were centers of royal administration. Others, such as the oases of the Hijaz, were clusters of fortified farming quarters, each belonging to a clan, rather than centrally planned cities. Mecca was a sanctuary settlement in a dry valley; Ṭāʾif, by contrast, was a highland town with orchards and vineyards, associated with the Thaqīf. Distinctions of this kind matter. An undifferentiated word like “town” conceals the fact that these were different kinds of communities with different economic bases.

Craft production and local exchange were widespread. Leather working, weaving, metalwork, pottery, and food processing supplied local markets. Later sources associate particular crafts with particular communities, such as goldsmiths with one of the Jewish clans of Yathrib, and Yemeni cloth and leather were widely valued. Although such attributions come from later tradition, they plausibly reflect specializations in settled communities. Markets, both permanent and seasonal, brought together farmers, herders, artisans, and merchants. Pastoralists exchanged animals, hides, wool, and dairy products for grain, dates, cloth, weapons, and tools, which made the economic relationship between town and desert one of interdependence.

Enslaved persons formed part of this economy. They worked in households and fields, in crafts, and in herding, and some were brought from East Africa, the Mediterranean world, or other regions. Later sources refer to Ethiopian and other enslaved or dependent persons in Mecca and other towns. The surviving evidence rarely allows us to see their experience directly, and their presence is a reminder that the social order contained deep inequalities of status, not only differences of kinship and occupation. Women also participated in economic life: tradition reports female property owners and traders, although the evidence is heavily filtered through later sources, and it is difficult to generalize across regions and periods.

Section 35.6

Kinship, Lineage, Tribe, Clan, and Clientage

Kinship was the fundamental language of social obligation in Arabia. Individuals identified themselves through descent (nasab), usually through the male line, and through membership in a nested series of groups: household, extended family, lineage, clan, and larger tribal unit. These groups provided protection, shared liability for injuries and debts, and claims to grazing, water, and land. The Safaitic inscriptions, which regularly give the writer’s name followed by a string of ancestors’ names, show how deeply genealogical identity was embedded in everyday life, even among mobile herders writing for themselves.

Yet “tribe” is a slippery category. The groups named in the sources, such as Bakr, Taghlib, Tamīm, Tayyiʾ, Kinda, Quraysh, and Aws, varied greatly in size, cohesion, and degree of centralization. Genealogies were also flexible. Groups could be absorbed into larger ones, split into new units, adopt ancestors, or change alliances, and genealogies were adjusted to reflect these changes. The elaborate genealogical schemes preserved in later Arabic works, such as the division of Arabs into northern (ʿAdnān) and southern (Qaḥṭān) lines, were systematized under the Umayyads and Abbasids and reflect later political concerns. They are not neutral records of descent from the sixth century. Treating tribes as fixed biological units would misunderstand both the evidence and the nature of kinship in this society.

Not everyone belonged to a group through descent. Individuals and small groups entered relationships of clientage and protection. A jār (protected neighbor) enjoyed the protection of a patron’s group; a mawlā (client, freed person, or ally) was attached to a patron; and sworn alliances (ḥilf) allowed groups to combine without a claim of shared ancestry. These mechanisms were central to Arabian society. They allowed foreigners, refugees, freed persons, and weaker groups to find a place, and they meant that political life was not simply a matter of blood ties. Kinship, in other words, was both a fact and a framework that could be extended by agreement.

Historical Vocabulary
Qabīla, Nasab, and the Problem of “Tribe”

The Arabic word qabīla is usually translated “tribe,” and nasab means genealogy or descent. English “tribe,” however, carries heavy baggage from colonial and evolutionary theories that imagined tribes as primitive, bounded, and biologically defined societies. The Arabian evidence suggests something more fluid: groups of varying scale, bound by claimed descent, shared interests, and alliances, which could expand, fragment, or merge. Historians of the region increasingly emphasize that genealogy was a flexible idiom for expressing political and social relationships rather than a straightforward record of biology.

This textbook therefore uses “tribe” and “clan” as convenient labels, and tries to specify, in each case, what kind of group the sources actually describe, whether a lineage sharing grazing land, a confederation formed by alliance, or a settled clan within an oasis.

Section 35.7

Chiefs, Kings, Confederations, and the Politics of Alliance

Political authority in most of Arabia was decentralized and negotiated. Groups were led by figures often called sayyid or shaykh, whose authority rested on reputation, generosity, wisdom, wealth, and the ability to mediate disputes and protect dependants. Such leaders did not command by right. They consulted other senior men, depended on consent, and could be abandoned if they failed. Assemblies, in which prominent men debated decisions, are reported in later sources, including the institution of an assembly house in Mecca. Arbitrators (ḥakam, pl. ḥukkām), who might be respected elders, or men credited with insight, such as the diviners called kuhhān, settled disputes between groups on the basis of custom.

Larger coalitions formed through alliance, common enemies, or the patronage of external powers. Confederations might combine several tribes for a campaign or a season and then dissolve. Their leaders sometimes received subsidies from Byzantine or Sasanian authorities, which increased their wealth and prestige but also made them dependent on imperial favor. The difference between a tribal chief and a “king” (malik) is therefore one of degree. Some rulers, such as the Lakhmid kings of al-Ḥīra, held a stable court, tax resources, and a military, but they governed with imperial backing. Others, such as the leaders of Kinda, tried to build kingdoms among tribes without the apparatus of a state.

The upshot is that most of Arabia lacked a state in the Roman or Persian sense: no permanent bureaucracy, no standing army, no regular taxation, and no monopoly of legitimate force. This did not mean chaos. Customary law, institutions of arbitration, sanctuary protections, and dense networks of alliance and obligation provided order, and many communities lived in long-established patterns of cooperation. But it did mean that political action was mainly a matter of building and maintaining coalitions, a feature that would remain important in the seventh century and later.

Section 35.8

Kinda and Experiments in Central Arabian Kingship

The history of Kinda offers the clearest case of an attempt to create a kingdom in central Arabia. Kinda was a confederation with roots in southern Arabia and strong links to Ḥimyar. According to later tradition and some external evidence, Kindite leaders in the fifth and early sixth centuries acted as representatives of Ḥimyarite power over tribal groups in central Arabia, collecting tribute and organizing military support. The traditional account of a ruler named al-Ḥārith ibn ʿAmr (sometimes with the epithet “the Cutter of Wings”) and his relatives, including Ḥujr Ākil al-Murār, portrays a dynasty that extended authority across central Arabian groups. The details, including names, genealogies, and dates, are uncertain, since the sources are late, partisan, and sometimes legendary.

Even so, the broad picture is credible and informative. It suggests that central Arabian leaders could exercise authority across kin groups, as delegates of a stronger southern power, and that such arrangements depended on external backing and on continuous negotiation with local chiefs. As Ḥimyarite power weakened and Kindite rulers came into conflict with rival Arabian and Lakhmid powers in the early sixth century, the central Arabian arrangement fragmented. Later Aksumite and Sasanian interventions in Yemen further transformed the southern political world from which Kinda had drawn support. Branches of Kinda dispersed to other regions, including Ḥaḍramawt, and the story of competing princes, often told through poetry attributed to the Kindite prince Imruʾ al-Qays, became a rich subject of later memory.

Kinda’s significance lies in what it reveals about political possibilities. It shows that supra-tribal authority was imaginable and attempted in central Arabia well before Islam, and that it relied on three ingredients: external patronage, tribute and military support, and prestige. It also shows why such experiments were fragile: without institutions that could outlast a ruler or a patron’s withdrawal, they dissolved. Historians should avoid reading Kinda as a failed proto-Islamic state or a mere precursor. It is better seen as one of several regional experiments, alongside the Ghassanids, the Lakhmids, and Ḥimyar, in organizing authority beyond the tribe.

Section 35.9

Raiding, Feud, Protection, Arbitration, and Sanctuary

Later Arabic tradition, and the poetry it preserves, pays great attention to raiding (ghazw) and to the “days of the Arabs” (ayyām al-ʿArab), prose narratives of battles and feuds between groups. These accounts are vivid but must be handled with care. Raiding was real: animals, the most mobile form of wealth, could be taken, and groups gained status from successful defense and retaliation. But raiding was usually conducted under customary limits, was often directed at specific rivals, and was balanced by norms of restraint, compensation, and hospitality. Presenting Arabian society as a permanent war of all against all gives the poetry’s heroic idiom too much weight as a description of daily life.

Conflict was managed through several institutions. A killing created an obligation of vengeance (thaʾr) for the victim’s kin, but it could be settled by payment of blood money (diya), traditionally reckoned in camels, and by negotiation, with the help of mediators. Individuals and groups could obtain protection through the institution of jiwār: a protected person, or jār, was under the guarantee of a patron, whose honor was at stake if the protected person was harmed. Oaths, alliances, and hospitality created further obligations. Disputes might be brought to an arbitrator known for impartiality and knowledge of custom, and in some cases to the guardians of a sanctuary.

Sanctuaries added another layer. A ḥaram or ḥawṭ was a protected space, often around a shrine, within which violence was prohibited and refugees could find safety. Some sanctuaries were associated with sacred months, in which fighting was suspended so that pilgrims and traders could travel and markets could operate. Such arrangements gave sanctuary towns an important function in regional politics as places of mediation and neutral exchange. They did not always hold. Tradition records conflicts, such as the “sacrilegious war” (ḥarb al-fijār) involving Meccan groups and others in the later sixth century, which was so named because fighting violated the sacred months. The very name shows that the norm existed and that its violation was notable.

Taken together, raiding, vengeance, compensation, protection, arbitration, and sanctuary were complementary parts of one political order. Conflict and cooperation were not opposites. A society without a state relied on groups’ ability to threaten retaliation, on shared norms about limiting it, and on institutions that could convert disputes into settlements. This also helps explain why strong leaders, when they emerged, were valued less for command than for the ability to hold coalitions together and to broker peace.

Historical Vocabulary
Ḥaram, Jiwār, and Protected Space

The Arabic root ḥ-r-m expresses the idea of the inviolable or forbidden. A ḥaram was a protected area, usually around a shrine or sacred site, where violence, the cutting of certain trees, and the hunting of certain animals were forbidden. A related institution, jiwār, was the protection extended by a patron to a client or guest. Both gave legal and moral force to the notion that some people and places were under protection, and both interacted with the social order of kin groups.

These categories matter because they reveal how Arabian society produced order without a state. They also show that sacred space was socially useful: it made markets safer and disputes easier to resolve. Islamic law later reorganized and reinterpreted the idea of the ḥaram, but the vocabulary predates it.

Section 35.10

Trade Routes, Seasonal Markets, and Arabian Exchange

Chapter 34 described Arabia’s external trade networks, from the incense routes to the maritime links of the Red Sea and the Gulf. This section views commerce from inside the peninsula. Goods moved at several scales. At the local scale, herders and farmers exchanged animals, dates, grain, and textiles. At the regional scale, caravans carried goods between Yemen, the Hijaz, Syria, Iraq, and eastern Arabia. At the long-distance scale, incense and aromatics from southern Arabia and the Horn of Africa, textiles, leather, silver, and spices traveled toward the Mediterranean, Mesopotamia, and the Indian Ocean. Arab merchants, guides, and guardians participated in all of these networks, and the caravan oases of the Hijaz were nodes in them.

Seasonal markets and fairs formed another institution. Later sources list gatherings at places such as ʿUkāẓ, near Ṭāʾif, Majanna, and Dhū al-Majāz, which, according to tradition, were held in the months when fighting was prohibited. They brought together traders, poets, and tribal delegations, and combined commerce, social meeting, and cultural performance. Other fairs are reported for eastern Arabia and Oman. The details come from later authors, who may idealize, but the combination of sacred months, protected space, and commerce is plausible and fits the sanctuary institutions described above.

Trade was important, but it should not be treated as a single explanation. Incense trade, once the backbone of South Arabian wealth, appears to have changed in scale and organization by late antiquity, in ways that scholars still debate, and other commodities, including textiles, leather, silver, foodstuffs, and enslaved persons, mattered. Trade also did not dissolve kinship or religious identities; it operated through them, with merchants relying on alliances, protection arrangements, and shared sanctuaries. Above all, caravan commerce alone cannot explain the importance of any single place, and Section 35.11 shows why this matters for Mecca. The wider commercial history of the region, including the Red Sea, the Gulf, and the Indian Ocean, is taken up in Chapter 51.

Section 35.11

Mecca, Quraysh, and the Problem of the Evidence

Mecca is the most important Arabian town in later history, and for that reason the pre-Islamic evidence about it is the most difficult to evaluate. The town lay in a narrow valley between rocky hills, in a location with little agricultural land, a limited water supply (including the well of Zamzam, according to tradition), and a sanctuary centered on the Kaʿba. Its inhabitants belonged to Quraysh, which later tradition divides into numerous clans, including Hāshim, ʿAbd Shams, Makhzūm, Zuhra, Taym, ʿAdī, Jumaḥ, Sahm, and ʿAbd al-Dār. Later accounts attribute to the ancestor Quṣayy ibn Kilāb the consolidation of Quraysh in Mecca and the creation of offices, such as the custody of the Kaʿba keys, provision of water and food to pilgrims, and an assembly house, though the historicity of this account is uncertain.

The evidence has serious limits. External references to Mecca before Islam are scarce and disputed; the identification of a place called “Macoraba” in the Greek geographer Ptolemy with Mecca, for example, is uncertain. No major pre-Islamic Meccan inscriptions or archaeological assemblages are available, since excavation in the sacred precincts is not possible. Most of what we know comes from Islamic-period sources, written from a perspective in which Mecca’s importance is already assumed. They describe a Meccan elite that managed the sanctuary, hosted pilgrims, and organized caravans to Syria and Yemen, a picture that has become standard in many textbooks.

Careful historians distinguish layers. What is plausible, because it fits the structural context, is that Mecca was a sanctuary settlement with a recognized leadership, connected to the wider network of caravan routes and markets and to Ṭāʾif and the surrounding tribes. What is traditional, but difficult to verify, includes the details of clan competition, offices, alliances (such as the “pact of the virtuous,” ḥilf al-fuḍūl), and the scale of Meccan commerce. And what is clearly later is the full narrative of Quraysh’s rise as organized by authors interested in the background of Muhammad’s tribe. The Qurʾan, Chapter 36 will show, is itself an important but allusive source, and its references need interpretation. For now the essential point is methodological: statements about Mecca’s pre-Islamic wealth, size, and role should be read as claims requiring support, not as settled facts. The Historical Debate feature later in this chapter returns to this problem.

What can be said with reasonable confidence is that Mecca’s society was socially differentiated, with leading clans and families, dependent groups, allies, and enslaved persons; that kinship and alliance structured politics, as elsewhere; and that its inhabitants were engaged in a mixed economy of trade, services to pilgrims, herding, and exchange with Ṭāʾif and surrounding tribes. Beyond this, the reader should treat the details with caution and recognize how much of Mecca’s pre-Islamic image was shaped by what it later became.

Section 35.12

The Kaʿba, Sanctuary, Pilgrimage, and Sacred Space

The Kaʿba, a cube-shaped structure, was the focus of the Meccan sanctuary. Later sources describe it as a shrine containing sacred objects, with a black stone set in a corner, surrounded by an open area where rituals of circumambulation (ṭawāf) took place. They also report that images of various deities were present, and associate the deity Hubal with it. The reliability of these descriptions is uncertain; our principal source for pre-Islamic idols, Ibn al-Kalbī’s Book of Idols, was composed in the Islamic period and presents a coherent system that may be more tidy than the reality. Archaeology cannot yet test most of this. Still, the broad claim that Mecca possessed a sanctuary of regional significance before Islam is credible, and Islamic tradition itself is unanimous that it did.

Pilgrimage practices extended beyond the town. Tradition describes rituals at sites in the vicinity, including ʿArafa, Muzdalifa, and Minā, performed at set times of the year and associated with sacred months. These rituals involved gatherings of tribal groups from various regions, who came for religious purposes and also for trade and social exchange. Some groups were reported to have special roles or restrictions in these rites, and tradition speaks of a group called the ḥums, associated with Quraysh, who observed distinctive practices. Other shrines in Arabia had their own pilgrimages and sacred days, so Mecca was one among several sanctuaries, though evidently an important one.

The sanctuary had social and economic consequences. It attracted pilgrims who needed food, water, lodging, and animals for sacrifice. It provided a neutral space for meetings and arbitration among groups. It gave the families that managed it status, resources, and connections with tribes across the region, and it contributed to the safety of travel during the sacred months. This explains much of Mecca’s importance without needing to assume that it was primarily a long-distance trading emporium. It also demonstrates the intertwining of religion, economy, and politics in a society that did not separate these domains. The chapter treats this as pre-Islamic institutional history. Muhammad’s relation to these institutions, and the confrontation with Meccan religion, belong to Chapter 36.

Section 35.13

Yathrib: Oasis Society before the Hijra

Yathrib, the oasis that would later be called Medina, was a large agricultural settlement about 400 kilometers north of Mecca, within a volcanic landscape of lava fields. Its name is attested before Islam. It was not a single compact town but a collection of fortified settlements, orchards, and date groves, separated by cultivated land and linked by routes and local alliances. Its inhabitants included Arab clans, principally the Aws and Khazraj, and Jewish clans, including the Banū Qurayẓa, Banū al-Naḍīr, and Banū Qaynuqāʿ, according to later tradition. Tradition traces the Aws and Khazraj to southern Arabian origins, and a story of migration from Yemen is attached to many northern groups. This story reflects genealogical schemes of a later age as much as actual history, and should not be accepted uncritically.

The economy was based on date palms, grain, and gardens, with crafts and local markets. Landholding and water rights were contested, and clans guarded their properties from fortified houses. According to Islamic-period sources, Yathrib’s social order was fractured by long-standing feuds between the Aws and Khazraj, with their allied clans, culminating in a major battle at Buʿāth, traditionally placed a few years before 622 CE. Jewish clans are reported to have been associated by alliance with one or the other Arab group, and to have been drawn into the conflict. The dating of Buʿāth and the exact alignment of alliances are uncertain, but the general picture of a divided oasis with no overarching authority is widely accepted.

This picture matters, but it should not be exaggerated into a portrait of constant civil war. Many residents continued to farm, trade, and intermarry across clan lines. What the tradition emphasizes is the absence of an accepted mechanism for settling disputes among leading clans, a problem that sanctuary and arbitration institutions elsewhere helped address. This is how Yathrib appears on the threshold of the period examined in Chapter 37, which treats the Hijra, the political organization of the oasis, and relations among communities after 622. Chapter 35 stops here, and it deliberately does not present Yathrib’s divisions as a preparation for what came next.

Section 35.14

South Arabia and the Political Legacy of Yemen

South Arabia had the oldest and most elaborate state tradition in the peninsula. From the first millennium BCE, kingdoms such as Saba (centered on Maʾrib, with its great dam and irrigation works), Qatabān, Maʿīn, and Ḥaḍramawt controlled terraced agriculture, incense production, and trade. They wrote in the Musnad script on stone and in a cursive script on wooden sticks, the latter attesting to everyday literacy. Their inscriptions record laws, treaties, and dedications. By about 300 CE the kingdom of Ḥimyar had absorbed most of the others, and in the fifth and sixth centuries its rulers dominated much of the peninsula’s south and sometimes claimed influence over central Arabia, as Section 35.8 showed. Chapter 34 narrated Ḥimyar’s religious turn, the Najrān persecution, Aksumite intervention, and the Sasanian takeover, and these events are not repeated here.

What matters for this chapter is the legacy. South Arabia contributed to the rest of the peninsula in several ways: as a source of incense, textiles, and agricultural products; as a model of kingship and literate administration; and as an origin of many tribal genealogies, whether historically accurate or not. Traditions of migration northward after the breach of the Maʾrib dam, recorded in later sources and associated with the Qurʾanic and Arabic memory of the “flood of the dam” (sayl al-ʿarim), reflect how southern origins were remembered. Archaeology confirms that the dam was damaged and repaired several times, including in the sixth century, as a royal inscription records, but the connection between dam failure and migrations is a later story, not demonstrated history.

It is also vital not to treat South Arabia as the exception from which the rest of Arabia was missing something. Yemen’s literate monarchy coexisted with, and influenced, the tribal and sanctuary societies of the north, and the contrast between them is part of what made Arabia’s political landscape plural. Late in the sixth century, however, South Arabia was under Sasanian control and its old state structures had collapsed, so that, by c. 600, its political weight in the peninsula had diminished sharply.

Section 35.15

Eastern Arabia, Oman, and the Persian Gulf

A textbook that treats the Hijaz as the whole of Arabia misses the eastern side of the peninsula, where societies were oriented toward Iran and the sea. Eastern Arabia, then often called al-Baḥrayn, included oasis agriculture at al-Aḥsāʾ and Qaṭīf, coastal and island settlements, and routes to the interior. In antiquity the region had been linked to Mesopotamia and the Indian Ocean, as Chapter 2 noted in connection with Dilmun and the Gulf. By late antiquity it was under Sasanian influence: Sasanian garrisons and officials are attested, and local rulers and tribes operated within a Persian imperial framework. Oman likewise had Sasanian connections, especially along the coast, and was part of Indian Ocean trade networks that Chapter 51 develops.

The region’s economy combined date cultivation, fishing, and, where evidence supports it, pearl diving, with maritime trade in goods such as dates, textiles, and pearls. The evidence for the scale of these activities in the sixth century is limited, and later practices should not be assumed. Tribal groups such as ʿAbd al-Qays, and the powerful groups of the Yamāma, including Ḥanīfa, played significant roles in regional politics. Christianity also had a strong presence: the Church of the East organized bishoprics in the region that the Syriac sources call Beth Qaṭraye, and archaeological remains of churches and monasteries on the Gulf coast and islands confirm this, as Section 35.19 explains.

Including eastern Arabia and Oman changes the picture. It shows that Arabia had a maritime face; that Sasanian imperial influence reached deeply into the peninsula; and that Christian and Persian elements coexisted with local Arab traditions. It also helps explain why, in the seventh-century conflicts and conquests treated in Chapters 38 and 39, eastern Arabia and Oman figure as distinct arenas rather than as extensions of the Hijaz. For students, the lesson is that Mecca and Yathrib are best understood as two points on a much larger and more varied map.

Section 35.16

Northern Arabia after the Jafnids and Lakhmids

Chapter 34 described the Ghassanid (Jafnid) and Lakhmid client kingdoms as instruments of Byzantine and Sasanian frontier policy. This section looks at what their weakening meant on the ground in northern Arabia and the Syrian and Mesopotamian steppe. The Ghassanids, Miaphysite Christians, were recognized by Byzantium under al-Ḥārith ibn Jabala from 529, and served as military allies and as patrons of churches and monasteries. After a rift with the Byzantine government in the early 580s, their leadership was weakened and divided. The Lakhmids, centered at al-Ḥīra and associated with the Church of the East, served the Sasanians until, around 602, the Sasanian king ended the dynasty. By c. 600, in other words, the two main systems for managing the desert frontier were either dissolved or damaged.

The consequences are less well documented than the causes. Arab groups that had served in the armies, received subsidies, or been protected by these rulers had to find new arrangements. Tradition preserves the memory of tensions, including the Battle of Dhū Qār, in which the tribal group Bakr ibn Wāʾil is said to have defeated a Sasanian-backed force. The date and scale are disputed, but the story shows how a victory against an imperial client could become a cherished memory. The broader result was a looser, more fragmented frontier, with groups such as Tayyiʾ, Kalb, Judhām, Lakhm, Taghlib, and Bakr operating in a space where imperial authority was less direct.

These changes unfolded as the Byzantine-Sasanian wars of the early seventh century, treated in Chapter 34, devastated provinces and imposed severe military and fiscal strain on both empires, while producing especially acute political instability in Sasanian Iran. Arab groups, Christian and non-Christian, were involved in these wars as soldiers, guides, and raiders. For the purposes of this chapter, the point is that the northern frontier by c. 600 was neither a stable buffer nor an empty zone. It was a changing environment of Christian networks, tribal groupings, and weakened imperial arrangements, which would matter greatly in the 630s, as Chapters 38 and 39 will show.

Section 35.17

Gods, Shrines, Rituals, and Arabian Polytheisms

Religion in pre-Islamic Arabia was plural, local, and changing. Inscriptions from across the peninsula name numerous deities: in the north, Allāt, Dushara (Dhū al-Sharā), Baʿalshamin, and others; in the south, gods such as ʿAthtar, Almaqah, and Wadd; and in the Hijaz, deities such as Manāt and al-ʿUzzā according to later tradition. Different regions, towns, and tribes venerated different gods or gave them different roles, and the same deity might be worshiped in different forms in different places. The phrase “Arabian polytheisms” (in the plural) is therefore more accurate than “the Arab pagan religion.” Ibn al-Kalbī’s Book of Idols, which lists many deities and their sanctuaries, offers a systematic picture, but it was written centuries later and may impose order on a more varied reality.

Sacred places included stone blocks (anṣāb), springs, trees, caves, built shrines, and sanctuaries with caretakers. Worship involved offerings of food and animals, libations, vows, processions, and, in some cases, pilgrimage. Divination was widespread: diviners (kuhhān) gave oracles, and techniques such as drawing arrows (azlām) were used to seek guidance. Votive inscriptions from the north show individuals asking deities for health, safety, or good fortune. Ritual specialists, shrine guardians, and sometimes dynastic families held hereditary roles, which connected religion to political and economic power.

The relationship of these cults to a high god has been much discussed. The word Allāh, “the god,” is attested in various pre-Islamic contexts, including Christian Arabic inscriptions, and the Qurʾan itself suggests that the idea of a supreme God was familiar to its audience (a matter for Chapter 36). How the term functioned in specifically polytheistic contexts is debated, and generalization is risky. What the evidence supports is that Arabians drew on a range of religious ideas, that local shrines and deities coexisted with ideas of a higher god, and that Jewish, Christian, and other monotheistic ideas circulated alongside. “Idol worship” as a description captures at most one visible element, and presenting pre-Islamic religion as simple or primitive reflects later polemic more than evidence.

Section 35.18

Jewish Communities in Arabia

Jewish communities were present in Arabia long before the seventh century. In Yemen, as Chapter 34 described, the Ḥimyarite elite adopted Judaism or a closely related monotheism, and Jewish presence there was substantial. In the Hijaz and northern Arabia, the evidence is different. Tayma, as noted, had a link to the Babylonian king Nabonidus in the sixth century BCE, and Islamic-period sources report significant Jewish populations at Khaybar, Fadak, Wādī al-Qurā, Tayma, and Yathrib. These communities were involved in agriculture, crafts, trade, and local politics, and some were reputed to hold substantial property.

Their origins are debated. Later tradition sometimes says they descended from refugees after the Roman destruction of Jerusalem, or from earlier migrations, and sometimes that they were Arabs who had converted. The evidence does not permit a definite answer, and both processes, migration and conversion, are plausible and may have operated together. What is clear is that these communities were integrated into Arabian society: they often had Arabic names, spoke Arabic, belonged to alliances with Arab clans, and participated in local conflicts, while keeping religious practices and knowledge connected to wider Jewish worlds. Later sources suggest contacts with Jewish centers in Mesopotamia and Palestine, a subject on which the evidence is limited. Chapter 31 described the rabbinic transformation of Judaism after the Roman wars, and Arabian Jewish communities existed in the same broad world, though their specific religious life is poorly documented.

A caution is needed. Because our sources were produced largely by later Muslim authors, they frame Jewish communities in terms of the relationship with Muhammad, a relationship examined in Chapter 37. Reading that framework backward into the pre-Islamic period risks anachronism: the later boundaries between “Jews,” “Arabs,” and “Muslims” did not apply in the same way earlier. Jewish Arabians were part of the society, not an external presence, and this chapter treats them accordingly.

Section 35.19

Christianity across the Peninsula

Christianity in Arabia was not a single church. Najrān, in the south, was a major Christian center whose community suffered persecution under the Ḥimyarite ruler, an event preserved in Syriac and Greek sources and treated in Chapter 34. In the north, the Ghassanids were Miaphysite Christians patronizing monasteries and churches, while the Lakhmids at al-Ḥīra were connected to the Church of the East, often called “Nestorian” in older literature, a label this textbook uses cautiously. These traditions disagreed about the nature of Christ, and Arab Christians were thus found in different, sometimes rival, ecclesiastical networks. Some tribal groups, including parts of Taghlib and Tayyiʾ, were reported to be Christian.

In eastern Arabia and the Gulf, bishoprics of the Church of the East are attested in Syriac sources for the region known as Beth Qaṭraye, and archaeological remains of churches and monasteries on islands and coasts support this. Christian inscriptions at Zabad (512), which combines Greek, Syriac, and Arabic, and at Ḥarrān (568), which is Greek and Arabic, show Christians using Arabic in religious and commemorative contexts alongside Greek and, at Zabad, Syriac. Monasteries in the desert margins served as places of hospitality, learning, and contact between settled and mobile communities. Later Arabic tradition remembers Christian monks and preachers (the figure of Quss ibn Sāʿida is a famous example, though his historical details are uncertain), and Christian vocabulary and stories entered Arabic poetry.

What did Christianity mean within Arabian society? It offered new rituals, texts, calendars, and institutions, and gave tribes and rulers connections with imperial churches and cities. It also became entangled with politics, since churches were tied to Byzantium or Iran. But it did not displace kinship and alliance, and Christian Arabs remained part of tribal and regional networks. It is also plausible that Christian ideas and practices reached the interior through traders, monks, and captives, although evidence for specifics in the Hijaz is limited and later tradition is the main source. As elsewhere, the lesson is that religious communities and Arabian social structures overlapped rather than excluded one another.

Section 35.20

Monotheistic Currents, “Ḥanīfs,” and the Limits of the Evidence

There is good evidence for monotheistic belief in pre-Islamic Arabia, but it is more diverse than later tradition suggests. In South Arabia, royal inscriptions of the fifth and sixth centuries refer to a single God, using terms such as Raḥmānān (“the Merciful”), sometimes in contexts that are Jewish, sometimes Christian, and sometimes seemingly independent of either. This is contemporary epigraphic evidence, and it demonstrates that monotheistic language was in active use in the peninsula. Jewish and Christian communities, as sections above show, were present across Arabia. Beyond these, the evidence for groups that might be called “Arabian monotheists” outside organized Judaism and Christianity is thinner.

The Arabic term ḥanīf appears in the Qurʾan, where it describes Abraham and is associated with a pure monotheism. Later biographical tradition identifies individuals in pre-Islamic Mecca and Yathrib as ḥanīfs, seekers who rejected idol worship and looked for the religion of Abraham. Several of these figures are linked in tradition to the biography of Muhammad, a matter Chapter 36 will consider. Scholars have asked whether the term derives from a Syriac word that meant “pagan” or “gentile” and was later reinterpreted positively, and whether the later category of ḥanīfs was a retrospective construction. The question is open.

The responsible position is cautious. We can say that monotheistic ideas were present and available, that Jewish and Christian communities existed in Arabia, and that Qurʾanic and later traditions refer to a category of Abrahamic seekers. We cannot reconstruct from this a neat evolutionary path by which Arabia gradually moved toward Islam. Doing so would make Islam seem predetermined, a teleology this textbook avoids. Monotheism in Arabia before Islam was a set of coexisting currents, and not a single stream leading to one outcome.

Section 35.21

Poetry, Oral Culture, Language, Memory, and Social Values

Pre-Islamic Arabic poetry is among the most celebrated bodies of literature from the ancient Middle East. The classical form was the qaṣīda, a long ode with a conventional structure: often an opening evocation of a deserted campsite and a lost beloved, a journey on a camel, and a concluding section of praise, boast, or satire. Poets such as Imruʾ al-Qays, Ṭarafa, Zuhayr, Labīd, ʿAntara, and al-Nābigha al-Dhubyānī are credited with famous odes, and anthologies such as the Muʿallaqāt gathered celebrated examples. Women, notably al-Khansāʾ, composed laments. Poetry expressed values such as generosity, courage, loyalty, and honor, and served as public speech: it praised patrons, mocked rivals, recorded battles, and could shape reputations.

The difficulty is transmission. The poems were collected and written down in the eighth and ninth centuries by scholars such as Ḥammād al-Rāwiya, al-Mufaḍḍal al-Ḍabbī, and al-Aṣmaʿī, a century or more after the poets’ presumed lifetimes. In the twentieth century, scholars such as D. S. Margoliouth and Ṭāhā Ḥusayn argued that much of this poetry was forged in the Islamic period. Later research, including studies of oral composition, has generally rejected wholesale dismissal while accepting that attributions, texts, and details may have been altered. The sensible conclusion is that the corpus contains authentic older material, and also later additions and revisions, and that it must be used with caution as evidence for values, language, and social imagery rather than for specific events.

Language and writing add another dimension. Arabic existed in several dialects, and poetry used a shared literary register that scholars debate how to characterize. Arabic was written in a script derived from the Nabataean Aramaic script, as the Namāra inscription (328) shows. Arabic appears in the Christian monumental inscriptions at Zabad (512) and Ḥarrān (568), and in the Jabal Usays military graffito, conventionally dated 528/529 and associated with the Jafnid ruler al-Ḥārith. Writing was used for monuments, commerce, and religion, but there is no evidence of a large Arabic book culture before Islam. South Arabian scripts and Aramaic, Greek, and Syriac were used in various regions. Oral performance, genealogy, and memory thus coexisted with developing literacy, and both shaped how Arabians remembered the past. The way later Muslims remembered this period, as the jāhiliyya, was itself a product of that later memory: a term whose meaning, “ignorance” or perhaps “hot-headedness” in some interpretations, shaped a negative image that the actual evidence of poetry, trade, craft, and religion does not support.

Section 35.22

Arabia around 600: A Diverse Society before Muhammad

By about 600 CE, Arabia can be described as a set of connected but distinct societies. Pastoral groups moved across steppe and desert, maintaining ties with oases and towns. Oases such as Yathrib and Khaybar supported date farming, crafts, and fortified households. Sanctuary towns such as Mecca combined ritual, mediation, and commerce, and highland towns such as Ṭāʾif had their own agricultural base. South Arabia retained the memory and some institutions of literate kingdoms but was under external control. Eastern Arabia and Oman looked to Iran and the sea. The northern frontier was in flux after the weakening of the Ghassanid and Lakhmid arrangements, and imperial rivalry affected everyone.

Social order rested on kinship, alliance, clientage, and custom, rather than on state institutions, with arbitration and sanctuary providing means of settling disputes. Religion was plural: local cults, Jewish and Christian communities, and monotheistic currents of several kinds coexisted. Culture included sophisticated poetry, emerging Arabic writing, and rich oral memory. There were deep inequalities of status and wealth, including slavery, and varying roles for women that the sources only partly reveal. Few, if any, of these features were unique to Arabia: they resemble patterns in other frontier and stateless societies of late antiquity.

The central lesson of the chapter is contingency. Nothing in this world made a prophet’s preaching, a new community, or a vast empire inevitable. Historians who read the earlier society as a preparation for Islam produce a smooth but misleading narrative. A better approach is to treat Arabia on its own terms, as a society with its own dynamics, and then ask how the events of the early seventh century unfolded within and against it. Chapter 36 takes up that question, beginning with Muhammad’s early life, the first revelations, and the opposition they encountered in Mecca.

Primary Source Window

OCIANA JaS 156: A Safaitic Inscription from Below

Creator: A writer who names himself as Ḥgy son of S²mg · Date: not independently dated; the Safaitic corpus as a whole is generally placed between roughly the first century BCE and the fourth century CE · Type: Short personal inscription in the Safaitic script · Siglum and location: JaS 156; found in Wādī al-Ġurābah (“Muṭabb ʿAsākir”), about 43 km from ʿArʿar, Saudi Arabia; present location of the stone unknown · Corpus: first published by A. Jamme (1971), now edited in OCIANA by A. Al-Jallad and M. C. A. Macdonald

Context: Tens of thousands of Safaitic inscriptions are known. Chapter 34 used a royal monumental inscription, which speaks with the voice of a ruler. This text is of a different kind: a brief personal record carved by an ordinary individual in the basalt desert, and catalogued in OCIANA under the subject “genealogy.”

Paraphrase: In OCIANA JaS 156, the writer identifies himself by name and father’s name, records that he grieved for five named individuals (one of whom is identified by a father’s name), and notes that he found a place with abundant pasture.

Analysis: In a few words the text shows how identity was expressed through descent, how grief for kin and companions was recorded, and how the search for pasture structured a herder’s year, all of which fit the picture of pastoral life and kinship in Sections 35.3 and 35.6. It also shows why such texts are valuable: no royal inscription or later narrative history would record this man’s name, his losses, or his good fortune with the herds.

Limitations: The inscription is undated and the stone’s current location is unknown, so it cannot be tied to a particular decade. The corpus as a whole predates c. 600 CE by centuries, so a text like this cannot be applied directly to Arabia at that time. One of the names is partly damaged, and the editors note an alternative reading of its letters. The text is short and formulaic, comes from a particular region and kind of community, and tells us nothing about politics or large-scale events. The reading of the word for “abundant pasture” rests on comparison with Arabic.

Historical Debate

How Commercial Was Mecca?

The question: Was Mecca before Islam a major hub of long-distance trade between the Indian Ocean and the Mediterranean, or a more modest sanctuary town whose commercial role was largely regional? The answer affects how we explain the Quraysh’s wealth, the social tensions in Mecca, and the background of Islam’s emergence.

Interpretation A — Mecca as a significant commercial center: Some scholars, building on the Islamic tradition and on the Qurʾanic reference to Quraysh’s winter and summer journeys, have argued that Mecca grew wealthy from caravan trade between Yemen and Syria and that its elite were merchants who also managed a sanctuary. W. Montgomery Watt’s mid-twentieth-century work is a classic statement of this approach, emphasizing the social and economic changes that commerce brought.
Interpretation B — Mecca as a regional sanctuary and market: Patricia Crone’s 1987 study argued that the evidence for Meccan involvement in the long-distance spice trade is weak, that foreign writers do not mention Mecca as a trading center, and that the geography of the place does not favor it as a transit hub. On this view Mecca’s trade was mostly local or regional, and the later image of a great emporium reflects retrospective magnification. Crone later explored whether Meccan leather and pastoral products might have supplied Roman military demand, while acknowledging that the hypothesis cannot presently be proven.
Interpretation C — Critics of the skeptical reconstruction: R. B. Serjeant, in a 1990 reply, defended the importance of Meccan trade and of the sanctuary (ḥaram) system that protected markets and exchange, and argued that Crone’s reconstruction had misread the sources. Other critics replied that regional trade of the kind Crone accepts could still have been substantial, and that the absence of external mentions is not decisive.

Evidence: All sides use the same limited evidence: the Qurʾanic reference, the later tradition, scattered external sources, and the geography. The dispute is therefore better seen as a question of scale and commodity structure (what was traded, over what distances, and with what profits) than as a contest between “trade” and “no trade.”

Why it matters: The answer shapes how historians explain Meccan society, and it also illustrates a general lesson: when sources are late and archaeological evidence is lacking, plausible narratives can harden into assumed facts.

Student task: Summarize each position, identify what evidence would be needed to decide between them, and explain what a moderate position might look like.

Multiple Perspectives

Five Arabian Vantage Points, c. 600 CE

A Qurashī notable in Mecca: Standing depends on kin, alliances, and the management of the sanctuary and its pilgrims. Mediation of disputes and hospitality bring prestige, and relations with Ṭāʾif and neighboring tribes matter as much as any distant trade.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Arabian Society from the First Centuries CE to c. 600

What Changed

  • South Arabian kingdoms consolidated under Ḥimyar and then fell to outside powers
  • Monotheism spread through Judaism, Christianity, and independent currents
  • Arabic began to be written, and Arab client kingdoms rose and weakened on imperial frontiers
  • Imperial rivalry intensified, drawing Arabian groups into larger wars

What Stayed the Same

  • Kinship, alliance, and clientage organized social obligations
  • Pastoralism, oasis farming, and caravan exchange remained economic foundations
  • Sanctuaries, arbitration, and custom provided order without a state in much of the peninsula
  • Wells, pastures, and oases continued to shape settlement and movement

Why the pattern matters: Seeing both change and continuity helps students avoid treating Arabia as either static or in rapid transformation toward Islam. Many structures that persisted would also matter after 622.

Cause and Effect

Why Arabia Was Politically Fragmented yet Widely Connected, c. 600

Long-term causes

Varied ecology, with scattered water and pasture, favored small, mobile, kin-based groups; imperial neighbors preferred client arrangements to direct rule; no stable state emerged in the center and west.

Short-term causes

The decline of Ḥimyar, the weakening of the Ghassanid and Lakhmid arrangements, and the military and fiscal strain of Byzantine-Sasanian war reduced outside patronage and left more space for local arrangements.

Effects

Politics relied on coalitions, arbitration, and sanctuaries; religious plurality persisted; trade, pilgrimage, markets, and poetry connected dispersed communities.

Complexity

Fragmentation did not mean isolation or disorder. Regional variation was large, and later developments could not have been predicted from these conditions alone.

Historical complexity: Structural conditions made some outcomes more likely than others, but they did not determine the events of the seventh century, which Chapters 36 through 39 examine.

Everyday Life and Social History

Three Households

A pastoral household. Its year is organized around the search for water and pasture. Women and men share labor: tending animals, milking, weaving tents and cloth, preparing dairy products, and caring for children. Wealth is held mainly in animals, and decisions about moving camp involve consultation among kin. Contact with oases and markets provides grain, dates, and tools, and relationships with protectors and allies matter in times of drought or conflict.

An oasis household. A family in a date-growing oasis works its palms through the year, pollinating, irrigating, and harvesting, with the help of dependents and enslaved or hired workers. The household lives in a fortified compound within a clan quarter, stores dates and grain, and trades surplus. Water rights and boundaries require negotiation with neighbors, and feuds can affect access to fields.

A town merchant or artisan household. In a western Arabian settlement, a family might combine small-scale trade, craft production, services to travelers and pilgrims, and shares in caravans. Its standing depends on its clan, on alliances, and on its ability to arrange protection for travel. Hospitality, credit, and reputation matter as much as capital.

These sketches are reconstructions from varied evidence, including inscriptions, archaeology, later tradition, and ethnographic comparison. Women’s roles, childhood, health, and the experience of enslaved people are poorly documented, and historians should say so openly rather than fill the gaps with modern assumptions.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that pre-Islamic Arabia was neither a uniform tribal society, nor an isolated pagan desert, nor merely a waiting stage for Islam. It was a varied set of societies, connected by trade, kinship, religion, language, and imperial politics, whose history must be reconstructed from fragmentary and uneven evidence. Section 35.1 established the methodological foundation: inscriptions, archaeology, external sources, poetry, the Qurʾan, and later Arabic tradition each preserve different kinds of information and each has distinctive limits.

Sections 35.2 through 35.5 described the regional worlds of the peninsula and the economies they supported. Pastoralists moved seasonally between water and pasture; oases supported date and grain agriculture, fortified households, and craft; towns varied from sanctuary settlements to highland garden towns; and markets and exchange tied these groups together. “Bedouin” was only one part of a society that included farmers, townspeople, and enslaved and dependent persons.

Sections 35.6 through 35.9 turned to social and political organization. Kinship and genealogy structured obligation, but tribes were flexible and genealogies were adjusted to changing alliances. Clientage and alliance extended social ties beyond descent. Authority was usually negotiated rather than commanded, with chiefs, assemblies, and arbitrators; Kinda illustrated an attempt at wider kingship that relied on outside patronage and proved fragile. Raiding, vengeance, compensation, protection, arbitration, and sanctuary together formed an order that worked without a state.

Sections 35.10 through 35.13 examined commerce and the two settlements most important to later history. Trade operated at local, regional, and long-distance scales, and seasonal fairs and sacred months connected commerce to sanctuary protection. Mecca’s pre-Islamic history is hard to reconstruct because the main sources are later and archaeology is restricted, and the Historical Debate showed how historians disagree about its commercial role. The Kaʿba and pilgrimage gave Mecca religious and social importance, while Yathrib appeared as a fractured oasis society on the threshold of the period treated in Chapter 37.

Sections 35.14 through 35.16 widened the lens beyond the Hijaz. South Arabia’s literate kingdoms and the legacy of Ḥimyar, eastern Arabia’s and Oman’s ties to Iran and the sea, and the shifting northern frontier after the weakening of the Ghassanid and Lakhmid arrangements show that Arabia had many centers and that imperial power reached far into the peninsula while never fully controlling it.

Sections 35.17 through 35.20 treated religion. Arabian polytheisms were plural and local, not a single idol cult; Jewish communities were long established and integrated in Arabian society; Christianity existed in several rival traditions and had its own presence in the north, south, and east; and monotheistic currents, including South Arabian inscriptions and the later tradition of ḥanīfs, were real but varied, and cannot be arranged into a neat path to Islam.

Section 35.21 considered poetry, language, and memory. The corpus of pre-Islamic poetry is rich but was transmitted orally and collected later, so it requires cautious use. Arabic writing was emerging in inscriptions, and the later notion of the jāhiliyya as an age of ignorance reflects retrospective judgment more than the evidence.

Section 35.22 drew these threads together. Around 600, Arabia was socially varied, politically fragmented yet connected, religiously plural, and increasingly exposed to the strains of imperial war. The features that characterized it, such as coalition politics, sanctuaries, alliances, and the interplay of herding, farming, and trade, were not unique but were shaped by the peninsula’s specific geography and neighbors.

The features of this chapter also demonstrated continuity and change. Kinship, pastoral and oasis economies, and customary order persisted, while states rose and fell, monotheism spread, and Arabic writing began. The Safaitic Primary Source Window illustrated how inscriptions can supply voices from below, while the Cause and Effect and Everyday Life features situated fragmentation and daily experience within broader structures.

Finally, the chapter has insisted on contingency. Nothing in the Arabia of about 600 CE made the career of Muhammad or the rise of Islam inevitable. Chapter 36 examines how Muhammad’s preaching actually began, and Chapters 37 through 39 follow the consequences. Those chapters will be more intelligible, and more surprising, for being read against the diverse and connected world described here.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it provides the setting without which the next chapters cannot be understood. The events of the early seventh century, treated in Chapters 36 through 39, did not occur in a vacuum. They arose within a society of herders, farmers, townspeople, merchants, poets, priests, monks, and rulers, with established institutions of kinship, arbitration, sanctuary, and alliance, and with religious communities of several kinds. Understanding that world makes later developments more intelligible, and it also makes them more remarkable.

The chapter has also modeled a way of thinking. Reconstructing the past from uneven evidence requires distinguishing what contemporaries wrote from what later generations remembered, acknowledging silences, and resisting stories that make outcomes look inevitable. These habits will be needed in every chapter of this volume, because the earliest centuries of Islam are themselves known largely through sources written later.

Several structures introduced here recur in later volumes: the importance of kinship and alliance in politics; the relationship between pastoral groups and settled states; the role of sanctuaries and pilgrimage; the entanglement of Arabia with Byzantine, Sasanian, and Indian Ocean worlds; and the coexistence of multiple religious communities. They did not remain unchanged, but they did not vanish either, and understanding them helps explain how the region’s later political and religious history unfolded.

Chapter 36 now turns to Muhammad and the Meccan revelations. It will ask how a preacher in this society began to speak, how his message was heard and opposed, and how historians use the Qurʾan as evidence. The world sketched in this chapter, plural, connected, and without a state, is the background against which those questions should be asked.
