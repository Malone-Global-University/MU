Chapter Opening

In 1467 the victorious Uzun Hasan inherited far more than the defeat of a rival. He inherited roads, cities, fiscal obligations, religious institutions, and a politically divided military world that had developed long before his own rule.

At Tabriz in the later fifteenth century a traveler could encounter an imperial claim that had changed hands more than once without the city itself disappearing. Markets, shrines, workshops, and fiscal offices outlasted the Jalayirids, Qara Qoyunlu, and Aq Qoyunlu. Yet the ruler capable of claiming those resources might owe his authority to Turkmen cavalry whose commanders maintained their own households and expected rewards. This tension between movement and settlement, military allegiance and civic administration, is the starting point of the chapter.

The Qara Qoyunlu and Aq Qoyunlu are often placed between two better-known histories: the conquests of Timur and the emergence of Safavid Iran. Such placement can conceal their independent importance. They governed Azerbaijan, eastern Anatolia, northern Mesopotamia, Baghdad, and much of western and central Iran in different periods. They negotiated with the Timurids, Mamluks, Ottomans, and Christian frontier powers, and their internal disputes shaped the political choices of merchants, scholars, military chiefs, and village communities.

Their names, conventionally translated Black Sheep and White Sheep, suggest more stable identities than the available evidence permits. Each was a confederation led by a particular ruling house but composed of changing alliances. A successful leader could draw followers through warfare, diplomacy, and access to revenue; an unsuccessful one could lose the support that sustained his title. Neither confederation was simply a nomadic army. Both gradually depended on urban administration, Persian scribal experience, law, fiscal privilege, and cultural patronage.

The chapter proceeds from the fragmentation of the Ilkhanid inheritance after 1335 through the rise of Qara Yusuf and Jahan Shah, the great Aq Qoyunlu victories under Uzun Hasan, and the succession struggles that followed the death of Yaqub in 1490. Its principal narrative closes with the Safavid capture of former Aq Qoyunlu territories through approximately 1508. The rise of Ismail is introduced only so far as it explains the dissolution of the earlier political system; his conquests and the creation of the Safavid state belong to Chapters 70 and 76.

This was a world of distinct religious and social communities. Armenian and Syriac Christians, Kurdish pastoralists and cultivators, Arab inhabitants of northern Iraq, Persian-speaking townspeople, and diverse Turkmen groups all formed part of the territories whose revenues financed government. Their experiences cannot be inferred only from court histories that celebrate victory. The chapter therefore considers seasonal pastures, roads, markets, taxation, women's patronage, religious institutions, and the practical consequences of war.

A major historical problem concerns the relationship between confederation and state. Did powerful chiefs prevent the creation of effective government, or did confederate military power and Persian administration work together to create genuinely territorial rule? The answer differs according to place, period, and institution. A governor could control an army but depend on a secretary to collect taxes; a ruler might legislate fiscal reform but lack the political support to implement it throughout the realm. Rather than assuming contradiction, the chapter examines how these relationships actually operated.

Finally, the evidence has limits. Persian histories, Venetian diplomatic reports, inscriptions, and records of religious endowments illuminate different parts of the story. Court writers might exaggerate sovereignty, outside envoys might misunderstand local institutions, and preserved buildings reveal only a fraction of past urban life. The four primary-source studies therefore use carefully identified editorial paraphrases, not unverified quotations. The guiding question is how the two Turkmen confederations governed, fought, and patronized a region whose institutions and political networks helped make the Safavid transformation possible—but did not make it inevitable.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain the fragmented post-Ilkhanid political landscape of eastern Anatolia, Azerbaijan, and Iraq.
  2. Distinguish the origins, leadership networks, and territorial development of the Qara Qoyunlu and Aq Qoyunlu.
  3. Analyze Qara Yusuf's recovery of Tabriz, victory in 1408, and the separate change of government in Baghdad.
  4. Trace Jahan Shah's campaigns, urban administration, and the limits of the 1458 occupation of Herat.
  5. Evaluate how Uzun Hasan consolidated power and defeated Jahan Shah and Abu Sa'id in 1467–1469.

  7. Interpret confederate councils, provincial commands, Persian administration, and soyurghal grants as connected institutions.
  8. Assess how taxation, urban markets, trade routes, and fiscal privileges affected state formation.

  11. Interpret the Blue Mosque, court chronicles, and Venetian accounts using source-critical methods.
  12. Explain Yaqub's reforms, succession conflicts after 1490, and the transition to Safavid control by about 1508.

Section 68.1

Eastern Anatolia, Azerbaijan, and Iraq after the Ilkhanate

The death of Abu Sa'id in 1335 ended the last relatively coherent phase of Ilkhanid rule. The political geography that remained was neither empty nor uncontested. In Azerbaijan, families connected with Mongol military commanders competed for access to Tabriz and its revenues. Jalayirid leaders developed authority in Baghdad and, for periods, Azerbaijan; Chobanid commanders also claimed the Ilkhanid inheritance. The cities of Tabriz, Baghdad, Maragha, and Mosul still possessed markets, religious establishments, skilled officials, and commercial connections. A new ruler could seize a town, but its effective government required the cooperation—or coercion—of people capable of collecting taxes, supplying armies, and maintaining daily life. The collapse of a dynasty therefore did not mean the collapse of political society.

Eastern Anatolia lay at the meeting point of several different kinds of territory. The basin around Lake Van connected highland pastures, fortified settlements, Armenian communities, and routes into Azerbaijan. Diyarbakır, known in many contemporary sources as Amid, commanded a strategic section of the upper Tigris region. Mosul and northern Mesopotamia connected the Euphrates-Tigris watershed to routes extending toward Syria and Iraq. In each district, possession of cities mattered alongside rights to pastures, movement through mountain passes, and the support of armed households. The same road might serve transhumant herders, traders transporting textiles, religious scholars, and military contingents. Regional geography shaped politics without determining in advance which dynasty would prevail.

The weakening of Mongol supreme authority multiplied the opportunities of local rulers. Dynastic princes, former Ilkhanid officials, military entrepreneurs, and the heads of mobile communities all sought influence. Some invoked inherited Ilkhanid titles or institutions; others relied on marriage, battlefield success, or appointments issued by stronger neighbors. This was not a simple division between a Persian-speaking urban society and a Turkish-speaking rural society. Language, occupation, religion, and political allegiance overlapped. An administrator could work for successive rulers; a tribal leader might hold land near a town; religious patrons could mediate between competing courts. The period's history is intelligible only if these intersecting networks remain in view.

The Ilkhanid fiscal and bureaucratic inheritance was particularly durable. Registers, scribal offices, grants of income, judicial specialists, and the Persian language of court administration helped later powers govern. Their survival did not guarantee stability: overlapping demands for revenue could damage farming communities and unsettle merchants. Nor did political rivalry prevent all commerce. Tabriz remained attractive precisely because it sat within established networks reaching toward the Caucasus, Anatolia, and Iran's interior. Baghdad's political importance likewise rested partly on urban institutions that military victors could not readily reproduce elsewhere. The struggle for these centers was a struggle for people, productive landscapes, reputations, and inherited governmental capacity.

The Qara Qoyunlu and Aq Qoyunlu emerged within this post-Mongol setting, not outside it. Their leadership drew strength from Turkmen cavalry and confederate relationships, yet each needed alliances with towns, local governors, merchants, and administrators to become more than a raiding force. Their rise also overlapped with the campaigns of Timur and the recovery of Timurid power in eastern Iran, discussed in Chapter 66, and with Ottoman consolidation farther west, discussed in Chapter 67. The resulting frontier did not belong naturally to any one state. Its rulers repeatedly made, contested, and remade authority. Chapter 68 examines how two confederations attempted to turn temporary military advantages into durable government.

Section 68.2

Turkmen Migration, Tribal Organization, Pastoralism, and Frontier Society

The term Turkmen in late medieval accounts identifies populations with historical connections to the Oghuz-speaking world, but it does not describe a single unified nation with one ruler or a uniform way of life. Oghuz migrations into western Asia had a long history extending through Seljuk expansion, and subsequent movements were influenced by Mongol warfare, political opportunity, and access to resources. By the fourteenth century many communities were established within Anatolia, the Armenian highlands, northern Mesopotamia, and Azerbaijan. Some lived primarily by herding; others cultivated land, served in garrisons, traded, or occupied towns. Historians must resist treating tribal labels in chronicles as immutable ethnic classifications.

Mobile pastoral economies depended on seasonal movement. In the warmer months herds could use upland grazing districts, often described by the term yaylaq; wintering grounds, or qishlaq, commonly lay in lower and more sheltered locations. Such movements linked ecological zones and created political stakes in routes, springs, pastures, bridges, and market access. A ruler who controlled a fortified town but could not guarantee safe passages for herds might fail to command the allegiance of chiefs. Conversely, warriors who used highland pasture could depend on grain, craft products, and commercial outlets supplied by settled populations. The economy of confederation was therefore neither entirely nomadic nor entirely urban.

Tribal politics involved more than obedience to a single hereditary chief. Confederations brought together lineages and military followings with their own leaders, rivalries, and expectations of reward. Success in war could attract new participants, while defeat or disputes over the division of territories might disperse them. Councils of chiefs, patronage relations, dynastic marriages, and negotiations over revenue all had political meaning. The ruling Bayandur house of the Aq Qoyunlu eventually stood at the head of a broader coalition; the Qara Qoyunlu likewise drew strength from alliances that could change with events. A confederation's unity was continually made through practical decisions, not guaranteed by a shared name.

The populations living alongside Turkmen military groups were no less important to the region's future. Armenian-speaking Christians maintained villages, churches, monasteries, and commercial institutions in various districts. Kurdish populations practiced pastoralism, cultivation, and forms of military or local lordship. Syriac Christian communities, Arab-speaking populations of northern Iraq, and Persian-speaking townspeople participated in distinct yet connected social worlds. Their boundaries did not map neatly onto a single administrative frontier. A village might pay dues to a governor who recognized a Turkmen prince, while an Armenian merchant dealt with a Muslim judge and a Christian ecclesiastical court in different aspects of life. This complexity discourages simple narratives of one community replacing another.

Military service and economic production formed a reciprocal, often coercive relationship. Tribal cavalry needed animals, grain, equipment, and routes; governments raised these resources through taxes, tolls, grants of fiscal rights, and extraordinary requisitions. Pastoralists could suffer from disrupted migration just as cultivators suffered from armies moving across fields. Commercial security encouraged exchange, but customs demands and warfare could make the same route hazardous. Women organized households and could possess and administer property, while the experiences of poorer women are less directly visible in the surviving court literature. Understanding this society requires attention to ecology, political negotiation, and the uneven documentary record. It is the society upon which both the Black Sheep and White Sheep dynasties attempted to build states.

Section 68.3

The Qara Qoyunlu: Origins, Tribal Networks, and Early Regional Authority

The Qara Qoyunlu, conventionally rendered Black Sheep, are first securely visible as a political confederation in the later medieval frontier north of Lake Van and toward northern Iraq. The name's literal meaning is clear, but its origin is not. Suggestions that it reflects animal symbolism or particular flock characteristics remain hypotheses rather than proof of a shared ancient ancestry. This uncertainty matters because the earliest political community was not a homogeneous tribe marching through history unchanged. It brought together several military groupings under leaders whose standing depended on alliances, local control, and their relations with older regional powers.

Bayram Khwaja belongs to the first generation of identifiable Qara Qoyunlu leaders. Under him and Qara Muhammad the confederation became increasingly important in the contest for territories around Van, Mosul, and the routes toward Azerbaijan. Its rulers encountered Jalayirid and other post-Ilkhanid authorities, alternately negotiating, acknowledging superiority, and fighting for local positions. A declaration of obedience could be a tactical measure when a larger army approached; it did not necessarily imply the permanent transfer of every local source of authority. The same complexity marked military alliances. Turkmen followers could serve a leader for opportunities in war but expect a share of protection, territories, or revenue afterward.

Geography encouraged the emergence of this kind of power. The mountainous districts around Lake Van supplied defendable positions and pastures, while connections to northern Mesopotamia and Azerbaijan opened access to urban wealth. Cities required military protection but also offered legitimacy through their mosques, markets, and administrative offices. A ruler with access to pasture alone might retain a confederate following; a ruler who could secure Tabriz or Baghdad could claim a larger territorial standing. The Qara Qoyunlu's later transformation must therefore be explained as a series of adjustments between military movement and territorial acquisition rather than as the execution of an original imperial plan.

Qara Yusuf inherited these opportunities and dangers. His career at the turn of the fifteenth century intersected with Timur's aggressive reordering of the region. He made and lost alliances, was displaced by superior military forces, and returned to political prominence after Timur's death. The early Qara Qoyunlu story is difficult to reconstruct exclusively from the standpoint of its own officials, because no comparable full official chronicle survives. Much of the evidence appears in Timurid narratives, later Persian histories, and the retrospective accounts of rivals. Such sources preserve important events but often portray confederate leaders in terms suitable to a different court's political message.

By the opening years of the fifteenth century the confederation had achieved considerable cohesion without eliminating competition within it. Loyalty to Qara Yusuf was tied to successes that could be remembered as the defense or recovery of territory, yet individual chiefs and princes retained their own interests. The resources of Lake Van, northern Iraq, and Azerbaijan would soon be translated into claims over Tabriz and Baghdad. That later expansion should not erase the first phase of the story: the Black Sheep were initially one contender within the post-Mongol frontier order. Their history exposes how a confederate military coalition could gradually adopt the ambitions, bureaucratic techniques, and ceremonial expectations of a territorial dynasty.

Section 68.4

The Aq Qoyunlu: Bayandur Leadership and Eastern Anatolian Power

The Aq Qoyunlu, conventionally White Sheep, developed as a separate Turkmen confederation whose leading house identified itself with the Bayandur lineage. Later genealogies connected the Bayandur to the legendary Oghuz Khan, offering a language of ancestry useful for political legitimacy. Those genealogies must be read in their dynastic and literary settings rather than as independently verified pedigrees. The precise significance of the White Sheep name remains debated, as with the Qara Qoyunlu. What can be reconstructed more securely is a changing political alliance whose forces moved through eastern Anatolia and neighboring highland pastures before its rulers controlled substantial towns and revenues.

Seasonal migrations between Armenian uplands and the lands of the upper Euphrates and Tigris formed part of the confederation's material setting. Access to summer and winter pastures brought its members into contact and sometimes conflict with established Christian settlements, Kurdish communities, and other Turkoman groups. The political center later associated with Diyarbakır was not yet the capital of a coherent empire. Controlling a fortress, passing through a grazing district, and receiving recognition from a larger sovereign were distinct forms of influence. The leadership tried to combine them while maintaining the trust of followers who could seek another patron.

Earlier leaders such as Tur Ali and his successor Qutlu are important because they show political relationships preceding the best-known figures, Qara Yuluq Osman and Uzun Hasan. Exchanges with the rulers of Trebizond included raids and marriage diplomacy. A marriage arranged in 1352 between a woman of the Komnenian ruling family and the house of Tur Ali helped reduce conflict in one sector of the frontier. Such alliances complicate any account of the confederation as engaged in an unvarying religious war against Christian neighbors. Cooperation could support safe passage, political recognition, or claims to particular border districts, even while other Christian settlements faced raiding.

The Aq Qoyunlu also interacted with rulers in Erzincan, Sivas, and the sphere of the Eretna polity. As authority shifted among provincial commanders and ambitious military households, the Bayandur leaders used local alliances to enlarge their autonomy. Competition with the Qara Qoyunlu became more consequential when both groups sought command of the same communications corridors and highland resources. Yet their early positions were not symmetrical: the White Sheep leadership remained particularly rooted in the Diyarbakır and upper Euphrates environment, while the Black Sheep were more closely associated with the Van and northern Mesopotamian zone. Both would eventually claim wider power, but they did so from different local foundations.

The history of the Aq Qoyunlu therefore requires several scales of analysis. At the level of a confederation, chiefs depended on seasonal resources and negotiated military service. At the level of a principality, a successful leader needed fortresses, towns, taxation, and relations with neighboring dynasties. At the level of a later empire, Uzun Hasan would inherit and transform these arrangements while ruling Iranian urban centers. John Woods's reconstruction of the transition from clan to confederation to empire is valuable precisely because it does not project the political system of the 1470s backward into the mid-fourteenth century. The eventual Aq Qoyunlu state was the outcome of contingent decisions, not the inevitable expression of an ancient tribal identity.

Division II — Qara Qoyunlu Expansion and the Struggle for Iran

Section 68.5

Qara Yusuf, Timur, and the Establishment of Qara Qoyunlu Power

The career of Qara Yusuf illustrates the dangers and opportunities created by Timur's campaigns. A leader of regional military importance in the 1390s, Qara Yusuf struggled to preserve his following while the conqueror sought to dominate Azerbaijan, Iraq, and the surrounding frontier. His political fortunes included displacement, refuge, and alliances with other rulers threatened by Timurid arms. The details of these moves are recorded by court historians with differing allegiances, so the chronology is best reconstructed through comparison rather than a single triumphant narrative. Timur's death in 1405 did not automatically restore Qara Yusuf's rule, but it weakened the immediate military pressure and opened possibilities for a determined return.

In 1406 Qara Yusuf reestablished himself in Tabriz, a recovery that preceded the better-known battle of 1408. Two years later his forces defeated Timurid opponents in Azerbaijan; Miran Shah, Timur's son, was killed in the conflict. This was more than an episode of frontier raiding. It undermined Timurid authority in a region that had been vital to Timur's wider imperial claims and signaled that a confederate leader could sustain territorial power there. Yet possession of Tabriz had to be maintained through officials, revenue, military security, and agreements with regional elites. The city's political importance derived from its inherited administrative institutions and commercial reach, not merely from its walls.

The struggle for Iraq unfolded in a related but separate sequence. Qara Yusuf defeated the Jalayirid Ahmad in 1410, and the change of authority in Baghdad involved subsequent action by Qara Yusuf's son Shah Muhammad, culminating in Qara Qoyunlu control around 1412. Describing these events as one undifferentiated conquest would conceal the role of Jalayirid remnants, local governors, and the confederation's own princes. Baghdad's position on the Tigris gave it religious and cultural significance, but control over surrounding agriculture and routes toward Mosul mattered equally. A frontier coalition acquiring the city faced the unfamiliar problems of an established urban government.

Qara Yusuf's regime combined direct military authority with negotiated local arrangements. His followers expected territory, provisions, and material reward; urban families expected some predictability in administration. Existing scribes, fiscal procedures, and legal personnel supplied skills that cavalry commanders could not readily replace. This dependence did not make the Qara Qoyunlu a thoroughly centralized state. Appointment of relatives to major territorial positions created new opportunities for competition. The same family network that helped extend power from Azerbaijan into Iraq might become a source of division when its most successful leader died.

Qara Yusuf's importance is therefore not adequately captured by the label opponent of Timur. He demonstrated that the western Iranian and Mesopotamian provinces could be reorganized after the Timurid conquests under a new confederate dynasty. His successes offered later rulers a political inheritance: Tabriz as an important capital, Baghdad as a provincial prize, and a coalition whose loyalty required constant management. His death in 1420 coincided with renewed Timurid intervention under Shah Rukh. The resulting instability reveals both the real achievements and the limits of this first phase of Qara Qoyunlu state formation. A state could survive its founder, but it needed a succession settlement to do so without costly conflict.

Section 68.6

Iskandar, Shah Rukh, and the Contest between the Qara Qoyunlu and Timurids

The death of Qara Yusuf in 1420 transformed the political settlement he had built into a contested inheritance. His sons and associates could claim portions of his authority, but no automatic mechanism required leading chiefs to accept a single heir. Iskandar became a principal claimant, while other family members and local governors pursued their own interests. The problem was larger than an argument over a royal title. Baghdad, Tabriz, and highland districts could supply separate armies and revenue streams. Where a prince commanded a garrison or a coalition of chiefs, he could resist a rival whose authority was accepted elsewhere. Confederate politics made succession a negotiation conducted partly through arms.

Shah Rukh, Timur's son and the dominant Timurid ruler based at Herat, intervened repeatedly to check the Qara Qoyunlu. Campaigns in 1420–1421, 1429, and 1434–1435 sought to restore Timurid predominance in Azerbaijan and prevent an uncontrolled western rival from emerging. Shah Rukh could mobilize large forces, but extending influence from Khorasan into Azerbaijan involved distance, provisioning, and the problem of leaving a dependable local governor after a campaign ended. Nominal submission after military defeat did not ensure continuing obedience. The frontier's political history thus oscillated between military demonstrations of Timurid power and the restoration of Qara Qoyunlu independence when invading armies returned east.

The Aq Qoyunlu were important participants rather than spectators. Their leader Qara Yuluq Osman maintained favorable relations with Timurid authorities and opposed Iskandar in frontier fighting. Such an alliance offered the White Sheep opportunities to advance against their Black Sheep rivals, but also carried danger. During the 1435 campaign Qara Yuluq, then an aging ruler, confronted Iskandar near Erzurum and was defeated and killed. The death weakened his dynasty just as wider Qara Qoyunlu succession struggles continued. Alliances among the Timurids, Turkmen groups, and Mamluks were therefore highly contingent: each leader pursued local advantages that could outlast or contradict the strategic purposes of a nominal superior.

Iskandar's position deteriorated as family antagonisms deepened. Shah Rukh could support rival claimants, including Jahan Shah, as a means of reducing Iskandar's military threat. Jahan Shah's emergence in the later 1430s must be understood within this interaction of Timurid interventions, tribal loyalties, and dynastic competition. A ruler recognized by Herat might still need victories and negotiations to turn that recognition into command of Azerbaijan. The conflicts affected civilian communities as armies crossed agricultural areas, took provisions, and challenged taxes demanded by the government already in place. A change in sovereign could be experienced locally as the arrival of another fiscal claimant rather than an abstract correction of legitimacy.

This period shows the limitations of treating medieval diplomacy in binary terms such as independence or vassalage. A Qara Qoyunlu ruler might send gifts and acknowledge Shah Rukh's precedence while directing his own armies and levying taxes. A Timurid court could proclaim authority without maintaining permanent officials in distant cities. A confederate chief might appeal to an outside dynasty while expecting substantial autonomy afterward. Read alongside Persian narrative histories, patterns of campaigning and appointment reveal an unstable hierarchy rather than a clear administrative border. The rise of Jahan Shah grew from this unsettled landscape. It also marked the beginning of a new question: whether the Qara Qoyunlu could transform a contested regional coalition into an Iranian state capable of governing far beyond Azerbaijan.

Section 68.7

Jahan Shah and the Expansion of Qara Qoyunlu Rule across Iran

Jahan Shah's long ascendancy, beginning in the later 1430s and ending in 1467, brought the Qara Qoyunlu to the greatest territorial extent of their history. He initially operated within a political order in which Shah Rukh's Timurid supremacy could still be acknowledged. Yet the governing realities of Azerbaijan depended increasingly on Jahan Shah's own household, commanders, and regional officers. The death of Shah Rukh in 1447 and the ensuing Timurid disputes reduced the prospect of sustained eastern intervention. Jahan Shah was able to pursue an extensive western and central Iranian program without confronting a single unified Timurid power. This opportunity did not remove the costs of conquest; it enlarged the territories across which his officials had to negotiate authority.

Azerbaijan and Tabriz remained fundamental, but Jahan Shah's campaigns and arrangements extended influence toward Persian Iraq, Fars, Kerman, and adjacent provinces. These regions were not interchangeable. Fars had long-established cities, agricultural systems, and administrative families; the interior plateau contained its own roads, towns, and fiscal relationships; Iraq was connected to a different riverine and commercial ecology. Governing each required more than allocating land to mounted warriors. The ruler needed people who knew established tax demands and could distinguish customary obligations from emergency military assessments. Expansion thus created an increasing dependence on administrators whose professional practices belonged to the Persianate and post-Ilkhanid governmental tradition.

The occupation of Herat in 1458 represented the geographical reach, and the limits, of Jahan Shah's ambitions. Herat was the great Timurid center of Khorasan, linked to scholarly and commercial institutions cultivated under Shah Rukh. Jahan Shah briefly entered the city during Timurid succession struggles, but the extension into Khorasan could not be made secure. Opposition, threats to his western political position, and the difficulty of sustaining an army far from its principal supports encouraged withdrawal and negotiation. A map showing Herat in his hands at one moment would therefore misrepresent the durability of his authority. Territorial extent has to be measured in time as well as space.

At court Jahan Shah cultivated the appearance and practices of sovereign government. Coinage, official titles, patronage, and public buildings were tools through which a confederate ruler could speak to urban subjects and rival dynasties. He is also associated with poetry, often under the pen name Haqiqi, indicating participation in literary environments not reducible to the language of tribal warfare. Yet the military coalition remained politically consequential. Commanders and princes expected substantial positions, and a central administration could not simply eliminate the social basis of cavalry recruitment. The ruler's political skill lay in combining confederate military strength with a territorial administration whose interests were sometimes different from those of the chiefs.

Jahan Shah's rule should not be narrated as uninterrupted prosperity or as an inevitable prelude to defeat. Agricultural communities confronted fiscal pressure and occasional campaigning, while urban elites could benefit from a more stable center of political authority. Religious patronage and literary activity coexisted with severe dynastic coercion. The state had acquired real breadth, resources, and cultural standing, but its territorial reach multiplied the consequences of conflict within the ruling family. Baghdad, in particular, exposed the tension between a prince with a major provincial power base and a father who claimed supremacy over the whole. To explain the collapse of 1467, the story must first account for these internal negotiations, not merely for Uzun Hasan's skill in battle.

Section 68.8

Baghdad, Dynastic Conflict, and the Qara Qoyunlu Political and Cultural Order

Baghdad occupied a distinctive place within Qara Qoyunlu government. Its location on the Tigris, established religious and commercial institutions, and relationship to the agricultural hinterland made it a prize worth more than its strategic position alone. Political authority there had passed through Jalayirid and other hands before Qara Qoyunlu control developed during the 1410s. The city's governors exercised influence over territories and networks not wholly dependent upon day-to-day direction from Tabriz. Such a province could strengthen the wider dynasty by supplying revenues and troops, but it could also become a base from which a prince challenged the reigning ruler. Baghdad's history therefore reveals a structural difficulty of territorial government.

Jahan Shah's son Pir Budaq illustrates the problem. Associated with the government of Baghdad, he resisted his father's authority and attempted to defend a regional political position. The conflict included more than a family quarrel: military followers, city elites, fiscal resources, and provincial appointments were at stake. Jahan Shah's forces eventually overcame the rebellion, and Pir Budaq died in 1466. The operation required effort that could otherwise have been directed against rivals outside the dynasty. A ruler whose army could defeat distant enemies still faced limits when provincial governors had acquired their own troops and networks of support. The costs of reconquest were paid by inhabitants as well as by ruling families.

The relationship between the capital and provincial centers was mediated by administrative personnel. Secretaries, treasury officials, judges, and tax collectors understood how urban income and rural production could be translated into usable state revenue. They were not all members of the dominant military confederation. Their careers help explain why changes in dynasty did not automatically change the way land was assessed or court business conducted. But bureaucratic continuity did not protect communities from arbitrary demands. A governor could impose extra requisitions, soldiers could seize supplies, and disputes over the right to collect taxes could create two rival sets of obligations. Administrative continuity and political instability could exist together.

The Qara Qoyunlu court also participated in the region's literary and architectural life. Jahan Shah's poetry and patronage linked him to Iranian elite expectations of cultivated rule. In Tabriz, the religious and charitable complex later known as the Blue Mosque demonstrated the resources and ambitions of the dynasty's household, especially its women. These achievements should not be measured only by comparing surviving buildings with the more extensive monuments of the Timurids. Destruction, subsequent rebuilding, and the accidents of preservation affect what remains visible. A surviving inscription may show the date of a foundation more securely than a courtly narrative can explain its political motives.

Jahan Shah's suppression of Pir Budaq restored his immediate command but did not dissolve the tension between confederate autonomy and monarchical ambitions. His later expedition against Uzun Hasan came after years in which territorial expansion and family conflict had placed heavy demands on the state. The defeat of 1467 would quickly expose the vulnerability of the political coalition, but that ending must not become the only measure of the earlier regime. The Qara Qoyunlu governed substantial populations, maintained cities, supported institutions, and shaped subsequent political practices. Their history is both a study of state building and a warning that territorial expansion does not by itself secure orderly succession or enduring loyalty.

Division III — The Aq Qoyunlu and the Construction of a Regional Empire

Section 68.9

Qara Yuluq Osman, Timur, and the Consolidation of Aq Qoyunlu Authority

Qara Yuluq Osman, also called Qara Osman, was the most important early architect of Aq Qoyunlu political power. His career emerged from the competitive politics of eastern Anatolia, where Turkoman chiefs confronted rulers in Sivas, Erzincan, northern Syria, and the upper Tigris. In 1398 he defeated and killed Qadi Burhan al-Din, a powerful ruler in central Anatolia, demonstrating that the confederation could affect the regional balance well beyond its seasonal pasturelands. That success also brought him into the field of larger powers. Expanding Ottoman influence and Timur's campaigns created risks for a leader who sought to turn military distinction into a secure territorial base.

Qara Osman joined Timur's side as the conqueror moved through western Asia and participated in the struggles that culminated in the Battle of Ankara in 1402. The alliance was not simply an expression of admiration for Timur; it offered opportunities for recognition and territorial gain. Timur confirmed his standing and the Bayandur family's authority over Amid, or Diyarbakır, which became central to Aq Qoyunlu government. Such recognition mattered in a world where political authority could be expressed through appointment as well as conquest. Yet receiving a stronghold from a great ruler did not guarantee the obedience of neighboring chiefs. Qara Osman still had to assemble a durable following and defend routes connecting the Euphrates, Tigris, and highland districts.

His administration combined military patronage with the beginnings of a territorial apparatus. Chiefs expected benefits for service, while towns and villages supplied revenue and labor. A princely household could not maintain prolonged authority through booty alone, especially when seasonal pasture interests required negotiation with sedentary communities. Qara Osman's rule shows evidence of officials and fiscal practices belonging to the Iranian-Islamic bureaucratic tradition, although the confederation's military elite remained dominant. Diplomatic relations with Shah Rukh's Timurid court and with the Mamluks gave him alternating forms of support and opposition. These larger states were interested in keeping rivals from monopolizing the borderland, not in creating an entirely independent Aq Qoyunlu empire.

The ruler also tried to manage succession and territorial administration through his sons. By assigning important districts to family members, he hoped to tie new acquisitions to dynastic loyalty and reduce dependence on individual chiefs. The method offered short-term advantages: princes could command troops and supervise revenue collection close to contested frontiers. It also created future rivals with independent power bases. On the larger frontier, Qara Osman's support for Timurid campaigns against Qara Qoyunlu princes made him a conspicuous enemy. In 1435 he was defeated near Erzurum by Iskandar's forces and killed. His death occurred in a theater where major states and confederations repeatedly intervened in one another's succession struggles.

Qara Osman's achievement was substantial despite the immediate political disorder following his death. The Aq Qoyunlu now possessed a recognizable territorial base, a leading dynastic house, significant chiefs, connections to larger governments, and practical administrative experience. These did not add up to the later empire, but they provided material from which ambitious successors could build. The years after 1435 underline a general feature of confederate state formation: military success attracted followers, while the death of an effective leader could release long-suppressed competition. Uzun Hasan inherited neither a stable centralized monarchy nor a meaningless tribal association. He inherited a contested political enterprise whose future depended on reconstructing its alliances.

Section 68.10

The Aq Qoyunlu Succession Struggles and the Rise of Uzun Hasan

The death of Qara Yuluq Osman opened a prolonged contest among members of the Bayandur ruling house. Ali, Hamza, Jahangir, and other princes held different claims, while chiefs and regional commanders sought to preserve their own positions. Ali could not maintain general recognition and eventually withdrew to Egypt; Hamza became influential but died before suppressing every rival. By the 1440s several dynastic branches continued to compete. Foreign powers were drawn into the dispute because an Aq Qoyunlu claimant could influence the balance among the Mamluks, Timurids, and Qara Qoyunlu. A political settlement in Diyarbakır was therefore also part of the diplomacy of the wider Middle East.

Jahangir's position illustrates how dependence on a powerful neighbor could divide a ruling family. Under pressure from Jahan Shah's Qara Qoyunlu forces, he accepted a settlement in 1452 recognizing Qara Qoyunlu superiority. This protected a particular political position but appeared to concede too much to his brother Uzun Hasan. The latter rejected the bargain and used military allies within the confederation to challenge Jahangir's authority. His opportunity came while Jahangir was away from Amid on a campaign. In 1452 Uzun Hasan seized the city in a relatively bloodless coup, demonstrating that command of a key fortress could transform a rival prince's prospects without an extended siege.

Uzun Hasan then pursued a combination of warfare, diplomacy, and coalition building. He signaled recognition of Mamluk authority in the north Syrian border environment to strengthen his position against the Qara Qoyunlu, while continuing to claim practical independence. His efforts to build loyalty among chiefs depended on military rewards and credible protection of their interests. Jahangir tried repeatedly to recover his former standing; the struggle continued until Uzun Hasan secured the main leadership by 1457. It is therefore misleading to describe his rule as if the acquisition of Diyarbakır in 1452 had instantly settled the confederation's internal disputes. Political consolidation was a process, not a moment.

The methods that proved effective in the succession struggle became useful in later expansion. Uzun Hasan understood that chiefs and regional elites might shift their allegiance if a ruler could offer better security, access to routes, or a share of revenue. He also cultivated connections with Christian rulers to the north, particularly Trebizond, and intervened in nearby Anatolian politics. These relationships were shaped by strategic calculation rather than fixed religious blocs. A ruler who raided Georgian territories could still marry into a Christian dynasty and negotiate protection for its subjects. In the eastern Anatolian frontier, diplomacy and violence were complementary methods for organizing the political landscape.

The consolidation of the Aq Qoyunlu under Uzun Hasan did not make the state immune to the contradictions of confederate rule. Military followers still expected distributed rewards, and princely kin retained claims. Yet the leadership now possessed a sufficiently coherent base to challenge Jahan Shah. The significance of Uzun Hasan's early career lies less in an inevitable rise to greatness than in his success at solving an immediate problem of succession. From that local achievement came control over the military coalition that would decide the struggle for Iran in 1467–1469. His later imperial victories were built upon lessons learned in the contested politics of Diyarbakır.

Section 68.11

Uzun Hasan's Defeat of Jahan Shah and Abu Sa'id, 1467–1469

By the mid-1460s Jahan Shah governed a larger and wealthier territorial state than the Aq Qoyunlu ruler in Diyarbakır. That disparity might suggest that Uzun Hasan's eventual victory was improbable, yet the political balance was less stable than a map implied. Jahan Shah's expeditions and his suppression of his son Pir Budaq's rebellion in Baghdad had imposed demands on the Qara Qoyunlu coalition. Uzun Hasan, meanwhile, had consolidated his chiefs, acquired experience in frontier campaigning, and strengthened positions that could support movement through eastern Anatolia. Each ruler depended on military followings responsive to season, supplies, and confidence in leadership. Geography and timing would become decisive when the larger Qara Qoyunlu army moved west in 1467.

Jahan Shah marched against Uzun Hasan late in the campaigning year. According to the principal historical reconstruction, premature winter conditions encouraged him to release much of his force to its winter quarters while his camp remained vulnerable. Uzun Hasan attacked and defeated the reduced army; Jahan Shah was killed in November 1467. The result should not be explained simply through individual courage or through claims of supernatural favor found in laudatory histories. It involved intelligence, seasonal conditions, the dispersal of an opponent's troops, and the capacity of a smaller coalition to exploit an unusual military opportunity. Court writers could subsequently transform that event into evidence of the victor's legitimacy, but the underlying practical circumstances remain central.

The death of Jahan Shah did not automatically deliver all Qara Qoyunlu territories to Uzun Hasan. Surviving princes and confederate groups attempted to defend or recover positions, and the political order collapsed during the following year. The Aq Qoyunlu occupied Azerbaijan and Tabriz as rivals lost the ability to organize a comparable coalition. Regional officials and local elites had to decide whether to recognize the new leadership; some may have done so to protect revenues and institutions, others because resistance was impractical. This process of incorporation deserves as much attention as the decisive battle. Controlling a capital required relationships with its merchants, clerks, religious patrons, and tax-paying hinterland.

A second challenge emerged from the east. The Timurid ruler Abu Sa'id sought to recover territories that had passed from Timurid control into Turkmen hands. Advancing into western Iran, he encountered severe logistical constraints and difficulty obtaining the support needed for a sustained campaign. Uzun Hasan exploited these weaknesses and the geography of the Caucasian approaches. Abu Sa'id was captured in early 1469 and executed shortly afterward. The defeat effectively ended a serious Timurid attempt to reestablish supremacy in Azerbaijan and much of western Iran. Again, victory was not only a battle: the isolation of an army in winter quarters and the securing of supply routes shaped the result.

The twin victories transformed Uzun Hasan's position. He now possessed territories reaching beyond eastern Anatolia into major Iranian provinces, and he shifted his government from Amid to Tabriz. The city was a recognized seat of authority through several successive dynasties; occupying it linked the new ruler to a potent regional political tradition. The enlarged realm provided additional tax resources and opportunities for diplomacy, but it was not governed simply by moving the same confederate command practices eastward. Persian officials, local elites, religious establishments, and provincial governments had to be incorporated into a wider system.

The events of 1467–1469 are therefore a useful test of the distinction between winning an empire and governing one. Uzun Hasan's military leadership overcame Jahan Shah and Abu Sa'id, yet his future depended on maintaining relations among Bayandur kinsmen, tribal chiefs, urban administrators, and regional populations. His victories removed rival supreme claims, not political disagreement. They also brought the Aq Qoyunlu closer to the expanding Ottomans and generated new disputes over Anatolian corridors. A confederation formerly anchored in Diyarbakır now had to formulate its place as a sovereign Iranian-centered state. That change helps explain the importance of fiscal organization and diplomacy during the next decade.

Section 68.12

Uzun Hasan, Ottoman Rivalry, Venetian Diplomacy, and the Battle of Otlukbeli

Uzun Hasan's expanding state stood across routes connecting Iran with central and western Anatolia, where Mehmed II had already strengthened Ottoman authority. Their rivalry grew from competing interests in the Anatolian principalities, the Black Sea, and strategic frontier positions. The history of Trebizond illustrates how these interests crossed religious boundaries. Aq Qoyunlu leaders had cultivated dynastic ties with the Komnenian ruling family, and Uzun Hasan married Theodora, a Trebizond princess. Such arrangements served diplomatic and territorial purposes. The Ottoman conquest of Trebizond in 1461 removed a valued ally and demonstrated Ottoman capacity to deploy substantial forces and siege technology against a Black Sea polity. Uzun Hasan did not then undertake a direct major response.

Competition also developed around the Karamanids and other Anatolian rulers whose independence was threatened by Ottoman centralization. For the Aq Qoyunlu, maintaining influence to the west could help secure approaches to their original territories and preserve access to trading connections. For Mehmed II, subordinate principalities could invite interference and undermine the security of routes between central Anatolia and the eastern marches. Neither ruler was simply defending a timeless ethnic frontier. Both were attempting to convert relationships with local dynasties into larger territorial systems, and each could portray the other's intervention as illegitimate. Shifting alliances made frontier politics unstable even before the armies met.

Venice became interested in Uzun Hasan because an effective eastern opponent could distract Ottoman forces and weaken Ottoman pressure on Venetian possessions and commerce. Diplomatic missions, correspondence, and plans to supply weapons linked the Aq Qoyunlu court with an Italian maritime republic. But diplomatic agreement did not erase material obstacles. Arms had to be carried through difficult terrain and controlled ports; allies had to coordinate distant campaigns without reliable communication; promises made for one season might become impractical in another. Venetian travelers' accounts of the court are important evidence, but their descriptions were shaped by the political hopes and anxieties of outsiders seeking an anti-Ottoman coalition.

In 1473 the rivalry resulted in a major campaign culminating at Otlukbeli in eastern Anatolia. Ottoman forces prevailed over Uzun Hasan's army. Artillery and firearms provided advantages, particularly within the organized Ottoman military establishment, yet it is too simple to describe the encounter as a pure contest between modern guns and obsolete cavalry. Leadership, tactical positioning, differences among participating contingents, terrain, and the organization of forces all contributed. Turkmen mounted troops remained formidable in other settings. Technology operated through institutions able to deploy, supply, and protect it, not as an independent force that made historical outcomes inevitable.

The defeat curtailed Uzun Hasan's capacity for further westward expansion. It also constrained the diplomatic strategy that had depended on coordination with the Ottomans' European enemies. But it did not cause the immediate dissolution of the Aq Qoyunlu empire. Uzun Hasan continued to govern a large Iranian realm until 1478, and Tabriz remained a significant center of authority. The government had to adjust its priorities: expansion against the Ottomans became more difficult, while maintaining central power over Iranian provinces and confederate chiefs remained possible. Students should distinguish a major strategic check from a terminal state collapse.

Otlukbeli also exposes the broader regional transformation. The Ottomans were developing more centralized military and administrative capabilities, while the Aq Qoyunlu were attempting to reconcile tribal political expectations with the needs of governing an extensive agrarian state. Both remained adaptable; both had internal tensions. Venetian diplomacy reveals a wider Mediterranean dimension to an ostensibly eastern Anatolian conflict. The battle should therefore be placed within state-building processes rather than treated merely as the triumph of one sovereign's army over another. Later Ottoman-Safavid competition would develop across some of these same routes, but that future was not predetermined in 1473.

Section 68.13

Tribal Confederation, Dynastic Authority, and the Structure of Turkmen Government

The governments of the Qara Qoyunlu and Aq Qoyunlu were built around dynastic households and military confederations, but neither institution can be equated with the modern idea of a centralized state. The ruler possessed substantial authority over war, diplomacy, appointments, and the distribution of resources. At the same time, leading chiefs commanded their own followers and expected consultation and reward. The Aq Qoyunlu council of amirs and tribal leaders could influence decisions about campaigns and succession. A ruler who ignored the council's military weight risked rebellion or withdrawal of support. Such limits were not evidence that there was no government; they describe a political order in which bargaining was part of sovereign power.

Military forces derived from several sources. Confederated groups supplied mounted warriors and horses, sometimes organized around kinship and patronage. Rulers also maintained household troops and guards more directly attached to their persons. Provincial commanders could mobilize men and resources from territories assigned to their care. The precise balance varied by reign, and descriptions written for one dynasty cannot automatically be applied to the other. Military leadership involved promises of pasture access, revenue, plunder, protection, and status. Victory strengthened a ruler's capacity to fulfill these promises; defeat could reveal how conditional the coalition's allegiance had been.

Dynastic princes were important instruments of government. Appointment of a son, brother, or cousin to Baghdad, Diyarbakır, or another strategic district connected a region to the ruling house while reducing the need for constant supervision from the capital. Yet a prince with military followers and provincial revenue might become a rival claimant. The conflicts involving Pir Budaq under Jahan Shah and Jahangir under the early Aq Qoyunlu illustrate this danger. Recurrent succession wars were not merely personal failings of ambitious men. They resulted from the interaction of family claims with a political system that gave commanders and regional constituencies real power to support competing heirs.

Territorial expansion widened the number of institutions a dynasty had to coordinate. Tabriz, Baghdad, Shiraz, and other cities possessed existing officials, judges, merchants, and religious foundations. Confederal chiefs did not necessarily have the administrative expertise to replace these institutions, while local officials could remain useful across political transitions. The employment of Persian bureaucrats was therefore a practical response to government on a larger scale. It also created friction. A chief who expected autonomous access to revenue might oppose reforms proposed by a vizier; a prince whose authority rested on a provincial household might distrust a central official. The ruler's household mediated rather than eliminated these competing claims.

Legitimacy drew on several repertoires. Bayandur genealogies linked the Aq Qoyunlu with Oghuz heroic tradition; Islamic titles and patronage spoke to scholarly and urban audiences; recognition from an external sovereign could offer temporary advantage on a contested frontier. Coinage, inscriptions, ceremonies, and correspondence advertised claims that still required enforcement. The rhetorical figure of an all-powerful sultan in a court chronicle should be compared with evidence of councils, bargains, and repeated resistance. Nor did the use of Persian administrative language imply that Turkmen identity vanished. Different forms of authority coexisted within one court.

These states therefore challenge a rigid contrast between tribe and state. Confederate organization provided the armed capacity through which territory could be won, but administration and trade were necessary to hold it. Conversely, an elaborate bureaucracy could not by itself ensure that mounted chiefs would obey. Under Jahan Shah and Uzun Hasan the tension grew sharper as government expanded beyond its original highland base. Recognizing this dual structure helps explain both the considerable achievements of the dynasties and their vulnerability after the deaths of strong rulers. The same institutions that sustained expansion could become the resources of rival factions during a succession crisis.

Section 68.14

Persian Bureaucracy, Landholding, Taxation, and Fiscal Reform

Government in a large Iranian territory required the repeated conversion of agricultural production and commercial exchange into money, supplies, or legally assigned rights. The Qara Qoyunlu and Aq Qoyunlu drew on administrative methods inherited through the Seljuk, Ilkhanid, Jalayirid, and Timurid periods. Persian was prominent in fiscal writing, correspondence, and historical narrative, and trained secretaries helped rulers govern districts whose populations did not share the military confederation's language or way of life. The employment of viziers and accountants was not a cosmetic addition to tribal command. It enabled the management of diverse revenue sources and the coordination of provincial authorities across substantial distances.

Landholding and fiscal arrangements were complicated by grants known as soyurghal or suyurghal. Depending on their scope, these might convey revenue rights, tax exemption, local administrative latitude, or special judicial privilege to military households or religious figures. They were not simply gifts of privately owned land in the modern sense. Large grants could give powerful beneficiaries considerable autonomy; smaller ones might support scholars or charitable interests without a corresponding military obligation. From the court's standpoint, extensive exemptions reduced ordinary access to revenue. From a beneficiary's standpoint, privileges could provide predictable resources and protection from changing tax officials. Conflict arose when rulers attempted to alter their terms.

Uzun Hasan is associated with efforts to regulate fiscal practice through measures remembered under the name Qanunname-i Hasan Padishah. These regulations belonged to a longer regional concern with standardizing assessments and limiting arbitrary exactions, but their surviving transmission and later use require careful interpretation. A legal formula's existence does not prove that taxes were collected everywhere according to it. Provincial governors, chiefs, clerical households, and merchants had different capacities to resist or negotiate. Reform might reduce one burden while transferring costs elsewhere. The distinction between legislative intention and actual practice is essential to any assessment of economic policy.

Trade taxation added a further layer. The tamgha, often associated with duties on merchandise and urban commerce, could be an important revenue source, though collection rights might be assigned to local holders of privilege. Customs houses, roads, and city gates were points where administrative authority became tangible to merchants. Excessive tolls could discourage movement; exemptions could attract favored groups while diminishing treasury income. The state needed resources for cavalry, guards, diplomatic missions, and royal buildings, but heavy demands could undermine the agricultural and commercial activities supporting those same expenditures. The fiscal system was a negotiated balance of competing interests rather than a single uniform rate imposed from Tabriz.

Under Yaqub, the official Qazi Isa Savaji undertook a more aggressive reform program. It sought to strengthen central authority by challenging some tax privileges and grants, including those enjoyed by religious dignitaries. The reformer's argument could appeal to Islamic legal categories while opposing practices associated with Mongol precedent, yet it was not simply a conflict between religion and secular power. Many scholars possessed material interests in existing grants; rulers might support religious institutions while contesting the economic privileges of individual beneficiaries. Measures against the tamgha and smaller soyurghal holdings affected different constituencies in different ways. Resistance grew partly because reform altered established arrangements for sharing state income.

The fiscal evidence provides an unusually clear view of the confederation's transformation. The political challenge was no longer only to attract cavalry and defend highland passages, but to maintain an administrative structure capable of financing government across Iran. Reforms could make central rule more plausible in principle while provoking coalitions capable of undermining it in practice. The fate of Qazi Isa's program after Yaqub's death in 1490 demonstrates these limits. Economic history, then, is indispensable to the explanation of political instability: changes in tax exemption and revenue control could matter as much as victory or defeat on a battlefield.

Section 68.15

Trade Routes, Urban Markets, Silk, and the Eastern Mediterranean Economy

The territories controlled by the Turkmen dynasties linked important commercial regions. Tabriz stood between Iranian interior markets, the Caucasus, and routes toward Anatolia; Diyarbakır connected the upper Tigris with the Euphrates and Anatolian passages; Mosul and Baghdad offered access to northern and central Mesopotamia. Routes through Erzurum and other highland centers could lead toward Black Sea and Mediterranean commerce. Trade did not require one enduring empire to exist, but predictable government and safe passage could reduce costs. Merchants therefore followed political events closely. A shift in the authority commanding a mountain pass or collecting dues at a city gate might change the profitability of an otherwise familiar journey.

Silk was significant in exchange between Iranian producers and foreign markets, but it was not the only commodity involved. Textiles, livestock, wool, metal goods, grain, luxury objects, and regional craft products moved through overlapping networks. The scale and direction of each trade varied by ecology and political conditions. A pastoral community might sell animals or wool and purchase grain and manufactured items; a town could process materials arriving from surrounding villages; an international merchant could combine imported goods with local products. The evidence is too uneven to assume that every caravan carried the same goods or passed through one fixed corridor. Commerce was a set of adaptable networks, not a single road on a map.

The political authorities sought to profit through customs duties, tolls, market supervision, and their ability to make trade routes safer. Yet a ruler's military needs could also threaten exchange. Armies required transport animals, provisions, and passage; repeated requisitions discouraged local producers and merchants. Negotiated privileges could protect particular communities but create grievances among competitors. The location of Tabriz made it attractive to rival dynasties precisely because administration and commerce there predated each of them. Its value could survive a change of ruler, though warfare might interrupt traffic and unsettle confidence. The history of trade therefore shows both continuity across dynastic replacement and sensitivity to political disorder.

Venice's interest in Uzun Hasan should be read partly through this commercial landscape. Venetian authorities sought partners against Ottoman expansion and maintained detailed diplomatic concerns about eastern Mediterranean access. An alliance with a strong ruler in Iran could promise strategic pressure on the Ottoman eastern frontier as well as opportunities for exchange. Yet political correspondence did not by itself ensure that goods or weapons could travel securely from European ports into the mountains of Anatolia. Actual supply routes, control of coastal positions, and the willingness of intermediary rulers mattered. The failure of plans to coordinate effectively against Mehmed II in 1473 demonstrates the difference between diplomatic vision and logistical capacity.

Trade also connected communities that rulers sometimes portrayed as separate. Armenian merchants, Muslim merchants, local Christian producers, and Turkmen intermediaries could interact at markets and caravan stopping points. The surviving record often describes these relationships through taxes, complaints, or court patronage rather than through ordinary conversations. That limitation matters. It is easy to infer total prosperity from an impressive urban building or universal collapse from a chronicle of war. Neither is a reliable measure of what happened to households across an entire region. Evaluating commercial life requires comparing political sources with material evidence and the institutional persistence of towns.

The central analytical lesson is that territorial rule and commercial geography reinforced one another without becoming identical. The confederations sought roads and cities because these supplied revenue and helped connect their dispersed territories. Their military strength could protect commerce, but their rivalries could also raise its risks. Control of Tabriz marked Uzun Hasan's transformation from a frontier leader into an Iranian sovereign partly because it connected him to administrative and commercial resources far beyond Diyarbakır. Studying the routes alongside armies and dynastic marriages explains why economic geography was an active part of regional state formation.

Section 68.16

Armenian, Kurdish, Arab, Persian, and Turkmen Communities: Everyday Life on the Frontier

The history of the Qara Qoyunlu and Aq Qoyunlu cannot be adequately written only through the careers of kings and chiefs. Most inhabitants of their territories lived in households whose immediate concerns involved land, animals, wages, water, rents, trade, and religious institutions. An Armenian cultivator in an upland valley, a Kurdish pastoral family moving between seasonal grazing grounds, and a Persian-speaking artisan in Tabriz encountered political authority in different ways. Their lives could be affected by the same dynastic war, but not necessarily in the same year or through the same mechanisms. A battle might change the collector of dues at a village, interrupt an annual migration, or bring extra demand for provisions to a city marketplace.

Rural production varied across the region. In well-watered valleys, cultivators maintained fields and irrigation works; elsewhere households combined crops, animals, and seasonal labor. Pastoral populations depended on movement through routes that military or fiscal authorities could restrict. Conflicts over pasture could therefore have economic consequences no less serious than disputed ownership of cultivated land. Local chiefs, village headmen, and representatives of religious establishments sometimes mediated demands imposed by more distant sovereigns. The resulting arrangements were unequal and could be coercive, yet they provided ways for communities to negotiate survival through recurrent dynastic changes. Not every episode of violence led to permanent depopulation, and not every treaty brought local security.

Urban life introduced different relationships. Merchants and craftspeople operated in markets where demand depended on agriculture, long-distance exchange, and courtly consumption. Revenue officials and customs collectors were immediately visible agents of government. A change in the ruler of Tabriz or Baghdad might alter the allocation of tax revenues or patronage without removing local expertise. Families connected with scholarship, the judiciary, or trade could maintain standing across successive courts. At the same time, lower-paid laborers and servants were especially vulnerable to price increases, military requisitions, and disruption of supply. Enslaved people also formed part of household and military economies, although the sources rarely record their experiences in their own words.

Religious diversity was substantial. Armenian Apostolic and Syriac Christian institutions preserved communal records, clerical leadership, monasteries, and networks of land and property. Muslim communities included participants in different legal, scholarly, devotional, and Sufi traditions. Kurdish, Arab, Persian-speaking, and Turkmen populations were not religiously or economically uniform. A ruler's Islamic proclamations did not determine every local interaction with Christian institutions. Protection, taxation, negotiation, episodic discrimination, or coercion could occur in different places and under different officials. Detailed community histories belong to Chapter 69, but the governments examined here cannot be understood without the people upon whom they relied for revenues and services.

Women's roles are particularly difficult to recover from court-centered writings, which usually devote more attention to the families of rulers than to those of agricultural workers. Elite women exercised influence through marriage alliances, property rights, endowments, and the organization of dynastic households. The foundation associated with Khatun Jan Begum in Tabriz provides unusually tangible evidence of female patronage. Such evidence should not be generalized to every woman in the region. Household labor, child care, craft production, market exchanges, and inheritance arrangements would have shaped women's lives far beyond the small number named in chronicles. Historical method requires acknowledgment of that imbalance, not an assumption that unrecorded work did not exist.

Local evidence can broaden the narrative. Armenian manuscript colophons sometimes record the circumstances of writing, political insecurity, taxation, or the identity of patrons. Inscriptions and religious foundations illuminate property and institutional survival. Coins and administrative documents can show rulers' claims or fiscal practices without describing all their effects. Read together, these materials resist the tendency of royal chronicles to make every event a story about competing men at court. The central theme is not that ordinary life was separate from high politics. It is that military change became historically significant through its consequences for communities whose choices, labor, and negotiations sustained the states themselves.

Division V — Religion, Cultural Patronage, Reform, and the Transition toward Safavid Iran

Sections 68.17–68.20

Section 68.17

Sunni Institutions, Sufi Networks, Shia Devotion, and the Safavid Order of Ardabil

Religious life in the Iranian-Anatolian frontier was varied and cannot be reduced to a single confessional label for each Turkmen dynasty. Sunni legal and scholarly institutions were important in cities and courts, while Sufi lodges, shrines, charismatic teachers, and forms of devotion to Ali and the Prophet's family circulated among multiple communities. The categories later associated with sharply defined Ottoman-Safavid confessional rivalry were not always applied in the same way in the fifteenth century. Historians must therefore distinguish formal legal affiliation, personal devotion, popular ritual, dynastic patronage, and the political claims of religious movements. These were overlapping fields of practice, not synonyms.

The Qara Qoyunlu are sometimes described as Shia and the Aq Qoyunlu as Sunni, but that opposition is too neat. Expressions of reverence for the Prophet's family did not always imply adherence to later institutional Twelver Shiism. Conversely, official Sunni patronage did not preclude alliances with Sufi figures or involvement in forms of religious devotion that later authorities might regard with suspicion. Court-sponsored buildings can indicate a ruler's desire for Islamic legitimacy while leaving many aspects of ordinary religious belief uncertain. The same dynasty could adopt different policies toward religious teachers as its political circumstances changed. A confessional history has to emerge from evidence rather than be imposed backward from the sixteenth century.

The Safaviyya order at Ardabil developed from the legacy of Shaykh Safi al-Din and accumulated spiritual prestige, followers, and material interests over generations. By the fifteenth century its leaders operated within political networks extending beyond one shrine or town. Shaykh Junayd pursued a more assertive military and political course and encountered resistance from Jahan Shah, whose authority was threatened by an increasingly active religious following. The relationship cannot be reduced to an abstract theological dispute. A Sufi leader with mobile armed supporters could become a rival political center; a ruler seeking order in Azerbaijan had to respond to both the religious appeal and the practical military capacity of that network.

Junayd developed ties with Uzun Hasan's family through marriage, creating a dynastic connection between Safavid leadership and the Aq Qoyunlu house. His successor Shaykh Haydar similarly drew on these political relationships. Such alliances could benefit both sides. A respected religious family could offer legitimacy and supporters to a confederate ruler; dynastic protection could give a Sufi movement access to routes, marriage connections, and opportunities for military recruitment. But the relationship was never certain to remain cooperative. As the Safaviyya attracted an armed following across Turkmen populations, its ambitions could become incompatible with the territorial authority of the Aq Qoyunlu monarchy.

The conflict under Yaqub culminated in Haydar's death during campaigning in 1488. The episode illustrates a general process by which devotional networks could acquire a military dimension and provoke opposition from rulers who had once supported related families. The evolution of the Safavid movement involved religious teaching, personal allegiance, dynastic marriage, competition for followers, and military organization. None of these factors alone explains its success. The later Qizilbash coalitions should be studied with attention to the political setting established by confederate service and rivalry, rather than treated as timeless religious formations waiting for a sovereign to appear.

The significance of Ardabil in this chapter is preparatory. The details of Shah Ismail's mobilization and the formation of the Safavid state belong primarily to Chapters 70 and 76. Chapter 68 asks instead how religious and political networks interacted before that outcome was known. A family that had supplied spiritual authority and alliances eventually became a competitor for sovereignty, while divisions among Aq Qoyunlu princes created opportunities for its followers. The careful interpretation avoids two opposite errors: imagining that Safavid success was inevitable from the order's origin, or treating religious commitment as merely a disguise for political calculation. Both devotion and power mattered, in ways that shifted over time.

Section 68.18

Tabriz, Persianate Literature, Architecture, and Cultural Patronage

Tabriz became a major center of Turkmen dynastic power because its institutions and symbolic importance preceded the confederations that governed it. The city had served Ilkhanid and Jalayirid rulers; its routes linked Azerbaijan with the Caucasus, Anatolia, and the Iranian plateau. Its literary, scholarly, commercial, and religious life gave rulers opportunities to present themselves as protectors and patrons rather than only conquerors. Under Jahan Shah, and later under Aq Qoyunlu leadership, this urban setting fostered a court culture combining Turkmen political identities with established Persianate expectations of cultivated sovereignty. A change in ruling house did not require the disappearance of the city's scribes, craftsmen, or religious institutions.

Jahan Shah participated in poetry under the pen name Haqiqi and supported artistic and scholarly activity. The significance of this participation is not that one ruler's verses reveal the private beliefs of all his subjects, but that literary accomplishment could function as a language of legitimacy. Persian and Turkic literary environments interacted within courts whose military leaders did not necessarily share the language or training of every secretary and poet. Manuscripts, calligraphy, textiles, ceramics, and architectural decoration were made through the work of skilled specialists. Patronage could generate employment and institutional continuity, even when the resources supporting it derived partly from taxation or conquest elsewhere.

The Blue Mosque in Tabriz provides an unusually concrete case. Known also as the Masjed-e Kabud and associated with the Mozaffariya complex, it was completed in 1465 under Qara Qoyunlu rule. An inscription at the portal records the date 25 October 1465 according to the conversion used in the relevant architectural study. The foundation's endowment was established by Khatun Jan Begum, wife of Jahan Shah, underscoring the institutional agency of a woman of the ruling household. Treating the structure simply as a monument built by the sultan would obscure the documented role of its patron and the material provisions supporting religious activity there.

The complex extended beyond the surviving mosque and mausoleum. Contemporary and later records describe facilities associated with a Sufi institution, water management, garden space, and nearby commercial property; some of the former buildings no longer survive. Properties held by members of the patron's family and revenues directed toward a foundation show how architecture connected household wealth with urban economy. The monument's tilework also reflects artistic relationships with Timurid and Ottoman architectural traditions. These observations support an account of a shared world of craftsmen, patterns, and patronage across dynastic boundaries. They do not prove that all artists or building materials moved freely or that religious institutions were identical from one court to another.

The political transition from Qara Qoyunlu to Aq Qoyunlu rule did not terminate the complex's history. Later activity and modifications under the new dynasty reveal the possibility of cultural continuity despite military replacement. The building's surviving fabric has also been altered by subsequent damage, earthquakes, and restoration; what can be inspected today is not an untouched fifteenth-century object. Architectural history requires comparison between inscriptions, material remains, historical descriptions, and modern conservation. A dated portal provides more secure chronological evidence than a picturesque legend, but it does not by itself reveal every function the patron intended.

The broader lesson extends beyond one mosque. Foundations could support teaching, worship, hospitality, and family commemoration; cultural patronage could project sovereign legitimacy and bind a court to local elites. Women, religious scholars, administrators, artisans, and merchants participated in the making of these institutions. The Qara Qoyunlu and Aq Qoyunlu should therefore not be presented as merely destructive interruptions between celebrated dynasties. Their cultural record is unevenly preserved, but its surviving evidence demonstrates active engagement with regional literary and artistic traditions. The historian's task is to interpret that achievement alongside military coercion and fiscal demands, not to use it as a substitute for explaining either.

Section 68.19

Yaqub, Religious Policy, Fiscal Reform, and the Struggle for Central Authority, 1478–1490

Uzun Hasan's death in 1478 opened a new succession conflict. His son Khalil briefly ruled but could not preserve the coalition, and Yaqub established himself as the principal Aq Qoyunlu ruler later that year. Under Yaqub, the court at Tabriz retained substantial prestige, and his reign is often remembered as a period of relative stability compared with the fighting that preceded and followed it. Stability, however, did not mean the absence of tension. Territorial expansion had created powerful military beneficiaries, privileged religious households, and regional officers with accustomed claims on revenue. These interests were not easily subordinated to a central government attempting to increase control over finance and appointments.

Yaqub's government employed Persian administrative specialists, among whom Qazi Isa Savaji became particularly influential. As a tutor and senior official, he supported measures intended to strengthen central authority and modify tax practice. The reform program challenged privileges that diverted revenues away from the treasury, especially certain soyurghal grants. Some were enjoyed by religious dignitaries and did not require regular military service. From the court's perspective these grants limited its fiscal capacity; from the beneficiaries' perspective they were longstanding rights essential to institutional support and family standing. The dispute was therefore over resources, political authority, and the legitimate basis of exemptions.

Reformers also engaged with categories of Islamic law and Mongol-derived fiscal custom. Measures directed at the tamgha could be presented as removing an objectionable tax, yet the effects depended on who had previously collected it and what revenue would replace it. Qazi Isa's program was not simply a clash between religion and state. Some religious figures resisted because their economic privileges were threatened; other learned officials participated in government. A centralizing policy could borrow religious language while redistributing income from particular clerical households to royal administration. To understand the conflict, one must ask which beneficiaries stood to gain or lose, rather than assume that all ulama or all tribal chiefs held one position.

Yaqub also confronted the growing political assertiveness of the Safavid order. Shaykh Haydar's armed following, supported by networks that transcended the territory of one state, created a potential military rival. The movement's existing marriage links with the Bayandur dynasty did not guarantee that it would remain politically subordinate. Haydar died in 1488 in circumstances connected with conflict in the Caucasus and opposition from Aq Qoyunlu authorities. The event demonstrates that patronage and kinship could give way to repression when spiritual followers became competitors for control over men and territory. Religious policy and dynastic security were therefore intertwined.

The court's cultural patronage during Yaqub's reign must also be considered alongside these conflicts. Learned figures and literary activity continued; Tabriz remained a significant center for government and art. An account that describes the period solely as a failing state would miss the resources and institutional ambitions that reformers were attempting to consolidate. Yet the political system remained vulnerable to factional pressure. The ruler's success depended on relationships among princes, chiefs, and officials whose positions could change quickly if he died. The ability to issue reforms was not equivalent to possessing an apparatus that would enforce them after the ruler's protection disappeared.

Yaqub died in December 1490. His death reopened succession struggles, and Qazi Isa's political protection vanished; the reformer was killed soon afterward. Much of the centralizing program did not survive. This outcome exposes both the ambition and the limits of fifteenth-century Aq Qoyunlu government. Yaqub could patronize religious institutions, oversee fiscal measures, and restrain some rivals during his lifetime, but he could not eliminate the competing centers of authority. The subsequent dynastic breakdown was not caused by a single unpopular tax policy. It reflected the interaction of succession uncertainty, confederate military politics, privileged revenue holders, and a religious movement capable of mobilizing support beyond the court.

Section 68.20

The Fragmentation of the Aq Qoyunlu and the Political Setting of Safavid Expansion, 1490–1508

The years following Yaqub's death brought a rapid succession of princes and political coalitions. His young son Baysunghur was initially elevated under the protection of powerful chiefs, but rivals contested the arrangements almost immediately. Rustam gained Tabriz with support from influential military factions; other leaders sought to restore or substitute different branches of the ruling family. The chronology of these claims is complicated because a prince might be acknowledged in one province while a rival controlled another. Rather than imagining a single unified monarchy experiencing a sequence of orderly accessions, the historian must examine shifting regional centers of power and the chiefs who could make or abandon a claimant.

The contest continued through Rustam's displacement and the short-lived rule of Ahmad, who returned with connections to Ottoman territory. His attempt to revive centralizing measures encountered resistance from confederate commanders and ended in his violent defeat in 1497. Provincial leaders then advanced different candidates in the west and the Iranian interior. Alvand, Murad, and other claimants held territories and allegiances that did not form a stable settlement. The same structural problem seen in earlier generations had intensified: confederate chiefs could obtain grants, appointments, and political advantage through competing princes, while none of those princes possessed sufficient independent resources to enforce durable unity.

The Safavid movement became increasingly important within this fractured environment. Following the deaths and persecution of earlier leaders, the young Ismail was sheltered by supporters and eventually mobilized an armed following. His authority rested partly on inherited spiritual charisma, partly on dynastic and political connections, and partly on the ability of his movement to attract Turkmen warriors dissatisfied with the existing order. Explaining this growth as an automatic consequence of religious belief would omit the opportunities opened by Aq Qoyunlu civil war. Explaining it simply as political opportunism would neglect the devotional loyalties that made the Safavid leadership distinctive. The two dimensions reinforced each other.

In 1501 Ismail defeated Aq Qoyunlu opposition and entered Tabriz, beginning the establishment of Safavid sovereign rule. The city's possession carried immense symbolic and administrative weight, but the political transformation did not instantly extend across every region formerly subject to the Aq Qoyunlu. Further campaigns brought central and southern Iranian provinces under Safavid control during the early 1500s. Different surviving princes maintained positions or sought outside alliances. Baghdad, Diyarbakır, and other territories followed their own sequences of transfer, and the broad transition continued through 1508. Precision matters because the fall of one capital is not equivalent to the simultaneous end of all provincial government.

The last effective Aq Qoyunlu sovereign authority in the principal Iranian and Mesopotamian territories disappeared through these campaigns, while members of the dynasty and confederate groups survived under changing conditions. The former ruler Murad did not simply vanish in 1508; he continued seeking support and was killed in 1514. Some Turkmen lineages entered the Safavid political system, including formations associated with the later Qizilbash, while other communities maintained local identities. Administrative personnel and established fiscal practices also continued to matter. A new ruling dynasty could attempt to refashion religion and sovereignty without eliminating the social and material inheritance upon which it depended.

The chapter concludes, therefore, with transformation rather than a simple replacement of one ruling people by another. The Qara Qoyunlu and Aq Qoyunlu had created systems of territorial rule from confederate military alliances, drawn upon Persian administrative traditions, shaped the commercial geography of Azerbaijan and Iraq, and cultivated religious networks whose political consequences they could not fully control. Their rivalries created opportunities for the Safavids, but they did not make the Safavid outcome inevitable. Chapter 69 follows the local communities that persisted amid these changes; Chapters 70 and 76 examine the military and religious mobilization through which Ismail established a new Iranian dynasty. The world inherited by the Safavids was troubled, but it was neither institutionally empty nor culturally barren.

Historical Debate — Were These Tribal Confederations or Territorial States?

One interpretation emphasizes the short-lived political coalitions of military chiefs, recurrent succession crises, and the frequent inability of rulers to enforce their wishes across provinces. The events after Qara Yusuf, Qara Osman, and Yaqub lend this interpretation considerable weight. The opposing emphasis highlights real territorial government, urban taxation, Persian administration, diplomatic correspondence, religious endowments, and programs of fiscal reform. Uzun Hasan’s rule from Tabriz and the survival of administrative institutions across dynastic replacement demonstrate capacities that cannot be captured by the label tribe alone.

These accounts need not be mutually exclusive. Confederal military organization and sophisticated urban government could coexist, and their relationship changed with the territorial scale of rule. Compare Woods’s account of clan, confederation, and empire with Roemer’s and Minorsky’s treatment of governmental structures. Ask whether the strongest measure of a state is the durability of a ruling dynasty, the regular collection of revenues, the authority of laws, or the persistence of institutions after conquest.

  2. Explain what the succession crises reveal and what they do not prove.
  3. Discuss whether the Safavid takeover indicates institutional collapse or political inheritance.

Continuities included Persian fiscal offices, commercial routes, urban judges, religious foundations, and the strategic importance of Tabriz and Baghdad. Changes included the rise of confederate military households to sovereign office, the transfer of capitals and revenue privileges among dynasties, the expansion of the Aq Qoyunlu toward Iranian interior provinces, and the religious-political mobilization of the Safaviyya.

Cause and Effect — Building, Expanding, and Losing a State

Long-term foundations: post-Ilkhanid fragmentation, seasonal pastoral economies, and the survival of urban administrative institutions. Immediate catalysts: major campaign victories, successful dynastic coalition building, and the capture of established capitals. Structural tensions: competing claims of chiefs, princes and holders of fiscal privilege. Results: new territorial government and literary patronage, but repeated vulnerability during succession. Final transformation: Safavid political-military mobilization amid Aq Qoyunlu fragmentation.

Everyday Life and Social History — Beyond the Court

A household in an upland settlement needed pasture rights, access to water, and protection for seasonal travel. In a farming village, the same year might be shaped by harvest conditions, rent, taxation, and requisitions for passing troops. Urban artisans and shopkeepers depended on commercial demand and security; merchants navigated tolls and shifting alliances. Religious institutions maintained property, education, and charitable activity. Court chronicles rarely record ordinary women or enslaved workers at comparable length to rulers, so local inscriptions, colophons, and endowments are essential checks on elite-centered narratives.

Chapter Summary

The Qara Qoyunlu and Aq Qoyunlu emerged in a frontier shaped by the collapse of Ilkhanid central authority, the persistence of Persianate institutions, and the political importance of Turkmen armed followings. Their governments linked upland pastures and military alliances to towns, agriculture, taxation, and long-distance commerce. From the beginning they were neither simply pastoral tribes nor uniform bureaucratic monarchies.

Qara Yusuf’s recovery of Tabriz in 1406, his victory over Timurid forces in 1408, and the later acquisition of Baghdad established Qara Qoyunlu regional authority. Repeated conflicts with Shah Rukh and among Qara Yusuf’s successors demonstrated the vulnerability of confederate succession. Under Jahan Shah the dynasty extended its reach into much of western and central Iran, but the failure to hold Herat and the revolt of Pir Budaq exposed limits to its territorial integration.

The Aq Qoyunlu developed from a separate Bayandur-led confederation associated with Diyarbakır. Qara Yuluq Osman supplied an early territorial foundation, and Uzun Hasan overcame dynastic rivals to become its leading ruler. His victories over Jahan Shah in 1467 and Abu Saʿid in 1469 transformed the Aq Qoyunlu into an Iranian-centered state ruling from Tabriz. Defeat by the Ottomans at Otlukbeli in 1473 restricted western ambitions without immediately dissolving the empire.

Urban and rural society remained crucial to both powers. Persian-speaking administrators managed tax records and office; soyurghal grants distributed revenues and privileges; commerce joined Tabriz and Baghdad to the Caucasus and Anatolia. Armenian, Kurdish, Arab, Syriac, Persian, and Turkmen communities lived under uneven combinations of coercion, negotiation, institutional continuity, and economic adaptation. Royal patronage produced durable evidence such as the Blue Mosque of Tabriz, whose endowment was associated with Khatun Jan Begum.

Yaqub and Qazi Isa attempted to strengthen central administration, but privileged revenue holders and military chiefs resisted. The Safaviyya grew into a politically consequential religious and military movement, and Aq Qoyunlu succession conflicts after 1490 opened opportunities for Ismail. Safavid authority spread through 1501–1508, though rival claimants and dynastic lines survived in altered forms. The outcome was a transformation of institutions and alliances, not the sudden arrival of an entirely new society.

Chapter Reflection

The history of the Turkmen confederations shows that a state could be built out of mobile military alliances without abandoning the skills, institutions, and productive landscapes of settled societies. Qara Yusuf, Jahan Shah and Uzun Hasan used different combinations of conquest, negotiation, patronage and administration. Their political projects had genuine achievements and repeated vulnerabilities. The apparent contrast between nomadic power and urban order becomes less persuasive when viewed from the perspective of the officials and households who sustained both.

The Safavid rise was shaped by the world the Turkmen dynasties had created: a region of rival princes, powerful chiefs, Persian administrative continuity, religious networks, and interconnected markets. The chapter also demonstrates the value of sources that speak from different positions. A Venetian envoy, a court historian, a religious foundation, and a village manuscript do not tell the same story, and none can replace the others. Chapter 69 follows local communities within this changing frontier; Chapters 70 and 76 address its subsequent imperial transformation.
