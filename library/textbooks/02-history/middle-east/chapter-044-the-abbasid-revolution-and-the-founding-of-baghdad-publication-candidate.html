Part XI — The Abbasid Imperial System

The Abbasid Revolution and the Founding of Baghdad

A History of the Middle East: From Ancient Civilizations to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

In October 775 the second Abbasid caliph, Abū Jaʿfar al-Manṣūr, died on the road to Mecca, having set out on pilgrimage from a capital that had not existed when his dynasty came to power a quarter-century earlier. The city he left behind, Madīnat al-Salām, "the City of Peace," had been founded on the west bank of the Tigris in 762 and was already drawing grain, merchants, officials, soldiers, and scholars from Iraq, Iran, Syria, and beyond. Few capitals in the history of the region were so clearly the product of a single political decision. Yet the decision made sense only because of everything that had happened in the years between the revolution and the funeral.

Chapter 43 traced the long crisis of Umayyad authority, the Second Fitna and its aftermath, the clandestine mobilization of the Hāshimiyya movement, the campaigns that carried black-bannered armies from Khurāsān into Iraq, and the defeat and death of the last Umayyad caliph, Marwān II, in 750. This chapter begins where that story ends. A revolution that has won is not yet a government. The victors still had to pay and discipline their soldiers, staff the provinces, deal with defeated Umayyad families and with supporters who had expected something different from the victory, and persuade the Muslim community that rule by the descendants of the Prophet's uncle al-ʿAbbās was legitimate, indeed that it was the fulfilment of what the revolution had promised.

The central argument of the chapter is that the Abbasids did more than replace one dynasty with another. In the quarter-century between 750 and 775 they redefined the basis of dynastic legitimacy, shifted the political center of the empire from Syria to Iraq and Iran, absorbed an eastern military and administrative constituency into the governing elite, and built a new capital that would remain the seat of the caliphate for most of the following five centuries. They also did so through conflict. Much of the story is one of consolidation against men who had helped make the dynasty: an Abbasid uncle, the powerful general Abū Muslim, rival claimants from the Prophet's family, and eastern movements whose expectations the new regime could not satisfy. Al-Manṣūr's reign is the hinge on which the chapter turns.

The chapter also takes up the problem of evidence, because the early Abbasid period is known mainly through narratives composed under the dynasty it describes. Al-Ṭabarī, al-Yaʿqūbī, al-Balādhurī, and the anonymous Akhbār al-dawla al-ʿabbāsiyya preserve vivid and sometimes dramatic accounts, but they were written or compiled a century or more after the events, within a political culture that had a strong interest in how the dynasty's origins were remembered. Coins, a small number of documents, the archaeology of sites such as Humayma in southern Jordan, and a contemporary Syriac chronicle supply independent checks. Throughout, the chapter distinguishes what the sources report from what historians can reasonably infer.

Geography, introduced in Chapter 2, is part of the explanation. The Tigris and Euphrates draw close to one another in central Iraq, and the point where Baghdad rose lay close to the narrowest stretch between them, where canals could link the rivers and where roads and boats converged from the Gulf, the Iranian plateau, the Jazīra, and Syria. Al-Manṣūr's choice of this site was a decision about political geography as much as about security. The chapter treats it in detail, including the circular design of the original foundation and the more irregular metropolis that grew around it.

This is not yet the Baghdad of the later translation movement, of Hārūn al-Rashīd, or of the great urban society described in Chapter 48. It is the Baghdad of its founders, a planned government enclosure with a palace, a mosque, guards, and offices, surrounded by suburbs and markets that were growing faster than the planners intended. Its society, its institutions, and its intellectual life are treated here only as far as is needed to explain the transition. Chapters 45 through 48 take up the fiscal and military system, the scholarly culture, the legal and theological traditions, and the everyday life of the mature caliphate.

A last point concerns terminology. "Abbasid" names a dynasty, but the coalition that brought it to power included Arab settlers from Khurāsān, Iranian soldiers and landholders, freed clients (mawālī), Kufan supporters, and members of the Prophet's wider clan. These groups did not share a single identity, and their interests diverged as soon as the fighting stopped. A history that treats the revolution as the triumph of "Persians over Arabs," or as the simple victory of one family, flattens a process in which the meaning of the new order was negotiated, and fought over, for a generation.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why the military victory of 750 did not by itself create a stable Abbasid government.
  2. Analyze the genealogical, religious, and political arguments by which the Abbasids claimed exclusive legitimacy, and compare them with rival Alid claims.
  3. Identify the groups that made up the revolutionary coalition and explain how their interests diverged after 750.
  4. Describe the reign of al-Saffāḥ and the early seats of Abbasid government in Iraq.
  5. Reconstruct al-Manṣūr's struggle against ʿAbd Allāh ibn ʿAlī, Abū Muslim, eastern rebels, and the Alid revolts of 762–763.
  6. Evaluate why the center of imperial power moved from Syria to Iraq and Iran, and what that shift meant for the army, bureaucracy, and court.
  7. Assess claims that an Iranian bureaucracy replaced an Arab one, using evidence for continuity and change in personnel and institutions.
  8. Describe the site, design, construction, and early growth of Baghdad, distinguishing the Round City from the wider metropolis.
  9. Evaluate how medieval texts, modern reconstructions, and archaeology each contribute to, and limit, knowledge of early Baghdad.
  10. Explain the limits of Abbasid authority in Iberia, North Africa, Syria, and the frontier zones.
  11. Analyze the succession politics that concentrated the caliphate in al-Manṣūr's line, and judge what had changed by 775.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why is a revolution's victory so often followed by conflict among its own supporters, and how does the early Abbasid state illustrate that pattern?
  2. On what grounds did the Abbasids claim to be the rightful leaders of the Muslim community, and why did rival claimants from the Prophet's family find those grounds unconvincing?
  3. Who actually composed the revolutionary coalition, and how should historians describe its ethnic, social, and religious makeup without anachronism?
  4. Why did al-Manṣūr consider it necessary to eliminate Abū Muslim, and what does the episode reveal about authority in the early dynasty?
  5. What combination of political, economic, and strategic reasons explains the move of the caliphate's center of gravity from Damascus to Iraq?
  6. How much of the Umayyad state survived under the Abbasids, and what was genuinely new?
  7. What can the texts and archaeology tell us about the design of the Round City, and where does knowledge give way to inference?
  8. Why did a capital that was planned as a controlled government enclosure grow into a commercial metropolis?

Historical Context

The Umayyad caliphate (661–750), examined in Chapters 41 and 42, had governed from Damascus through a Syrian-based army, an administration that gradually shifted from Greek and Persian to Arabic, and a ruling group drawn largely from the Arab settler elite and the Syrian garrisons. It had presided over the greatest territorial expansion in the history of the region, from Iberia to Sindh and Transoxiana. It was also a state under strain. Questions about who could rule, how non-Arab converts were to be taxed and honored, how the spoils and revenues of empire were to be distributed, and what religious authority the caliph possessed had remained unresolved since the First Fitna (Chapter 40). The Second Fitna and the revolts of the 740s turned those tensions into open civil war (Chapter 43).

Into that crisis came the Hāshimiyya, a movement that called for the rule of "an agreeable one from the family of Muḥammad" (al-riḍā min āl Muḥammad) without initially naming him. Its organizers, working from Kūfa and Khurāsān through a network of missionaries, found their most receptive audience among Arab settlers and local Iranian populations in the east, many of them aggrieved by the political order of the late Umayyad period. The movement's armies, led on the ground by Abū Muslim and Qaḥṭaba ibn Shabīb, defeated Umayyad forces in Iran and Iraq, and in late 749 Abū al-ʿAbbās was proclaimed caliph in Kūfa. Marwān II was killed in Egypt in 750. Chapter 43 narrated those events; this chapter assumes them.

Three aspects of the inherited situation shape the rest of the chapter. First, the revolution had been fought in the name of the Prophet's family, but the family was larger than the Abbasid branch, and Alid claimants, descendants of ʿAlī and Fāṭima, had real support. Second, the revolution's army was an eastern army, whose loyalties were to its commanders and its cause rather than automatically to the new caliph. Third, the political geography of the empire was open: Syria remained a hostile former heartland, al-Andalus and parts of North Africa were distant, and Iraq, where the new rulers established themselves, was the richest and most urbanized province but had its own factional politics. How the Abbasids managed these problems between 750 and 775 is the subject of what follows.

Division I

Revolutionary Victory and Abbasid Legitimacy

Section 44.1

Reconstructing the Early Abbasid State: Sources and Historical Problems

Anyone who studies the early Abbasid state works with a paradox. The period is richly narrated, but almost all of the narrative was composed or compiled after the dynasty had become established and by writers who knew how the story ended. The great compendium of Muḥammad ibn Jarīr al-Ṭabarī (d. 923) covers the years 750–775 in the volumes translated into English as The History of al-Ṭabarī, Volumes XXVII (the revolution's culmination), XXVIII (Abbasid Authority Affirmed), and XXIX (Al-Manṣūr and al-Mahdī). Al-Ṭabarī worked by collecting and arranging earlier reports, often giving several versions of the same event, and his sources included historians such as al-Madāʾinī, al-Wāqidī, and ʿUmar ibn Shabba, whose own works survive only in fragments or through him.

Other Arabic sources supplement him. Aḥmad ibn Abī Yaʿqūb al-Yaʿqūbī (active in the later ninth century) wrote both a universal history and a geographical work, the Kitāb al-buldān, whose description of Baghdad is the earliest systematic account of the city's layout. Aḥmad al-Balādhurī (d. c. 892) preserved biographical and genealogical material in his Ansāb al-ashrāf. The anonymous Akhbār al-dawla al-ʿabbāsiyya assembles traditions favorable to the dynasty's origins and is valuable partly because its bias is so legible. The Baghdad historian al-Khaṭīb al-Baghdādī (d. 1071) preserved earlier descriptions of the founding in his History of Baghdad, though writing three centuries after the event.

Non-Arabic and non-narrative evidence matters especially here because it can lie outside the dynasty's control. The Chronicle of Zuqnin, a Syriac chronicle associated with a monastery near Amid and completed in the 770s, gives a contemporary and sometimes bleak view of Abbasid rule in the Jazīra. Byzantine chroniclers such as Theophanes, writing in the early ninth century, preserve information on the frontier. Coins record caliphal names, mints, and dates. Archaeology at Ḥumayma in southern Jordan, where the Abbasid family had lived before the revolution, illuminates the dynasty's origins; at Baghdad itself the early city lies beneath the modern one and is largely inaccessible.

These conditions create specific interpretive problems. The Abbasid narrative stresses a patient, well-organized, divinely favored movement, so accounts of the revolution's origins tend to smooth over disagreement. Heroes and villains are sharply drawn: Abū Muslim appears as loyal servant and as arrogant threat, depending on the transmitter. Speeches attributed to caliphs are usually literary reconstructions, even when they preserve authentic themes. Numbers (of troops, workers, victims, expenses) are conventional and should not be taken literally. The chapter therefore signals source dependence wherever it matters and avoids presenting a single transmitted version as established fact.

Section 44.2

From Revolutionary Coalition to Imperial Government, 750

The defeat of Marwān II at the Greater Zāb in January 750, and his death in Egypt later that year, did not give the Abbasids a functioning state. What they had was an army, a political program, and control of Kūfa and much of Iraq. Governing an empire from the Atlantic coast of Africa to the Indus required filling the gap left by an Umayyad administration that was now discredited but not obsolete. Provincial governors needed to be named and, where necessary, sent with troops to take up their posts. Taxes had to be collected from provinces that had been disrupted by civil war. The soldiers who had won the revolution expected pay, land, and rank, and the new regime had to establish how they would be supported.

These problems were intertwined. The coalition was an alliance of convenience built around a shared enemy, and its component groups had different views of what victory should bring. Khurāsānī Arab settlers wanted a stake in government and a fairer share of revenues. Iranian participants wanted status and security. Kufan supporters expected influence in the city that had received the new caliph. Members of the Abbasid family, numerous and ambitious, expected governorships and commands. Members of the wider Hāshimite clan, including Alids, expected recognition for a movement that had spoken in the name of the whole family. No single arrangement could satisfy all of them.

The early Abbasids responded by dispersing family members and trusted commanders into provincial posts. Abū al-ʿAbbās's uncles and brothers were assigned to Syria, the Jazīra, Egypt, and Fārs, binding the great provinces to the ruling house. Abū Muslim kept the east. This created a system in which the Abbasid family and its principal general held the major governorships, and where the caliph's executive authority depended on the loyalty of men who had helped create him. It also created the structural problem that dominates the next several divisions: powerful governors, especially those with their own armies, could become rivals as readily as agents.

One should not read the early Abbasid period as simple improvisation. The revolutionary organization had trained cadres, written correspondence, and secretarial expertise, and the Abbasid movement included people with administrative experience from Kūfa and Khurāsān. The new regime also inherited the Umayyad dīwāns and the staff who ran them (Section 44.8). The creativity lay less in inventing institutions than in the political management of a regime whose legitimacy, army, and administrative class came from different sources.

Section 44.3

Abbasid Genealogy and the Claim to Legitimate Leadership

The Abbasids traced their descent to al-ʿAbbās ibn ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib, an uncle of the Prophet Muḥammad. This made them Hāshimites (members of the clan of Hāshim, from which the Prophet came) and, in the language of the time, Qurashīs, a membership that Sunni and many other Muslims had long regarded as a prerequisite for the caliphate. The Umayyads were also Qurashīs, but of a different clan; the Abbasids could claim closer kinship to the Prophet. The line that produced the first caliphs ran from al-ʿAbbās through his son ʿAbd Allāh and grandson ʿAlī to Muḥammad ibn ʿAlī (d. 743), who is remembered as the head of the family and the architect of the movement, and then to his sons Ibrāhīm "the Imām" (d. 749, after imprisonment by the Umayyads), Abū al-ʿAbbās, and Abū Jaʿfar.

Before the revolution the family had lived at Ḥumayma, in what is now southern Jordan, where excavations have uncovered a small settlement with a house, a church, and water installations. This quiet provincial seat is a reminder that the dynasty did not emerge from the court. Its claim to leadership developed in dialogue with other claims within the Prophet's family. According to a tradition preserved in the sources, Abū Hāshim, a son of Muḥammad ibn al-Ḥanafiyya (himself a son of ʿAlī by a woman of the Ḥanīfa tribe), bequeathed leadership to Muḥammad ibn ʿAlī the Abbasid shortly before his death. Whether this bequest happened as reported is uncertain, and modern historians have treated it as at least partly a later justification. But it shows that the dynasty drew on, and competed for, the authority of an imamate transmitted within the Hāshimite family.

The Abbasid argument relied on several overlapping claims. They were agnatic relatives of the Prophet through his uncle; they were Hāshimites in the sense the revolution's slogan required; they had, as a family, borne the sacrifice and risk of the movement; and God had given them victory. The last point mattered. Success in battle was read in the political culture of the time as a sign of divine favor, and the new caliphs came to stress that rule was entrusted to them by God. They also adopted regnal titles (laqab), "al-Saffāḥ" and "al-Manṣūr," the latter meaning "the Victorious" or "aided by God." "Al-Saffāḥ" is ambiguous: it can be understood as "the generous" or as "the shedder of blood," and later writers exploited both senses.

The strength of this claim was relative. It satisfied supporters who wanted a Hāshimite ruler and no more; it did not satisfy those who held that the Prophet's closest descendants, through ʿAlī and Fāṭima, had a stronger title. The Abbasids had to argue not only that they were the rightful rulers but that their rivals within the Prophet's family were not.

Section 44.4

The Prophet's Family, Alid Claims, and the Meaning of the Revolution

The revolutionary slogan summoned support for "the agreeable one from the family of Muḥammad," and its vagueness was strategic. It allowed the movement to attract Alid sympathizers, Kufan Shiʿi circles, and those who simply wanted an end to Umayyad rule, without committing in advance to a particular candidate. When the revolution succeeded, the question of what "the family" meant had to be answered. The Abbasids answered that the promise had been fulfilled in them. Many Alids and their supporters answered that it had not.

The earliest crisis arose within the Kufan phase of the revolution. Abū Salama al-Khallāl, a Kufan mawlā who had acted as a principal organizer and was remembered by the title "wazīr of the family of Muḥammad," is reported to have hesitated over the choice of caliph and to have favored an Alid. The sources disagree on how far he intended this and on how far he may have been maligned. In 750 he was killed on the orders of Abū al-ʿAbbās, with Abū Muslim's involvement according to some accounts. Regardless of his actual intentions, the killing showed that the dynasty would not tolerate an alternative.

Alid opposition thereafter followed two tracks. Some Alids, notably the Ḥusaynid line represented by Jaʿfar al-Ṣādiq in Medina, avoided open rebellion and counseled their followers against it, a course that contributed to the later quietist tradition in Twelver Shiʿism (Chapter 47). Others, especially the Ḥasanid branch, believed the leadership was theirs. The Ḥasanid Muḥammad ibn ʿAbd Allāh, later called al-Nafs al-Zakiyya ("the Pure Soul"), had been recognized as the awaited figure by many Hāshimites, including, according to some traditions, members of the Abbasid family at a gathering at al-Abwāʾ before the revolution. The reliability of this story is uncertain, but its circulation shows that Abbasid and Alid supporters understood the revolution's promise differently.

The Abbasid reply was developed over time. Al-Manṣūr's correspondence with Muḥammad (Section 44.15) states the central argument: that an uncle took precedence over the descendants of a daughter in matters of inheritance and that the Prophet's family had been vindicated through the Abbasids' victory. The argument was both legal (invoking inheritance norms) and political (invoking success). It would continue to shape Abbasid claims, and Alid rejection of those claims would be a recurrent source of rebellion.

Historical Debate

Abbasid versus Alid Legitimacy: Was the Revolution "Stolen"?

The question: Was the Hāshimiyya revolution originally intended to bring an Alid to power, with the Abbasids exploiting it, or was it Abbasid in leadership from the start?

Interpretation A — The movement was Abbasid from its origin: Some historians, emphasizing the Abbasid family's long organizational role and the Ḥumayma base, argue that the Abbasids directed the movement throughout and that its ambiguity was a tactic to broaden support. The Abbasid-friendly sources (notably the Akhbār al-dawla) support this reading.
Interpretation B — The meaning of the movement was open and contested: Other scholars argue that the movement's early supporters included Alid sympathizers and that many in its early base expected an Alid or at least an open selection, so that the Abbasid claim was in significant part asserted after the victory. The story of Abū Salama and the later Alid revolts are cited as evidence of unresolved expectations.

Evidence: Both views rely on the same sources, which were written under the Abbasids and shaped by the need to justify their rule. Neither side can fully escape the problem that narratives of the dynasty's origins were composed after the contest was settled.

Why it matters: The debate bears on whether the later Alid revolts were reactions to a betrayal or the expression of a competing claim that was always present. This chapter treats the revolution's meaning as contested from the start without assuming bad faith on either side.

Student task: Explain why the vagueness of the revolutionary slogan helped the movement before 750 and hurt the regime after it.

Section 44.5

Who Had Made the Revolution? Khurāsānī Arabs, Iranians, Mawālī, and Military Leaders

The soldiers who carried the black banners from the east are often called "Persians," and later tradition, including the later Arab nationalist and Iranian nationalist historiographies, tended to cast the revolution as an ethnic struggle. The evidence supports a more complicated picture. Khurāsān, the great eastern province centered on Merv, Nishapur, and Balkh, had been settled since the conquests by Arab garrisons that had married into local populations and in many cases become landholders. Alongside them lived Iranian dihqāns and townspeople, Sogdian and Bactrian communities, and soldiers of various origins. The revolution drew on grievances among Arab settlers, who felt excluded from power by the Syrian-dominated state and who were divided by tribal rivalries, as well as among Iranian converts and non-Muslim communities who sought relief from taxation and status discrimination (Chapter 42).

The leadership reflected this mix. The principal army commanders included Arabs such as Qaḥṭaba ibn Shabīb, from the Ṭayyiʾ tribe, and his son al-Ḥasan, alongside Abū Muslim, whose origins the sources dispute (they variously describe him as an Iranian client, a freedman, or of Arab descent), and many others of Iranian and Arab background. The mobilization depended on naqībs (local leaders) in Merv and its surrounding districts, many of them Arab settlers. It would be inaccurate to call the revolution either an Arab or an Iranian movement; its most reliable summary is that it was a Khurāsānī movement, in which Arabs and Iranians participated, shaped by local and regional rather than purely ethnic interests.

After 750 the soldiers expected the rewards of victory. They received stipends from the dīwān, and many were settled in Iraq or retained in the caliph's service. Their loyalty was, however, double. They had been recruited by Abū Muslim and the movement's cadres, and many saw themselves as defenders of the revolution rather than as servants of any individual ruler. A caliph who was anxious about his authority had good reason to watch them. The history of the following decades, including the conflict between al-Manṣūr and Abū Muslim, the Khurāsānī reaction to Abū Muslim's death, and the creation of dependable guard units, is in large part about how to turn a revolutionary army into a dynastic one.

Historians have debated the composition of the coalition and its meaning for the new state; the question is taken up in the debate box in Division IV. For now, the key point is that the victors were not a single group with a single program, and the governing elite that emerged from the revolution was in the process of being created, not simply inherited.

Division II

Al-Saffāḥ and the First Abbasid Government

Section 44.6

Abū al-ʿAbbās al-Saffāḥ and the Establishment of Caliphal Authority

Abū al-ʿAbbās ʿAbd Allāh ibn Muḥammad, proclaimed in Kūfa in late 749, ruled for less than five years and died of what the sources describe as smallpox in 754, in his thirties. His reputation in the tradition is mixed. He is presented as generous and, in some reports, as dependent on his advisers and on his brother. He is also remembered as ruthless toward Umayyads and rivals, which is the sense in which his regnal name was most often understood by later writers. The sources are not neutral, and it is difficult to separate the individual from the stereotypes attached to him.

The early rhetoric of the regime is preserved in sermons attributed to the caliph, particularly the address delivered at the Kūfa mosque on his proclamation. The text in al-Ṭabarī is a literary reconstruction, but its themes are consistent with other evidence: the Abbasids present themselves as the family of the Prophet that had been wronged, as the champions of the community against Umayyad tyranny, and as rulers who would distribute the community's wealth fairly. These themes served as a political program in a community that had expected deliverance from injustice. The delivery of that promise was another matter.

Executive government in these years was a family affair. The caliph's brothers and uncles held major commands. Abū al-ʿAbbās's household included his wife Umm Salama of the Makhzūm clan, whose influence is noted in the sources, and a circle of advisers that included Khālid ibn Barmak, the first of the Barmakid family to serve the dynasty. Early government continued to function through the secretaries and offices inherited from the Umayyads, and the caliph's authority rested on a combination of revolutionary legitimacy, the army, and personal ties.

The reign's significance is in establishing the framework within which al-Manṣūr would work. Al-Saffāḥ's authority was real but shared: Abū Muslim controlled the east, uncles governed Syria and the Jazīra, and the caliph's own ability to choose and dismiss was limited. The reign shows the revolutionary regime at its most dependent on coalition partners, which is why the problem of divided authority becomes acute under his successor.

Section 44.7

Kūfa, al-Hāshimiyya, and al-Anbār: The First Seats of Abbasid Government

The Abbasids' first capital was Kūfa, the Iraqi garrison city founded after the conquests, which had been the base of the revolution's western wing and had a long association with support for the Prophet's family. Abū al-ʿAbbās was proclaimed there, and the city served as the initial seat of government. It was familiar territory to the movement, but also a difficult one: Kūfa had its own factional politics, an urban population with strong Alid sympathies, and a record of Shiʿi political agitation. Staying there made the new regime visible but also exposed it.

Abū al-ʿAbbās soon moved to a series of locations nearby. The sources refer to a settlement called al-Ḥāshimiyya (the name designating the Hāshimite family, not a single site), first near Kūfa and then, under al-Saffāḥ, at al-Anbār on the Euphrates, in the northern part of the Iraqi alluvium. Al-Anbār, a former Sasanian site, offered a defensible location on the main river and access to the roads toward Syria and the Jazīra. Al-Manṣūr later built another Hāshimiyya near Kūfa in the 750s, and the moves between these places can be difficult to follow in the sources, where the same name is used for different locations.

The common features of these settlements are revealing. They were located in Iraq, not in Syria, and they were conceived as palace-garrison complexes, places where the caliph could live in proximity to his guard, his family, and his officials, but apart from the established urban populations. They combined military, ceremonial, and administrative functions, and they stayed in the neighborhood of the Euphrates. The impulse that would lead to Baghdad was already present: a ruler who wanted a secure base that was not identified with any existing city and not controlled by any local faction.

It is notable, too, how different this pattern was from that of the Umayyads. Damascus was a long-established city with a Syrian elite and a Syrian army; the Umayyad caliphs lived in desert palaces but remained tied to Syria. The Abbasid palace-camps were rooted in a different political landscape. They represent a deliberate relocation of the caliphate toward the lands between the rivers, where the new regime's supporters were concentrated and where Syrian influence was weakest (see Section 44.17).

Section 44.8

Former Umayyad Elites, Political Reprisals, and Administrative Continuity

The treatment of the defeated Umayyads is one of the best-known and most contested topics of the early period. Tradition reports that the Umayyad family and its supporters were hunted down. The most notorious episode is the banquet at the Abū Fuṭrus river in Palestine, in which ʿAbd Allāh ibn ʿAlī is said to have invited the surviving Umayyad notables and had them killed. Other accounts describe killings at Basra and elsewhere, and the desecration of Umayyad tombs at Syrian sites, including the exhumation of the body of the caliph Hishām. Many of these reports serve rhetorical purposes in the later tradition, and details vary, but the broad picture, that the new regime deliberately eliminated the Umayyad leadership, is credible.

Some members of the family escaped. The most important was ʿAbd al-Raḥmān ibn Muʿāwiya, a grandson of Hishām, who fled across North Africa and in 756 established an Umayyad emirate in Córdoba (Section 44.36). Others took refuge in the Hijaz or in the Byzantine borderlands. The Umayyad past was not obliterated; it was relocated.

Alongside reprisal there was practical continuity. The empire's offices, registers, and tax system were staffed by men whose skills were indispensable. Many of the secretaries who had served the Umayyads, including Iraqi, Persian, and Christian scribes, continued in office or were replaced by colleagues of similar background. The tax systems of Iraq and Iran, which depended on local landholders and on existing registers, could not be rebuilt overnight. Even the army, which had to be reorganized, drew on Arab tribal contingents of various origins as well as the Khurāsānīs. The weight of evidence suggests a regime that removed its enemies at the top while relying on much of the existing administrative base below.

This combination of violence and continuity is a pattern in revolutionary transitions. The Abbasids' victory rested on armed force, but their government depended on routines inherited from their predecessors. The balance between the two shifted over time, and it is part of the explanation for why the new regime, for all its rhetoric of rupture, preserved so much of the state it had overthrown.

Section 44.9

Establishing Control over Syria, Iraq, the Jazīra, and Egypt

The central provinces were not equally receptive to Abbasid rule. Syria, the former Umayyad heartland, had been the base of the dynasty's army and of a regional aristocracy that owed its position to Umayyad patronage. Resistance was strongest there. Within months of the revolution, revolts broke out in northern Syria, where a leader known as Abū al-Ward is reported to have rebelled in Qinnasrīn, and a claimant from the Sufyānid branch of the Umayyads, Abū Muḥammad al-Sufyānī, rallied support. These uprisings were suppressed by Abbasid commanders, notably ʿAbd Allāh ibn ʿAlī, but the Syrians' experience of the conquest was one of defeat and the removal of their privileges, and the region remained a source of tension.

In the Jazīra (upper Mesopotamia), the new regime faced similar difficulties, mingled with a different history. The region had been a base for Umayyad support but also for dissident movements, and its population included Arab tribes, Christian communities, and Kurdish and other pastoralists. The Syriac Chronicle of Zuqnin, written in the 770s, describes heavy taxation, population registration, and military exactions in the Jazīra, a view from the provincial population rather than the court. Its testimony should be read with the awareness that it comes from a Christian monastic author, but it gives a counterweight to the Abbasid narrative of justice restored.

Iraq, the core of the new empire, was more secure but not quiescent. Kūfa and Basra had their own factions, and Iraqi support for the dynasty was uneven. Egypt, where the last Umayyad had been killed, was assigned to Abbasid governors who sought to reestablish revenue flows. The Abbasids' control of Egypt was secure by the later 750s, though the province continued to be an important source of revenue and a recurrent site of local resistance in later decades.

The pattern is of a regime whose authority was strongest where its army and supporters were concentrated and weakest in former Umayyad strongholds. The solution was not uniform: in Syria, it involved military suppression and the gradual replacement of local elites; in Iraq, the cultivation of a supportive base; in Egypt and the Jazīra, the appointment of loyal governors. Each province was governed in its own way, a point that matters again in Section 44.38.

Section 44.10

Abū Muslim, Khurāsān, and the Limits of Al-Saffāḥ's Authority

No figure better illustrates the paradox of the revolution than Abū Muslim. He had organized the revolt in Khurāsān, commanded its armies, and been the key to its success. After 750 he remained in the east as governor of Khurāsān, controlling the territory from which the revolutionary army had come and maintaining a network of loyal commanders. The sources depict him as an administrator who had absolute authority over appointments, finance, and the army in the province, and who was, in effect, independent of Iraq. In 754 he traveled on the pilgrimage in company with al-Manṣūr, an occasion that, as Section 44.11 shows, placed both men in the Hijaz when al-Saffāḥ died.

His relationship with the Abbasid caliphs is a matter of dispute. The sources suggest that he deferred to al-Saffāḥ, whom he had helped to elevate, but they also record the new rulers' unease. Several episodes recorded in the tradition (Abū Muslim's mediation of Abbasid family disputes, his involvement in the removal of Abū Salama, and the fact that his name was invoked in his region alongside, or even ahead of, that of the dynasty) suggest a man whose standing exceeded that of any other subject.

For the Abbasids, the problem was structural rather than personal. A regime that depends on a single, immensely powerful governor with a private army is vulnerable. Abū Muslim could, if he wished, withdraw the east from Abbasid control, or promote a rival. Whether he intended anything of the sort is uncertain; the sources written after his death had every reason to emphasize his ambition, and Khurāsānī narratives, by contrast, emphasize his loyalty. What is clear is that his position made a confrontation likely once a caliph arose who was determined to rule without partners. That caliph was al-Manṣūr.

Division III

Al-Manṣūr and the Struggle for Dynastic Control

Section 44.11

The Accession of Abū Jaʿfar al-Manṣūr, 754

Abū Jaʿfar ʿAbd Allāh ibn Muḥammad, a younger brother of al-Saffāḥ, was born around 714 and was, by the reports, the more austere and calculating of the two. His mother, Salāma, is reported in several sources to have been a Berber concubine, a detail that indicates the diversity of the early dynastic household even if its particulars cannot be verified. He had been an organizer in the revolution, served as a governor and commander under his brother, and was the most experienced member of the family in matters of statecraft. Later tradition gave him the nickname "Abū al-Dawāniq" ("father of the small coins"), a reference to a reputation for frugality that the sources sometimes treat as admirable and sometimes as miserly.

Al-Saffāḥ died at al-Anbār in June 754. He had designated Abū Jaʿfar as his successor, with ʿĪsā ibn Mūsā, a nephew, as the next in line. The arrangement was intended to prevent a succession dispute, and the sources record that written instruments of allegiance had been deposited in the palace. At the moment of al-Saffāḥ's death, however, Abū Jaʿfar was far away, on the pilgrimage in the Hijaz in the company of Abū Muslim. News reached him there, and he returned to Iraq. ʿĪsā, who had been in Kūfa, secured the oath of allegiance on his behalf, and Abū Jaʿfar, proclaimed as al-Manṣūr, was recognized.

The accession was nevertheless precarious. Abū Muslim's presence in the pilgrim caravan and his early pledge to the new caliph were helpful, but they also demonstrated how dependent al-Manṣūr was on the east. The new ruler faced three immediate problems: a relative with a competing claim, the continuing power of Abū Muslim, and the long-term question of how to ensure that the caliphate would remain in his line. The three issues were interlinked, and the order in which al-Manṣūr dealt with them says a good deal about his method.

The sources describe al-Manṣūr as determined, secretive, and attentive to detail. They attribute to him sayings and speeches that set out his conception of authority: that the caliph was God's agent and the guardian of His treasury, entitled to obedience and accountable to God alone. As with earlier speeches, the wording is the product of literary transmission, but the emphasis on divinely entrusted sovereignty corresponds to the direction of Abbasid ideology (Section 44.21). The reign that followed was one in which the caliph's personal control, exercised through informants, appointed officials, and an expanding guard, became the dominant fact of Abbasid government.

Section 44.12

ʿAbd Allāh ibn ʿAlī and the Challenge from Within the Abbasid Family

The first challenge to al-Manṣūr came from an uncle. ʿAbd Allāh ibn ʿAlī, a brother of Muḥammad ibn ʿAlī and thus an uncle of the first two caliphs, had been one of the revolution's leading commanders: he led the campaign that defeated Marwān II and subsequently governed Syria, where he commanded a substantial army and directed the suppression of Umayyad resistance. He was older than al-Manṣūr, a major figure in the dynasty, and ambitious. When al-Saffāḥ died, ʿAbd Allāh, then at the head of an army on the Byzantine frontier, proclaimed himself caliph.

His claim, as the sources report it, rested on the assertion that al-Saffāḥ had promised the succession to whichever member of the family led the campaign against the Byzantines. Whether such a promise was ever made is doubtful, and the story may have been devised to justify his action. What is clearer is that ʿAbd Allāh had an army, including many Khurāsānī soldiers, and a strong claim as the family's most successful commander. He drew support from Syrian forces who had served under him and from Khurāsānīs who respected his record. The challenge was serious.

Al-Manṣūr's response was to send Abū Muslim against him. The decision was characteristic: it used the most powerful figure in the coalition to defeat another potential rival, and it set two possible threats against each other. In November 754 the forces met near Nisibis in the Jazīra. According to the accounts, Abū Muslim outmaneuvered ʿAbd Allāh, whose army broke, and the uncle fled to Basra, where his brother Sulaymān protected him. Eventually he was handed over to al-Manṣūr and imprisoned. He died in 764, reportedly when the house in which he was confined collapsed. Some sources treat the collapse as an accident; others suspect it was engineered.

The episode demonstrated that the principal danger to the dynasty lay within the Abbasid family. It also strengthened Abū Muslim at the moment when al-Manṣūr wished to weaken him. After the battle, Abū Muslim seized the spoils of ʿAbd Allāh's camp, and al-Manṣūr's attempt to send an official to inventory them is reported to have angered him. The conflict that followed (Section 44.13) was rooted in the shifting balance of power that the victory had created.

Section 44.13

Abū Muslim's Elimination in 755

The relationship between al-Manṣūr and Abū Muslim deteriorated through the winter of 754–755. The sources dramatize the quarrel with a series of exchanges and reported insults, and different transmitters give contradictory accounts of who was to blame. The structural facts, however, are plain. Abū Muslim was the real master of the east. He had defeated ʿAbd Allāh, controlled Khurāsān, and had a network of commanders loyal to him. Al-Manṣūr offered him the governorship of Syria and Egypt, apparently as a way of removing him from Khurāsān; Abū Muslim refused, withdrew toward the east, and acted as though he considered himself free to decide his movements.

The reported turning point was a summons to meet the caliph. Abū Muslim hesitated, consulted his advisers (some of whom urged him not to go), and eventually came to al-Manṣūr's camp near al-Madāʾin, the former Sasanian capital region on the Tigris. The sources describe a series of meetings in which al-Manṣūr reproached him, and a final audience in which guards concealed in the tent struck him down, in February 755. The caliph, according to the accounts, made a public statement defending the killing, accusing Abū Muslim of disobedience and of seeking to undermine the dynasty.

Whether Abū Muslim was in fact preparing to rebel is unknowable. The tradition, shaped by the victor's version and by the later popularity of Abū Muslim among Khurāsānī and Iranian audiences, gives contradictory portraits: the loyal servant betrayed, the ambitious general justly removed, the semi-legendary hero. Modern scholars generally interpret the killing as a political act in which al-Manṣūr eliminated a figure whose power was incompatible with his own. The details of the death reflect the conventions of historical writing as much as the event itself.

The consequences were immediate. Al-Manṣūr had removed the most powerful man in the coalition and shown that he would not be bound by revolutionary debts. This strengthened the caliph's position in Iraq but increased the danger in the east. Abū Muslim's death would be remembered as a betrayal, and it became a rallying point for movements that expected a more radical or different outcome from the revolution. Appointments in Khurāsān following his death went to officials chosen by the caliph, and the east was reintegrated into the Abbasid system with greater direct control from Iraq.

Section 44.14

Eastern Resistance, Revolutionary Expectations, and the Sunbādh Revolt

The killing of Abū Muslim provoked immediate resistance in the east. The best-documented reaction was the revolt of Sunbādh (Sinbādh), a man from the Nishapur region who, the sources say, had been a close associate of Abū Muslim. In 755 he raised a following in Khurāsān, marched westward, and seized Rayy, where he reportedly captured the treasury that Abū Muslim had deposited. His rebellion lasted around seventy days before an Abbasid army defeated him near Hamadan, and he was killed in flight.

The sources present Sunbādh as a "Magian" (a term used for Zoroastrians and associated groups) and describe his movement as having heterodox beliefs, including the claim that Abū Muslim was not dead and the intention to destroy the Kaʿba. These reports come from hostile writers and should be treated with caution. They fit a standard pattern in which defeated movements are labeled as religious deviants, and the label may obscure the grievances that actually drove the rising, including loyalty to a murdered patron, resentment of the Abbasid center, and the hope that the revolutionary promises would be fulfilled. Whether the beliefs attributed to Sunbādh were actually held is uncertain.

Sunbādh's revolt was not an isolated event. In 758 a group known as the Rāwandiyya, Khurāsānīs holding extreme views of the caliph's status (according to the sources they regarded al-Manṣūr as the embodiment of the divine), attacked his palace at al-Hāshimiyya near Kūfa and were repulsed only after fighting. In the years after Abū Muslim's death, other eastern rebels appeared, including the governor ʿAbd al-Jabbār al-Azdī, who rebelled in Khurāsān around 758–759 and was suppressed by al-Mahdī's forces, and, at the end of the reign, in the late 760s, the large revolt of Ustādhsīs in the Herat and Sīstān region.

What these movements reveal is that the Abbasid victory had released expectations that the dynasty could not control. Some followers of the revolution believed it had inaugurated a new age; others wanted a continuation of Abū Muslim's regional autonomy; others were Iranian or local figures who found in the revolutionary idiom a language of protest. The Abbasids dealt with them by force, by reorganizing the east (Section 44.18), and by sending al-Manṣūr's son al-Mahdī to Rayy as a viceroy with a substantial army. The era's heterodox movements, such as those of the later al-Muqannaʿ under al-Mahdī, belong to the same story of eastern disillusion, but are reserved for later treatment.

Section 44.15

Muḥammad al-Nafs al-Zakiyya and the Alid Revolt in Medina, 762

The most dangerous challenge to al-Manṣūr's legitimacy came from the Ḥasanid branch of the Alids. Muḥammad ibn ʿAbd Allāh ibn al-Ḥasan, whose father was a respected figure among Medinans and a descendant of both ʿAlī and Fāṭima, had been regarded by many as the expected leader. Some reports say the Abbasids themselves had pledged allegiance to him before the revolution; if so, they later resented being reminded of it. After the revolution, Muḥammad and his brother Ibrāhīm went into hiding, and the caliph made repeated efforts to locate them, including the arrest and imprisonment of their father and other Ḥasanids, several of whom died in custody.

In 762 Muḥammad, then around forty-five years of age according to the sources, rose in Medina. He gained control of the city, seized the treasury, and was recognized as caliph by many Medinans. Medina had been a politically marginal city for a century, but it retained prestige as the city of the Prophet and the home of an educated religious elite whose support lent his rising moral weight. The Abbasid governor was expelled. Jurists and prominent families, including members of the Quraysh and the Anṣār, took sides, and the sources note that some Medinan scholars gave him their allegiance while others, such as Jaʿfar al-Ṣādiq, avoided it.

The correspondence between Muḥammad and al-Manṣūr, preserved in al-Ṭabarī, sets out the rival claims. Muḥammad asserted that he was the rightful ruler by descent from ʿAlī and Fāṭima and that the Abbasids had usurped the movement. Al-Manṣūr replied that an uncle ranked ahead of a daughter's descendants in inheritance, that the Abbasids had avenged the Prophet's family, and that Muḥammad's father and ancestors had failed to claim the caliphate. The exchange is probably embellished, but it shows both the arguments and the tone of the dispute.

Muḥammad's chances depended on support outside Medina, and these were slim. The caliph sent a force under his nephew ʿĪsā ibn Mūsā, and in December 762, just outside Medina, Muḥammad's modest army was defeated and he was killed. The revolt failed, but it demonstrated that the Abbasids' claim to represent the Prophet's family remained contested in the very city where the Prophet had ruled. It also had lasting effects on the dynasty's relations with the Hijaz and with Alid communities. Chapter 47 considers the broader development of Shia communities and their divergent responses.

Creator: Letters attributed to Muḥammad ibn ʿAbd Allāh and to al-Manṣūr, preserved by al-Ṭabarī (Volume XXVIII) · Date: 762; compiled c. 900 · Type: Political correspondence in a historical compilation

Context: The letters are presented as an exchange between rival claimants during the Medina revolt. They may be reworked, but they show arguments that were in circulation.

Paraphrase: Muḥammad claims precedence as a descendant of ʿAlī and Fāṭima, the Prophet's daughter, and accuses the Abbasids of exploiting a movement that was meant for his family. Al-Manṣūr replies that the Prophet left no surviving sons, that an uncle's claim as an inheritor outranks that of a daughter's line, that al-ʿAbbās was among the Prophet's closest relatives, and that the Abbasids had vindicated the family by overthrowing the Umayyads.

Analysis: The exchange shows that the legitimacy dispute was conducted in the language of kinship, inheritance, and historical service. It does not settle who was "right," but it shows how each side reasoned.

Limitations: The letters survive in a work of the Abbasid era, and the style may have been refined by later authors. Alid sources, which survive mostly in later Shia compilations, would present the dispute differently.

Section 44.16

Ibrāhīm ibn ʿAbd Allāh, Baṣra, and the Defeat of the Alid Challenge, 762–763

While Muḥammad rose in Medina, his brother Ibrāhīm had been operating in southern Iraq. The two uprisings were coordinated in design, though not in execution. Ibrāhīm had spent time in Basra, a city with a mixed population of Arab tribes, clients, merchants, and Muʿtazilī-inclined scholars, and had cultivated support there. In the autumn of 762, he seized the city, drawing backing from a range of groups, including some jurists and theologians who disliked Abbasid rule. He then extended his authority to Wāsiṭ and parts of Fārs and Ahwāz, and his following grew.

Ibrāhīm's movement, unlike that of his brother, was positioned to threaten the heartland. Basra and Wāsiṭ controlled the Tigris and the roads to the Gulf; if Ibrāhīm could take Kūfa, he would threaten the new Abbasid base. The sources report that al-Manṣūr was at his Hāshimiyya near Kūfa with few troops and, at one point, was in serious danger. They describe him as sleeping on a prayer mat in his palace, refusing to move, and sending urgent summons to his commanders. These stories belong in part to the literary tradition of the dynasty's near escape, but the severity of the threat is credible.

The decisive step was the recall of ʿĪsā ibn Mūsā from Medina and the dispatch of troops to confront Ibrāhīm. In February 763, the armies met at Bākhamrā, about forty kilometers from Kūfa. Ibrāhīm's forces, dominated by Basrans and Kufan volunteers, had early successes, but the Abbasid army, composed of Khurāsānīs and others, regrouped and won. Ibrāhīm was killed, and his head was sent to the caliph. The Alid challenge was effectively over.

The victory consolidated al-Manṣūr's authority in several ways. He had defeated the most credible rival claimants, he had shown that the Khurāsānī army was loyal to the dynasty rather than only to its former commanders, and he had put down a revolt that had briefly commanded broad support in Iraq. It also left bitterness among Alid supporters and influenced the later direction of Shia political activity. Al-Manṣūr's decision to found his capital in this same period, and the pace of the construction, are closely linked to the experience of the revolts (Section 44.23). The traditional foundation date of 762 coincides with the very months in which both uprisings occurred, a circumstance that has prompted historians to ask how closely the decision and the crisis were connected.

The two Alid movements should be kept analytically separate. Muḥammad's revolt in Medina rested on the prestige of the Hijaz and on religious scholars; Ibrāhīm's in Basra drew on the social and political networks of southern Iraq and threatened the regime's core. They did not share a single constituency or strategy, and their separate failures say something about the difficulty of mounting a unified opposition.

Division IV

Reorganizing Imperial Authority around Iraq and Iran

Section 44.17

From Damascus to Iraq: The New Geography of Imperial Power

The Umayyads had ruled from Syria, and the Syrian army had been the backbone of their power. The Abbasids, after the revolution, did not return to Damascus. Their decision to base the caliphate in Iraq, and to look east toward Iran, was among the most consequential of the dynasty's choices. It was not a single decision but a series of choices: Kūfa, then al-Hāshimiyya, then al-Anbār, and finally Baghdad. Each placed the caliph in the middle Euphrates and Tigris region rather than in the Mediterranean provinces.

Several considerations lay behind this. Iraq, and especially the alluvial Sawād, was the empire's richest agricultural province and the largest single source of tax revenue, as discussed in Chapter 2. Its canals and its rivers made it a natural center for the collection and storage of grain and for the movement of goods. The region lay between Iran and Syria, close enough to the Iranian plateau to maintain contact with the east and to the Jazīra and Syria to monitor the west. It was also the region where the dynasty's supporters were concentrated, in Kūfa and among the Khurāsānī troops, and where it had the strongest base of loyal soldiers.

Equally important was what Iraq was not. It was not Syria, where the Umayyad military establishment and its clients had their roots and where resistance to the Abbasids had been strongest. Moving the capital eastward reduced the proximity of the former ruling group and placed the new rulers where their opponents had less leverage. It also shifted the weight of political influence: the Syrian Arab aristocracy lost its centrality, and the new ruling elite was drawn from Iraqis, Khurāsānīs, and the Abbasid family and its clients.

The move did not mean that the Mediterranean provinces were abandoned, but it altered their relationship to the center. Syria and Egypt became provinces, important for revenue and for the frontier with Byzantium, rather than the heartland of the empire. The eastern orientation also meant greater openness to Iranian political and cultural traditions, a theme that recurs in the later sections of this division and in Chapter 49. The new geography of power was therefore not only a matter of location but of the composition and culture of the elite.

Section 44.18

The Khurāsānī Army and the Military Foundations of Abbasid Rule

The Khurāsānī troops who had made the revolution remained the core of the Abbasid army, but their role changed. In the revolution they had followed the commands of Abū Muslim and his lieutenants; under al-Manṣūr they had to become the caliph's army. The process was gradual and depended on pay, settlement, and the cultivation of loyalty. Many were settled in Iraq, and the registers of the dīwān al-jund assigned them regular stipends. Their commanders were drawn into the court through appointments to governorships and commands.

The reign provided several tests of their loyalty. The Rāwandiyya attack on the palace in 758 was repulsed by troops who remained loyal. Khurāsānī commanders led the armies against ʿAbd Allāh ibn ʿAlī, against Ibrāhīm at Bākhamrā, and against eastern rebels. Their performance demonstrated the dynasty's military resources, but it also showed their continuing importance as an independent force: a caliph who relied on soldiers whose first allegiance was to a movement or to a commander could be in danger. Al-Manṣūr's strategy involved dividing and balancing: he used some Khurāsānī units against others, promoted commanders personally loyal to him, and established a dedicated guard (ḥaras) close to his person.

The most important development was the emergence of a notion of dynastic loyalty distinct from revolutionary loyalty. The soldiers and their descendants, later called abnāʾ al-dawla, were attached to the dynasty by the stipends and honors it conferred, and by their interest in the survival of the regime that supported them. In the early decades this was a fragile bond, and in the next generation it would be tested by the civil war between al-Amīn and al-Maʾmūn, treated in later chapters. For now, it is enough to see that the Khurāsānī army became the principal instrument of Abbasid power and a distinctive feature of the new state.

The army was not purely Khurāsānī. Arab tribal contingents from Iraq and the Jazīra, units from Syria, and, over time, clients and slaves entered the forces. Chapter 45 deals with the full structure of the army and the later shift to slave soldiers; Chapter 52 considers the role of Turkic soldiers. In the 750s and 760s, the Khurāsānī element was dominant, and the problem of managing it was the principal military challenge of the age.

Historical Vocabulary
Khurāsāniyya, mawālī, and abnāʾ al-dawla

Khurāsāniyya denotes the eastern soldiers associated with the Abbasid Revolution; its membership cannot be equated with a single ethnicity. Mawālī (singular mawlā) refers to relationships of clientage, including some freed people and converts, not to a uniform Persian social class. Abnāʾ al-dawla, or “sons of the dynasty,” became a term for the Khurāsānī military establishment and its descendants associated with Baghdad. These categories partly overlap but belong to different political and social contexts.

Section 44.19

Iranian Participation and the Formation of a New Governing Elite

A common but misleading account describes the Abbasid revolution as bringing an Iranian bureaucracy to power in place of an Arab one. The evidence points to a more complex process. Iranian participation in government was substantial and increased under the Abbasids, but it built on a long Iranian presence in Islamic administration, particularly in the fiscal offices of Iraq and the east. Under the Umayyads, many scribes in the eastern and Iraqi administration had been Persian speakers or from Iranian families, and the former Sasanian administrative class had adapted to Islamic rule (Chapters 39 and 42). What changed in 750 was the visibility, status, and political weight of some Iranian-origin officials and soldiers.

The individuals who illustrate this are varied. Khālid ibn Barmak, from a family associated with a Buddhist temple at Balkh according to later tradition, served al-Saffāḥ and al-Manṣūr in financial and provincial roles, including a governorship in Ṭabaristān, and his family would rise to prominence in the following generation. Abū Ayyūb al-Mūriyānī, a secretary from Khūzistān, became al-Manṣūr's chief official before his disgrace around 770. Ibn al-Muqaffaʿ, from Fārs, wrote on statecraft and translated Middle Persian works. The astrologer Nawbakht, of Iranian origin, and Māshāʾallāh, a Jewish astronomer associated with Basra, helped to select the time for the founding of the capital, as later accounts of Baghdad report.

But the governing elite was never exclusively Iranian. Arab officials such as Abū Salama (a Kufan client) and many Arab tribal leaders served the regime. The caliph's family and clients were central, and the secretarial class included Arabs, Iranians, Aramaic speakers, and Christians. Moreover, the Iranians who served were not a single group: they came from different regions, held different religious affiliations, and had different cultural ties. Their rise reflects the dynasty's need for administrators and the opening of careers that the Umayyad system had restricted.

The most defensible summary is that the Abbasid elite was more heterogeneous than the Umayyad, that Iranian-origin officials and soldiers gained influence, and that the court absorbed a significant measure of Iranian political and cultural style, as Section 44.22 shows. This was a change in composition and culture within an Arabic-speaking, Islamic state, not the replacement of one ethnic ruling class by another.

Historical Debate

The Nature of the New Ruling Elite

The question: Did the Abbasid revolution bring non-Arab Iranians into power, or did it primarily transfer power among Arab groups?

Interpretation A — A revolution of Arabs of Khurāsān: M. A. Shaban and others argued that the revolution was led by Arab settlers in Khurāsān and that the army was largely Arab, and that the "Persian" reading overstates the role of non-Arabs. Under this view, the Abbasid elite remained predominantly Arab in composition, even as it moved eastward.
Interpretation B — A coalition with significant Iranian participation: Other scholars, including Moshe Sharon and Elton Daniel in different ways, emphasize the participation of Iranian soldiers, landholders, and clients, and the eastern regional character of the movement. Daniel's work on Khurāsān emphasizes regional and local dynamics rather than a simple ethnic conflict.

Evidence: Both sides use the same sources: lists of commanders, accounts of recruitment, and the careers of officials. The ethnic labels in the sources are not always reliable, and distinctions among Arab, mawlā, and Iranian blurred over generations of settlement.

Why it matters: The debate bears on how historians describe the Abbasid state, whether as an Arab empire with new regional features, an Islamic empire with an increasingly mixed elite, or something else. This chapter treats the revolution as regionally Khurāsānī and the elite as heterogeneous.

Section 44.20

Secretaries, Offices, and the Early Abbasid Bureaucracy

Abbasid government in the 750s and 760s depended on the secretarial class and the offices (dīwāns) that it staffed. The caliphate had inherited from the Umayyads a set of administrative functions: a chancery for correspondence, a tax office that kept registers of land and population, an army register, and offices for the post and for the caliph's seal. The Abbasids expanded and reorganized these, but their early development was incremental, and many arrangements known from later periods were still taking shape. This section examines the formation of institutions; the full departmental structure, fiscal analysis, and army economics are the subject of Chapter 45.

The officials' work was largely written. Letters passed between the caliph and governors, and orders were issued in the caliph's name. Fiscal reports documented revenues from the provinces, and registers recorded land and tax obligations. The skills required included Arabic style, accounting, knowledge of provincial conditions, and, in some provinces, the use of Persian or Aramaic. The profession was often hereditary, and families such as the Barmakids and others served generation after generation. Writers of the time, notably Ibn al-Muqaffaʿ, developed a literature on the secretary's craft and the ethics of service.

A chief minister, the wazīr, emerged during this period. Abū Salama bore the title in the revolutionary period, and under al-Manṣūr the position was held by officials such as Abū Ayyūb al-Mūriyānī. The vizierate's precise functions and status were not yet fixed; it was, in these years, a personal arrangement of trust between caliph and minister, and al-Manṣūr's treatment of his ministers, who were often dismissed and punished, shows that he regarded the office as an instrument of his own authority rather than an independent power. Tradition attributes to him a comment that the empire rested on four kinds of officials: the judge, the chief of police, the tax administrator, and the postmaster who reported on all the others. Whether or not he said it, the saying reflects the importance of information to the regime.

The postal and intelligence service (barīd) had existed under the Umayyads, but al-Manṣūr's emphasis on it was notable. Its officials reported on provincial governors, judges, and fiscal officers, giving the caliph independent information. It was one of the instruments by which a ruler in Iraq could oversee provinces that stretched thousands of kilometers. Surviving evidence for the early Abbasid bureaucracy is thin and largely literary, and historians reconstruct its operations from narratives, later administrative handbooks, and fragments of documents.

Section 44.21

Caliphal Legitimacy in Practice: Sermons, Coinage, Pilgrimage, and Public Authority

Legitimacy had to be made visible. In the early Islamic world, the principal ways of displaying the caliph's authority were the Friday sermon, in which the ruler's name was proclaimed in the mosques of the empire; coinage, which carried the caliph's authority across the markets; the pilgrimage, which connected the caliph to the sacred centers of Islam; and the public ceremonies and appointments by which the regime displayed its power. The Abbasids used all of these.

The sermon was an essential medium. By having the khuṭba delivered in the caliph's name, provincial communities affirmed their allegiance, and the substitution of a name or its omission was a sign of rebellion. The sermons of the early caliphs, as preserved, emphasize the theme of divine selection and the caliph's role as guardian of the faith. Al-Manṣūr is reported to have described himself as God's sovereign authority on earth, a formulation that moved toward the view that the caliph held his office by divine appointment, a claim that was developed more fully in later Abbasid ideology. The reports are traditions, and their wording should not be treated as verbatim.

Coinage in this period remained close to Umayyad models. Dirhams were aniconic, bearing Arabic inscriptions with the profession of faith and the mint name and date, and the Abbasids initially continued this tradition, adding caliphal names and religious formulas as time went on. Coins from mints in Iraq, Iran, and elsewhere document the distribution of the regime's authority. Coins naming Madīnat al-Salām as a mint appear in the 770s. Coinage was a conservative medium, and the continuity of its design is one of the clearest pieces of evidence that the Abbasids inherited the monetary system of their predecessors.

Relations with the holy cities were more fraught. Al-Manṣūr, like other caliphs, appointed the leader of the pilgrimage, and the Abbasids took care to ensure that the Hijaz was loyal. The revolts of 762 showed that Medina, in particular, could not be taken for granted. The dynasty also used pilgrimage as a means of display and generosity, and al-Manṣūr died on pilgrimage in 775. The black color associated with the dynasty, according to tradition adopted from the revolution's banners, became part of its public identity, though its meaning (mourning for the Prophet's family, or a sign of the revolution) is explained in different ways in the sources.

Section 44.22

The Abbasid Court and the New Language of Kingship

The Abbasid court under al-Manṣūr was a more formal and hierarchical institution than that of the Umayyads, and it increasingly displayed a language of kingship that drew on late antique and, especially, Iranian models. Access to the caliph was controlled by a chamberlain (ḥājib), and audiences were arranged by rank. Courtiers, family members, commanders, secretaries, and religious figures took places according to protocol, and petitioners approached through officials. The caliph's household, composed of wives, concubines, children, eunuchs, and servants, formed a separate sphere with its own influence, as the sources' references to the roles of royal mothers and wives indicate.

The influence of Sasanian court practice is often asserted, and it is plausible: many of the secretaries and advisers were of Iranian background, and the Sasanian court at Ctesiphon, close to the site of Baghdad, remained a physical and cultural presence. Texts translated from Middle Persian by Ibn al-Muqaffaʿ, including the Book of Kalīla and Dimna and works on royal etiquette, brought these traditions to Arabic readers. But the transmission was selective. The caliph remained a Muslim ruler whose authority rested on religious claims, and the court's ceremonial borrowed from Iranian models without becoming Sasanian. The documentation of the early period is thinner than for the later eighth and ninth centuries, and some of the ceremony described in later sources may reflect later developments.

A recurring tension in the sources is between the caliph as religious leader and the caliph as king (malik). The Umayyads had been criticized for acting like kings, and the Abbasid revolution had been presented as a restoration of rightful, religiously grounded leadership. In practice the Abbasids built a court that resembled earlier monarchies in its trappings. Al-Manṣūr's own style, reported as austere and exacting, contrasted with the later splendor of Hārūn al-Rashīd's court, but the foundations of a more elaborate royal culture were laid in his reign. The new language of kingship accommodated both the claims of religious leadership and the practices of imperial rule, and the interplay of the two remained a defining feature of Abbasid political culture.

Visual and spatial evidence also matters. The layout of the Round City, discussed in Division V, expresses a conception of authority in architectural terms: the caliph at the center, surrounded by guards and officials, the city organized around the palace and the mosque. The court's ceremonial and the city's design were parts of the same project, one that sought to give the dynasty a distinctive and enduring public form.

Historical Vocabulary
Khalīfa and Malik: Caliph and King

The term khalīfa (caliph) originally meant "successor" or "deputy," and it was used for the leaders who followed the Prophet. By the Umayyad period some also used the formula "deputy of God." Malik (king) carried associations with dynastic, hereditary, and non-Islamic rule, and critics used it against the Umayyads. The Abbasids sought to be caliphs in the religious sense while building a dynastic monarchy in practice. Historians use the contrast to ask how early Islamic rulers understood their authority, with the caution that the two roles overlapped and that the terms themselves were used polemically.

Division V

The Founding and Design of Baghdad

Section 44.23

Why Al-Manṣūr Chose to Found a New Capital

By the early 760s, the caliphate had moved its court several times within Iraq. Al-Manṣūr's own Hāshimiyya near Kūfa was close to a city with strong Alid sympathies, and the revolts of 762 and 763 demonstrated how close the danger could come. The interim palaces were exposed: the Rāwandiyya had attacked one in 758, and Ibrāhīm's army had advanced to within a short distance of another. A ruler who had eliminated Abū Muslim and defeated Alid rivals still lacked a secure and fully controlled base from which to govern.

A new capital offered several advantages. It could be located where the dynasty wished, rather than within an existing city whose factions and traditions shaped political life. It could be designed for defense, with walls, controlled entrances, and quarters for the caliph's guard and officials. It could be supplied by routes the regime controlled. And it could symbolize the new dynasty's character, as other ruling houses in history had expressed their authority by founding cities. Al-Manṣūr's contemporaries and successors regarded the foundation as a deliberate act of statecraft, and later writers recorded elaborate stories about his motives.

The chronology of the decision is uncertain. The traditional foundation date is 762 (145 AH), the same year as the Medina and Basra revolts, and some historians suggest that the experience of the unrest influenced both the urgency and the design. Others point out that planning must have preceded the crisis, since it involved site surveys, the selection of the location by advisers, and the assembly of workers and materials. The sources do not allow a firm resolution, and this chapter treats the revolts as a context that likely confirmed and accelerated, rather than created, a decision that had been under consideration.

One striking feature of the traditions is the sense that the choice was a considered one. According to reports preserved by al-Ṭabarī and al-Khaṭīb, al-Manṣūr inspected several sites, including the old Sasanian region around al-Madāʾin, and rejected some for reasons of climate, water, or supply. This account is probably partly legendary, but it reflects the importance contemporaries attached to the site's qualities, which Section 44.24 examines.

Section 44.24

The Baghdad Site: Tigris, Euphrates, Canals, and the Mesopotamian Landscape

The site chosen lay on the west bank of the Tigris, in the central Iraqi alluvium, near the place where the Tigris and Euphrates come closest to one another. As Chapter 2 noted, the rivers approach within a few tens of kilometers here, and a network of canals had for centuries linked them. The most important of these was the canal later called the Nahr ʿĪsā, which carried Euphrates water east to the Tigris and allowed boats from Syria and the upper Euphrates to reach the Baghdad region. Other canals, including the Ṣarāt and, branching from it, the Karkhāyā, watered the surrounding lands and provided routes for transport and supply.

Before the founding, the site was not empty. A village or small market settlement named Baghdad (a name of Persian origin, with a disputed etymology) existed there, and the surrounding region had a long record of settlement, agriculture, and Christian monastic life under the Sasanians. The area was close to the former Sasanian metropolitan region, a cluster of cities on the Tigris that the Arabs called al-Madāʾin ("the cities"), including Ctesiphon, the great Sasanian capital. The location therefore had inherited advantages: established irrigation, a productive hinterland, and a tradition of imperial presence.

Its strategic advantages were considerable. The Tigris provided downstream navigation to Wāsiṭ, Basra, and the Gulf, and the Euphrates canal system gave access to Syria and the Jazīra. Roads radiated outward to Kūfa and the Arabian pilgrimage route, to Basra, to Khurāsān through the Zagros passes, and to Syria. The surrounding Sawād was among the most productive agricultural regions in the empire, and the city could draw on its grain and other produce. Traditions attribute to al-Manṣūr a statement that, from this place, anything the empire produced could reach him by water, and that no enemy could easily cut his supply.

The site also had drawbacks. The river floods that Chapter 2 described, along with the shifting channels, created risks, and the summer heat was severe. Later flooding and canal changes affected parts of the city. But the weight of the advantages is clear, and the choice anticipated the long-term importance of the location: Baghdad would remain the dominant city of the region for centuries, benefiting from the same conditions that al-Manṣūr's advisers identified.

Section 44.25

The Foundation of Madīnat al-Salām, 762

The sources report that al-Manṣūr himself participated in the foundation. Having selected the site, he is said to have had the outline of the city marked on the ground, and a well-known tradition describes lines of ashes or cotton seeds soaked in naphtha being set alight to trace the circuit of the walls. The date and hour of the first brick were chosen by astrologers, including Nawbakht and Māshāʾallāh, according to the account in al-Ṭabarī and later sources. The caliph is said to have laid the first brick, with a prayer for the city's prosperity. These stories are the kind of foundation narratives found in many cultures, and their details should be treated as tradition rather than record, but the involvement of astrologers reflects the intellectual world of the court (see Section 44.33).

The official name chosen was Madīnat al-Salām, the City of Peace. The name has a Qurʾānic resonance, since the Qurʾān refers to a "home of peace" (dār al-salām) as a name of Paradise. Its choice also expressed the regime's claim to bring order after civil war. The older name, Baghdād, continued in use in everyday speech and eventually became the standard name. The city was also known as the Round City (al-Madīna al-Mudawwara) and as al-Manṣūriyya after its founder.

Construction began in 762 and, according to the traditions, continued for about four years, with the principal structures completed by 766. It is important to distinguish the foundation from the completion and from later phases. The Round City was the initial, planned enclosure; later additions, including the caliph's palace of al-Khuld on the Tigris, the settlement of markets in al-Karkh, and the growth of the eastern quarters, followed in the 760s and 770s. The date 762 is a convention marking the beginning, and even the sources disagree about aspects of the chronology.

The revolts of 762–763 interrupted but did not halt the project. The sources describe the caliph returning to the building site after the defeat of Ibrāhīm, and the pace of work resumed with the security that victory provided. The completion of the Round City thus coincided with the regime's consolidation, and the city that emerged was in some sense a monument to the survival of the dynasty.

Section 44.26

The Round City: Walls, Gates, Roads, and Defensive Planning

The Round City was a circular enclosure, with a diameter that modern reconstructions put at roughly two kilometers or somewhat more, depending on the source and method used. A defensive moat surrounded it, outside which lay a sloping glacis, and within this were two concentric walls: an outer wall of moderate height and a higher, thicker inner wall, with a space between them. The descriptions mention towers and fortified gate complexes, and the entire system was designed to resist attack and to control movement into the center. The dimensions given in the sources vary, and the reconstructions that scholars have drawn, beginning with the early twentieth-century work of Guy Le Strange and K. A. C. Creswell and followed by Jacob Lassner's detailed study, rest on medieval descriptions rather than excavated plans.

There were four gates, set at roughly equal intervals around the circuit, and each was named for the region toward which its road led: Bāb al-Kūfa, Bāb al-Baṣra, Bāb Khurāsān, and Bāb al-Shām (Syria). The names mapped the empire onto the city, linking Baghdad to the lands from which its revenues and soldiers came. Each gate had a double structure with iron doors, and above the gate was a chamber that, according to the sources, the caliph could use as an audience room or lookout. The iron doors were, by some reports, taken from earlier buildings elsewhere in Iraq and Syria, a detail that, if true, shows the reuse of prestigious materials.

From each gate a road led toward the center. Between the inner wall and the central area, the plan provided a ring of residential sectors for the caliph's family, officials, and guards, divided by the main roads into quarters. The sources describe covered arcades lined with shops along the four main avenues, indicating that markets were originally planned within the city; they were later moved outside (Section 44.30). The inner area was thus organized around the control of access: a visitor passed through the gates, along the avenues, and into the central open space only under supervision.

The circular design has prompted comparisons. Sasanian cities in Fārs, such as Gūr (Fīrūzābād) associated with Ardashīr I, were circular, and Parthian and other earlier cities in the region provided other precedents. Some scholars have also pointed to the planned cities of the Hellenistic and Roman world. Whether al-Manṣūr's planners drew directly on any of these is uncertain: no document says so, and the circle may have been adopted because it offered defensive and symbolic advantages, a point examined in the debate below. Traditions report that the circular form was chosen to place the caliph equally close to every part of the city, an explanation that is plausible but may be a later rationalization.

Section 44.27

Palace, Congregational Mosque, and the Spatial Organization of Power

At the center of the Round City lay an open space containing two principal buildings: the caliph's palace and the congregational mosque. The palace, called the Golden Gate Palace (Qaṣr Bāb al-Dhahab) in the sources, held the caliph's residence and audience hall, and above its main chamber rose a green dome, a landmark that, according to later writers, was visible from a considerable distance and that stood until it collapsed in 941. Reports add that the dome was surmounted by a mounted figure, though such details come from late accounts. The mosque stood adjacent to the palace, so close that, according to the sources, the caliph could pass from one to the other. The mosque's proximity to the palace expressed the union of political and religious authority, and the caliph's leadership of the Friday prayer made the relationship concrete.

The arrangement was not an innovation in the Islamic world: Umayyad governors and caliphs had built mosques next to their residences, notably in Kūfa and Basra. What was new was the scale and the spatial control. Access to the center was restricted; the surrounding quarters were reserved for people tied to the court; and the wider public could reach the mosque only under the supervision of guards. The sources report that al-Manṣūr was concerned with security, and the later opening of the mosque to public use required adaptation. Over time, the Round City's central space, intended as an inner sanctum, became a more complex site as the city grew.

The urban plan thus functioned as a political statement. The caliph at the geometric center, the gates named for provinces, the surrounding quarters for guards and officials, and the placement of mosque and palace together presented an image of a ruler to whom all regions converged, protected by a loyal retinue. This reading is an interpretation by modern scholars, but it is consistent with the logic of the design and with the ideology of Abbasid authority described in Section 44.21. The caliph's authority was both religious and imperial, and the architecture gave it spatial form.

The interior arrangements changed over time. The palace was later supplemented by other residences, and al-Manṣūr himself, according to the traditions, built the palace of al-Khuld outside the Round City, on the Tigris, around 773, possibly because the original palace was cramped or because he wished to live beside the river. The Round City then became principally an administrative and military precinct, with the caliph's residence nearby but not within it. This development warns against treating the foundation plan as a complete or permanent description of the city.

Section 44.28

Building an Imperial Capital: Labor, Materials, Finance, and Construction

Building the Round City was a major logistical undertaking. According to al-Yaʿqūbī and later writers, al-Manṣūr summoned architects, engineers, surveyors, craftsmen, and laborers from across the empire, including Syria, Mosul, the Jabal (the Iranian highlands), Kūfa, Wāsiṭ, and Basra. Reports mention a workforce on the order of one hundred thousand, a number that should be read as an expression of vastness rather than an accurate count; medieval writers used round numbers to convey scale. Whatever the true figure, the project must have required thousands of workers over several years, a mobilization that drew on the labor and expertise of many regions.

The principal building material was baked brick, along with sun-dried mud brick, with timber and stone for particular features. Brick had been used in Mesopotamia for millennia, and the plain of Iraq provided clay. The traditions report that bricks of large size were used, and that workers were organized in teams. Material was also taken from earlier buildings. A well-known tradition describes al-Manṣūr considering the demolition of the Sasanian palace at Ctesiphon, with its great vaulted hall, to reuse its bricks; Khālid ibn Barmak is reported to have advised against it, on grounds of omen or of respect, and the caliph, after beginning the work, found the cost of demolition too high and stopped. The story is told in several versions, and it is difficult to know how much is historical, but it signals both the attraction of readily available materials and the symbolic weight of the Sasanian remains.

Financing came from the state treasury, drawing on the revenues of the provinces, especially Iraq. One tradition, preserved by al-Khaṭīb al-Baghdādī, gives a total expenditure of a little under five million dirhams, a modest sum by the standards of later Abbasid spending, and a figure that scholars have treated with caution. The tradition that al-Manṣūr was a careful and even stingy manager of funds may have influenced the way costs were remembered. Wages for laborers are also reported, at levels that, if accurate, indicate payment in coin, which would be consistent with the monetary character of the economy in Iraq.

The construction also brought social change. The project concentrated workers and their families, created demand for food and supplies, and drew merchants who followed the construction camps. Supplies of grain, timber, and other goods arrived by river and canal. These flows laid the foundations for the commercial districts that would soon develop beside the Round City (Division VI). The building of Baghdad, in other words, did not only produce a capital; it created an economic and social network that would outgrow the planned enclosure.

Everyday Life and Social History

Building Baghdad: The Workforce and Its Needs

The builders of Baghdad rarely appear by name in the sources, which concentrate on the caliph, his advisers, and the plan. What can be inferred is that thousands of people lived for years on or near the building site. Brickmakers dug and formed clay and fired it in kilns, requiring fuel that had to be brought in; masons and carpenters worked under architects and surveyors; carters and boatmen carried bricks, timber, and food; cooks, water carriers, and small traders supplied the workers' daily needs. Some workers were probably free wage earners and others, perhaps, dependent laborers, though the sources do not specify the proportions.

These workers came from different regions and religions, including Muslims and Christians, speakers of Arabic, Persian, and Aramaic, and others. Nearby monasteries and villages, whose agricultural and artisan populations were part of the local landscape, were affected by the arrival of the project. The evidence is thin, and the lives of these ordinary people can be recovered only through inference and comparison, an approach that historians of Abbasid society (Chapter 48) will develop further.

Section 44.29

Reconstructing Early Baghdad: Historical Texts, Urban Archaeology, and Uncertainty

The Round City presents an unusual problem. It was one of the most famous cities of the medieval world and the best described of the early Abbasid foundations, but it has left almost no visible remains. The area lies beneath the modern city of Baghdad, where continuous occupation, later building, river changes, and the destruction of the Mongol sack in 1258 (Chapter 63) have obscured or erased the early layers. Excavation of the Round City itself has not been possible, and surviving traces are limited. Knowledge therefore rests largely on texts.

Those texts have particular characteristics. Al-Yaʿqūbī's description is the earliest systematic account. Al-Ṭabarī preserves traditions about the founding. Al-Khaṭīb al-Baghdādī, writing in the eleventh century, compiled a large body of earlier descriptions, including measurements, but the dimensions he and others give do not always agree. Medieval writers used units of measurement, such as the cubit, whose lengths varied, and their figures sometimes served rhetorical rather than precise purposes. Modern reconstructions therefore involve choices about which sources to prioritize and how to convert units.

Scholars have disagreed on matters such as the exact diameter, the number and arrangement of the walls, the layout of the interior sectors, and how closely the design adhered to the textual descriptions. Some have questioned whether the Round City was ever fully realized in the form the texts describe or whether subsequent alterations changed it substantially. There is also debate on the relation between the Round City and the wider settlement: Jacob Lassner and others stress the distinction between the planned governmental enclosure and the urban sprawl that quickly grew around it. Françoise Micheau's work on Baghdad as an imperial foundation and the scholarship of Hugh Kennedy have emphasized the city's development as a process in which the Round City was an initial nucleus, not the full metropolis.

This distinction is essential. The Round City should not be read as if it already contained the city that later became famous for its markets, scholars, and population. The Baghdad of the ninth century was a vast, irregular agglomeration spread across both banks of the Tigris, with the Round City as only one part. Treating the early foundation and the later metropolis as a single entity leads to errors about size, population, and social composition. The sources' own vocabulary sometimes blurs the distinction, and historians must read carefully.

Historical Debate

Why Was the City Round? Design and Imperial Symbolism

The question: Was the Round City's circular design a practical choice, a symbolic statement, or the adoption of an older model?

Interpretation A — Practical and defensive: The circle minimized the length of walls relative to the enclosed area, offered uniform defense, and placed the caliph equidistant from the gates and sectors. On this view, the design reflected the security concerns of a ruler shaken by recent revolts.
Interpretation B — Symbolic and inherited: Other scholars see in the circle an expression of cosmic or imperial order, drawing on Iranian, Mesopotamian, or Hellenistic traditions of planned or circular cities. The role of astrologers in the founding, and Sasanian circular foundations in Fārs, are cited in support, though without evidence of direct derivation.

Evidence: The sources report explanations for the design, but they are late and may be rationalizations. No contemporary text states the planners' intentions, and the archaeological evidence is minimal.

Why it matters: The question bears on how historians read the relationship between Abbasid ideology and urban form, and on how much the Abbasids borrowed from earlier imperial traditions. This chapter treats practical and symbolic readings as compatible and treats any claim of direct architectural derivation as unproven.

Student task: Evaluate which kinds of evidence could distinguish a practical from a symbolic motive, and explain why such evidence may be unavailable.

Division VI

Baghdad's Early Court, Communities, and Urban Economy

Section 44.30

Markets, Suburbs, and the Growth beyond the Round City

The Round City was conceived as a government enclosure, but no capital can function without markets, and the demand for goods and services created by a court, a garrison, and a large construction workforce attracted commerce almost at once. According to the sources, shops were first set up in the arcades along the four main avenues inside the walls. Within a decade, however, al-Manṣūr moved them out. The traditional date is 157 AH (773–774), and the reasons given include security: traders and visitors from outside the city might spy on the caliph's household, and the crowds and activity of a commercial district were difficult to reconcile with the controlled access the plan sought. Some traditions connect the move to the visit of a foreign envoy, but this detail is uncertain.

The markets were relocated to al-Karkh, a district to the south of the Round City, along the Karkhāyā canal. The site was already inhabited, and the caliph's decision stimulated rapid growth. Al-Karkh became the principal commercial district of western Baghdad, with specialized markets (sūqs) organized by trade, an arrangement that medieval writers described in detail and that would characterize Middle Eastern cities for centuries. The caliph is reported to have supervised the layout, assigning plots and ensuring the supply of water and facilities. Al-Karkh was thus partly planned and partly spontaneous, a mixture that shaped the character of the growing metropolis.

The relocation reveals something about the regime's priorities. The security of the caliphal enclosure came before the convenience of commerce, but the commercial district was not suppressed; it was given a defined place, and the caliph's interest in its prosperity was evident. The distinction between a restricted political center and a vibrant commercial periphery would remain a feature of Baghdad's topography. It also shows that the city's economic life developed alongside, rather than within, the initial plan.

Growth did not stop at al-Karkh. Villages and estates in the surrounding region were absorbed by the expanding city, and the construction of residences for members of the court and the army led to the development of new suburbs. By al-Manṣūr's death in 775, Baghdad was already something more than the Round City: a metropolitan region in formation, whose population and economy extended well beyond the walls. The next sections follow that growth.

Section 44.31

River Crossings, al-Ruṣāfa, and the Developing Urban Landscape

Baghdad developed on both banks of the Tigris. The Round City and al-Karkh lay on the west bank, but by the late 760s a second major settlement had begun on the east. The impetus was the return of al-Manṣūr's son and heir al-Mahdī from his post in Rayy, bringing a large Khurāsānī army. The sources report that the troops camped on the east bank and that a settlement grew up around them, with a palace for al-Mahdī and a mosque. This district was called al-Ruṣāfa (or the Camp of al-Mahdī) and would become one of the principal quarters of the city.

The existence of a second center had several implications. It created a division of the city into the "Western side" and the "Eastern side," linked by bridges of boats, which the sources describe and which allowed movement between the banks. It gave the heir apparent his own residence and base, in a pattern in which the princes' households became centers of patronage and military support. And it placed a large part of the Khurāsānī army in a settlement distinct from the Round City, so that the troops were near the capital but not within its walls, a layout that reflected both the caliph's caution and the need for space.

The development of the east bank also illustrates the way the city grew through the initiatives of different actors. Members of the Abbasid family, commanders, officials, and merchants acquired land and built residences. Estates (qaṭāʾiʿ) were granted by the caliph and developed by their recipients, and the resulting pattern was more irregular than the Round City's geometry. The Tigris, with its ports, quays, and ferry points, was the artery of this growth, and boats carried goods and passengers between the districts and along the river to Wāsiṭ, Basra, and beyond.

By 775, then, Baghdad consisted of several distinct elements: the Round City, with its government enclosure; al-Karkh and other western suburbs; the palace of al-Khuld on the river; and the growing quarters of the east bank. The terms used in the sources sometimes refer to the Round City alone and sometimes to the whole agglomeration, and modern writers must take care to distinguish them. The city's later size and wealth were the product of a process that had begun but was far from complete at the time of the founder's death.

Historical Vocabulary
Miṣr: The Garrison City

The word miṣr (pl. amṣār) refers to the military settlements founded during the early Islamic conquests, such as Kūfa, Basra, and Fusṭāṭ in Egypt, where Arab soldiers were stationed and registered for pay. These cities became major urban centers with mixed populations. Baghdad differed from the earlier amṣār: it was founded by a caliph as an imperial capital, not as a conquest-era camp, and it was planned for the court and administration rather than primarily as a tribal garrison. Historians use the contrast to show how the Abbasids reshaped urban foundation as an instrument of state power.

Section 44.32

The People Who Built and Served the Capital

The population of early Baghdad was diverse, and the sources, though focused on the elite, give glimpses of its range. At the center were the caliph's family and household: wives, concubines, children, relatives, eunuchs, and servants. Around them stood the court officials, the secretaries and ministers, the chamberlains, and the guards. The soldiers, many of them Khurāsānī, lived in the quarters assigned to them and in the camps on the east bank, and they brought their families and dependents. The army's presence shaped the city's social composition and its economy.

The civilian population included merchants, shopkeepers, craftsmen, porters, boatmen, and laborers. Many came from Iraqi towns such as Kūfa, Basra, and Wāsiṭ, from the Sawād villages, and from further afield, including Syria, Iran, and Arabia. Some were attracted by opportunities of trade or employment; some were brought to work on the construction; others were soldiers' clients or slaves. Slavery was an ordinary feature of the urban economy, with enslaved people serving as domestics, laborers, and in other roles. Chapter 48 examines slavery and social life in depth; here it is enough to note that the capital's development relied on both free and unfree labor.

Religious diversity was also a feature. The majority of the population was Muslim, but Christians, many belonging to the Church of the East (the "Nestorian" church of Iraq and Iran) and to Syriac Orthodox communities, lived in the region, and monasteries dotted the countryside. Jewish communities had a long history in Babylonia. Zoroastrian communities persisted in Iraq and more widely in Iran. The sources show Christians serving as physicians and secretaries, and the dynasty's reliance on non-Muslim expertise is one of the continuities with earlier regimes. The later prominence of Baghdad as a center of the Church of the East and of Jewish scholarship lies beyond 775, but their foundations were being laid.

Women are less visible, and the sources focus on royal women, whose influence, including that of al-Manṣūr's own relatives and of al-Mahdī's mother, appears in political narratives. For ordinary women, little direct evidence survives from this period. The early Baghdad population should therefore be pictured as a mixture of courtiers, soldiers, officials, merchants, workers, and slaves, speaking several languages and practicing several faiths, held together by the economic and political pull of the new capital.

Section 44.33

Courtly Learning, Literary Patronage, and Early Translation

Al-Manṣūr's court was not yet the center of the great translation movement of the ninth century, and the later institution known as the House of Wisdom did not yet exist in its mature form. Yet the intellectual life of the court was real, and it laid foundations for what followed. The caliph valued eloquence and practical knowledge, and the secretarial class cultivated Arabic prose style, administrative skill, and the literature of advice (adab).

The figure who best illustrates this is Ibn al-Muqaffaʿ (c. 720–c. 756/759), a Persian-born secretary who had served officials in Iran and Iraq. He translated from Middle Persian into Arabic, most famously the animal fables of Kalīla wa-Dimna, whose origins lay in an Indian work, and wrote on statecraft, including an essay addressed to the caliph on the conduct of the court and army and the need for a consistent legal code. His writing helped to establish Arabic prose as a vehicle for political and ethical reflection. He was executed in Basra, apparently on the orders of the governor, in circumstances the sources explain in different ways, and his career shows both the opportunities and the dangers of life at court.

Other intellectual activity included the compilation of historical and religious works. Ibn Isḥāq's biography of the Prophet, according to tradition, was composed in this milieu under the caliph's patronage. Astrology and astronomy attracted court attention, as the role of Nawbakht and Māshāʾallāh in the founding indicates. Tradition reports that al-Manṣūr summoned a physician from the medical school tradition at Jundīshāpūr, Jūrjīs ibn Bukhtīshūʿ, to treat him, beginning a long association between the dynasty and a Christian family of doctors. A tradition of Indian astronomical texts reaching Baghdad in the 770s and being translated is also reported, though the details are uncertain.

These episodes should be treated as beginnings. The systematic translation of Greek scientific and philosophical works, the establishment of libraries, and the growth of scholarly communities developed more fully under al-Mahdī, Hārūn, and al-Maʾmūn, and Chapter 46 gives them full treatment. What is clear for the period of this chapter is that the court's culture was open to Persian, Indian, Greek, and Syriac learning through its secretaries, physicians, and astrologers, and that Baghdad, as a new city without a long literary tradition, attracted scholars and writers from elsewhere.

Section 44.34

Baghdad and the Emerging Networks of an Imperial Capital

By its first decade, Baghdad had become the hub of several overlapping networks. The administrative network was the most direct: orders from the caliph and his officials went out to governors and tax collectors, and reports and revenues came back. The postal service connected the capital with the provinces along established roads, and officials, couriers, and informants traveled constantly. The caliph's authority, exercised from a single place, depended on the speed and reliability of these communications.

Commercial networks converged on the city. The Tigris and Euphrates, the canals, and the roads brought grain, textiles, metals, and luxury goods from Iraq, Iran, Syria, and farther afield. Goods from the Gulf and the Indian Ocean arrived through Basra, and caravans from Khurāsān and the Iranian plateau came through the Zagros passes. Baghdad's position made it a natural point of exchange, and the merchants who gathered in al-Karkh formed part of a trading system that extended across the Islamic world (Chapter 51 treats the wider networks).

Religious and scholarly networks also developed. The pilgrimage route from Iraq to Mecca, the Darb Zubayda in its later form, began to carry pilgrims from Baghdad and the east, and the caliph's role in sponsoring the pilgrimage connected the capital to the holy cities. Scholars, jurists, and transmitters of tradition from Kūfa, Basra, Medina, Syria, and Khurāsān traveled to Baghdad, drawn by patronage, students, and the opportunity for discussion. The movement of officials and soldiers between provinces and capital created further links.

The governing question of this division is how a royal foundation became a city serving many constituencies. The answer lies in the convergence of political, economic, and cultural functions. The caliph's decision to build a capital created a concentration of demand and authority that drew in commerce and population, and the institutions of the empire, once centered on the city, gave it a role far beyond the court. Baghdad in 775 was not yet the great metropolis of the ninth century, but it had acquired the connections that would make it one.

Division VII

The Abbasid Empire Beyond Baghdad

Section 44.35

Khurāsān, Transoxiana, and the Eastern Frontiers

The east was the revolution's base, and its governance remained a central concern. After Abū Muslim's death, Khurāsān was governed by officials appointed by the caliph, and members of the Abbasid family and trusted commanders were sent to oversee it. Al-Mahdī's residence at Rayy, from around 760, gave the dynasty a forward base. The province's Arab settlers, Iranian landholders, and the local rulers of surrounding regions had to be managed. Tabaristan, on the southern Caspian, had been ruled by local Iranian princes, the Ispahbads, and was brought under Abbasid control around 760, and Khālid ibn Barmak later served as its governor.

Beyond Khurāsān lay Transoxiana (Mā warāʾ al-nahr), a region of oasis cities such as Bukhārā and Samarqand and of Sogdian, Turkic, and other populations. Here the Abbasids inherited the frontier relationships established under the Umayyads, including relations with local princes and with Turkic groups and Tibetan and Chinese powers. In 751, near the Talas river in what is now Kazakhstan or Kyrgyzstan, an Abbasid force and its allies met a Tang Chinese army under Gao Xianzhi. The Tang forces were defeated, with the defection of Karluk Turks reported as decisive.

Talas has often been given an exaggerated significance. It was a regional engagement in a wider contest for influence in Central Asia, and the Tang withdrawal from the region had more to do with the An Lushan rebellion that began in 755 and weakened the Tang than with a single battle. The claim that the captured Chinese prisoners introduced papermaking to the Islamic world rests on late and disputed traditions; paper was known and made in Central Asia before Talas, and its adoption in Baghdad, usually dated to the 790s, is treated in Chapter 46. The battle is best understood as one episode in the shifting politics of the eastern frontier.

More important for the Abbasids was the long-term process by which Khurāsān and Transoxiana were integrated into the empire, through military settlement, conversion, and local alliances. The east supplied soldiers, revenue, and a cultural orientation that would deeply influence the dynasty. It was also the source of repeated revolts, and the Abbasids' management of this region, balancing control and accommodation, would shape the later development of regional dynasties examined in Chapters 49 and 50.

Section 44.36

Syria, Egypt, North Africa, and the Survival of Umayyad Power in Iberia

In the western provinces, Abbasid authority was real but uneven. Syria, after the early revolts, was governed by Abbasid family members, among them Ṣāliḥ ibn ʿAlī, with garrisons and appointed officials. Egypt, the great grain-producing province, was administered by governors who sent revenue to Iraq, and its Coptic Christian majority and Arab Muslim settlers continued to live under the tax regimes inherited from earlier periods. Both provinces remained within the empire, though their elites had lost status and their relation to the center was one of subordination rather than partnership.

Further west, control weakened. In Ifrīqiya (roughly modern Tunisia and eastern Algeria), the Abbasids faced rebellions by Khārijite groups, Ibadi and Ṣufrī, who rejected the Abbasid caliphate. Ibadi forces led by Abū al-Khaṭṭāb seized Qayrawān (Kairouan) in the late 750s, and an Abbasid army retook it in 761. Order was restored more durably in 772 under the governor Yazīd ibn Ḥātim. In the further Maghrib, independent Khārijite polities emerged, and the region's Berber populations, who had their own grievances against the conquest-era regime, were not easily governed from Iraq. Later chapters on the fragmentation of Abbasid authority take up the consequences.

The most dramatic limit on Abbasid sovereignty was in al-Andalus. ʿAbd al-Raḥmān ibn Muʿāwiya, a grandson of the Umayyad caliph Hishām who had escaped the massacres of 750, made his way across North Africa and landed in Iberia in 755. In 756 he defeated the governor Yūsuf al-Fihrī near Córdoba and established himself as emir. He did not claim the title of caliph, but he refused submission to the Abbasids, and the Umayyad emirate became a permanent independent state. Tradition reports that al-Manṣūr, who admired his resourcefulness, called him the "Hawk of Quraysh," though the attribution is uncertain. An Abbasid attempt to recover Iberia, through a local supporter named al-ʿAlāʾ ibn Mughīth who rebelled in 763, failed; his head is said to have been sent to the caliph at Mecca.

The survival of Umayyad power in Córdoba meant that, within a few years of the revolution, the Muslim world no longer had a single political authority. The Abbasids remained the dominant power and claimed universal legitimacy, but their claim was no longer coextensive with their control. The event is a milestone in the territorial limits of the caliphate, and it set a pattern that would recur: the fragmentation of the empire into regional states within a framework of nominal caliphal unity, a process treated in Chapter 50.

Section 44.37

Byzantium, the Caucasus, and the Diplomacy of Al-Manṣūr

The northern frontier faced the Byzantine Empire, ruled during almost all of al-Manṣūr's reign by Constantine V, a capable military leader. The Umayyad-era pattern of annual raids across the Taurus continued, and the civil war of the revolution had given the Byzantines an opportunity. In the early 750s Constantine's forces captured and sacked frontier cities, including Melitene (Malatya) and Theodosiopolis (Karin, modern Erzurum). The Abbasids responded in the later 750s by restoring and refortifying frontier towns such as Malatya, Mopsuestia (al-Maṣṣīṣa), and Adana, and by settling garrisons. These measures laid the groundwork for the organized frontier zone of the later eighth century.

Al-Manṣūr's policy mixed force with diplomacy. Raiding expeditions, usually in the summer, continued, but the caliph avoided large-scale conquests and concentrated on defending the frontier. Contacts between the two empires also included truces and exchanges of prisoners, though the chronology of particular arrangements is uncertain. The Byzantines were themselves occupied with wars on the Balkan frontier against the Bulgars and with the internal conflict over religious images. The result was a frontier characterized by mutual raiding rather than decisive campaigns, and the Abbasids did not attempt to take Constantinople, in contrast to the Umayyad sieges earlier in the century.

The Caucasus raised separate problems. Armenia and Albania (Arrān) were controlled through governors and through arrangements with local Armenian noble families, whose cooperation was necessary for tax collection. The Khazars, a Turkic polity north of the Caucasus, raided into Armenia in the early 760s, and Abbasid governors responded with military and diplomatic measures. A governor of Armenia, Yazīd ibn Usayd al-Sulamī, is reported to have married a daughter of the Khazar khagan as part of a diplomatic arrangement, though the marriage ended with her death. Armenian resentment of Abbasid taxation led to a major revolt in the mid-770s, just at the end of the period this chapter covers.

Throughout, the distinction between expeditions and incorporation is important. Abbasid armies could reach deep into Byzantine territory or the Caucasus, but they did not establish lasting provincial administration beyond the frontier zone. The northern policy was one of security and management, consistent with al-Manṣūr's general approach of consolidating the empire's existing territory.

Section 44.38

Imperial Unity and the Limits of Direct Rule

Taken together, the evidence of the provinces shows that Abbasid government in 775 was an arrangement of different degrees of control rather than a uniformly administered state. In Iraq, the dynasty ruled directly through officials and garrisons. In Khurāsān and the east, it ruled through governors backed by the army, with significant dependence on local landholders and princes. In Syria, Egypt, and the Jazīra, it appointed governors but relied on existing tax systems and local elites. In North Africa, control was intermittent. In Iberia, it was absent.

This variety reflected both the limits of premodern communication and the political logic of the regime. A government centered in Iraq could not supervise every district. It relied on a combination of appointed officials, military force, the postal and intelligence network, fiscal oversight, and the cooperation of local elites who had their own interests in preserving order. Governors might be moved or dismissed, but the process was slow, and a governor with an army and local support could become a quasi-independent ruler. Al-Manṣūr's own career showed the danger.

An imperial capital did not therefore imply uniform control over every province. The caliph in Baghdad was a source of legitimacy, a collector of revenue, and a military commander, but he governed through intermediaries whose loyalty had to be secured by incentives and monitored by informants. The empire's unity was real in its shared religion, its fiscal and legal framework, and its recognition of the caliph's name in the Friday sermon, but it coexisted with significant regional autonomy.

This structure had strengths: it allowed the empire to adapt to local conditions and to draw on local expertise. It also carried the seeds of later fragmentation, as regional governors accumulated power and as the center's ability to enforce its will diminished. The relationship between central authority and provincial autonomy would be a central theme of the Abbasid age, to which the next chapters, beginning with the fiscal and military institutions of Chapter 45, return.

Division VIII

Succession, Consolidation, and Historical Significance

Section 44.39

The Succession Struggle: ʿĪsā ibn Mūsā and the Rise of al-Mahdī

Al-Saffāḥ's arrangement had named al-Manṣūr as his successor and ʿĪsā ibn Mūsā as the next heir. ʿĪsā, a nephew of both men, was a capable commander who had defeated Muḥammad al-Nafs al-Zakiyya and Ibrāhīm, and who held a prominent position in Kūfa. For al-Manṣūr, who wished to transmit the caliphate to his own son, Muḥammad, later called al-Mahdī, ʿĪsā's established claim was an obstacle. Allowing the succession to pass to a cousin's line would have broken the concentration of authority that al-Manṣūr had worked to build.

The sources describe a long campaign of pressure. Al-Manṣūr reportedly urged ʿĪsā to step aside, offering inducements, and when ʿĪsā resisted, used threats and the manipulation of public opinion. Courtiers and commanders were sounded out, and the caliph exploited his control of appointments, revenue, and the army. Around 764–765 (147 AH), al-Mahdī was designated first heir, with ʿĪsā relegated to second place. Al-Mahdī was then the governor of the east, based at Rayy, and he had married into influential families. The sources preserve different accounts of how willingly ʿĪsā accepted the demotion, and later writers sometimes shaded the story to favor or criticize the caliph.

Oaths of allegiance to al-Mahdī were taken from the leading figures of the court and army, and the formal ceremonies were intended to bind the elite. ʿĪsā nevertheless remained second in the succession when al-Manṣūr died in 775. His definitive exclusion was pursued under al-Mahdī, after this chapter's chronological endpoint, with sources placing his further renunciation around 160 AH (776–777 CE). The episode is significant because it shows a caliph shaping the succession by his own authority rather than by family consensus, and because it shifted the dynasty's center of gravity from the wider Abbasid family to the descendants of al-Manṣūr. Later Abbasid caliphs were, with few exceptions, his descendants.

The succession politics also reveal the importance of the household. Al-Mahdī's mother, Umm Mūsā of the Ḥimyar tribe of South Arabia, and other royal women played roles in the court's maneuvers, and marriages were used to build alliances. The caliphate was becoming a dynastic institution with its own rules of precedence, a development that would have major consequences for the rivalries among al-Rashīd's sons in the next generation.

Section 44.40

Al-Manṣūr's Death in 775 and the Accession of al-Mahdī

In 775 al-Manṣūr, then in his sixties, set out for Mecca on pilgrimage. He had been in poor health, according to the sources, and fell ill on the road. He died in October 775 near Mecca, at a place named in the sources as Bi'r Maymūn. The sources report that he was buried in or near Mecca and that, to prevent desecration of his grave, a number of graves were dug so that none could tell which held him, a tradition reflecting the memory of how the Abbasids had treated Umayyad tombs.

The succession passed smoothly. The chamberlain, Rabīʿ ibn Yūnus, who was with the caliph's party, secured the oaths of the army and officials for al-Mahdī, while al-Mahdī himself was in Baghdad. ʿĪsā ibn Mūsā, whatever his earlier feelings, took the oath. The orderliness of the transition contrasts with the crises of 754 and demonstrates the success of al-Manṣūr's efforts. Al-Mahdī was recognized throughout the empire, and the sources report that he began his reign with gestures of generosity, including the release of prisoners and the distribution of money, though these details come from sources inclined to compare the new caliph favorably with his stern father.

Al-Manṣūr's reputation among later writers was double. He was praised as the true founder of the Abbasid state, a shrewd and tireless ruler who laid the foundations of the empire's institutions and its capital. He was also remembered as stern, suspicious, and ruthless, a man who killed his allies and rivals. The sources attribute to him a large treasury left to his successor, but the figures given vary and are not reliable. His reign is better judged by what it achieved: the elimination of rivals, the stabilization of the regime, the foundation of Baghdad, and the transmission of power to his own line.

The limits of his consolidation were also apparent. The east remained restless, the western provinces were only partly controlled, Alid opposition persisted, and the fiscal system was heavily dependent on administrative expedients. Al-Mahdī would face a new set of challenges, including renewed eastern unrest and the problem of reconciling the dynasty with its critics. This chapter does not follow his reign; it stops at the transfer of power, leaving the fuller account of al-Mahdī and his successors to later chapters.

Section 44.41

From Revolutionary Movement to Imperial System: What Had Changed by 775?

By 775 the Abbasid regime had traveled a long way from the revolution. It had begun as a coalition of discontented groups united by opposition to the Umayyads and had become a dynastic monarchy centered on a purpose-built capital. The transformation involved violence, adaptation, and institution-building, and its results can be assessed along several dimensions.

Many Umayyad practices survived. The fiscal system, based on land and poll taxes administered through registers, remained. The dīwāns, the secretarial class, and the postal service continued. Coinage followed Umayyad models. The caliph's role as leader of the pilgrimage, patron of the Friday sermon, and commander of the army was inherited. The Arabic language of administration, established under the Umayyads, remained in place. Much of the empire's daily functioning, in other words, was continuous.

At the same time, much had changed. The center of gravity had moved from Syria to Iraq and Iran. The ruling elite included a much larger component of Khurāsānī soldiers, Iranian officials, and eastern clients, and the Syrian Arab aristocracy had lost its dominance. The dynasty's legitimacy rested on kinship with the Prophet and on the claim that it had fulfilled the revolution's promise, rather than on Umayyad dynastic precedent. Authority had become more concentrated: al-Manṣūr had eliminated the powerful partners of the early years, and the caliphate was passing within a single line. The court had acquired a more formal and Iranian-influenced character. And a new capital, Baghdad, had been built to express and secure this order.

The chapter's central conclusion is that the Abbasid achievement was neither a mere change of dynasty nor a complete rupture. It was a reorganization of imperial authority that combined inherited institutions with new political foundations. The Khurāsānī coalition mattered because it supplied the army and part of the elite, but its eventual subordination to the dynasty was the condition of the regime's survival. Baghdad mattered because it gave the dynasty a secure, controllable, and symbolically powerful center in the most productive and best-connected region of the empire.

The next chapter turns from the founding to the working of the system: the viziers, secretaries, and departments; the structure and costs of the army; the fiscal arrangements of the Sawād and other provinces; and the economic life that supported the caliphate. The Baghdad of the founder, a government enclosure and a growing market district, would become the capital of a state whose institutions are the subject of Chapter 45.

Cause and Effect

Revolutionary Success, Dynastic Consolidation, and Urban Foundation

Long-term causes

Umayyad political crisis, grievances of Khurāsānī settlers and non-Arab Muslims, competing claims within the Prophet's family, and the shifting weight of Iraq and the east.

Short-term causes

The victory of 750, the independent power of Abū Muslim and ʿAbd Allāh ibn ʿAlī, the Alid revolts, and the insecurity of the interim palaces.

Immediate effects

Elimination of internal rivals, defeat of Alid claimants, creation of a guard and informant system, and the founding of Baghdad.

Long-term and regional consequences

A dynastic monarchy based in Iraq, a mixed ruling elite, a major commercial capital, and the loss of Iberia and parts of North Africa as the first signs of later fragmentation.

Historical complexity: These outcomes were not inevitable. Al-Manṣūr's choices mattered, as did the contingencies of the revolts, and the sources' accounts reflect later judgments about their success.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how the Abbasids converted a revolutionary victory into an imperial system between 750 and 775. The victory over Marwān II did not itself create a government. The new regime depended on a coalition of Khurāsānī soldiers, Iranian and Arab supporters, Kufan allies, and members of the Prophet's wider family, whose expectations diverged as soon as the fighting ended. The sources that tell this story were composed under the dynasty, so the chapter has treated them critically and used coins, a Syriac chronicle, and archaeology as checks where possible.

The Abbasid claim to legitimacy rested on descent from al-ʿAbbās, the Prophet's uncle, on the promise of the revolution, and on the claim of divine favor. Rival Alid claimants, descended from ʿAlī and Fāṭima, argued that the leadership belonged to them. The dispute over what the revolutionary slogan had meant was never fully resolved, and it shaped the Alid revolts of 762–763 and later Shia political activity.

Al-Saffāḥ's short reign established the executive government, mostly from Iraqi sites such as Kūfa, al-Hāshimiyya, and al-Anbār. It combined reprisals against the Umayyad leadership with continuity in the administrative staff, and it left authority divided among the caliph, Abbasid family governors, and Abū Muslim, who controlled Khurāsān.

Al-Manṣūr's reign was defined by consolidation against former partners. He defeated his uncle ʿAbd Allāh ibn ʿAlī with Abū Muslim's help and then killed Abū Muslim in 755. Eastern movements, including Sunbādh's revolt, the Rāwandiyya, and others, showed how strong revolutionary expectations remained. In 762–763 he defeated the Alid revolts of Muḥammad al-Nafs al-Zakiyya in Medina and Ibrāhīm ibn ʿAbd Allāh in Basra, ending the most serious challenge to the dynasty's claim.

The center of imperial power moved from Syria to Iraq and Iran. Iraq's revenue, waterways, and location between Syria and Iran, together with its distance from the former Umayyad establishment, made it a natural base. The Khurāsānī army became the principal military instrument, and the court and bureaucracy absorbed Iranian officials and styles, though the elite was heterogeneous rather than simply Persian.

Institutions developed incrementally. The secretarial class, the dīwāns, the emerging vizierate, and the postal and intelligence service gave the caliph the means to govern at a distance. Legitimacy was made visible through the sermon, the coinage, the pilgrimage, and a more formal court. Chapter 45 examines the mature fiscal and military system.

Baghdad, founded in 762 as Madīnat al-Salām, was the physical expression of the new order. Its site between the Tigris and Euphrates offered supply, communication, and security. The Round City, with its walls, four gates, and central palace and mosque, was a planned government enclosure. Its design, labor, cost, and exact dimensions are known only through medieval texts and modern reconstruction, and many reported figures are source-dependent.

The city grew beyond its plan. Markets were moved to al-Karkh, the east-bank district of al-Ruṣāfa developed around al-Mahdī's army, and a diverse population of courtiers, soldiers, officials, merchants, laborers, and slaves, Muslim, Christian, Jewish, and Zoroastrian, gathered there. Courtly learning, including the work of Ibn al-Muqaffaʿ, began to connect the capital to Persian, Indian, Greek, and Syriac traditions, though the great translation movement lay in the future.

Beyond Baghdad, Abbasid authority was uneven. The east was integrated through military settlement and local alliances; Syria and Egypt were governed through appointed officials; North Africa was contested; and in 756 ʿAbd al-Raḥmān I established an independent Umayyad emirate in Córdoba. On the northern frontier, defense and diplomacy took precedence over conquest. The empire's unity coexisted with regional autonomy.

By 775 al-Manṣūr had secured the succession for his son al-Mahdī at the expense of ʿĪsā ibn Mūsā, concentrating the caliphate in his own line. The Abbasids had preserved much of the Umayyad fiscal and administrative framework while changing the geography, personnel, ideology, and spatial form of the state. The result was a dynastic monarchy centered on Baghdad, whose institutions are the subject of the chapters that follow.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has shown that political victory and political order are different achievements. The Abbasid revolution overthrew the Umayyads in 750, but it took a quarter-century of conflict, adaptation, and construction to create a stable imperial system. In that process the dynasty redefined legitimacy, relocated the center of the empire, absorbed new military and administrative constituencies, and built a capital. These were not predetermined outcomes; they were produced by choices, struggles, and contingencies that the sources preserve unevenly.

The chapter also illustrates larger processes in Middle Eastern history. The shift from Damascus to Baghdad moved the caliphate toward the lands of the old Sasanian and Mesopotamian empires, as Chapter 2's discussion of the Tigris and Euphrates anticipated. The incorporation of eastern soldiers and Iranian officials points toward the Persianate culture examined in Chapter 49. The loss of Iberia and the uneven control of North Africa foreshadow the political fragmentation traced in Chapter 50. The tension between caliphal religious authority and monarchical practice recurs in later Abbasid and successor states.

Baghdad's founding also reveals how cities shape and reflect political power. A government enclosure became a commercial metropolis, and a planned center was surrounded by the growth that no plan could control. The institutions, scholarship, and social life of that metropolis are the subjects of Chapters 45 through 48, and its later fate, including its destruction in 1258, is treated in Chapter 63.

For the history of the Middle East as a region, the Abbasid foundation matters because it created a durable framework within which Arabic-speaking and Persian-speaking, Muslim and non-Muslim communities interacted for centuries, while leaving questions of legitimacy, regional autonomy, and the proper relation between religion and rule open. Understanding how that framework was built is essential to understanding what it later enabled and what strains it bore.
