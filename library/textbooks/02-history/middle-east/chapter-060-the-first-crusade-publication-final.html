The First Crusade

A History of the Middle East: From Ancient Civilizations to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

In July 1099, armies that had departed western Europe under a religious vow broke into Jerusalem. For some survivors it seemed the culmination of a penitential pilgrimage; for many inhabitants of the city it was a catastrophe. The path between those experiences ran through diplomacy, massacres, sieges, alliances, and profound uncertainty.

The First Crusade is sometimes summarized as a simple military expedition from Europe to the Holy Land. Such a description conceals the competing political worlds the armies encountered. Their advance began with a Byzantine request for help against Turkish forces, but the papal summons at Clermont turned that request into an armed penitential pilgrimage with a far wider constituency and purpose. Neither the emperor nor the pope could wholly control the movement that followed.

The years 1095–1099 brought together Latin Christian reform, aristocratic military households, pilgrims with few resources, Byzantine diplomacy, and the interests of rulers across Anatolia, Syria, and Egypt. The expedition did not fight one uniform Muslim government. Seljuk and Syrian authorities competed with one another, while the Fatimid court in Cairo governed according to its own priorities. Armenian and Syriac Christian political communities likewise pursued local interests rather than acting as automatic supporters of all western armies.

The movement also inflicted violence far from Jerusalem. In 1096 contingents associated with crusading attacked Jewish communities in the Rhineland, killing people and coercing conversions. Some Christian officials offered protection, but often failed to prevent catastrophe. Those events belong to the First Crusade’s history as surely as the later battles at Nicaea and Antioch. The chapter examines the Hebrew chronicles as sources that preserve both persecution and the memory constructed around it.

When the princes reached Constantinople, their military cooperation with Alexios I made important victories possible, but the relationship was burdened by conflicting expectations about imperial territories. Nicaea surrendered to Byzantine officials in 1097; Antioch later became the object of an argument over independent Latin rule. At Edessa the newcomers entered an Armenian political crisis. These developments demonstrate that the journey toward Jerusalem was simultaneously creating new states and new diplomatic disputes.

Antioch became the expedition’s most severe sustained ordeal. The crusaders endured hunger and a prolonged siege, captured most of the city, and then confronted Kerbogha’s coalition. Religious belief helped organize their understanding of hardship, but a military explanation must also account for roads, food, horses, the politics of allied commanders, and the timing of an army’s arrival. Victory did not erase quarrels among the expedition’s own leaders.

Jerusalem had returned to Fatimid control in 1098. Its capture in July 1099 was therefore a confrontation with Egyptian authority, not the final battle against a single Seljuk army. The victors killed many Muslim and Jewish inhabitants. Contemporary depictions of extreme bloodshed include rhetorical and scriptural imagery; the event’s cruelty can be established without inventing a precise tally of deaths. Describing the killing directly is necessary to understanding the political and human consequences of the expedition.

The twenty sections that follow move from the political and religious conditions of 1095 to the immediate settlement after Ascalon in August 1099, with only a brief epilogue on Baldwin I in 1100. Chapter 59 explained the preceding transformation of Anatolia; later chapters will treat the developed Crusader states and subsequent wars. Here the emphasis is the expedition itself: why people joined, how they traveled and fought, what contemporaries recorded, and why its outcomes remained fragile.

Historical Context

The First Crusade began amid the political consequences of the Seljuk expansion discussed in Chapter 57, the Seljuk institutions examined in Chapter 58, and the transformation of Anatolia presented in Chapter 59. In 1095 Byzantine rulers had not restored unified inland control, Syrian governments competed after the death of Malik Shah, and Egypt’s Fatimid court exercised independent influence in Palestine. Latin Christians viewed the Eastern churches and the sacred places of Jerusalem through their own reform-era commitments.

This chapter concentrates on the campaign of 1095–1099. Its organization follows the initiative and recruitment, travel to Byzantium, Anatolian and Edessene campaigns, the siege of Antioch, and Jerusalem. The 1100 coronation of Baldwin I is a brief concluding reference only. The later County of Tripoli, the mature Latin states, and later Crusades are reserved for subsequent chapters.

Division I — The Eastern Mediterranean, Papal Reform, and the Call to Crusade

Section 60.1

60.1 — The Eastern Mediterranean before the First Crusade

The eastern Mediterranean in 1095 was a network of competing governments rather than two armies waiting for a religious war. Alexios I Komnenos ruled from Constantinople, but Byzantine authority over much of inland Anatolia remained contested after decades of civil wars and regional military settlements. The Seljuks of Rum, associated with Kilij Arslan I, were not the same government as the Great Seljuk empire. Their ability to direct Turkish military and pastoral groups differed by district. Coastal cities, agricultural communities, fortresses, and commercial routes maintained relationships with several authorities at once. A traveler could cross changing jurisdictions without crossing a clearly marked national border.

East of Anatolia, the death of the Great Seljuk ruler Malik Shah in 1092 had aggravated rivalries among princes, commanders, and their households. In Syria, Antioch was governed by Yaghi-Siyan; Aleppo and Damascus followed different political courses; Mosul had its own military leadership. Regional rivalries were not minor distractions: they affected who could send an army, which roads remained open, and whether a threatened city could expect assistance. The title or loyalty a commander professed did not always describe his actual resources. The crusaders would repeatedly exploit these divisions without necessarily understanding the political map that made them possible.

The Fatimid caliphate, based in Cairo, represented another center of authority. Its ruling house adhered to Ismaili Shiʿism and competed politically with Seljuk-aligned Sunni rulers, but their policies were not reducible to a doctrinal dispute. Under the powerful vizier al-Afdal Shahanshah, Egypt pursued interests in the coastal towns and Jerusalem, alternating diplomacy and force. The city of Jerusalem itself changed hands during these years: in 1095 it was not a permanent possession of the Egyptian government, whereas by the time the crusaders arrived in 1099 its defenders were Fatimid. This chronology is essential to understanding negotiations, war aims, and the final siege.

Christian communities also occupied different political and ecclesiastical positions. Greek-speaking Romans regarded the Byzantine emperor as their ruler; Armenian and Syriac churches retained distinct liturgical and institutional traditions; Latin Christians arriving from the West differed in religious discipline and political expectations. Within any one community, merchants, clergy, commanders, and cultivators might disagree over alliances. The inhabitants of Edessa, Antioch, and Jerusalem were not passive bystanders waiting for outsiders to make history. They made decisions about security, taxes, worship, and the survival of property as military conditions changed. Differences among Christians could matter as much as the distinction between Christian and Muslim rule.

The geography reinforced these divisions. Mountain passes linked Cilicia with the Anatolian plateau; the Orontes valley connected Antioch with inland Syria; coastal anchorages helped armies receive supplies, while fortified cities could resist for months. Pilgrimage routes, merchant networks, and diplomatic messengers joined distant regions even when governments were hostile. The First Crusade must therefore be studied both as a movement of Latin armies and as an episode within societies that already possessed elaborate traditions of government, religion, and exchange. Its success and brutality emerged through interactions with these older worlds, not through the arrival of an expedition in an empty landscape.

Section 60.2

60.2 — Latin Christendom, Pilgrimage, Reform, and Sacred Warfare

By the late eleventh century, Latin Christian pilgrimage joined physical travel to an expectation of religious benefit. Jerusalem mattered because of its association with the life, death, and resurrection of Jesus and the Church of the Holy Sepulchre. Pilgrims also traveled to Rome and other sacred places; they encountered danger, expense, and local political negotiations on the road. The First Crusade did not invent pilgrimage. What changed was the joining of a long-distance devotional journey to organized armed service presented as a penitential undertaking. For many participants, the destination and the religious obligation remained inseparable from the campaigns undertaken to reach it.

Papal reform provided an institutional setting for that innovation. Reformers challenged certain practices of clerical appointment, debated the independence of ecclesiastical authority, and sought to regulate violence among Christians. Councils and preaching networks connected Rome to bishops, monasteries, and local patrons. Yet a movement that condemned private violence could also endorse warfare under specified religious purposes. The contradiction was addressed by distinguishing allegedly illegitimate fighting from armed action undertaken in obedience to a religious summons. Such reasoning developed within older Christian discussions of warfare and did not form a single, fully standardized body of crusading law in 1095.

The papacy did not control Europe as a modern executive government controls an army. Its appeals traveled through councils, clerics, aristocratic affinities, and local communities; individual households negotiated how to respond. Feudal obligations, inheritance claims, family reputation, and patronage remained important, but the religious choices were not merely disguises for these interests. Surviving wills and charters document substantial commitments of property made to finance journeys. Some participants hoped for advantage abroad, but many faced the real prospect of losing wealth, security, or their lives. It is historically misleading to reduce this varied movement either to pure selfless devotion or to greed.

The language of the enterprise also requires care. The modern historian's term First Crusade groups events that contemporaries did not always describe with a single name. Writers often used the vocabulary of a journey, pilgrimage, expedition, or cross-bearing. The cloth cross worn on clothing eventually became a recognized sign of the undertaking, but ritual forms were still developing. The later existence of organized crusading institutions should not be projected back upon Urban II's first preaching. What emerged in 1095–1096 was a mobilization in which participants, preachers, and chroniclers sometimes interpreted the obligations of the journey differently.

The connection between religious devotion and violence is central, not incidental. Crusaders could combine prayer, fasting, liturgical processions, relic veneration, and military assault within a single account of righteous action. At the same time, church leaders did not universally approve every act committed by contingents who took the cross. The attacks on Jewish communities in 1096 make that limitation particularly visible. A scholarly history must explain both the appeal of the expedition to its supporters and the damage it inflicted on people excluded from its understanding of sacred purpose. These questions will recur at Antioch and Jerusalem, where claims of divine favor accompanied documented killings.

Section 60.3

60.3 — Alexios I, the Council of Piacenza, and the Byzantine Appeal

Alexios I Komnenos had been emperor since 1081 and governed a state still capable of substantial mobilization, negotiation, and strategic recovery. The transformation of Anatolia described in Chapter 59 had deprived Constantinople of effective control over important inland routes and provincial centers. Byzantine authorities also confronted western and Balkan threats. The emperor's response combined military reform, fiscal expedients, alliances, and recruitment of foreign soldiers. Latin troops were already familiar to Byzantine commanders as useful but sometimes troublesome partners. An appeal for assistance was therefore not evidence that the empire had ceased to function; it belonged to a continuing diplomacy of manpower and war.

In March 1095 Byzantine envoys appeared at the Council of Piacenza, where Urban II presided over a large ecclesiastical gathering. Accounts of the appeal report a request for western help against Turkish forces and emphasize the difficulties confronting eastern Christians. The precise language of the embassy is not preserved as a Byzantine memorandum, and we should avoid supplying an invented text. Its timing mattered: the pope was already engaging with questions of church order, authority, and Christian cooperation. A request for soldiers could be integrated into a much larger program of religious mobilization, even if that was not what the emperor originally anticipated.

Alexios probably wished to secure forces that could be coordinated with imperial strategy, retake defensible towns, and reopen routes across western Anatolia. He had reasons to prefer commanders who recognized imperial diplomatic obligations and who would not treat recovered Byzantine possessions as prizes for themselves. Those aims differed from the religious and territorial ambitions subsequently expressed by Latin participants who sought Jerusalem. It would be wrong to assume that the emperor ordered or explicitly sanctioned an independent conquest of Palestine. Nor should one suppose that he was wholly surprised to encounter western enthusiasm: communication and preparation continued between the 1095 appeal and the arrival of the armies.

Byzantine perceptions of the West were also diverse. Normans had attacked Byzantine territory in earlier decades, yet western mercenaries served imperial armies and Latin merchants traded with eastern ports. The emperor therefore had experience both with cooperation and with conflict. Once large crusading contingents arrived, their food requirements, armed retainers, and different chains of command posed problems even before any campaign began. Negotiations over oaths and formerly imperial cities were designed to reduce those risks. Anna Komnene's later history depicts her father as a prudent manager of dangerous visitors; its privileged court perspective must be read alongside Latin explanations of the same encounters.

The Council of Piacenza helps define the chapter's analytical starting point. Byzantine need was a condition of the First Crusade, not a sufficient explanation for its creation. Between March and November 1095, papal initiative, preaching, and religious objectives changed the scale and meaning of assistance. Once people across Latin Europe began to prepare, the enterprise acquired a momentum that no individual emperor, pope, or prince could direct completely. The mismatch between the expectations of Constantinople and the intentions of some western leaders would first become visible at Nicaea and later erupt over the question of who possessed Antioch.

Section 60.4

60.4 — Urban II at Clermont: Preaching, Penance, and Historical Memory

At the Council of Clermont in November 1095, Pope Urban II issued an appeal for an armed journey eastward. The date conventionally assigned to the famous sermon is 27 November. The public message combined concern for eastern Christians, the religious attraction of Jerusalem, and a penitential promise for suitable participants. Urban's initiative drew upon the reform papacy's efforts to direct Christian society, but it also offered warriors a way to place dangerous military activity within an approved religious vocation. That combination helps explain why the announcement attracted a response well beyond the circle of bishops who heard it.

No authenticated stenographic transcript of Urban's Clermont address survives. Fulcher of Chartres, Robert the Monk, Baldric of Dol, Guibert of Nogent, and other writers supplied versions after the expedition had already achieved its famous result. Their accounts share themes but vary in political emphasis, emotional imagery, and details about the audience. The versions do not simply provide a set of interchangeable quotations from the pope. Some authors portrayed the eastern appeal primarily as a duty toward other Christians; others amplified descriptions of sacred places or the sufferings of pilgrims. Historians compare them with Urban's surviving letters and the wider conventions of ecclesiastical preaching.

The promised spiritual benefit should be explained with precision. Medieval penitential practice distinguished sin, confession, satisfaction, and ecclesiastical obligations, though the precise theology of indulgences developed further in subsequent centuries. Urban offered remission of penance associated with proper participation in the journey; this must not be flattened into a blanket guarantee that a violent participant would automatically receive absolution for any act. The undertaking also carried expectations about intention and discipline, even when actual behavior fell far short of those ideals. Written formulations from 1095–1096 and subsequent interpretations should be kept distinct.

Preaching spread through episcopal and monastic networks, through aristocratic households, and by figures such as Peter the Hermit. There was no single reception. Some nobles assembled organized retinues and arranged finances; others declined or delayed. People without large estates joined movements that had weaker resources and more uncertain leadership. The use of crosses on clothing gave individuals a visible sign of commitment, but there was not yet one universal administrative method for enrollment. The very success of recruitment intensified the difficulty of regulating travel, provisioning, and violence on the way to the East.

Clermont has acquired a later symbolic life as the supposed instant birth of every subsequent crusade. That retrospective perspective conceals the uncertainties of 1095. Urban could not foresee the exact route or political structures that would result, and the participants who began preparations had no shared operational plan for the sieges of Antioch or Jerusalem. The historian's task is to reconstruct a campaign's emerging purposes rather than read its final outcome backward into its origin. The chapter's next division therefore studies what the preaching actually set in motion: households raising resources, popular contingents moving before the princes, persecutions in the Rhineland, and difficult negotiations with Byzantium.

60.5 — Taking the Cross: Recruitment, Vows, Resources, and Households

Joining the expedition was a decision with consequences well beyond the battlefield. The powerful knight needed horses, servants, armor, weapons, provisions, and funds for an uncertain journey; a household also needed arrangements for land, dependents, and outstanding obligations. Charters describe gifts and mortgages made to monasteries or churches, sometimes with detailed provisions for relatives. Such documents reveal substantial financial sacrifice, although they cannot reveal every participant's inner motive. Families debated who should travel and who would manage property in the absence of an heir or patron. Women could join the journey or sustain it through household administration and local networks.

The aristocratic contingents were not homogeneous. Raymond of Toulouse, Godfrey of Bouillon, Bohemond of Taranto, and other leaders possessed different resources, political ties, and expectations. Some had military experience in campaigns against Byzantium or neighboring rulers. Others relied on networks of retainers who followed through bonds of service and kinship. Ordinary armed men were often connected to larger households rather than enrolled in a centralized papal army. The relationships among commanders, their entourages, and the clergy accompanying them would affect discipline and the settlement of disputes. Even the leaders disagreed about who would possess towns captured during the expedition.

Recruitment reached people with fewer resources, including laborers, small propertyholders, clerics, servants, and noncombatants. Travel required maintaining food supplies and securing passage through territories where local rulers might resent armed strangers. Participants who fell sick, lost animals, or exhausted their funds could become dependent on charity and cooperation. The surviving Latin narrative chronicles tend to name noble leaders; less wealthy travelers appear most clearly during episodes of shortage, quarrel, or religious excitement. Their presence mattered because the expedition's direction was not always decided only by its princes. Collective pressure would later help compel the march from Antioch toward Jerusalem.

Motivations included the penitential appeal, love of sacred places, hopes of protecting eastern Christians, family loyalty, adventure, political ambition, and anticipated opportunities for advancement. These impulses cannot be sorted neatly into separate groups of good pilgrims and selfish warriors. A participant might believe sincerely in the religious meaning of the journey while also expecting honor and material compensation. Equally, most could not reliably anticipate profitable conquest. The costs of travel were immediate, whereas potential possessions in the East were uncertain and often contested. Research based on charters challenges the assumption that crusading was mainly a commercial expedition devised to find estates for younger sons.

The cross carried moral claims that the expedition could not consistently fulfill. Chroniclers described fasting, prayers, pilgrim status, and acts of communal discipline; they also recorded pillage, intimidation, and conflicts over supplies. Bishops and other religious leaders could counsel peace without possessing the force necessary to impose it on every band. Participation conferred no automatic unity of command. Once large numbers of people began traveling toward Constantinople, the difficulty of provisioning and regulating them produced confrontations with Christian communities along the route. That tension between penitential identity and coercive behavior became particularly stark during the popular movements of 1096.

Section 60.6

60.6 — The People's Crusade: Peter the Hermit, Walter Sans Avoir, and Civetot

The groups often collected under the modern label People's Crusade began moving east before the larger princes' armies had completed their preparations. Peter the Hermit preached and attracted many followers; Walter Sans Avoir led another important contingent. Neither figure commanded every group that left western or central Europe in 1096. The travelers included armed men as well as people with limited military resources, so the common portrait of a single helpless crowd of peasants is misleading. Their organizational diversity mattered: different bands arrived at borders at different times, made distinct arrangements with authorities, and reacted differently to problems of food and discipline.

Travel through Hungary and the Balkan provinces exposed the danger of gathering people without reliable provisions. Some groups fought with local populations or imperial troops; others negotiated passage or received organized assistance. Chroniclers recount violence at places such as Belgrade and Niš, but details vary and the actions of one contingent must not automatically be transferred to all. The Byzantine state had to balance protecting its inhabitants with the advantages of moving the western visitors onward. Alexios did not face an orderly regiment whose leadership could guarantee compliance. The emperor's agents were compelled to manage a series of partially independent armed movements.

When Peter and other survivors reached Constantinople in the summer of 1096, the emperor offered arrangements for their reception. A large body crossed the Bosporus into northwestern Anatolia, where supplies, military intelligence, and the discipline of reconnaissance became pressing concerns. Different participants raided nearby settlements and quarreled about strategy. Accounts depict rumor, factional rivalry, and enthusiasm for an early victory. The extent to which Peter could restrain the forces is debated, and he was not present directing the final engagement. The distance from safe Byzantine bases made the contingent vulnerable to an organized Turkish attack.

Kilij Arslan I and his commanders defeated the principal popular forces near Civetot on 21 October 1096. The encounter demonstrated that local military opponents possessed effective mobility and could exploit an ill-coordinated advance. Many crusaders were killed or captured; others escaped or were rescued through Byzantine intervention. The fate of individual noncombatants is difficult to reconstruct comprehensively. Survivors and eyewitness narratives later contributed to the memory of an expedition punished for disorder or insufficient preparation, but those moral judgments do not replace military explanation. Its defeat did not terminate the wider movement because the much larger princely armies were still on the road.

The People's Crusade should therefore be analyzed as part of the mobilization's scale and unpredictability, not merely as an amusing prelude to the supposedly serious campaign. It reveals how preaching could recruit beyond established military networks, how the logistics of travel could become a source of violence, and how divergent chains of command complicated relations with Byzantine officials. It also reminds students that the western response included people whose names rarely entered chronicles. The disastrous end near Civetot would shape the expectations of Alexios and western princes when they later planned an attack on Nicaea and further operations inland.

Section 60.7

60.7 — The Rhineland Massacres of 1096: Jewish Communities and Religious Violence

During the spring and summer of 1096, bands associated with the crusading mobilization attacked Jewish communities in the Rhineland and neighboring areas. Major centers affected included Speyer, Worms, Mainz, and Cologne; events differed across communities and did not follow one identical sequence. Forces associated with Count Emicho played an important role in the most devastating assaults. Perpetrators presented Jews as religious enemies, sometimes arguing that violence should begin nearer home before confronting distant Muslims. Such reasoning was neither an inevitable expression of Christian pilgrimage nor a command issued by Urban II, but it was connected to the same religious mobilization that sent armies east.

Jewish communities were not isolated enclaves without institutions of their own. They maintained congregations, scholarly traditions, charitable arrangements, commercial ties, and relationships with rulers and bishops. In some places bishops offered refuge or attempted to restrain attackers. Those interventions were uneven and often insufficient; local Christian townspeople could also participate in violence. The resulting attacks involved killings, seizure of property, intimidation, forced baptism, displacement, and the destruction of communal life. A history centered entirely on crusader commanders would miss how these events altered the prospects of Jewish households far from the Middle Eastern battlefield.

Jewish narratives recorded decisions made under conditions of extraordinary coercion. The Mainz Anonymous and the chronicle associated with Solomon bar Simson preserve memories of resistance, desperate flight, coerced conversion, and acts of martyrdom, including self-killing or the killing of kin to avoid forced baptism. Those accounts require contextual care: they are not instructions for the historian to idealize death, and their commemorative language can shape how events are arranged or described. Nor does the presence of religious literary convention make the underlying persecution doubtful. Robert Chazan's scholarship emphasizes studying both the violent attacks and the ways Jewish survivors and later authors reconstructed them.

Christian accounts supply additional evidence about the movement of armed bands, efforts at protection, and the actions of specific leaders. They may omit experiences prominent in Hebrew sources or present the perpetrators' conduct within a different moral framework. The sources are uneven in chronology and attribution, so precise local sequences must be reconstructed with attention to each account's provenance. Not every crusader participated, and many western travelers never passed near the attacked communities. At the same time, qualifying responsibility must not sever the massacres from the broader expedition. Their timing and perpetrators demonstrate the destructive potential of a mass religious campaign operating beyond effective controls.

The Rhineland massacres belong in the central narrative of the First Crusade because they expose a fundamental contradiction. An appeal that celebrated penitence and the protection of Christian sacred places could also encourage violent hostility toward vulnerable neighbors. They became a defining memory in medieval Ashkenazic Jewish history and affected relationships long after the armies departed. Their study broadens the chapter beyond the route to Jerusalem and requires the historian to give Jewish testimony independent weight, rather than treating victims merely as supporting evidence about Latin zeal. The experience of 1096 also provides a framework for analyzing violence against civilians during the later capture of Jerusalem.

Section 60.8

60.8 — The Princes' Crusade: Routes, Leadership, and Agreements with Alexios

From the late summer of 1096 into the spring of 1097, major princely contingents made their way toward Constantinople. Godfrey of Bouillon traveled through central Europe; Bohemond and Tancred came from the Norman political world of southern Italy; Raymond of Toulouse led a large southern French following; Robert of Normandy, Robert of Flanders, Hugh of Vermandois, and others arrived by different routes. These groups were assembled through personal lordship and family networks rather than a unified state army. They possessed different military resources, baggage trains, and ideas about leadership. Delays, negotiation, and occasional violence along the routes meant that arrival at Constantinople was staggered rather than simultaneous.

Alexios faced both an opportunity and a security problem. Large western armies could help retake Anatolian cities, but they also required food and passage near the imperial capital. Some leaders already had hostile histories with Byzantium. Through carefully arranged reception, diplomacy, gifts, and pressure, the emperor sought to bind them to imperial objectives. Particular attention was given to towns that had formerly belonged to the Byzantine Empire: rulers in Constantinople did not wish the expedition to replace Turkish authority with independent Latin possession. This issue would reappear with increasing force at Antioch, far from direct imperial supervision.

The oaths made by leading crusaders varied in language and political meaning. Some accepted obligations resembling homage or promised restoration of former imperial holdings, while others resisted or negotiated exceptions. Latin and Byzantine accounts present the arrangements differently, reflecting competing assumptions about authority and the credibility of sworn promises. The emperor could not impose complete obedience simply by receiving formal declarations. The princes likewise depended on Byzantine naval support, supplies, guides, and access through the Bosporus. Cooperation therefore rested on practical interests even where relations were strained. It would be an error to describe this phase as either perfect alliance or inevitable betrayal.

The western leaders brought separate religious advisers and frequently held councils to settle disputes. Adhemar of Le Puy, the papal representative, possessed important moral influence but was not the commander-in-chief of a modern expeditionary force. The presence of several powerful princes created flexibility: men and supplies could be organized under multiple leaders. It also generated competition over status and military success. Some contingents cooperated in sieges while others detached to pursue opportunities or establish political ties. Relationships formed in Byzantium and along the march made later decisions over Edessa and Antioch possible, even though those results had not been specified at Clermont.

By spring 1097, the expedition had to move from diplomacy and recruitment to warfare in Anatolia. The princes required transport across the Bosporus and coordination with Byzantine troops; Kilij Arslan I had to defend or abandon the forward position at Nicaea. The first major campaign would test whether western leaders could cooperate with one another and with the emperor when a disputed city was actually taken. The events at Nicaea show why the crusade's military history cannot be separated from Byzantine politics. Western victory depended on eastern assistance, but the success could also intensify disagreement about who deserved the results.

Section 60.9

60.9 — The Siege of Nicaea: Byzantine Cooperation and a Contested Victory

Nicaea stood near the approaches to Constantinople and served as an important western center of Kilij Arslan I's authority. The city possessed formidable defenses and access to the lake, making a straightforward assault difficult. Crusader commanders approaching in May 1097 could not simply surround it with an unsupported army. Byzantine forces, ships, engineers, and supply arrangements were crucial to the operation, while the presence of several Latin contingents enabled sustained pressure around the walls. Geography shaped the siege: control of water access and routes into the city mattered as much as numbers of soldiers positioned outside its gates.

Kilij Arslan had initially underestimated the strength and coordination of the arriving expedition, in part because the earlier popular movement had been defeated so easily. His return to confront the attackers did not break the siege, and the city faced increasing pressure. Accounts vary about tactical episodes and the timing of negotiations; the basic outcome is secure. Byzantine representatives secured Nicaea's surrender on 19 June 1097. The negotiated result limited opportunities for an uncontrolled Latin sack and brought a strategically valuable city back under imperial authority. For Alexios, that was precisely the sort of recovery his diplomacy had sought.

Some Latin participants resented the settlement. They expected compensation for their hardship and may have regarded the emperor's arrangements with the defenders as an affront to their claims. Other crusaders recognized that access to Byzantine supplies and transport remained indispensable. Chroniclers' emphasis on grievances should not be converted into proof that all leaders had become hostile to Constantinople. Relations remained practical and fluid: military partners could disagree about distribution of booty while continuing to cooperate. Nicaea therefore exposed a structural problem in the expedition: Latin and Byzantine notions of legitimate possession were not always compatible, even when both parties fought the same opponent.

The surrender also affected the wider political map. Turkish forces lost an important northwestern base, and the crusading army acquired a route toward the Anatolian interior. But Nicaea did not end Turkish authority across the peninsula. Kilij Arslan could continue resistance in mobile formations, and the plateau offered opportunities to attack an enemy whose supply line extended far from the capital. Nor did the city automatically become a permanent solution to Byzantine frontier defense. Political stabilization required garrisons, communications, revenues, and continued diplomacy. The distinction between capturing a fortress and securing a region should remain visible throughout the chapter.

The Nicaea episode provides an early test of competing explanations for crusader success. Western manpower and resolve mattered, but the operation depended upon knowledge, resources, and naval capacity supplied by the Byzantines. The city's defenders and neighboring populations also made choices about negotiation and survival that affected the outcome. A source comparison between Anna Komnene and Latin historians can reveal how each community claimed credit for the settlement. The central lesson is not that one side deserved victory more than another, but that the expedition succeeded through an alliance whose members never fully agreed about the political meaning of conquest.

Section 60.10

60.10 — Dorylaeum and the March across Anatolia: Battle, Heat, and Supply

After Nicaea, the crusading army moved toward the Anatolian plateau in formations that could travel and forage more effectively than a single continuous column. This division also created vulnerability. On 1 July 1097 near Dorylaeum, Turkish mounted forces confronted an advance contingent and attempted to overwhelm it before supporting crusaders could arrive. The battle tested mounted combat, missile fire, defensive coordination, and the ability to sustain an exposed position. Latin accounts emphasize the endurance of one contingent followed by the timely arrival of others. The evidence does not justify an exact universal figure for either army's strength.

The Turkish forces exploited mobility, seeking to disrupt formation and pressure troops with archery and repeated attacks. The crusaders possessed armored cavalry and infantry able to protect a position until additional men reached the field. The eventual defeat of Kilij Arslan's forces was important because it permitted movement farther into the plateau. It did not render the land secure, nor did it demonstrate that one military culture always defeated the other. Both sides adapted tactics to terrain and circumstance. The crusading commanders learned that maintaining communication among divided forces could determine whether a temporary tactical disadvantage became disaster.

The subsequent march imposed losses that cannot be understood through battlefield narratives alone. Summer heat, uncertain water sources, damaged or limited stores, and difficult grazing endangered both people and horses. A knight who lost his mount could no longer perform the same military role, while a pack animal's death reduced the baggage available to everyone. Chroniclers describe thirst and the search for wells, as well as the strain of crossing areas where hostile forces had removed supplies. Routes were shaped by local knowledge, provisions, and the need to find passable approaches to Cilicia. Movement was itself a military operation.

The expedition's logistical burden affected noncombatants with particular severity. Women, servants, laborers, clergy, and the sick moved with contingents whose speed and protection varied. Competition for food could increase tension with local agricultural communities, including Christians whom the crusaders professed to defend. Accounts often praise endurance under hardship, but the cost of that endurance was unequally distributed. The army also depended on trading relationships, guides, and local supplies that survive only in scattered reports. To reconstruct the campaign responsibly, students must ask how the force ate, obtained water, replaced animals, and preserved its capacity to fight after weeks of travel.

Dorylaeum marks a transition from initial success to the demanding realities of sustained campaigning. It is tempting to draw a solid arrow on a modern map and assume that the army marched inevitably toward Syria. In reality, detachments took different routes, the commanders had incomplete information, and their capacity depended on relationships with communities ahead of them. The subsequent events in Cilicia and Upper Mesopotamia show how the journey could generate new political goals. The road to Jerusalem passed through territories where Armenian leaders, Byzantine governors, Turkish commanders, and local communities could alter the expedition's direction.

Section 60.11

60.11 — Cilicia, Armenian Communities, and the Entry into Northern Syria

The Taurus mountains divided the Anatolian plateau from Cilicia and the northern Syrian approaches. Routes through passes concentrated travelers near fortresses and settlements whose rulers could control food, transit, and military shelter. In the autumn of 1097 crusading detachments associated with Baldwin of Boulogne and Tancred moved into this frontier environment. Their competition for towns illustrated the difficulties of shared leadership. It also revealed how opportunities along the route could encourage ambitions not explicitly included in the original objective of reaching Jerusalem. The armies had passed from imperial Anatolia into a region where multiple local Christian and Muslim authorities negotiated their survival.

Armenian communities were especially important to these negotiations. Their political organizations had been reshaped by Byzantine annexations, warfare, and regional rulers described in Chapters 56 and 59. Some leaders regarded western soldiers as useful allies against particular opponents; others feared losing the autonomy they had preserved amid previous changes of power. Local Armenian military figures supplied guides, intelligence, and access to strongholds, but such cooperation involved reciprocal expectations. A military force might gain supplies in return for protection, recognition of property, or support for a local claimant. To call Armenian actors merely helpers of the crusaders would erase their independent political judgment.

Relations were sometimes antagonistic. Baldwin and Tancred disputed authority in Cilician towns, and western commanders could threaten allies as well as opponents. The distinctions among Greek Orthodox, Armenian Apostolic, and Syriac Christian traditions did not disappear because the newcomers also identified as Christians. Doctrinal differences, language, patterns of landholding, and rival claims to ecclesiastical property could become points of friction. At the same time, military cooperation did not require complete theological agreement. The political flexibility of this frontier allowed new alliances to emerge quickly, but it also made them vulnerable to disputes over the possession of gates, towers, revenues, and hostages.

The journey toward Edessa and Antioch also highlighted the importance of urban society. Fortified towns connected pastureland, village cultivation, craft production, and long-distance trade. Local inhabitants might welcome the restoration of order while fearing the costs of another armed garrison. Merchants and clergy could mediate between unfamiliar commanders and populations speaking different languages. Latin narratives tend to frame these negotiations as episodes in a heroic pilgrimage; Armenian and Byzantine accounts place greater emphasis on the interests of established rulers. Both perspectives require comparison because the making of new states was inseparable from the choices of people who had lived there before the crusaders arrived.

The Cilician phase therefore should not be treated as a brief geographical bridge between battles. It shows that the expedition's political consequences began before Jerusalem. The same leaders who pledged cooperation with Alexios could acquire interests of their own, while local actors could seek advantage from the presence of western troops. Baldwin's decision to move away from the main army toward Edessa was especially consequential. The result would be the first durable Latin-ruled polity of the crusading era, created through a contested local succession rather than the simple conquest of a city defended by a Muslim army.

Section 60.12

60.12 — Baldwin, Thoros, and the Establishment of Latin Rule at Edessa

Edessa, now Şanlıurfa, lay in Upper Mesopotamia at the meeting of commercial and military routes. Its inhabitants included a substantial Armenian Christian population, but local political life also involved Syriac and other communities and relations with neighboring Muslim rulers. Baldwin of Boulogne reached the region in early 1098 with a comparatively small force seeking allies and opportunities. The ruler Thoros faced military and political pressures that made an alliance with an armed outsider attractive. This context is essential: Baldwin did not arrive at an empty city, and Latin authority was built within existing structures of urban power and personal allegiance.

Thoros adopted Baldwin as a son or political heir in a ceremony reported by later sources, but the meaning of this arrangement was not identical to a modern private adoption. It reflected an attempt to secure political protection and succession through a relationship recognized by the local elite. Thoros, who maintained relationships with the Byzantine world, faced opposition among inhabitants and powerful families. In March 1098 an uprising ended in his death, and Baldwin assumed rule. The sources do not agree on the degree of Baldwin's planning, involvement, or ability to prevent the killing. The historian should identify what is documented and what remains accusation or retrospective explanation.

Baldwin's new authority depended on compromise as much as force. Local Armenian leaders supplied connections, military resources, and a degree of legitimacy, while the Latin ruler brought an armed following and relationships with the larger crusading movement. His marriage and diplomatic alliances helped consolidate his place within regional society. Neither side possessed unlimited freedom: the surrounding political map included Turkish commanders, competing Christian magnates, and towns that could change allegiance. A chronicle focused on Baldwin's personal courage may obscure the negotiations and risks undertaken by Edessa's own inhabitants, who faced uncertainty about taxes, property, and religious arrangements.

The political structure that developed at Edessa is conventionally called the County of Edessa, the first durable Latin state associated with the First Crusade. It was not a territorial gift from Urban II, and the precise boundaries and institutions of the later county should not be imposed on its earliest years. The new regime had to secure fortresses, manage local offices, negotiate with church leaders, and respond to attacks. Baldwin's appointment or adoption did not automatically transfer every surrounding district to Latin possession. His achievement was the creation of a viable regional authority within an already complex frontier, rather than the instantaneous establishment of a modern state.

Edessa reveals how the First Crusade could produce consequences beyond its declared goal. A contingent detached from the march toward Jerusalem took advantage of a local political crisis, while Armenian elites pursued their own interests through an unfamiliar western leader. The creation of Latin rule did not eliminate existing populations or terminate their political agency. It did, however, introduce a new ruling connection that would affect wars and alliances for decades. The chapter now turns to Antioch, where the stakes were larger, the defenses far stronger, and disputes about Byzantine rights and independent Latin possessions even more difficult to resolve.

Section 60.13

60.13 — Antioch under Siege: Defenses, Supply, and the Winter of 1097–1098

Antioch controlled routes between northern Syria, the Orontes valley, and the Mediterranean coast. Its formidable walls, towers, and elevated citadel reflected its importance as a city of trade, ecclesiastical prestige, and military command. Yaghi-Siyan, the Turkish governor, held the city within a fractured regional political environment. He sought assistance from other rulers, but their relationships were shaped by rivalry and suspicion. The crusaders arrived in October 1097 and began a siege that would last through the winter and spring. Its duration was not evidence that the attackers lacked courage: the defenses were difficult, and the army could not easily surround every approach.

The besiegers depended upon roads toward the port of St. Symeon and upon local markets, raiding, and outside reinforcements. Supplies did not arrive at a predictable rate. Winter rain, hunger, disease, and the loss of animals undermined military effectiveness. Some participants deserted or sought safer quarters; leaders argued over how to allocate remaining food and protect supply routes. The defenders, meanwhile, could conduct sorties, maintain communications, and hope for help from neighboring powers. The siege became a contest of endurance and regional diplomacy as much as a matter of assaulting walls. Both communities inside and outside Antioch bore its costs.

Antioch's inhabitants were not one uniform group. Muslim officials and soldiers governed a city that also contained Christian communities with distinct religious connections. Urban residents might favor survival over the political ambitions of either side, yet their opportunities for neutrality were narrow. The besiegers likewise included clerics, servants, and other noncombatants whose health and food supplies affected the entire army. Descriptions of desperation in Latin chronicles must be read as accounts of material conditions as well as attempts to portray suffering as part of a sacred journey. The historian should ask which shortages are independently plausible and how the authors connected hardship to moral meaning.

Regional Muslim rulers attempted to respond, but cooperation was constrained by rivalries among Aleppo, Damascus, Mosul, and other centers. The crusaders also faced the problem of relief forces arriving while their own strength was diminished. Military engagements outside the walls could win temporary supplies without delivering victory in the siege. The apparent inability of Muslim commanders to coordinate should not be portrayed as a permanent property of Muslim politics: specific personal and dynastic interests impeded cooperation at this moment. Nor were Latin leaders consistently united. Both camps experienced conflicting priorities, uncertain intelligence, and the difficulty of commanding forces supplied by different patrons.

The prolonged struggle made Antioch the expedition's central crisis. Had the besiegers dispersed during the winter or failed to secure access to the coast, the march to Jerusalem might have ended. A breakthrough would depend partly on contacts inside the city and partly on timing before a major relief coalition arrived. The experience also sharpened competing claims: some western leaders considered Antioch subject to promises made to Alexios, while Bohemond increasingly sought possession himself. Military exhaustion and political rivalry thus developed together. The city's capture would not end the danger, because the crusaders could immediately become the defenders of a city still threatened from outside.

Section 60.14

60.14 — The Capture of Antioch: Bohemond, Firuz, and the Citadel

By late spring 1098 the crusaders were under growing pressure to obtain a decisive result before Kerbogha's advancing forces arrived. Bohemond of Taranto cultivated relations with an insider commonly identified in the Latin narratives as Firuz, who controlled access to part of Antioch's defenses. The reliability of every detail about this man and his motives is debated; sources offer stories shaped by the desire to explain a dramatic breach. Bohemond also negotiated with fellow princes over what he would receive if his plan succeeded. The political bargain was connected directly to the military operation: success might provide him a claim to rule.

During the night conventionally associated with 2–3 or, in some reconstructions, 3–4 June, crusaders entered Antioch and opened access to their companions. The exact sequence and dating of movements are reconstructed from imperfect and sometimes conflicting chronicles. Much of the city fell, but its citadel continued resisting. Latin accounts celebrate the achievement, while also acknowledging widespread killing and the disorder accompanying the assault. The defenders and civilians were not all able to escape. The capture should be recorded as a violent military event, not transformed into a bloodless exchange of flags or a single moment when every fortified position passed into Latin hands.

Yaghi-Siyan's government collapsed in the city, yet the conquerors were now exposed to the approaching army from Mosul. Supplies remained scarce and the new rulers had not secured a stable defense. The citadel could threaten them from above, while Kerbogha could attempt to close the roads outside the walls. A successful siege had reversed roles without solving the strategic problem. The crusaders needed to consolidate a defensible perimeter, restore morale, and prevent their mixed contingents from disintegrating before they could fight again. Their predicament was severe enough that contemporary narratives often framed survival as a question of divine favor or punishment.

Bohemond's claim also reopened the dispute about earlier Byzantine agreements. Had Antioch once been an imperial possession that should now be returned to Alexios, or could the prince who engineered its capture possess it as a reward? The answer depended upon different interpretations of oaths, imperial support, and promises that some leaders argued the emperor had failed to fulfill. Raymond of Toulouse and others resisted Bohemond's ambitions for reasons combining loyalty, rivalry, and their own political calculations. Treating the dispute as a personal quarrel alone would miss its constitutional significance: the outcome would determine whether one major crusader conquest reinforced Byzantium or produced a new Latin principality.

Antioch illustrates the difference between conquering walls, holding a city, and receiving accepted political authority. The operation associated with Firuz opened the city but did not resolve the citadel, outside armies, or competing leadership claims. Its chronology should not be overstated where contemporary evidence differs. Its violence should not disappear behind military admiration. The next crisis would put the exhausted crusaders under siege by Kerbogha's coalition. How the expedition survived that reversal requires attention to military logistics, disagreements among its opponents, and the religious expectations of men who believed their pilgrimage was nearing failure.

Section 60.15

60.15 — Kerbogha, the Holy Lance, and the Battle of Antioch

Kerbogha, the powerful ruler of Mosul, assembled a force that brought together contingents whose commanders did not necessarily share the same regional objectives. His campaign did not proceed directly to Antioch. During the spring of 1098 he spent time attempting to reduce Edessa, where Baldwin's new authority presented a threat and an opportunity. This delay affected the schedule of operations. Kerbogha's coalition arrived only after the crusaders had taken most of Antioch. Thomas Brosset's modern reassessment emphasizes the strategic consequences of the Edessa operation and the difficulty of coordinating commanders with distinct interests. These details explain more than a vague claim that the Muslims lacked unity.

Within Antioch the Latin armies endured hunger, fear, and uncertainty. Some accounts describe desertion and attempts to escape; leaders struggled to sustain discipline while a hostile army controlled the approaches. The reported discovery of the Holy Lance by Peter Bartholomew offered a powerful focus for religious expectation. The relic's authenticity was questioned even among crusaders, and the surviving reports cannot verify a supernatural event. Historians can nevertheless study the discovery's effects on morale, leadership disputes, and the language through which participants interpreted their circumstances. Belief was itself a historical force even when particular claims of miracle remain unprovable.

Kerbogha's coalition faced problems of trust and political ambition. Syrian rulers might fear the consequences of allowing a powerful commander from Mosul to dominate Antioch as much as they feared Latin conquest. Arguments over leadership, supply, and the future disposition of the city restricted cooperation. This does not mean the coalition was incapable of fighting or that every unit abandoned the field for the same reason. Modern reconstructions must evaluate the campaign stages, the positions of particular contingents, and the changing incentives of allied commanders. The crusaders also had internal disputes; their eventual concentration for battle was an achievement under unusually severe conditions.

On 28 June 1098 crusader forces emerged from Antioch and defeated Kerbogha's army. Latin narratives present the victory through religious imagery, processions, relics, and assistance from heaven; Muslim accounts and modern analysis supply other interpretive frameworks. A responsible account describes the military action and recognizes the limits of source detail rather than simply repeating stories of heavenly riders or assigning unsupported casualty totals. The rout was consequential because it lifted the external siege and permitted the crusaders to remain in possession of much of Antioch. Yet the citadel and the political dispute over the city's future required further resolution.

Kerbogha's failure was not predestined. A more rapid arrival, stronger agreement among regional commanders, or a different outcome at Edessa might have placed the crusaders in a far worse position. Equally, Latin survival was not reducible to one relic or one heroic commander. Supply, timing, military cohesion, and political rivalry interacted with the participants' religious confidence. The campaign is a useful case study in multiple causation: an army can possess substantial military strength and still fail to achieve its objective if its members disagree over what victory would mean. Antioch's outcome made the march to Jerusalem possible, but did not make it immediate.

Section 60.16

60.16 — Antioch after Victory: Rivalry, Ma'arrat, and the Decision to March

After Kerbogha's defeat, disputes over Antioch prevented the expedition from resuming a unified march at once. Bohemond pressed his claim to the city; Raymond of Toulouse resisted; some participants insisted that sworn obligations to Alexios remained relevant. The emperor's failure to join the siege became a source of recrimination, but his absence must be placed within the information and dangers confronting Byzantine commanders at the time. Medieval accounts frequently assign blame with hindsight. Their claims cannot substitute for a reconstruction of what each side knew when decisions were made. Possession, religious duty, and leadership prestige had become intertwined.

Adhemar of Le Puy, the papal representative, died in August 1098, depriving the army of a figure who had helped mediate disputes. The Holy Lance controversy further divided participants, since claims about divine approval could bolster the authority of particular leaders. Disease and exhaustion remained serious. The expedition also faced the question of whether it was becoming a series of private principalities rather than a pilgrimage to Jerusalem. Some common participants demanded movement south and were impatient with prolonged arguments over Antioch. Their pressure illustrates why lower-ranking crusaders could influence strategy even without controlling the principal commands.

The capture of Ma'arrat al-Nu'man in December 1098 exposed the violence and desperation of the continuing campaign. Latin chronicles describe killings and extreme food scarcity, including allegations that some crusaders consumed human flesh. These reports must not be sensationalized, nor can every literary detail be accepted at face value. They are nevertheless part of the surviving testimony and cannot simply be omitted. Muslim inhabitants experienced a destructive conquest, while Latin authors used hunger and brutality to represent an army under extraordinary strain. An adequate narrative distinguishes the securely attested assault and killing from disputed reports about the scale and circumstances of cannibalism.

Continuing negotiations with regional rulers affected the decision to move south. The crusaders sought provisions, safe passage, and sometimes payments from towns seeking to avoid attack. These arrangements were provisional; no universal alliance joined the Latin army to one Muslim political camp. The need to supply men and animals competed with pressure to fulfill the expedition's religious goal. After months of delay, important contingents left Antioch behind and resumed the journey toward Palestine in early 1099. Bohemond remained linked to Antioch, highlighting the fact that the original expedition was now producing distinct political enterprises with different rulers and geographical priorities.

The passage from Antioch to Jerusalem therefore resulted from negotiation among leaders, pressure from ordinary participants, available supplies, and the symbolic power of the destination. Military victory had not created permanent harmony among the crusaders, and their new holdings had complicated their relations with Byzantium. The campaign's next phase moved toward territory claimed by the Fatimid government in Egypt. Jerusalem had changed rulers in 1098, and the political choices of al-Afdal would influence the final advance. The chapter must now follow diplomacy along the Syrian coast before turning to the violence of the capture itself.

Section 60.17

60.17 — The March to Jerusalem: Coastal Diplomacy and Fatimid Palestine

In early 1099 the main crusading forces moved southward from northern Syria, using routes that balanced access to supplies with the danger of attacking fortified cities. Coastal towns possessed ports, markets, and local rulers whose priorities were not identical. Some negotiated provisions, payments, or temporary neutrality rather than risk a siege; others resisted or distrusted the visitors. The Latin army could not simply conquer every settlement on its way to Jerusalem. Its limited manpower and weary animals encouraged bargaining. The resulting movement was a sequence of practical political decisions rather than an unbroken triumphal procession toward a predetermined prize.

The Fatimid government in Cairo had recovered Jerusalem from Turkish-associated authorities in 1098. Consequently the garrison defending the city in 1099 served the Egyptian regime, not the Great Seljuk sultan. Vizier al-Afdal pursued Egyptian interests in Palestine and considered diplomatic possibilities concerning the western expedition. Some sources suggest expectations that the newcomers might serve as a counterweight to Turkish rivals, but the degree of understanding between the parties is disputed. Historians should not invent a formal lasting alliance. By 1099 the failure to reconcile competing claims over Jerusalem made direct conflict increasingly likely.

The march passed through societies that had experienced decades of competition among regional governments. Muslim, Christian, and Jewish inhabitants did not necessarily share the policies of those controlling their fortresses. Merchants could benefit from selling provisions and also fear requisition; local Christian communities could recognize sacred associations with Jerusalem without welcoming foreign occupation. The crusaders' religious expectations encouraged them to interpret successful movement as evidence of divine guidance. Yet guides, intelligence, livestock, and access to drinking water remained indispensable. The chapter should connect these concrete requirements to the decisions of commanders negotiating with towns along the road.

Reaching the area around Jerusalem brought new logistical problems. The city was inland, supplied through roads and surrounding villages whose resources had been disrupted by warfare. Fatimid authorities could defend the walls while limiting the besiegers' access to water. The crusaders had lost many companions since 1096 and could not assume another long siege would be survivable. Their hope of taking the city required siege construction, cooperation among rival leaders, and supplies from the coast. These preparations show why the earlier victories at Nicaea and Antioch did not make Jerusalem's fall inevitable.

Section 60.18

60.18 — The Siege and Capture of Jerusalem: Warfare, Ritual, and Mass Killing

The crusaders reached Jerusalem in early June 1099, beginning the siege conventionally dated from 7 June. The walls presented a difficult obstacle to a force that lacked abundant timber, water, and reliable supplies. The Fatimid governor Iftikhar al-Dawla directed the city's defense. Latin leaders sought timber and constructed siege towers and engines, aided by supplies and skills linked to the Mediterranean coast. The defenders prepared fortifications and attempted to frustrate assaults. Religious importance did not cancel military practicalities: the outcome depended on engineering, positioning, access to resources, and the defenders' limited ability to obtain relief.

Participants also performed fasting, prayer, and penitential processions, including a procession around the city described in the Latin sources. Such rituals expressed conviction that the siege belonged to a sacred journey and helped sustain communal morale. They did not constitute independent proof that supernatural intervention determined the battle. Chroniclers often arranged events to emphasize the relationship between repentance and victory. A historian can explain the real influence of shared belief on discipline while also reconstructing the siege's material circumstances. Rival princes had to cooperate sufficiently to coordinate attacks despite unresolved disagreements about future government.

On 15 July 1099 the crusaders broke into Jerusalem. The conquest was followed by extensive killing of Muslim and Jewish inhabitants, including people seeking refuge; other people were captured, ransomed, or escaped. The scale cannot be given as a securely established total. Latin narratives sometimes celebrate violence and describe extraordinary rivers of blood using scriptural imagery; Arabic and Jewish memories emphasize catastrophe from other perspectives. Such differences demand careful analysis, but source criticism must not become a pretext for minimizing the killings. The conquest combined strategic success for the attackers with terror and loss for inhabitants.

Jerusalem's significance to Christianity, Islam, and Judaism predated the arrival of the western armies. Sacred places, homes, markets, and places of worship belonged to communities that had lived under changing rulers. Their experiences cannot be recovered solely from proclamations made by the conquerors. A full account therefore asks who governed the city before the siege, how civilians were affected, what happened to religious sites, and how each source represented victory or suffering. The capture fulfilled a central Latin objective while creating new political obligations and unresolved questions about legitimate rule over the city.

Everyday Life and Social History

The Cost of War to Communities and Households

For an urban household, a siege meant securing water, protecting stored food, coping with rising prices, and fearing the consequences of an assault. For peasants near military routes it could mean requisitioned harvests, trampling of fields, displacement, and bargaining with soldiers. Caravan merchants faced closed routes but also opportunities to sell essentials. These effects varied by place and must not be narrated as one universal experience.

Jewish communities along the European routes, Armenian and Syriac Christian towns in the East, and Muslim residents of besieged cities appear unevenly in contemporary records. A responsible social history compares chronicles with charters, local institutional records, archaeological evidence, and the study of settlement rather than inventing diary-like testimonies of ordinary families.

Section 60.19

60.19 — Godfrey of Bouillon, Ascalon, and the Settlement of 1099

Victory at Jerusalem created an immediate question of government. On 22 July 1099 Godfrey of Bouillon was chosen to lead the new Latin authority, but sources differ over the formal title and the balance between ecclesiastical and secular leadership. The phrase advocate of the Holy Sepulchre is associated with his position, although it should not be treated as an uncontested contemporary constitutional formula. Church leaders also possessed claims and expectations concerning the city's sacred institutions. The new regime had to hold territory, secure supplies, establish relations with local populations, and defend against opponents who had not accepted the conquest.

The Fatimid government still possessed substantial military strength. Its forces advanced toward the southern Palestinian coast, leading to the Battle of Ascalon on 12 August 1099. The crusading army achieved an important victory, but the result did not hand it permanent control of the entire coast. Ascalon itself remained a crucial Fatimid position. The distinction illustrates a recurring pattern: battlefield victories could protect new Latin holdings without transforming every neighboring town into a securely governed possession. Military leaders also had to decide whether to remain in the East or fulfill their vows and return home.

Many participants did return to western Europe, leaving a relatively small body of defenders to maintain the newly acquired positions. The survivors faced problems of recruiting settlers, supporting garrisons, collecting revenue, and negotiating with preexisting religious communities. The political territories at Edessa, Antioch, and Jerusalem were different enterprises, not provinces of a single centrally organized Latin state. The later County of Tripoli had not yet been founded. These distinctions prevent the historian from treating the political geography of the twelfth century as if it already existed fully formed in the summer of 1099.

Godfrey died in 1100, and Baldwin of Boulogne moved from Edessa to become ruler in Jerusalem, receiving a royal coronation at Bethlehem on 25 December 1100. That brief epilogue clarifies how a monarchy followed the initial settlement, but the mature institutions of the kingdom belong to subsequent chapters. The central achievement of 1099 was the survival of several fragile Latin authorities amid older governments and populations. Their future would depend not only on religious conviction but also on diplomacy, military resources, agriculture, trade, and the ability of new rulers to negotiate with people under their authority.

Section 60.20

60.20 — Historical Synthesis: The First Crusade and Its Immediate Legacy

The First Crusade emerged from an unusual convergence of circumstances. Alexios sought military assistance, Urban II promoted an armed penitential journey, western households raised resources, and participants interpreted distant warfare through pilgrimage and religious promise. Once the movement began, no single leader controlled every contingent or decision. The defeat of the People's Crusade and the Rhineland massacres demonstrated the dangers and violence of mobilization. The princely armies nevertheless negotiated passage and benefited greatly from Byzantine support, particularly at Nicaea. Their later conflicts with the emperor show how cooperation could coexist with incompatible political expectations.

Military success depended upon logistics and contingency. The army survived the Anatolian plateau, used local alliances in Cilicia and Edessa, endured the long siege of Antioch, and defeated Kerbogha's divided coalition. Religious beliefs helped participants endure hardship, but they do not replace analysis of supply, command, intelligence, and rival political interests. Neither a permanently united Christian front nor a uniformly divided Muslim world explains every campaign. Leaders in each region made choices under changing conditions, and a different arrangement of resources or alliances could have produced another result.

The new Latin authorities established during the expedition were consequential but precarious. Edessa emerged from a local succession dispute involving Armenian elites; Bohemond's Antioch rested on a contested conquest; Jerusalem required government after a violent capture. The political changes did not erase Greek, Armenian, Syriac, Muslim, or Jewish populations. Relations among communities included negotiation, hierarchy, exchange, and conflict. It is therefore mistaken to equate the establishment of Latin rule with the creation of an ethnically or religiously homogeneous society. The older institutional and economic landscape continued to shape what conquerors could accomplish.

The human cost belongs within the explanation, not outside it. Violence against Jews in the Rhineland and mass killing at Jerusalem challenge any narrative that celebrates religious devotion without examining its consequences for others. At the same time, reducing all participants to cynical aggressors would fail to explain vows, sacrifice, and perseverance. A historically serious interpretation holds these realities together, distinguishes source testimony from rhetorical amplification, and avoids treating subsequent centuries of warfare as inevitable. By 1099 the eastern Mediterranean had gained a new set of Latin rulers and conflicts, but its future remained politically open.

Chapter Summary

The First Crusade emerged from an interaction of several political and religious worlds. Byzantine officials sought military help, while Pope Urban II’s preaching promoted an armed penitential journey toward Jerusalem. The crusading call drew upon pilgrimage traditions, papal reform, the religious prestige of sacred places, and the choices of families with very different levels of wealth. The survival of multiple later versions of Clermont’s sermon requires source comparison rather than fabricated direct quotations.

Recruitment had substantial financial and social costs. Nobles, knights, clerics, servants, laborers, and noncombatants prepared for dangerous travel without a unified central military command. Earlier popular groups reached Byzantium before the larger princely armies, and their defeat near Civetot in October 1096 demonstrated the risks of poor coordination. Military experience, provisioning, and the ability to maintain alliances would later shape the fortunes of the principal expedition.

In 1096 attackers associated with crusading forces killed and coerced Jewish communities in the Rhineland. Efforts at protection by some bishops and officials did not prevent disaster. Hebrew chronicles preserve memories of resistance, forced baptism, flight, and martyrdom, while also presenting the events through communal religious interpretation. These persecutions formed part of the crusading mobilization and must not be detached from the history of the expedition merely because they occurred outside the eastern Mediterranean.

The princely armies negotiated with Alexios I at Constantinople. Byzantine supplies, guides, shipping, and troops were essential to the successful siege of Nicaea, which surrendered to imperial authorities in June 1097. The Latin armies subsequently defeated Turkish forces at Dorylaeum and survived the difficult march across Anatolia. Cooperation with Byzantium persisted alongside disputes over oaths and the ownership of recovered imperial cities.

In Cilicia and Upper Mesopotamia the crusaders confronted political communities whose interests were not those of western princes. Armenian leaders negotiated alliances to protect their own positions. Baldwin of Boulogne established authority at Edessa in March 1098 after Thoros was killed in a local upheaval, an event for which his precise responsibility remains disputed. Edessa became the first durable Latin-ruled polity associated with the First Crusade.

The siege of Antioch exposed the logistical and political fragility of the expedition. Hunger, disease, contested leadership, and regional Muslim rivalries prolonged the struggle. Crusaders entered most of Antioch in June 1098, but its citadel remained held. They then faced the forces of Kerbogha of Mosul. The subsequent Latin victory reflected the interaction of military cohesion, morale, timing, and the structural weakness of a coalition whose commanders disagreed about their objectives.

After Antioch the army remained divided over control of the city and obligations to Byzantium. The death of Adhemar of Le Puy and religious disputes complicated cooperation. The capture of Ma’arrat al-Nuʿman brought further killings and reports of extreme hunger. Pressure from many participants, together with diplomatic and logistical considerations, contributed to the decision to continue southward toward Jerusalem in 1099.

Jerusalem was defended by Fatimid forces because Egypt had recovered it in 1098. The crusaders besieged and captured the city in June–July 1099, killing many Muslim and Jewish inhabitants. Latin narratives frequently used religious imagery to interpret the conquest; Arabic and Jewish memories framed the catastrophe differently. Historians can establish mass violence without accepting unsupported numerical totals or treating biblical images of bloodshed as literal measurements.

Godfrey of Bouillon was selected as ruler after the capture, and a Fatimid army was defeated near Ascalon in August 1099. These victories did not create a fully unified, stable Latin state across the coast. Edessa, Antioch, and Jerusalem possessed separate rulers and unresolved relationships with older populations. The First Crusade thus changed the eastern Mediterranean while leaving its political, religious, and social future open; the mature history of the Latin states follows in later chapters.

Chapter Reflection

The First Crusade asks students to consider how deeply held religious convictions can coexist with political bargaining, military calculation, and violence. To explain participation, neither an idealized account of devotion nor a cynical account of greed is sufficient. Individual decisions were shaped by vows, kinship, available resources, and conditions that changed throughout the journey.

The same attention to multiple causation is necessary for the expedition’s success. Byzantine support, Armenian alliances, the endurance of Latin contingents, and rivalries among regional Muslim rulers each mattered in specific settings. The course of events at Antioch demonstrates how different decisions might have produced a different outcome. The historian must resist explaining a complicated campaign by a single inevitable factor.

Historical interpretation also depends on who preserved the evidence. Latin, Greek, Armenian, Hebrew, and Arabic texts describe overlapping events but were written for different audiences and purposes. Critical analysis should neither assume every claim is literal nor dismiss testimony about suffering because it is shaped by moral or religious language.

The conquest of Jerusalem opened a new political period without removing the older societies of the region. Chapter 60 ends at the beginning of Latin territorial government rather than telling its entire later history. The enduring question is how to reconstruct a transformative episode accurately while attending to both the actions of rulers and the experiences of the people whose lives those actions altered.
