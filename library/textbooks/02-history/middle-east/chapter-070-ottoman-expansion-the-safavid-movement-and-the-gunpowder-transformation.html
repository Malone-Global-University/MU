Chapter Opening

In the spring of 1452, on the European shore of the Bosphorus at its narrowest point, thousands of laborers began raising a fortress of stone. Within roughly four months the walls and towers of Rumeli Hisarı stood directly across the strait from an older Ottoman fortress on the Anatolian side. A little more than two decades earlier no observer could have confidently predicted that the Ottoman dynasty would become the master of Constantinople; and in 1452 no observer could have predicted that, within a single lifetime, a teenage descendant of a Sufi shaykh from Ardabil would proclaim himself shah in Tabriz and that the two powers born of these developments would meet on a plain in Azerbaijan.

This chapter is about those two trajectories and the collision between them. The first begins with a young sultan, Mehmed II, who came to the Ottoman throne in 1451 as the ruler of a large but not yet imperial state centered at Edirne, holding territory in southeastern Europe and Anatolia, and facing at its very center the capital of an empire that had shrunk to little more than a city and its surroundings. The second begins far from that city, in the northwestern Iranian town of Ardabil, where a Sufi order that had originated in the fourteenth century slowly acquired disciples among Turkmen pastoralists, political exiles, and discontented subjects across Azerbaijan, eastern Anatolia, and Syria. In 1501 this order produced a dynasty. Between those two points lies the transformation of the late medieval Middle East.

Earlier chapters have prepared the ground. Chapter 64 and Chapter 66 examined how Mongol and Timurid conquest dismantled older political arrangements across Iran and Anatolia. Chapter 65 explained the Mamluk Sultanate, which controlled Egypt, Syria, and the holy cities of the Hijaz. Chapter 67 traced the Ottomans from a frontier principality among the Anatolian beyliks to a state that survived Timur's invasion, the civil war known as the Interregnum, and recovery under Murad II. Chapter 68 followed the Turkmen confederations, especially the Qara Qoyunlu and the Aq Qoyunlu, who dominated Iran, Iraq, and eastern Anatolia until the end of the fifteenth century. Chapter 69 turned to Kurdish principalities, Armenian communities, Syriac institutions, and the local worlds that lay between the larger powers. Chapter 70 gathers those threads.

The chapter's central question is how Ottoman territorial expansion, Safavid religious and military mobilization, and the changing use of gunpowder weapons together transformed the late medieval Middle East into a region dominated by competing imperial systems. The question has three parts, and each resists a simple answer. The conquest of Constantinople was a decisive turning point, yet it depended on a long program of political, naval, and logistical preparation rather than on any single weapon. The Safavid movement changed the political map of Iran, yet it did so through religious ideas, kinship networks, and tribal loyalties whose character was far more complex than the later label of a "Shia dynasty" suggests. And the battle of Chaldiran in 1514, often presented as the day firearms defeated cavalry, was an episode in a longer contest over the loyalties of frontier populations that neither empire fully controlled.

Students should hold three cautions in mind. First, none of the outcomes described here was inevitable. The Ottoman siege of 1453 might have failed, as earlier Ottoman sieges had; Ismail might have remained a fugitive hidden in Gilan; and the Ottomans might have been defeated at Chaldiran. Second, religious and ethnic labels must be handled with care. The Safavid movement was led by a dynasty rooted in Persianate Sufi networks whose military strength came from Turkish-speaking Turkmen warriors, and the Ottoman state drew soldiers and officials from many communities. Third, military technology is an explanation only when it is joined to state capacity: the money, labor, roads, discipline, and political authority that make a weapon effective.

The evidence for these events is rich but uneven, and it is shaped by the political commitments of those who produced it. For 1453 there are Greek, Latin, Italian, Slavonic, and Ottoman accounts, including the diary of a Venetian eyewitness and later Ottoman chronicles written in praise of the dynasty. For the Safavid rise there are Persian chronicles written under the dynasty's patronage, Venetian reports, Ottoman histories hostile to the Qizilbash, and poetry attributed to Ismail himself. The chapter's source studies ask students to read these materials as testimony by interested parties, not as neutral reports.

The chapter is organized into five divisions of four sections each. Division I examines the conquest of Constantinople and the creation of an Ottoman imperial capital. Division II follows Ottoman consolidation under Mehmed II and Bayezid II. Division III turns to the Safavid movement from its Sufi origins to the capture of Tabriz and the first decade of Safavid rule. Division IV explains the rivalry that led to Chaldiran and the frontier that emerged afterward. Division V steps back to analyze gunpowder warfare, legitimacy, social consequences, and the imperial order that resulted. The chapter closes Volume IV; Chapter 71 opens Volume V with the Ottoman conquest of Syria, Egypt, and the Hijaz.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why Constantinople mattered strategically to the Ottoman state in 1451 and why its conquest resulted from deliberate preparation rather than inevitability.
  2. Analyze the roles of fortification, diplomacy, naval operations, logistics, manpower, and artillery in the siege of 1453.
  3. Describe how Mehmed II transformed conquered Constantinople into an imperial capital through settlement, endowment, religious reorganization, and construction.
  4. Trace Ottoman expansion in the Balkans, the Black Sea, and Anatolia between 1453 and 1481, and explain the importance of administrative consolidation.
  5. Evaluate the development of Ottoman central institutions, including the palace, military households, timar assignments, and kanun, while distinguishing fifteenth-century forms from those of the sixteenth century.
  6. Explain how the Safaviyya changed from a Sufi order into a militant movement and eventually a ruling dynasty.
  7. Describe the Qizilbash as a political, military, and religious network and distinguish early Safavid devotion from later institutionalized Twelver Shiism.
  8. Assess the causes of Ottoman-Safavid conflict, including borderland loyalties, taxation, dynastic rivalry, and religious claims.
  9. Reconstruct the campaign and battle of Chaldiran and evaluate the relative importance of firearms, cavalry, logistics, and leadership.
  10. Explain the limits of Ottoman victory in 1514–1515 and how the Ottoman-Safavid frontier emerged.
  11. Evaluate the concept of "gunpowder empires" and the strengths and limits of technological explanations for imperial formation.
  12. Interpret primary sources from Venetian, Ottoman, Greek, and Safavid traditions, identifying the perspective and limitations of each.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did the Ottoman conquest of Constantinople succeed in 1453 when earlier Ottoman attempts had failed?
  2. How did conquest differ from consolidation, and what institutions turned conquered territory into a functioning empire?
  3. How did a Sufi religious order become a dynasty, and what changed in the movement's ideas between the fourteenth century and 1501?
  4. Why did Turkmen warriors in Anatolia and Azerbaijan give their loyalty to the Safavid leadership?
  5. To what extent was Ottoman-Safavid conflict religious, and to what extent was it territorial, fiscal, and dynastic?
  6. What does the battle of Chaldiran reveal about the relationship between firearms and cavalry warfare?
  7. Why did the Safavid state survive a major defeat while the Ottomans failed to hold Tabriz?
  8. How did ordinary people, including cultivators, artisans, merchants, and religious minorities, experience imperial expansion?
  9. How useful is the phrase "gunpowder empires" as a description of the Ottomans and Safavids?
  10. How did the events of 1451–1515 prepare the regional order examined in Volume V?

Historical Context

By the middle of the fifteenth century the political map of the Middle East bore little resemblance to the one inherited from the Abbasids and Seljuks. The Mongol conquests of the thirteenth century, examined in Chapters 63 and 64, had destroyed the Abbasid caliphate in Baghdad and established the Ilkhanate in Iran. The Ilkhanate's collapse in the 1330s fragmented political authority, and Timur's campaigns, discussed in Chapter 66, devastated cities and reorganized elites across Iran, Iraq, Syria, and Anatolia without creating a durable state. His successors, the Timurids, retained the eastern lands and the great cities of Herat and Samarkand but not the west. In the west, Turkmen confederations, whose rise Chapter 68 describes, filled the vacuum.

Two Turkmen confederations dominated the Iranian-Anatolian frontier. The Qara Qoyunlu held Azerbaijan and Iraq until about 1467; their rivals the Aq Qoyunlu, under Uzun Hasan, defeated them and built a state with its capital at Tabriz. Uzun Hasan sought alliances with Venice and eventually came into conflict with the Ottomans, whose victory over him at Otlukbeli in 1473 is treated here only for what it reveals about Ottoman capability. After his death in 1478 the Aq Qoyunlu declined into succession wars, a condition that explains why the Safavid challenge succeeded so quickly.

Egypt and Syria were governed by the Mamluk Sultanate, whose military slave elite, examined in Chapter 65, controlled Cairo, Damascus, Aleppo, and the holy cities of Mecca and Medina. The Mamluks had halted the Mongols at Ain Jalut and ended the crusader states, and they retained great prestige. Between the Mamluk and Ottoman realms lay the Taurus and eastern Anatolian frontier, where principalities such as Dulkadir and Ramazan maintained a precarious autonomy. Chapter 69 showed that the Kurdish emirates, Armenian and Syriac communities, and mountain societies of the same borderlands had their own institutions, loyalties, and strategies of survival, and they remain participants in this chapter's story.

The Ottoman state, as Chapter 67 recounted, had expanded from a small frontier principality in northwestern Anatolia to a power that held Bursa, Edirne, and much of the Balkans. Bayezid I's defeat by Timur at Ankara in 1402 almost ended it. The Interregnum was resolved by Mehmed I and then Murad II, who consolidated Ottoman authority and defeated Crusader armies at Varna in 1444 and Kosovo in 1448. Byzantine Constantinople, in contrast, was a diminished city, surrounded by Ottoman territory and dependent on the diplomatic goodwill of Italian maritime republics. Chapter 70 begins at the moment this imbalance was about to be resolved.

One further inheritance matters. Gunpowder weapons had been known in the Middle East since the fourteenth century, and Ottoman armies had used cannons and handheld firearms in the later fourteenth and early fifteenth centuries. What changed in the period studied here was the scale at which a state could produce, transport, and deploy them, and the institutions that integrated them into an army. This chapter therefore treats gunpowder as part of a wider transformation in state capacity rather than as an independent cause.

Division I

Constantinople and the Transformation of Ottoman Imperial Power

How the conquest of Constantinople altered sovereignty, strategy, economic geography, and the character of Ottoman dynastic government.

Section 70.1

Mehmed II, the Byzantine World, and the Strategic Situation in 1451

Murad II died in Edirne in early February 1451, and his son Mehmed, then about nineteen, returned to the throne he had briefly held as a boy in 1444–1446. That earlier reign had ended when Murad resumed power in the aftermath of the Crusade of Varna, and Mehmed's second accession came with the memory of that humiliation and with a political environment he did not fully control. The great viziers of Murad's court, above all Çandarlı Halil Pasha, had served the previous ruler and doubted the young sultan's judgment. Mehmed's household, by contrast, included commanders and advisers more willing to contemplate bold action, among them Zaganos Pasha and Şihabeddin Pasha. The first years of the reign were therefore not simply a story of a ruler imposing his will but of a court negotiating strategy.

The state Mehmed inherited was already large. It held most of western and central Anatolia, including Bursa, the early capital, and it controlled much of the Balkans south of the Danube. Edirne, on the Maritsa river in Thrace, served as the principal seat of government and the staging point for European campaigns. Chapter 67 described how this dual presence in Anatolia and Europe gave the Ottomans advantages unavailable to the Anatolian beyliks: manpower from two regions, revenue from agriculture and trade in both, and the ability to shift armies between theaters. The weak point was the connection between them. Ottoman troops crossed between Europe and Asia at Gallipoli and by ship, but they did so in the shadow of a hostile city that sat astride the straits.

That city was the capital of an emperor whose realm had been reduced to Constantinople itself, parts of the Morea in the southern Peloponnese, and a few Aegean islands and coastal places. Constantine XI Palaiologos, who had been crowned in Mistra in 1449, ruled a city of far fewer people than it had held in its prime, with large areas inside the walls given over to gardens, fields, and abandoned districts. Estimates of its population around 1450 are uncertain and range from tens of thousands to somewhat higher figures; no census exists. What it still possessed was symbolic weight as the seat of a Roman imperial tradition, a spectacular site, and a strong circuit of fortifications. Byzantine emperors had long survived by diplomacy, playing Ottoman princes against one another and seeking Western help.

That help depended on the maritime republics and the papacy. Venice held colonies and trading privileges and maintained a quarter in the city; Genoa controlled Galata (Pera) across the Golden Horn and Chios in the Aegean; and the papacy, committed in principle to Crusade, demanded the union of the Greek and Latin churches as the price of aid. These powers had commercial interests that did not always coincide with defending Constantinople. Venice and Genoa traded with the Ottomans and could not afford to be shut out of Ottoman markets, and many Greeks resisted union, so Byzantine diplomacy gave Constantine promises of assistance, sometimes real, sometimes late, that never added up to a relief force sufficient to break an Ottoman siege.

The straits mattered because they were both a commercial corridor and a military one. The Bosphorus linked the Black Sea, whose grain, salt, fish, slaves, furs, and later Caffa's trade fed Mediterranean markets, with the Sea of Marmara and the Dardanelles beyond. A power holding both banks could tax or halt that traffic and move forces freely. Yet the possession of Constantinople offered more than a better crossing. For a dynasty that portrayed itself as a champion of Islamic warfare against Christendom, capture of the great city carried religious prestige, since Islamic tradition from the early centuries had linked its fall to the community's destiny. It also promised a central capital for a state that straddled two continents, and an end to the persistent possibility that a Byzantine pretender, supported from the West, might be sponsored to unsettle Ottoman succession.

Historians have therefore emphasized that Mehmed's decision to attack in 1453 was a calculated move rather than the fulfillment of a destiny. Murad II had besieged the city in 1422 and failed. Mehmed's early actions, including an agreement with Constantine and a campaign against Karaman in 1451–1452, kept options open, and Halil Pasha urged caution. What changed the calculation was a combination of the sultan's need to establish authority at home, the apparent weakness of Western relief, and the opportunity to dominate the strait through a new fortress. The next section follows that preparation in detail.

Section 70.2

Preparing the Conquest: Rumeli Hisarı, Diplomacy, Fortifications, and Ottoman Logistics

The first overt step was the building of a fortress. Early in 1452 Mehmed gathered materials and labor, much of it drawn from provincial officials and from Greek villages near the Bosphorus, and in April construction began at a narrow point on the European shore where the channel runs fast and shipping must pass close to either bank. The fortress, called Boğazkesen ("strait-cutter") in Ottoman sources and Rumeli Hisarı in later usage, was completed in about four months, in late summer 1452. It stood opposite Anadolu Hisarı, the fortress Bayezid I had built on the Asian shore in the 1390s, so that Ottoman artillery in the two forts could command the channel. The speed of construction attests to the logistical power of the Ottoman state, which could mobilize thousands of workers, masons, and quarrymen and supply stone, lime, and iron on a fixed schedule.

The fortress changed the diplomatic situation. A Byzantine embassy protested, and Mehmed's reply made clear that the sultan considered the land his own to build on. Soon afterwards, in November 1452, a Venetian ship attempting to pass without stopping was sunk by Ottoman fire and its crew executed or captured; the incident demonstrated that the Bosphorus could now be closed. The Black Sea trade on which the city depended for grain could therefore be interrupted at will, and Italian merchants had to reckon with the new reality. Constantinople, which had survived largely because the Ottomans lacked sufficient naval and artillery capacity to isolate it, was now vulnerable to blockade.

Meanwhile the Ottomans built a fleet and assembled artillery. A fleet was gathered at Gallipoli in the winter of 1452–1453, composed of triremes, smaller oared galleys, and transport vessels, though it was not equal to the best Venetian and Genoese warships. The artillery included large bombards cast at Edirne and elsewhere. A foundry master known in the sources as Orban or Urban, reported as Hungarian or from the Balkans, is said to have first offered his services to the Byzantines and then, unable to obtain adequate payment, to have worked for Mehmed. The great cannon associated with him is described by chroniclers as capable of firing very heavy stone shot, but the surviving descriptions vary in detail, and later accounts exaggerate its size and effect. The Ottomans also relied on lighter guns, handgunners, sappers, miners, and engineers. Mehmed's army assembled in the spring around Edirne and moved toward the city in March, with road crews and supply columns preparing the way.

On the other side stood formidable defenses. The Theodosian Walls, built in the fifth century and reinforced later, ran for about six kilometers across the peninsula and consisted of an outer wall, an inner wall with towers, and a moat. Sea walls along the Marmara and Golden Horn were weaker but protected by currents and a chain across the mouth of the Golden Horn. Yet the defenders were few. The emperor could count on perhaps seven thousand fighting men according to the commonly cited figure, made up of Greeks and a substantial contingent of foreigners including Venetians, Genoese, Catalans, and others, though figures differ among sources and none is verified. Opposing them was an Ottoman army that sources place in the tens of thousands, with estimates ranging very widely. The disproportion was real, but a wall network that could be manned by a few thousand also meant that the attackers could bring their numbers to bear in only limited places.

The diplomatic front was fraught. Constantine appealed to Rome and Venice, and in 1452 the cardinal and former archbishop Isidore of Kiev arrived with a small papal contingent. A proclamation of church union was read in Hagia Sophia on 12 December 1452, but it provoked bitter resistance among many Orthodox clergy and laity, who regarded union as submission to Latin authority, and some pious residents reportedly avoided the cathedral thereafter. The episode illustrates how the Byzantine state's survival strategy collided with the religious identity of its own people. Venice debated sending a fleet and approved one, but it arrived too late; Genoese and Venetian volunteers present in the city did fight, but the Italian states did not commit themselves to a relief expedition.

By the beginning of April 1453 Mehmed had in place the preconditions of a siege: control of the Bosphorus, an army and guns assembled in Thrace, a fleet at Gallipoli, and the knowledge that no coordinated Christian relief was imminent. Students should resist the temptation to explain the result by any one of these. The conquest came about because an Ottoman state with an effective fiscal and logistical system chose to concentrate its resources on a single objective, against a defender whose diplomatic alternatives had narrowed to almost nothing.

Section 70.3

The Siege and Fall of Constantinople, April–May 1453

The Ottoman army arrived before the walls on or about 2 April, and the formal siege is dated from 6 April, when the bombardment began. Mehmed's tent was pitched opposite the Mesoteichion, the middle section of the land walls, where the Lycus valley created a depression. Ottoman troops dug trenches and set up the big guns in a few places, notably near the Romanus Gate, with smaller artillery elsewhere. The guns fired slowly, since a large bombard could be fired only a handful of times a day and needed time to cool and be reloaded, but over weeks they damaged the outer walls and towers. The defenders responded by repairing breaches at night with timber, earth, and barrels, using the labor of soldiers, townsmen, and women. Greek and Italian sources agree that the wall was not simply smashed down; it was steadily worn and patched in a contest of attrition.

Naval operations were an early focus. On 20 April a small Genoese convoy, with a Byzantine ship carrying grain, fought its way through the Ottoman fleet and reached the Golden Horn, a morale boost for the defenders and a blow to the Ottoman admiral Süleyman Baltaoğlu, who was reportedly dismissed. Mehmed's response was ingenious. On 22 April he had a number of ships dragged overland on greased wooden rollers from the Bosphorus, behind Galata, and launched them in the Golden Horn above the chain. Ottoman sources and Western accounts agree that the maneuver occurred, though they differ on the precise route and the number of vessels. It forced the defenders to spread their limited manpower along the sea walls of the Horn, and the Ottomans built a floating bridge for guns and troops across the upper part of the inlet.

For several more weeks the siege took the form of repeated assaults, mining operations, and counter-mining. Ottoman engineers dug tunnels under the walls, and the defenders, led by engineers including a German or Scottish expert named in the sources as Johannes Grant, countermined and fought underground. A siege tower was burned. Provisions in the city dwindled and rumors circulated that no help was coming; Venetian vessels sent to scout found no relief fleet. In Mehmed's camp the war party and the cautious party continued to argue, and the sultan reportedly weighed offers of negotiation, including a proposal that Constantine leave the city, which the emperor refused. Late in May the Ottomans decided to make a general assault, preceded by a day of rest and prayer.

The final assault began before dawn on 29 May. Ottoman forces launched successive attacks involving irregulars, Anatolian contingents, and eventually the Janissaries, pressing defenders already weakened by weeks of bombardment and exhaustion. A crucial crisis developed around the Lycus valley and the Mesoteichion, where Giovanni Giustiniani Longo, the Genoese commander defending a particularly vulnerable stretch, was wounded and withdrew; the cohesion of the defense deteriorated. Ottoman troops then broke through and overran sectors of the land defenses. One late Byzantine narrative describes a small gate called the Kerkoporta being left open, but historians dispute the episode and its significance: it cannot be treated as the established explanation for the fall. Constantine XI died amid the fighting, though the exact circumstances remain uncertain and later legends elaborate them. The sultan entered the city later that day.

The consequences for the population were severe. Under the conventions of the time, a city taken by storm could be plundered for a limited period, and the sources describe killing, looting of churches and houses, and the seizure of many inhabitants as slaves, with captives later ransomed or sold. Estimates of casualties and captives vary by source and cannot be firmly established; contemporary figures run to tens of thousands of enslaved but are not reliable census data. Many people took refuge in Hagia Sophia and were captured there. Mehmed is reported to have halted the plunder after a period and to have ordered the protection of buildings, though the extent of the damage and of his control is uncertain. The Venetian and Genoese who survived, and the Greek elite who escaped, spread their own accounts across Europe, where the fall provoked shock and calls for Crusade that produced little action.

The role of artillery needs careful statement. Contemporary and later writers, impressed by the bombards, often gave the guns the starring part. Modern scholarship generally treats them as significant but not alone decisive: they accelerated damage to the walls, but the defenders repaired much of it; the walls had already withstood earlier assaults; and the outcome turned on manpower, the sequence of assaults, Italian divisions, the cutting of supply, the Horn operation, and the cumulative strain of seven weeks. Historians continue to debate how much the Ottoman guns mattered relative to these factors, and the sources, written by participants with reasons to emphasize one explanation or another, do not settle the question. The honest conclusion is that artillery contributed to a result that required a whole military system.

Section 70.4

From Constantinople to an Ottoman Imperial Capital: Reconstruction, Religion, and Sovereignty

Taking the city was easier than making it a capital. The conquered Constantinople was depopulated by deaths, enslavement, and flight, and many of its buildings were damaged. Mehmed, who did not at first make it the seat of government, set about repopulating and rebuilding it. The sultan ordered the repair of walls and the restoration of the city's infrastructure, including water supplies and markets, and he used a mixture of coercion and inducement. Historians describe this as a state-directed program that combined sürgün, the forced resettlement of populations from conquered or controlled regions, with incentives such as houses, exemptions from certain taxes, and economic privileges. Greeks from the Black Sea and Aegean, Muslims from Anatolia, Armenians, and Jews from Ottoman towns in the Balkans and Anatolia were brought in, and the reorganization of the population was one of the main political acts of the reign.

Historical Vocabulary
Sürgün

In Ottoman usage sürgün (literally "exile" or "driving out") referred to the state's transfer of people from one place to another, sometimes forcibly and sometimes with inducements. It served several purposes: repopulating depleted cities such as Constantinople, settling frontier regions, removing political opponents or restive groups, and bringing skilled artisans to the capital. Scholars debate how coercive particular movements were and how they varied by group and decade. The term is best understood as an instrument of imperial governance, not as a single policy applied uniformly.

The religious reorganization of the city was just as significant. Hagia Sophia, the great sixth-century church, was converted into a mosque shortly after the conquest, as were other churches over time, while others remained in Christian use. The conversion of the cathedral had profound symbolic meaning, signaling the transition of sovereignty, and it was accompanied by endowments to support it. At the same time Mehmed arranged for the Orthodox community to have a leader. After the death of the previous patriarch, Gennadios Scholarios, a learned monk and a prominent opponent of union with Rome, was chosen as patriarch and installed, probably in January 1454. This choice recognized the importance of the Orthodox church as an institution, provided the government with an interlocutor, and distanced the community from the Latin West. Over the following years the Armenian community, whose settlement in the city grew, also acquired a patriarch (the traditional date is 1461), and the Jewish community was organized under rabbinical leadership. These arrangements are often grouped under the later label of the millet system, but historians now stress that they developed gradually and unevenly and did not constitute a formal legal framework in the fifteenth century (see Chapter 75).

Mehmed's building program was extensive. A bedesten, a covered market for valuable goods, was constructed in the 1450s, laying the foundation of the commercial district later known as the Grand Bazaar. A first palace, later called the Old Palace, was built on the third hill, and from around 1459 a new palace complex rose at the tip of the peninsula that became Topkapı. In the 1460s Mehmed commissioned a great mosque complex, the Fatih complex, with colleges and charitable institutions, on the site of the former Church of the Holy Apostles. Wealthy officials were encouraged to found endowments (waqfs), which built mosques, baths, schools, and markets and provided revenue for upkeep. The endowments thereby served as urban developers, tying the new elites to the city's growth. Research on the post-conquest city, including İnalcık's study of Mehmed's policy toward the Greek population and the city's Byzantine buildings, emphasizes both the reconstruction and the preservation or adaptation of existing structures.

Students should avoid two simplifications. The first is the idea that Byzantine Constantinople vanished at once. Byzantine buildings, street plans, water systems, and some institutions continued in use; Greek craftsmen, clergy, and merchants remained part of urban life; and Byzantine imperial ceremonial and symbolism influenced Ottoman court practice. The second is the notion that the city was renamed Istanbul in 1453. The name Istanbul, probably derived from a Greek phrase for "to the city," was in popular use before the conquest and circulated alongside Constantinople (in Ottoman usage, Kostantiniyye) for centuries. Official coinage and documents often used Kostantiniyye, and the shift to Istanbul as the standard international name came much later, in the twentieth century.

Finally, the conquest altered the meaning of Ottoman sovereignty. Mehmed adopted the style of a universal ruler, and the titles used in his court included claims to inherit Roman imperial authority, a claim reflected in the Greek writings of Kritoboulos, who addressed the sultan as a successor to the emperors, alongside Islamic titles. The capture of the capital strengthened the sultan against the old frontier nobility and the great vizierial families. One of the first consequences was political: Çandarlı Halil Pasha, who had opposed the venture, was dismissed and executed soon after the conquest, and the offices of state increasingly passed to men of the sultan's own household. The conquest thus fed directly into the centralizing program examined in §70.7.

Division II

Ottoman Consolidation across Anatolia, the Balkans, and the Eastern Mediterranean

How Mehmed II and Bayezid II expanded and reorganized Ottoman authority, creating the foundations for confrontation with the Safavids.

Section 70.5

Mehmed II and the Incorporation of the Balkans and Remaining Byzantine Territories

The fall of Constantinople did not end the Ottoman campaign in southeastern Europe; it opened a new phase of it. In the years after 1453 Mehmed's armies moved against the Serbian Despotate, the Morea, Bosnia, Wallachia, Albania, and Venetian and Genoese outposts. The sequence shows a method that combined conquest with administrative absorption. Serbia, which had been a tributary state, was pressed hard in 1454–1456 and, after the death of Despot Lazar Branković and succession quarrels, its capital Smederevo fell in 1459. The Ottomans converted most of the Despotate into provinces governed by Ottoman officials, though Serbian nobles who accepted Ottoman authority could be absorbed into the military-administrative class and Orthodox church institutions continued to operate.

The Morea followed. The brothers of the last emperor, Thomas and Demetrios Palaiologos, ruled fragments of the Peloponnese in rivalry with one another, and Ottoman intervention, taking advantage of their quarrels and of local revolts, brought most of the peninsula under Ottoman control between 1458 and 1460, when the stronghold of Mistra surrendered. Venice retained several ports, notably Modon and Coron, which remained a source of tension. In the north, Bosnia's king Stephen Tomašević was defeated in a swift campaign in 1463, and the kingdom was annexed. The Bosnian aristocracy faced a choice between resistance and accommodation; the conversion of a substantial portion of the population to Islam over the following generations is a subject of ongoing scholarly attention, and historians reject both the idea of mass forced conversion at the moment of conquest and the idea that it happened spontaneously, favoring explanations that combine social incentives, church weakness, and local circumstances.

Not every campaign succeeded. In Albania, the Ottoman-trained commander Gjergj Kastrioti, known as Skanderbeg, organized a durable resistance after 1443 and defeated several Ottoman expeditions; he held out, with Venetian and Hungarian support, until his death in 1468, and Ottoman consolidation of Albania was only completed after Venetian Shkodër fell in 1479. In the Danube region, Mehmed's campaign against the Wallachian prince Vlad III, remembered as Vlad the Impaler, in 1462 met stiff resistance, though Ottoman influence was eventually restored by installing a rival. Hungary under Hunyadi and then Matthias Corvinus remained a power; Hunyadi's defense of Belgrade in 1456 thwarted Mehmed's attempt to take the fortress, one of the major Ottoman setbacks of the reign.

The Venetian war of 1463–1479 was a protracted struggle over the Aegean and Adriatic. Mehmed captured Negroponte (Euboea), the major Venetian base, in 1470; Venice, in alliance at times with Uzun Hasan of the Aq Qoyunlu, raided Ottoman coasts, but it could not reverse the strategic balance. In the treaty of 1479 Venice ceded Shkodër and other places and agreed to pay tribute for trade privileges. The episode shows how commerce and conflict intertwined: the Ottoman state wanted the revenue and goods that Venetian trade brought, and Venice wanted access to Ottoman markets, so both sides returned to the negotiating table.

The central argument of this section is that expansion required more than battlefield success. Conquered lands had to be surveyed, assigned to cavalrymen as timars, and governed by kadis (judges) and sancak governors. Local conditions varied. In some places, existing landholders were integrated into the military-administrative class; in others, lands were confiscated and redistributed; elsewhere, vassals paid tribute for a time before annexation. Christian populations paid the poll tax (cizye) and other taxes but kept their churches and law courts in matters of personal status, and some Christian soldiers served the Ottomans as auxiliaries. The devshirme, the periodic levy of boys from Christian villages for palace and Janissary service, drew young people out of Balkan communities and into the sultan's household, a system whose burdens fell heavily on those families even as it created channels of advancement for some of the recruits. Consolidation depended on this combination of coercion, accommodation, and institutional reach.

Section 70.6

Trebizond, the Anatolian Principalities, and the Ottoman Eastern Frontier

The Ottoman eastward drive proceeded in parallel with its western campaigns. In 1461 Mehmed turned against two remaining Anatolian polities on the Black Sea coast. In the west of the coast, the principality of Candar (Isfendiyarids), with its center at Kastamonu and the port of Sinope, was absorbed after its ruler's submission and the Ottoman seizure of Sinope. Further east, the Empire of Trebizond, ruled by the Komnenoi dynasty as a successor to Byzantium, fell in August 1461 after a campaign in which Ottoman forces, supported by a fleet, moved across the mountains and by sea. Its emperor David Komnenos surrendered and was taken to Constantinople, and some of the Greek population was resettled in the capital. The fall of Trebizond ended the last Byzantine successor state in Anatolia and gave the Ottomans a strong hold on the southern Black Sea, with its trade routes to the Caucasus, the Crimea, and the interior of Anatolia and Iran.

The conquest of Trebizond also changed the strategic relationships along the frontier. The Aq Qoyunlu ruler Uzun Hasan had married a Trebizond princess and was a diplomatic patron of that state; its fall made him an immediate rival of the Ottomans in the northeast. In central and southern Anatolia the main obstacle was Karaman, the Turkmen principality centered at Konya and extending into Cilicia, which had long competed with the Ottomans. Mehmed campaigned against Karaman repeatedly, in the mid-1460s and again in 1471–1474, taking Konya, annexing much of its territory, and forcing the Karamanids' remnants into the rugged Cilician hills. The Ottoman state also had to consider the Mamluks, whose sphere extended over Syria and who considered the Cilician plain and the Taurus passes within their security zone, and who supported Karaman claimants at times.

Uzun Hasan, for his part, had built a powerful state based in Tabriz and stretching across Iran, Iraq, and eastern Anatolia, as Chapter 68 explains. He sought an alliance with Venice against the Ottomans, who were at war with the Republic, and sent envoys to the West. The Venetians promised firearms and artillery and sent a few guns and gunners, though little arrived in time. In 1473 Mehmed, in a major campaign led partly by his son Mustafa and partly by himself, advanced into eastern Anatolia and met the Aq Qoyunlu at Otlukbeli (also written Başkent), near Erzincan, on 11 August. The Ottomans won decisively. The detailed political and military history of the Aq Qoyunlu belongs to Chapter 68; what matters here is that the battle showed that the Ottoman army, which combined disciplined infantry, artillery, a large cavalry, and strong logistics, could defeat the largest eastern Turkmen army in a pitched battle.

The consolidation of the eastern frontier relied on fortresses, supply routes, and local rulers. Section 70.7

Imperial Centralization: The Palace, Military Households, Revenue, and Ottoman Government

Mehmed II's reign saw a significant expansion of central authority. The sultan's household, the palace, became the core of government. Inside it, pages recruited from the devshirme and from captives were trained for service as officials and officers, and from the ranks of such men came many of the grand viziers, provincial governors, and commanders. The grand vizier acted as the sultan's chief minister and presided over the imperial council (divan), where state business, appeals, and military affairs were discussed. During the 1450s and 1460s the sultan replaced the older Turkish-Muslim notable families who had dominated high office, above all the Çandarlı, with men of slave-household origin, such as Mahmud Pasha, who served as grand vizier for much of the 1450s and 1460s and was himself of Serbian-Greek background. This shift gave the sultan officials whose careers depended on him but also created a new elite with its own interests and rivalries.

Historical Vocabulary
Timar and Kanun

A timar was a grant of the right to collect specified revenues from a district, given to a cavalryman (sipahi) in return for military service; the sipahi was expected to equip himself and a number of armed retainers in proportion to the grant's value. A kanun was a sultanic regulation issued by the ruler, covering taxation, land, criminal penalties, and administrative procedures, and existing alongside sharia law. Mehmed II is associated with an early collection of kanun, though the surviving texts and their dating present difficulties, and the fully elaborated systems of the sixteenth century, especially under Süleyman (Chapter 72), should not be projected back onto his reign.

Military organization was changing as well. The core of the army remained the provincial cavalry of timar holders, who were mobilized in provincial contingents, but alongside them stood the kapıkulu, the sultan's household troops, including the Janissary infantry, paid in cash from the treasury, and the household cavalry. The Janissaries had grown from a small corps to several thousand men under Mehmed and were increasingly equipped with handheld firearms, though their weapons mix included bows and swords for much of this period. Artillerymen, armorers, wagoners, and other specialists formed additional corps. The financing of this force required cash, and the state's fiscal capacity rose with the growth of trade, the proceeds of conquest, and the taxation of newly acquired territories, supported by land surveys (tahrir) that registered households, lands, and obligations.

The central innovations were not always popular. Mehmed's fiscal policies included a reorganization of landholding in which many lands that had been held as private property (mülk) or as pious endowments (waqf) were taken into the state revenue system, and the details, dated variously to the 1470s, reduced the incomes of established groups, including some religious foundations and descendants of old families. Historians debate the scale of this confiscation and its date, but there is agreement that it caused resentment and that Mehmed's successor, Bayezid II, partially reversed it as part of the political bargain by which he took power. The currency was also manipulated, with silver coinage debased, which affected merchants and soldiers.

The relationship between the ruler and religious institutions was complex. The ulama, the learned class that supplied judges and jurists, were integrated into the state through appointments, salaries, and the system of madrasas that Mehmed expanded, notably around the Fatih complex. The sultan appointed a chief jurisconsult (the office of şeyhülislam became prominent later) and relied on the ulama to confer religious legitimacy, while the Sufi orders that were popular in rural and frontier areas received varying degrees of support, tolerance, or suspicion. These relationships mattered when a competing religious movement appeared on the eastern frontier, because the Ottoman government's relations with Sufi and heterodox groups were not uniform.

Students should keep in mind that the institutional synthesis in this chapter is partial. The mature Ottoman system, with its codified law, elaborate hierarchy of offices, and settled fiscal practices, was a product of the sixteenth century. What Mehmed created was a centralizing government with an expanded household, a bigger army with more salaried troops, and a more intrusive fiscal apparatus. This increased power but also strained relations with local elites, with old Muslim families, with timar holders who feared the loss of their position, and with Turkmen groups who felt marginalized by a state that favored household recruits. Those strains help explain the social basis on which Safavid propaganda would later operate.

Section 70.8

Bayezid II, Dynastic Consolidation, Maritime Trade, and the Ottoman World before 1512

Mehmed II died in May 1481 while preparing a campaign whose target remains uncertain. In the aftermath, his two surviving sons, Bayezid, governor of Amasya, and Cem, governor of Karaman, contested the throne. The Janissaries and much of the household supported Bayezid, and the grand vizier Karamani Mehmed Pasha, who had favored Cem, was killed in the turmoil. Bayezid reached Istanbul first and was proclaimed sultan; Cem proclaimed himself ruler at Bursa and was defeated near Yenişehir in June 1481. Cem fled to the Mamluk sultan in Cairo, tried again to win the throne, failed, and then found refuge with the Knights of Rhodes, who sent him to Europe. He spent years as a valuable hostage in the hands of the Knights, the French crown, and the papacy and died in Naples in 1495. His presence in Europe gave Western powers leverage over Bayezid and, because he was a potential rival, restrained Ottoman policy toward them.

Bayezid II has often been described as a pious, peace-seeking sultan in contrast to his father. That description simplifies. He did make peace, restored some of the properties his father had taken into the state, and cultivated the ulama and Sufi shaykhs. But he also fought. A war with the Mamluks from 1485 to 1491 over Cilicia and the Syrian frontier ended inconclusively and revealed the limits of Ottoman capacity in the southeast; it also warns against reading the conquest of 1516–1517 back into the earlier period. In the west Bayezid campaigned in Moldavia and against Hungary, and he captured the fortresses of Kilia and Akkerman on the Black Sea coast in 1484, strengthening the Ottoman hold on routes to the north.

The reign's naval history is important. The war with Venice that began in 1499 saw the Ottoman fleet, which had grown considerably and had been rebuilt with Greek and Italian expertise, defeat the Venetian navy off Zonchio (Sapienza) in 1499 and capture Lepanto (Inebahtı) in the same year, followed by Modon, Coron, and Navarino in 1500. Peace in 1503 left the Ottomans in possession of key Greek ports. The war demonstrated that the Ottoman Empire had become a Mediterranean naval power, a development whose consequences in the sixteenth century fall within Volume V. The state also welcomed Sephardi Jewish refugees expelled from Spain in 1492, who settled in Istanbul, Salonica, and other cities, bringing commercial networks and technical skills, though accounts of Bayezid's own role are partly legendary and the integration of the refugees was gradual.

Commercial life flourished. The Black Sea trade, secured by the conquest of Caffa in 1475 (when the Ottomans seized the Genoese colony and brought the Crimean khanate into vassalage), the Bosphorus tolls, and Istanbul's markets generated customs revenues. Bursa remained the principal market for Iranian silk, and caravans carried raw silk from Gilan and Shirvan through Tabriz and Erzurum to Bursa, where Italian merchants bought it. Agricultural products, grain, wool, and timber moved through ports and rivers, and the cities of the Balkans and Anatolia grew. This prosperity gave the state revenue but also placed it in competition for the silk routes with Iranian rulers, who were also interested in the lucrative trade.

Inside Anatolia the pressures were growing. Taxation, the survey of lands, the reduction of timars, the cost of campaigns, and the growing power of household officials unsettled Turkmen tribes and Sufi lodges, many of which preserved traditions of devotion to Ali and his family that were not identical to the religious positions favored by the government's legal establishment. The Safavid movement offered an alternative focus of loyalty to such groups, and in the last years of Bayezid's reign, as the old sultan aged and his sons Ahmed, Korkud, and Selim jockeyed for position, dissatisfaction found an outlet in revolt. The central argument of this section is that Ottoman consolidation had not removed political conflict. It had produced a stronger state, but one whose administrative complexity and social tensions were becoming more visible just as a new rival emerged on its eastern border.

Division III

The Safavid Religious Movement and the Emergence of a New Iranian Power

The development of the Safaviyya from a Sufi institution into a militant political movement, the rise of the Qizilbash, and the establishment of Safavid sovereignty.

Section 70.9

The Safaviyya of Ardabil: Sufi Origins and Religious Authority

The Safavid story begins not with a dynasty but with a holy man. Shaykh Safi al-Din Ishaq of Ardabil (1252–1334) was a Sufi master in the northwestern Iranian town of Ardabil, a place on the trade and pilgrimage routes of Azerbaijan, within reach of the Caspian lowlands, the Caucasus, and Tabriz. He was the disciple and son-in-law of Shaykh Zahid Gilani, whose order he inherited, and he built the community that came to be named for him. His followers, drawn from townspeople, merchants, officials, and rural populations, regarded him as a spiritual guide. In the Mongol Ilkhanid period, when Ardabil lay within a political order that generally patronized religious figures, the order received the favor of local rulers and the respect of the Mongol court, and it accumulated endowments. A hagiographical work written about two centuries later, the Safwat al-Safa, is the principal source for his life; it was composed by a disciple, Ibn Bazzaz, and has been revised in later copies, which scholars have shown to reflect later ideological concerns, so it must be used carefully.

The order's early character was Sunni, in the Shafi'i legal tradition, and it fit into the wider pattern of medieval Sufism examined in earlier volumes, in which orders organized disciples around a master through ritual, devotion, and genealogies of spiritual transmission. Safi al-Din's descendants inherited the leadership. His son Sadr al-Din Musa expanded the order's wealth and influence; later heads, among them Khwaja Ali and Ibrahim, maintained the lodge at Ardabil and received visits from rulers. Timur, during his western campaign in the early 1400s, visited the shrine at Ardabil, according to the sources, and confirmed its privileges and released prisoners at the request of the shaykh. The episode, reported in later Safavid and other sources, illustrates the prestige of the order as an institution of regional importance, a recipient of political favors and a mediator between rulers and subjects.

Over the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries the Safaviyya changed in ways that remain debated. Genealogical claims to descent from the seventh imam of the Twelver Shia, Musa al-Kazim, appear in sources produced after the early period; modern scholars have shown that the claim was probably elaborated in the course of the fifteenth century and that the order's earlier Sunni affiliation was gradually replaced by devotion to the family of the Prophet and to Ali. Some historians describe this as a gradual "Shi'itization," others as a process in which religious practices incorporated reverence for Ali without implying a systematically Twelver framework. The distinction matters, because it prevents the assumption that the Safaviyya was always what it later became.

Equally important is the order's transformation from a religious institution to a political and military one. Authority in a Sufi order rested on the spiritual charisma of the master, who was viewed as a guide capable of intercession. In the later fifteenth century the Safavid masters, particularly Junayd and Haydar, expanded the idea of the master so that he could be seen as the "perfect guide" (murshid-i kamil) and as an embodiment of sacred authority, and among certain followers the claims moved toward ideas of divine presence in the leader. Such language was dangerous to established religious authorities and could be politically potent: a leader with an obedient following could mobilize it for war. This development lay at the center of the movement's eventual success and of its conflict with the Ottomans.

Students should therefore avoid describing Safavid history as a straight line from Safi al-Din to Shah Ismail. The order of Ardabil was a changing institution that took on different religious meanings for different constituencies and in different generations. It was a Sufi lodge with property, a network of disciples in Iran, Anatolia, Syria, and the Caucasus, a lineage claiming sacred descent, and, in due course, a militant movement. Each of these aspects mattered, and no single label covers all of them. The next section follows the stage at which the order acquired a martial identity and a Turkmen military following.

Section 70.10

Shaykh Junayd, Shaykh Haydar, the Qizilbash, and Turkmen Religious Mobilization

The turning point came in the mid-fifteenth century under Shaykh Junayd, who succeeded to the leadership around 1447. Junayd's succession was contested by a relative, Jafar, who had the backing of the Qara Qoyunlu ruler Jahanshah, and Junayd left Ardabil. His exile took him through Anatolia and Syria, where he gathered followers among Turkmen communities in regions such as Karaman, Teke, and the Syrian borderlands, populations with their own tribal structures, grievances against the territorial powers, and religious practices often centered on devotion to Ali. Junayd's journeys seem to have broadened the order's geographical reach and its military character. He is reported to have declared holy war against Christian Trebizond and the Caucasus, in a way that linked religious devotion to raiding and tribal warfare, and he also developed his claims to spiritual primacy.

His crucial political relationship was with the Aq Qoyunlu. Uzun Hasan, who was in the process of defeating the Qara Qoyunlu, welcomed Junayd at his court at Diyarbakır and gave him his sister Khadija Begum in marriage, a link that bound the Safavid line to the Aq Qoyunlu ruling house. Junayd subsequently made forays into the Caucasus, and he was killed in 1460 in a battle against the Shirvanshah. The Aq Qoyunlu relationship remained important: Junayd's son Haydar, born of Khadija Begum, later married Alamshah (Halima) Begum, a daughter of Uzun Hasan and of Despina Khatun, who was of Trebizond Greek ancestry according to tradition; their son would be Ismail. Thus the founder of the Safavid state was the grandson of Uzun Hasan on his mother's side, a fact that gave him a claim of dynastic kinship with the rulers whose lands he would later conquer.

Haydar, who came to lead the order in the 1460s and 1470s, completed its martial transformation. Safavid tradition states that in the 1470s or 1480s he introduced a distinctive red headgear with twelve gores, the taj-i Haydari, to be worn by his disciples; the twelve folds were later associated with the twelve imams, though contemporary understanding of the symbolism was probably varied. The red cap gave the followers an identity and a name, Qizilbash (Turkish for "red head"), the term used by outsiders and eventually by the group itself. The disciples organized by tribe and clan, drawn from Turkmen groups that later bore names such as Ustajlu, Shamlu, Rumlu, Takkalu, Dhu'l-Qadir, Afshar, and Qajar, and the term came to designate the military elite of the Safavid state. They were Turkish-speaking, which cannot be separated from the fact that the Safavid leaders, whatever their remote origin, were themselves mainly Turkish-speaking by this period.

Historical Vocabulary
Qizilbash

Qizilbash ("red head" in Turkish) refers to the Turkmen followers of the Safavid leadership, named for their distinctive red cap. The term describes a combination of religious devotion, tribal military organization, and political loyalty to the Safavid master. Qizilbash religious ideas included reverence for Ali and the Prophet's family and, in some circles, the belief that the leader was a divinely guided or even divinely inhabited figure. They were not simply early Twelver Shia, and their relationship to the clerical Shiism that the Safavid state later promoted was contested. The Qizilbash were also tribal confederates with their own interests, which led to bitter competition for office within the new state and to tension with the Persian-speaking bureaucracy.

Haydar's activities were increasingly aggressive. He led campaigns against Circassians in the Caucasus and against the Shirvanshahs of Shirvan, whom the Safavids regarded as enemies for their role in Junayd's death. The Aq Qoyunlu rulers who followed Uzun Hasan, particularly Ya'qub, saw Haydar's growing power as a threat; they feared that a charismatic leader with a military following might challenge them, and they allied with the Shirvanshah. In 1488, in an expedition against Shirvan, Haydar was defeated and killed, with the Aq Qoyunlu forces aiding the Shirvanshah's army. His death was a disaster for the order, and Ya'qub's government imprisoned Haydar's surviving sons and pursued the movement. The Safavid followers, however, did not disperse; they transmitted the memory of the martyred master and of vengeance against his killers, giving the movement a cause.

It is important to explain why this mobilization was possible. For Turkmen groups in Anatolia and Azerbaijan, a charismatic religious leader offered a way of organizing a following that the existing states did not provide. The Ottoman state, with its taxation and cadastral surveys, the Aq Qoyunlu, with their factionalism, and the Mamluks, with their distant rule, left many communities with unmet grievances, and the Safavid master claimed sacred authority and offered participation in a successful, righteous cause. The movement was an alliance of religious devotion, kin networks, military opportunity, and social discontent. Students should not reduce it to any one of these, and should resist the assumption that the early Qizilbash can be equated with later institutional Shiism; their religious language and practices were more varied than that.

Section 70.11

Shah Ismail, the Qizilbash Campaigns, and the Capture of Tabriz, 1488–1501

Ismail was born in 1487 in Ardabil and was only a baby when his father died. The Aq Qoyunlu ruler Ya'qub, who had imprisoned the family, died in 1490, and the succession wars that followed gave the Safavid princes an opportunity. Ismail's eldest brother Sultan Ali led the order for a few years until Rustam, a rival Aq Qoyunlu prince, had him killed in 1494 after Sultan Ali tried to support Rustam's opponents. Ismail, then a child, was sent into hiding in Gilan, the Caspian province of rice-farming communities and forested mountains where local rulers, notably Karki Mirza Ali of Lahijan, sheltered him. He was educated there and surrounded by loyal tutors and guardians. Most accounts of his childhood come from later Safavid chronicles that glorify the future shah, and details of his upbringing should be treated cautiously.

In 1499, when he was twelve, Ismail left Lahijan and went to Ardabil, then moved west to Erzincan in eastern Anatolia where the Qizilbash had gathered. His call to the followers drew thousands from the Anatolian Turkmen groups, among them the Ustajlu, Shamlu, Rumlu, Takkalu, and Dhu'l-Qadir, and he soon had perhaps several thousand armed men, according to the sources, with some Safavid numbers inflated. Soon after, the Qizilbash marched against the Shirvanshah Farrukh Yasar, whom they regarded as the killer of Junayd and Haydar. In 1500–1501 they defeated and killed him at the battle of Gulistan and ravaged Shirvan, a campaign that fused vengeance, religious war, and political ambition.

The Aq Qoyunlu state was by then in disarray. Rival princes, including Alvand and Murad, divided its territories and fought each other and an array of local commanders. Ismail's forces defeated Alvand's army at Sharur, near Nakhichevan, in 1501, and they advanced on Tabriz, the former capital of Uzun Hasan. In the summer of 1501 (July, according to most reckonings), Ismail entered Tabriz and was proclaimed shah, taking the title of the ruler of Azerbaijan and Iran. At the age of fourteen he thus became the head of a state. The sources, which include Persian chronicles written under the Safavids, Venetian dispatches, and Ottoman histories hostile to him, agree on the broad outlines of these events but differ on numbers, on the scenes of the enthronement, and on the ceremonies in Tabriz.

The proclamation had two dimensions. As a political act it declared the establishment of a new sovereign dynasty that claimed the legacy of Persian kingship, an Iranian tradition of monarchy that Ismail invoked with titles and, later, the use of the royal name Ismail alongside the imagery of the Persian epic. As a religious act it declared Twelver Shiism the religion of the realm. The coins struck and the Friday sermon (khutba) delivered in Tabriz carried the names of the twelve imams, a public statement of religious identity, and the Qizilbash were instructed to impose it. This was a bold step in a city whose population was predominantly Sunni, and it entailed religious coercion, including, according to many sources, the cursing of the first three caliphs and punishment of those who refused. The sources report violence in Tabriz and elsewhere, but claims about the number of victims, which sometimes run to many thousands, are not verifiable and should be treated as propaganda or rumor rather than as demographic fact.

Section 70.12

Early Safavid Expansion, Religious Policy, and the Transformation of Iranian Sovereignty

The first decade of Safavid rule was one of rapid conquest. Ismail's forces defeated remaining Aq Qoyunlu princes, notably Murad, and spread through western and central Iran. By around 1503 they held Hamadan and much of the Iranian plateau; Shiraz, Yazd, and Kerman followed, and by 1508 the Safavids captured Baghdad, which gave them control of Iraq and the Shia shrine cities of Najaf and Karbala. Ismail ordered the restoration and patronage of these shrines, which had a profound symbolic effect. Chapter 76 will examine the political institutions and conquests of the Safavid state in full; here the important point is the speed with which the Aq Qoyunlu inheritance passed to the new dynasty, a testimony to the weakness of the old regime and to the strength of the Qizilbash armies.

Government required more than Qizilbash soldiers. The Safavid system relied on a partnership, often uneasy, between the Turkmen military elite and a Persian-speaking bureaucratic class. Persian officials, many of whom had served the Aq Qoyunlu and earlier regimes, handled finance, correspondence, and administration, using traditions that stretched back to the Seljuks and Ilkhanids, as Chapter 58 and Chapter 64 explained. The Qizilbash chiefs governed provinces as emirs and held commands, and the shah was their spiritual leader and their sovereign. The tension between these groups, along with rivalry among the tribes, became one of the defining features of early Safavid politics, and the position of the chief minister, the vakil, a Persian office, became a flashpoint.

Religious policy was a bold experiment. Iran in 1501 had a predominantly Sunni population in its cities and countryside, with Shia communities concentrated in specific places such as Qom, Kashan, parts of Khorasan, and Jabal Amil in Lebanon. The state's declaration of Twelver Shiism required experts to explain and administer it. Since Iran lacked a large corps of Twelver legal scholars, Ismail's government invited scholars from Arab Shia centers, such as Jabal Amil and Bahrain, to come to Iran, a process that Chapter 77 will treat in detail. The introduction of Shia public rituals, the cursing of the first three caliphs, the changing of the call to prayer, and the promotion of commemorations of Husayn's martyrdom were public tools of change. The government used coercion, and Sunni scholars and communities in places like Tabriz, Herat, and Shiraz suffered exile, execution, or forced conversion, while others adapted.

The transformation was neither instantaneous nor uniform. Many Sunnis remained in Iran for generations, particularly in the peripheries; Sufi orders persisted; Qizilbash religious ideas continued to coexist awkwardly with the clerical Shiism being introduced; and rural populations preserved local practices. The picture is one of uneven change, in which a ruling elite imposed religious markers while the actual religious life of the population evolved slowly. Historians therefore prefer to speak of a long process of confessionalization that continued across the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, and this chapter treats only its opening phase.

The northeastern frontier was the arena of the Safavids' other great contest. The Uzbek leader Muhammad Shaybani Khan, a descendant of Genghis Khan's line, had conquered Samarkand, Bukhara, and Herat from the Timurids by about 1507 and threatened Khorasan. In December 1510, near Marv, the Safavid army defeated and killed Shaybani Khan. The victory secured Khorasan, including Herat, for the Safavids and checked the Uzbeks for a time, though the Uzbek threat did not disappear and the frontier remained contested for generations. It also meant that the Safavids' attention was divided: while Ismail was in the east in 1510, Ottoman affairs in Anatolia moved toward crisis, and shortly afterwards he found himself challenged in the west.

Finally, the transformation of Iranian sovereignty must be understood as both a rupture and a continuity. Ismail's government created a new religious identity for the state, and it ended the political fragmentation of the Aq Qoyunlu era, but it also drew on Persian traditions of kingship, administration, art, and court culture. The Safavid dynasty presented itself simultaneously as the leader of a religious community and as the heir to Iranian monarchy, a double claim that gave it unusual flexibility. The later institutionalization of Shiism and the longer history of Safavid government belong to Chapters 76 and 77; the point here is that by 1510 a new Iranian power existed, with armies, ambitions, and a religious message that reached far into Ottoman territory.

Section 70.13

Anatolian Turkmen Communities, Safavid Influence, and the Crisis of Ottoman Loyalty

The Ottoman-Safavid conflict began not at a border but inside Ottoman Anatolia. The eastern and southern parts of the peninsula, from Amasya and Tokat to Teke, Karaman, and the Taurus, contained large numbers of Turkmen pastoralists and village populations whose ties to the Ottoman state were relatively recent and often resented. Many belonged to Sufi lodges and dervish networks, including Bektashi, Kalenderi, and Abdal groups, that revered Ali and the Prophet's family and followed practices that urban jurists regarded as unorthodox. They were not Twelver Shia in a systematic sense, and Ottoman and Safavid observers alike used labels loosely. What mattered politically was that these communities had a religious vocabulary compatible with Safavid appeals and often had grievances against the central government.

The grievances were concrete. Ottoman surveys of land and population registered households for taxation and assigned lands to timar holders, and under Mehmed II some endowed and private lands had been absorbed into the revenue system. Turkmen groups that had formerly lived with considerable autonomy found themselves subject to registration, tax collection, and military obligations, and the policy of settling nomads or moving them to new areas, in some cases through sürgün, disrupted livelihoods. Local timar holders and former frontier warriors who felt displaced by the household-recruited elite shared in the discontent. In some regions drought, rising prices, and the burdens of campaigns made matters worse. The Safavid leader, by contrast, offered the vision of a just ruler who valued his devotees.

The Safavids actively cultivated this support. Missionaries and agents, called khalifas, traveled through Anatolia carrying the leader's message, collecting contributions, and recruiting fighters. Pilgrims went to Ardabil, and young men left Ottoman territory to join the Qizilbash. Ottoman sources describe these activities as subversion, and the government grew alarmed at the movement of people and money toward a rival ruler. In the last years of Bayezid II's reign, the sultan's officials tried to limit the migration, restricted contact between Anatolia and Iran, and punished suspected agents, though the policy was inconsistent as Bayezid sought to avoid war.

The crisis came in 1511 with the rebellion of Şahkulu (Shah Kulu, “servant of the shah”), a religious leader whom hostile Ottoman accounts called Şeytan Kulu (“servant of the devil”) and who was associated with Safavid-oriented networks in the Teke region near Antalya. Beginning in the spring, his revolt attracted Turkmen followers and other dissatisfied groups. The rebels defeated several Ottoman detachments and moved across Anatolia. Grand vizier Hadım Ali Pasha pursued them and was killed at Çubuk, near Sivas, on 2 July 1511, according to a report sent by Prince Ahmed and preserved in Ottoman records. Contemporary and later accounts differ over Şahkulu's fate; his death in that engagement should not be asserted as certain. Some surviving rebels moved toward Safavid territory, while others scattered. The revolt was among the most serious domestic crises of Bayezid II's later reign.

The meaning of the rebellion is debated. Ottoman writers interpreted it as heresy and sedition inspired by Ismail, and Safavid sources claimed it as an expression of loyalty to their shah. Modern historians, drawing on Ottoman registers and narrative sources, see it as a convergence of causes: social and fiscal grievances, the position of Turkmen and dervish groups, local disputes, dynastic competition among the Ottoman princes, whose rivalries drained the state's attention and resources, and a real measure of Safavid religious influence. Ismail's own involvement was limited; he did not send a significant army, but his prestige was the banner under which the rebellion marched. Students should note that the conflict was thus rooted partly in competing forms of sovereignty over populations who did not necessarily recognize the boundaries their rulers drew.

The revolt's consequences were substantial. It made evident that the Safavid movement could affect Ottoman internal politics, and it intensified the arguments over Bayezid's cautious policy. Among the Ottoman princes, Selim, the governor of Trabzon, who had been in contact with the northeastern frontier and who saw the Safavid threat as serious, took a hard line, while Ahmed, governor of Amasya and for a time the favorite of the old sultan and several viziers, was seen as less committed to confronting the Safavids. The rebellion thus fed directly into the succession struggle that is the subject of the next section.

Section 70.14

Selim I, Dynastic Succession, Sunni Legitimacy, and the Road to War, 1512–1514

Bayezid II had many sons, but three had survived to maturity: Ahmed, Korkud, and Selim. The Ottoman system of succession, which did not specify a single heir, meant that princes served as provincial governors and competed for power on their father's death, with the winner often killing his rivals, a practice later regularized in some form as fratricide. By 1511 Bayezid was old and in poor health, and the factions around his sons were active. Selim, whose governorship at Trabzon was far from the center, wanted a post closer to Istanbul and, when refused, crossed into Europe and sought the support of the Janissaries and of the Crimean Tatars, among whom his father-in-law was a prominent figure. He campaigned in Thrace and fought his father's forces at Çorlu in 1511, where he was defeated but not eliminated.

The Janissaries, discontented with Bayezid's cautious policy and attracted by Selim's aggressiveness, were decisive. In the spring of 1512 they pressed Bayezid to abdicate, and on 24 April he did so, in favor of Selim, retiring toward his birthplace and dying within weeks, circumstances that led some to suspect poisoning though there is no firm evidence. Selim then secured his position by eliminating rivals. Ahmed, who tried to resist from Anatolia, was defeated and executed in 1513, Korkud was killed, and several nephews were put to death. The speed and ruthlessness with which Selim consolidated power show the political stakes and the danger of a protracted succession struggle during a period of Safavid expansion, and the episode is a key example of the state's tolerance for violence in dynastic politics.

Selim's attitude toward Ismail was uncompromising. He regarded the Safavid shah as a religious and political threat and was determined to deal with him before undertaking other campaigns. His preparations included measures inside the empire. Ottoman officials compiled lists of suspected Safavid supporters in the eastern provinces, and some were imprisoned, exiled, or killed. Later narrative sources and European reports give very large figures for the numbers killed or imprisoned, with numbers such as forty thousand or more appearing, but these are literary or rumor-based estimates that cannot be verified, and historians treat them as indications of the scale of memory and fear rather than as demographic data. What can be said is that the government took harsh measures against suspected sympathizers, that the measures included registration of people, and that they intensified religious suspicion of Turkmen and heterodox populations.

The legal and ideological preparation was equally important. Ottoman jurists issued opinions (fatwas) declaring the Qizilbash apostates and justifying war against them, with the opinion attributed to the chief jurist Kemalpaşazade (Ibn Kemal) and others in the tradition. Such opinions provided religious justification for a campaign that, in political terms, was a move against a rival state. Propaganda portrayed Ismail as a heretic and tyrant who had desecrated Sunni mosques and tombs and killed scholars, and Ottoman writers emphasized the Safavid persecution of Sunnis in Iran. Safavid writers responded by emphasizing Ottoman oppression. The two sides therefore deployed Sunni and Shia identities as tools of legitimacy, but they did so in service of political aims; Selim's government also contained plenty of men who had been close to Sufi and heterodox milieus, and Ismail's army contained Turkmen who had, only recently, been Ottoman subjects.

Diplomacy and intelligence also played a part. Letters passed between the rulers, in which Selim reproached Ismail and demanded submission, and Ismail answered with defiance, according to the Ottoman compilations that preserve them. Spies and merchants carried news. Ismail was in Khorasan or Iraq during some of these months and did not at first take the Ottoman threat as seriously as he might have, and the Safavid government was also focused on the Uzbeks. By the spring of 1514 Selim had assembled an army and decided to march east.

Section 70.15

The Battle of Chaldiran, August 23, 1514: Firearms, Cavalry, and Imperial Competition

Selim left Istanbul in March 1514 with an army that Ottoman and European sources place in the range of several tens of thousands, with estimates, including some above one hundred thousand counting non-combatants and support troops, varying widely and unverifiable. The core consisted of the Janissaries, equipped increasingly with handheld firearms, the household cavalry, a large train of artillery, and provincial timar cavalry, supplemented by irregular forces. The march across Anatolia was long, through Sivas and Erzincan toward Azerbaijan, and it posed severe problems of supply. The route was difficult, forage was thin, and the Ottoman command reportedly faced grumbling among the Janissaries about the length of the campaign and the hardship, a political problem the sultan managed through a combination of discipline and persuasion.

Ismail's army, also reckoned in tens of thousands in the sources but probably smaller than the Ottoman host, consisted principally of Qizilbash cavalry, mounted archers and lancers who were fearsome in charge and committed to the shah. Safavid commanders had experience of fighting the Aq Qoyunlu and the Uzbeks, in battles where mobile cavalry had prevailed. The Safavids lacked a large body of firearm-equipped infantry and had little artillery. Ismail also followed a strategy of withdrawing and devastating the land to deprive the Ottomans of forage, drawing them deeper into Azerbaijan. Some of his commanders reportedly wanted to attack the Ottomans while they were still struggling in the approach, but Ismail, believing a charge would carry the day, or unwilling to appear to avoid battle, chose to fight on the plain of Chaldiran, northwest of Tabriz, on 23 August.

The Ottomans deployed defensively, with firearm-equipped Janissaries and artillery supporting the center, and mounted formations on the wings. The Anatolian right wing was commanded by Hadım Sinan Pasha, while the Rumelian left was commanded by Hasan Pasha. Some accounts describe guns defended by carts, chains, or earthworks, but details differ and should not be reconstructed with false precision. Safavid cavalry attacks were fierce. On the Ottoman right, Sinan's troops used supporting artillery against Ustajlu forces; on the Ottoman left, the Rumelian wing came under intense pressure, and Hasan Pasha was killed. Selim's center held, however, and Janissary gunfire, artillery, reinforcements, and the wider Ottoman tactical system helped break the Safavid assaults.

Accounts describe Ismail fighting personally and being wounded, while important Safavid commanders, including Muhammad Khan Ustajlu, were killed and the Qizilbash army retreated. The Ottomans seized the Safavid camp and baggage and entered Tabriz in early September. Reports about captured women of Ismail's household are especially difficult to reconcile. Ottoman and later Persian traditions differ over whether Tajlu Khanum was captured, for how long, and what became of her; some accounts also identify Behruza Khanum among the captives. One interpretation holds that Behruza remained in Ottoman custody while Tajlu returned, but that reconstruction is disputed and should not be represented as certain. The battle was an unequivocal Ottoman military victory, yet neither the exact number of prisoners nor the fate of particular household members can be established from a single interested source.

The explanation of the result has been much debated. The Ottoman firearms and artillery were doubtless important, as they enabled infantry to repel cavalry charges, but they were part of a larger system that included disciplined infantry, defensive field tactics, logistics, command arrangements, and numerical superiority. The Safavid army was not incapable of using firearms in principle; its commanders lacked a developed infantry force for them and a tactical doctrine adapted to Ottoman methods. The Safavids' own tactics, reliant on shock cavalry, and Ismail's decision to give battle on the Ottoman terms rather than prolonging the withdrawal also mattered. Historians therefore describe Chaldiran not as a day when guns defeated horses but as an engagement in which an army organized around state-supplied infantry and artillery defeated a cavalry-based tribal army under a leader who chose to fight at a disadvantage. The standard date, 23 August 1514, is given in the Encyclopaedia Iranica article on the battle and in most modern accounts.

The consequences for Ismail were profound. The defeat shattered the aura of invincibility that had surrounded him, a significant matter for a leader whose authority among the Qizilbash rested partly on a belief in his divine protection. Reports from Safavid sources suggest he withdrew into melancholy and a reduced role in subsequent campaigning, though later Safavid chroniclers may have exaggerated this. The Qizilbash chiefs, who now saw that the shah was fallible, grew more assertive, and the political fights among them intensified. Chaldiran was a decisive military defeat for Ismail, but it was not the destruction of the Safavid state, as the next section explains.

Section 70.16

After Chaldiran: Tabriz, Eastern Anatolia, Kurdish Alliances, and the New Imperial Frontier

Selim entered Tabriz on or about 5 September 1514 and stayed about a week. The city, the commercial and political center of Azerbaijan, was thus in Ottoman hands, and some of its notable craftsmen and others were sent to Istanbul, according to Ottoman sources, an example of the use of forced transfers to enrich the Ottoman capital. The sultan visited the principal buildings and received some local notables. But he did not stay. The Ottoman army, far from home, in a devastated region, with its supply lines stretched and the winter approaching, was restless. The Janissaries, who had been dissatisfied from the outset, refused to go further or to winter in Iran, and Selim, aware of the danger of mutiny, withdrew toward Anatolia and wintered at Amasya.

Safavid authority therefore survived. Ismail returned to Tabriz after the Ottoman withdrawal and resumed his rule, and the Safavid state retained its core territories in Azerbaijan and Iran. The reasons are partly logistical: the Ottoman army could win a battle but not hold a distant capital under the conditions of the time. They are also political: the Safavid regime, though shaken, retained the loyalty of many Qizilbash groups and had no immediate rival to replace it. Selim's retreat left the Safavids a chance to rebuild, and in the years that followed they would develop their own firearm-equipped forces. The pattern of Ottoman campaigns reaching Tabriz or Baghdad and withdrawing would recur through the sixteenth century, as Chapter 78 will show.

The consequences were more durable in the frontier districts. In 1515 Ottoman forces defeated Ala al-Dawla of Dulkadir and established Ottoman overlordship there, initially governing through a member of the ruling house; the separate Dulkadir eyalet developed later, probably early in Süleyman I's reign. In the upper Tigris region, Ottoman commanders including Bıyıklı Mehmed Pasha negotiated with Kurdish rulers and fought Safavid-appointed governors. Diyarbakır came under Ottoman authority in 1515, but control elsewhere was neither immediate nor permanent. Mardin's town was surrendered in October 1515 and then retaken by Safavid forces; Ottoman troops defeated the Safavid commander near Koçhisar in May 1516, and the fortress finally fell after a further siege in late 1516 or early 1517, with differing dates in the records. Kemah and other fortresses followed their own campaign chronologies. These events illustrate a progressive conquest shaped by negotiation, military reversals, and local allegiances, not a single annexation of the whole frontier in 1515.

The incorporation of these regions depended on local rulers, above all the Kurdish chiefs of the mountainous borderlands. Many Kurdish rulers had been under Aq Qoyunlu authority and then Safavid authority, and many were Sunni and uneasy with Safavid religious policy. The Ottoman effort to win them was led by Idris Bidlisi, a Kurdish scholar and administrator from Bitlis who had served the Aq Qoyunlu and then the Ottomans and who, with the sultan's authority, negotiated with the chiefs, offering confirmation of their hereditary lands, the status of a self-governing district under tribute and military obligations, and the protection of the Ottomans. Many Kurdish emirs accepted, and the arrangement became a model for later Ottoman handling of the region. Their detailed institutional history belongs to Chapters 69 and 74. Here it is enough to note that the Ottoman victory depended on the strategic choices of local rulers, who chose Ottoman overlordship for reasons of religion, interest, and security, not because of coercion alone.

The frontier that emerged was neither fixed nor continuous. Between the Ottoman and Safavid heartlands lay mountains, plateaus, and frontier fortresses, held by garrisons and local chiefs, with trade and pilgrim routes crossing them. The Ottomans restricted trade with Safavid lands, including the silk trade, and increased surveillance of travelers and religious figures. Frontier fortresses were garrisoned and supplied. Over the next decades the line shifted with every campaign, and it was only in the seventeenth century that a recognizable border was stabilized by treaty, as Volume V will explain. The key point for this chapter is that the frontier emerged through campaigns, negotiations, and the changing allegiances of local societies. The Ottoman-Safavid order was the result of such processes, not of a single treaty line drawn after one battle. It also set the stage for the next Ottoman move, directed not eastward but southward against the Mamluks, whose ruler had been wary of Ottoman victory and of the Safavid alternative alike.

Section 70.17

Gunpowder Weapons, Siege Artillery, Infantry, and the Transformation of Warfare

Gunpowder weapons were not a single technology. Large siege guns, or bombards, fired heavy stone or later iron shot and were used to batter walls, but they were slow to load, difficult to move, and expensive to cast and transport. Smaller field guns could be deployed in battle, protecting infantry formations or breaking charges, and could be moved on carts. Handheld firearms, of which the Ottoman tüfek is the standard term, ranged from early matchlock arms to more developed muskets and were effective in massed fire when handled by trained soldiers who could be protected during the slow process of reloading. Each had a different tactical purpose, and an army that used them effectively needed gunsmiths, founders, saltpeter and sulfur, wagons and draft animals, disciplined infantry, and a treasury able to pay for them.

The Ottoman state excelled at assembling these elements. It controlled sources of saltpeter and metals in the Balkans and Anatolia, ran foundries at Istanbul, Edirne, and other places, and employed specialist craftsmen, including converts and foreign experts, within the state's organization. Gábor Ágoston's studies emphasize the Ottomans' capacity to adopt and adapt, producing guns locally, buying technology from Europe, and integrating artillery corps into the household troops. Fortification design also changed, as walls were lowered and thickened and earth ramparts built to absorb shot, a process whose fuller development belongs to the sixteenth century. The Janissaries' increased use of firearms, along with a salaried and drilled structure, made them effective in the defensive formations seen at Chaldiran.

A comparison of three engagements illustrates the point. At Constantinople in 1453 the guns were important in a siege of walls, where they worked alongside manpower, naval operations, and engineering, as §70.3 showed. At Otlukbeli in 1473, according to Ottoman and other accounts, artillery and handgunners contributed to the defeat of an Aq Qoyunlu army that, despite Venetian promises, had little comparable firepower; here the guns were part of a field battle. At Chaldiran in 1514 the combination of infantry firearms and cannon, deployed defensively, repelled a cavalry charge. In each case the weapons mattered only because they were integrated with logistics, tactics, and command. And in none was the outcome predetermined by the possession of guns alone.

The Safavid response was significant. After Chaldiran, the Safavids, who had witnessed the effect of Ottoman firepower, began to acquire firearms and to train infantry, though the process was gradual, constrained by the preferences of the Qizilbash cavalry elite, who regarded firearms as unmartial or as a threat to their status, and by fiscal limits. Under Shah Ismail's successor, Tahmasp I, Safavid forces included musketeers, the tofangchis, and in the 1500s and 1600s the Safavids acquired artillery, with major military reforms taking place under Shah Abbas I, as Chapter 79 will discuss. The differences between Ottoman and Safavid military technology in 1514 should therefore not be treated as permanent. They reflected organization and resources at a given moment.

Technology did not make empires on its own. Historians who study the early modern period, including Ágoston and Kenneth Chase, point out that the adoption of firearms depended on specific political and economic conditions, and that the same weapons existed in many societies that did not create large empires. Chase's work on the global history of firearms stresses the interaction between gunpowder weapons and the steppe cavalry traditions of Eurasia; Ágoston emphasizes Ottoman institutions and fiscal capacity. This section introduces the broader scholarly debate over the phrase "gunpowder empires," taken up in the Historical Debate below. The core lesson is that a gun is a tool within a system, and the system is what decided battles.

Historical Debate

Gunpowder Empires and the Causes of Ottoman-Safavid Transformation

The question: Did gunpowder weapons cause the rise of large, centralized Islamic empires in the sixteenth century, or were they one factor among many? The term "gunpowder empires," associated with the work of Marshall Hodgson and elaborated by William McNeill in The Age of Gunpowder Empires, 1450–1800 (1989), grouped the Ottomans, Safavids, and Mughals as states whose consolidation was linked to artillery and firearms.

Interpretation A — Firearms were a major driver of state formation: Proponents argue that the cost of artillery, the need for specialized production, and the effectiveness of firepower against fortresses and cavalry favored states that could centralize resources, and that the Ottoman capture of Constantinople and victory at Chaldiran illustrate the military advantage of gunpowder. On this view, the new technology helped large, bureaucratic states overcome tribal and decentralized rivals.
Interpretation B — Technology depended on prior political and fiscal capacity: Critics, among them Gábor Ágoston and others, note that the Safavids rose to power before adopting firearms at scale, with cavalry and religious mobilization, that Ottoman centralization preceded and enabled gunpowder use, and that the label obscures differences among the three states. They also stress that Ottoman victories involved logistics, tactics, and organization, and that the Safavids and Mughals developed gunpowder forces after their foundation. On this view, the term is a misleading shorthand.

Evidence: The debate rests on the same material: chronicles of battles, archival records of foundries and payments, and comparative evidence from other regions. The disagreement concerns causation and the comparability of the cases.

Why it matters: This chapter treats gunpowder as significant but not sufficient, and treats religion, institutions, fiscal capacity, and frontier societies as co-equal factors.

Student task: Using the evidence of Constantinople, Otlukbeli, and Chaldiran, argue for one interpretation, and explain what the opposing interpretation would say in response.

Section 70.18

Sunni and Shia Imperial Legitimacy, Dynastic Authority, and Political Ideology

Both dynasties needed to explain why they ruled. The Ottoman claim rested on several pillars. The dynasty could point to its record as a frontier warrior state fighting the Christian powers, a role that conferred prestige in the Islamic world. It claimed hereditary right through the house of Osman, with the principle that only members of the dynasty could rule. It governed through law, issuing kanun and appointing judges to administer sharia, and it supported the religious establishment through endowments, colleges, and offices. After 1453 it added the claim of imperial inheritance, the capital of the Romans, and the title of a ruler of two continents. These claims would be extended after 1517, when control of Mecca and Medina and the Mamluk inheritance added new dimensions, as Chapter 71 will describe.

The Safavid claim was different in emphasis. At its center was the person of the shah, who was the heir of the Safavid masters and thus a spiritual guide for the Qizilbash, and in the eyes of some devotees a sacred figure. The dynasty asserted descent from the family of the Prophet through the seventh imam, giving it a lineage that few rivals could match. It also invoked the Persian tradition of kingship, with the titles and imagery of Iranian monarchy, and it proclaimed Twelver Shiism as the faith of the state. The combination allowed the shah to appeal both to devotional Turkmen followers and to Persian administrators and townspeople familiar with royal tradition, though the arrangement contained tensions between charismatic and legal conceptions of authority.

Both states used institutions to communicate power. Court historians wrote chronicles that portrayed the ruler as divinely favored; poets praised victories; religious scholars issued opinions affirming the legitimacy of the dynasty; and architecture, ceremonies, and processions displayed authority. The Ottoman mosque complexes and palaces of Istanbul were statements about sovereignty, as were the Safavid shrines and the ritual of the Friday sermon in the shah's name. Coinage and the khutba were basic markers of sovereignty in the Islamic world, and the Safavid choice to place the twelve imams on coins and in the sermon was as significant as the Ottoman Friday prayers in Hagia Sophia. Public rituals, including commemorations of Husayn's martyrdom in Safavid territory, built a shared devotional calendar.

The relationship between political declaration and religious change must be handled carefully. A ruler could announce a confession as the state religion in a day, but institutional change took generations. Jurists had to be trained, mosques and colleges built, books produced, and local practices transformed. The Ottoman side faced a similar gap: the state positioned itself as the champion of Sunni orthodoxy against the Safavids, but its own population included many people whose devotion to Ali and Sufi practices did not fit the legal definitions the jurists applied. Neither empire's population was religiously homogeneous, and neither was politically uniform. The contest was therefore never simply between two sects, since each state contained a variety of communities and had to manage them.

Students should therefore understand the rivalry as simultaneously territorial, dynastic, military, economic, and religious. The Ottoman and Safavid rulers contested control of the borderlands, claimed rival forms of Islamic sovereignty, competed for the silk trade and the shrine cities, and mobilized armies whose soldiers believed they fought for righteous causes. Religion supplied language and organization for these conflicts, but it did not on its own explain them. The emphasis on Sunni-Shia opposition in later narratives obscures how frequently rulers from the two states pursued policies determined by interest and how much cooperation, trade, and exchange also existed.

Section 70.19

Trade, Population, Migration, Religious Communities, and the Social Costs of Imperial Transformation

The political changes of 1451–1515 reached ordinary people through taxation, conscription, migration, and violence. Cultivators in the Balkans and Anatolia, who made up most of the population, felt the arrival of Ottoman officials who registered their households and lands and levied taxes in kind and in cash. Conquest could bring a period of devastation, with the burning of fields and the flight of villagers, but once administration was established, taxes might be lower or more predictable than under the previous rulers, depending on the place; evidence varies, and historians are cautious about general claims. Pastoral groups, especially Turkmen, faced settlement, registration, and the loss of grazing rights, which, as seen above, fed unrest. In Iran, the Safavid conquest meant new rulers and new tax demands, and the population of Azerbaijan, which had suffered the wars of the Aq Qoyunlu, now supplied soldiers and revenue to the new state.

The cities experienced the changes most visibly. Constantinople was repopulated and rebuilt, as shown in §70.4, and its growth drew migrants, merchants, and artisans. Bursa remained the silk center, and Edirne, Salonica, and other cities grew. Tabriz was a major commercial hub, connecting Iran with the Black Sea, Anatolia, and the Mediterranean, and its capture by Ottoman troops in 1514 and the removal of its craftsmen illustrate the disruption that war could bring. The silk trade was especially sensitive. Raw silk from Gilan and Shirvan flowed westward through Azerbaijan to Ottoman markets, where Italian and other merchants bought it, and both empires saw its taxation as valuable. The Ottoman government, concerned about trade that enriched Safavid territory, sought to restrict it, an effort that later became an embargo policy, to be discussed in Volume V.

Religious communities experienced the changes differently. Greek Orthodox people in the capital faced conquest, enslavement, and resettlement, but also the creation of a patriarchate that gave their church a recognized place. Armenians, whose numbers in Istanbul and Bursa grew, built a communal structure, while Jews, both those already in Ottoman lands and, after 1492, the Sephardi refugees, became an important part of commercial and artisan life. In the Balkans, Christian communities paid special taxes, supplied boys to the devshirme, and in some regions saw gradual conversion to Islam. Kurdish chiefs gained confirmation of their positions by choosing Ottoman suzerainty, and Kurdish villagers and pastoralists felt the changes through the taxes and military demands their chiefs transmitted. Under the Safavids, Sunni communities faced pressure and sometimes violence, while Shia scholars from outside Iran gained patronage. In all cases outcomes depended on place, timing, and local relationships, and students should resist the assumption that expansion produced the same results everywhere.

Enslaved people were a significant element in these societies. Captives taken in war, from sieges such as Constantinople or from raids in the Balkans and the Caucasus, were sold, ransomed, or retained by their captors, and slavery was integral to the household system of the Ottoman court and elites, in which enslaved and levied recruits were trained for service. Women, whether as members of elite households, as artisans' wives and workers, as peasant laborers, or as captives, appear less frequently in the sources, which were written mostly by men, but their labor and their roles in households and endowments were fundamental. Women could hold property and found endowments under Islamic law, and some did, as later chapters on family and gender will show.

The overall picture is of a period that brought both opportunity and burden. New imperial systems offered security of trade routes, access to larger markets, administrative order, and avenues of advancement for some, including the soldiers, scholars, officials, and merchants who benefited from the new capitals. They also imposed heavier taxation, military levies, forced resettlement, religious pressure, and the destruction of war on others. The social costs of the imperial transformation fell unevenly, and the sources tend to record the experience of elites better than that of ordinary people. The wise student keeps both aspects in view and recognizes that historical judgment requires attention to who gained and who bore the costs.

Section 70.20

From the Late Medieval Frontier to the Ottoman-Safavid Imperial Order

The events described in this chapter transformed the political geography of the Middle East in a single lifetime. The Ottoman conquest of Constantinople gave the dynasty a capital that joined its European and Anatolian possessions and a source of prestige that echoed across the Mediterranean and the Islamic world. The subsequent conquests in the Balkans and on the Black Sea turned a large regional state into a multi-ethnic empire with an administrative system capable of governing diverse populations. In the east, the Safavid movement, which began as a Sufi order and became a force of Turkmen warriors, founded a dynasty that unified much of Iran for the first time since the Mongol era, reconnecting a plateau fragmented since the fall of the Ilkhanate and giving it a new religious identity.

Their meeting was the result of interacting factors. Military technology, though important, mattered chiefly as part of larger institutions of recruitment, supply, and finance. Administrative consolidation gave the Ottomans the resources to field large armies and the household elite to manage them, but it also created tensions with groups that felt excluded. Religious mobilization gave the Safavids an unusually devoted following and an ideological claim, but it also created a dependence on Qizilbash chiefs and a conflict with established religious norms. Commercial resources, especially the silk trade and the customs revenues of ports and straits, supported both states. And the choices of frontier societies, whether Turkmen groups joining the Qizilbash, Kurdish chiefs choosing Ottoman suzerainty, or Anatolian villagers joining Şahkulu, decided where the line of rivalry fell.

The two states should be compared without being equated. The Ottomans had a deeper bureaucracy, a salaried household army, and a large revenue base from territories with different economies. The Safavids had a charismatic dynasty, a tribal military elite, and a narrower fiscal base, and they were still in the process of building the institutions of government. Both claimed to represent true Islam, but with different conceptions of authority. They also shared much: Turkish-speaking military elites, Persianate court culture, Sufi backgrounds, and the heritage of Mongol and Turkmen political traditions. Their later histories, which Volume V will relate, were not determined in advance by the events of 1514.

Chaldiran created an enduring strategic imbalance without eliminating Safavid sovereignty. The Ottomans demonstrated their effectiveness in major engagements and extended their control into eastern Anatolia and parts of northern Mesopotamia, but the Safavid state survived and continued to govern Tabriz after the Ottoman withdrawal. The frontier remained a zone of conflict and negotiation over the following generations. The new balance also affected the Mamluk Sultanate. Selim subsequently conquered Mamluk-controlled Syria and Egypt in 1516–1517; the Hijaz entered Ottoman sovereignty largely through the allegiance of the Sharif of Mecca rather than through Ottoman armies storming Mecca and Medina. The military campaigns and the complex incorporation of the Arab provinces and holy cities belong to Chapter 71. They would transform the scale of Ottoman authority across the Middle East.

The transition from the late medieval order to the early modern one was therefore marked by the replacement of the shifting, multi-polar world of Mongol successor states, Turkmen confederations, Mamluks, and Anatolian principalities by a more bipolar structure of two large empires, flanked by the Mamluks, who would soon vanish, and the Uzbeks, who threatened Safavid Khorasan. This was a transition in scale, resources, and ideology, but it did not erase older institutions and loyalties; Persian bureaucracy, Turkmen military culture, Sufi networks, and local autonomy persisted within the new states. The concluding argument of the chapter is that the Ottoman-Safavid order resulted not from gunpowder or religion alone, but from the interaction of military technology, political institutions, fiscal capacity, dynastic ambition, religious mobilization, and the choices of frontier societies. With that structure in place, Volume V turns to the mature empires.

What Changed

Cause and Effect

Conquest, Mobilization, and Imperial Rivalry

Long-term causes

The collapse of the Ilkhanate and Timurid dominance left a political vacuum; the Ottoman state had grown across two continents; Turkmen confederations and Sufi networks offered organized followings; Constantinople had been weakened over generations.

Short-term causes

Mehmed II's accession and ambitions; the Aq Qoyunlu succession wars; Selim's seizure of power; Safavid recruitment in Anatolia; the Şahkulu revolt and Ottoman fears of subversion.

Immediate effects

The fall of Constantinople; the establishment of Safavid rule in Tabriz; the Ottoman victory at Chaldiran; the incorporation of eastern Anatolian districts and Kurdish chiefs into the Ottoman sphere.

Long-term consequences

A bipolar Ottoman-Safavid regional order; religious confessionalization in Iran; the Ottoman turn toward the Arab provinces; the development of a contested frontier lasting generations.

Everyday Life and Social History

Cities, Villages, Markets, and the Displaced

In rebuilt Constantinople, a family relocated by sürgün from a Balkan or Anatolian town might receive a house abandoned by earlier residents, find a place in a neighborhood organized around a mosque or church and its endowed market, and take up a craft or trade. In the countryside, villagers paid taxes recorded in survey registers, which also recorded their households, crops, and mills, and gave labor or goods for campaigns. Pastoral households moved with their flocks between winter and summer pastures, negotiating with settled neighbors and tax collectors. In the cities of Iran and Anatolia, merchants, artisans, and religious figures organized themselves in guilds, lodges, and endowments, and Friday sermons, festivals, and commemorations marked the rhythm of public life.

Source limitations are acute. Chronicles concentrate on rulers and battles; tax registers record households as units of revenue; and the experiences of women, the enslaved, and the poor must be inferred from legal records, endowment deeds, and occasional narrative asides. Historians supplement these with archaeology, architecture, and comparative evidence, but must acknowledge how much remains unknown about ordinary life during these decades of transformation.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the transformation of the late medieval Middle East into a region dominated by competing imperial systems. It began with the Ottoman conquest of Constantinople, which was the outcome of deliberate political, naval, and logistical preparation. The fortress of Rumeli Hisarı, the assembly of a fleet and artillery, the diplomacy that isolated the Byzantine emperor, and the sultan's mobilization of manpower together made possible a siege that earlier Ottoman rulers had been unable to complete.

The siege of 1453 was a contest between two military systems. Ottoman artillery damaged the walls, but the defenders repaired much of the damage, and the outcome depended also on the Golden Horn operation, repeated assaults, the exhaustion of the defenders, and the wounding of Giustiniani. Historians continue to debate the weight of artillery relative to these factors, and the sources, from Barbaro's diary to the Ottoman chronicles, each reflect their authors' interests. The conquest brought killing, captivity, and displacement to the city's inhabitants.

After the conquest Mehmed II rebuilt Constantinople as an imperial capital through resettlement, endowments, construction, and the reorganization of religious communities, including the installation of Gennadios Scholarios as patriarch. The city did not become an entirely new place overnight; Byzantine buildings and institutions persisted, and the name Istanbul coexisted with Constantinople for centuries. The conquest also changed Ottoman sovereignty, strengthening the sultan against older elites and encouraging claims of imperial inheritance.

Ottoman expansion continued in the Balkans, on the Black Sea, and in Anatolia. Serbia, the Morea, Bosnia, Trebizond, Candar, and much of Karaman were incorporated, and the victory at Otlukbeli demonstrated Ottoman superiority over the Aq Qoyunlu. Not every campaign succeeded, as Belgrade and Skanderbeg's Albania showed. Consolidation required surveys, timars, kadis, taxation, and the devshirme, and it produced a more centralized state with greater fiscal and military capacity, though one that strained relations with local elites and Turkmen groups.

Bayezid II's reign continued this consolidation while revealing its tensions. His struggle with Cem, the war with the Mamluks, the naval war with Venice, the arrival of Sephardi refugees, and the growth of commerce marked a period of stability and growth, but internal discontent in Anatolia and the rivalry among his sons set the stage for crisis.

In Iran, the Safavid order grew from a fourteenth-century Sufi institution at Ardabil into a militant, Turkmen-based movement under Junayd and Haydar, adopting the red cap that gave the Qizilbash their name. Ismail, whose family was hunted after Haydar's death, emerged from hiding, gathered Qizilbash followers, and captured Tabriz in 1501, proclaiming himself shah and declaring Twelver Shiism the religion of the state. The early movement should not be equated with later institutional Twelver Shiism.

The new dynasty expanded rapidly, taking Baghdad in 1508 and defeating the Uzbeks at Marv in 1510. Its government rested on an uneasy partnership between Qizilbash military chiefs and Persian-speaking bureaucrats, and its religious policy involved coercion and patronage in a population that remained religiously diverse. The transformation was gradual, uneven, and a continuation as well as a break with earlier Iranian traditions.

Ottoman-Safavid conflict arose from Safavid influence among Anatolian Turkmen, fiscal and social grievances, the Şahkulu revolt, dynastic competition within the Ottoman house, Selim's accession, and the use of Sunni and Shia identities as instruments of legitimacy. At Chaldiran in 1514 the Ottoman combination of Janissary firearms, artillery, and organization defeated the Qizilbash cavalry. The victory was decisive but limited: Selim could not hold Tabriz, and the Safavid state survived.

The aftermath saw the incorporation of eastern Anatolian districts, the winning of Kurdish chiefs through Idris Bidlisi, and the emergence of a contested frontier shaped by campaigns, negotiations, and local choices. The gunpowder transformation was real but dependent on state capacity, and the concept of "gunpowder empires" remains contested. Social consequences, including resettlement, enslavement, taxation, trade disruption, and religious pressure, fell unevenly across communities.

The chapter's conclusion is that the Ottoman-Safavid order resulted from the interaction of military technology, institutions, fiscal capacity, dynastic ambition, religious mobilization, and the choices of frontier societies. It closes Volume IV by showing how the fragmented world of Mongol successors, Turkmen confederations, and Anatolian principalities gave way to larger empires, and it leads directly to Chapter 71 and the Ottoman conquest of Syria, Egypt, and the Hijaz.

Key Terms

Rumeli Hisarı
The fortress built in 1452 on the European shore of the Bosphorus, which allowed Ottoman artillery to command the strait together with Anadolu Hisarı.
Anadolu Hisarı
The fortress on the Asian shore of the Bosphorus built by Bayezid I in the 1390s.
Theodosian Walls
The triple system of fortifications protecting Constantinople on its landward side.
Golden Horn
The inlet on the northern side of Constantinople, guarded by a chain and entered by Ottoman ships dragged overland in 1453.
Bombard
A large early cannon used to batter fortifications; slow to load and difficult to transport.
Union of the churches
The Byzantine attempt, proclaimed in 1452, to reunite Greek and Latin churches in return for Western aid; opposed by many Orthodox.
Sürgün
The Ottoman practice of transferring populations, forcibly or with incentives, to repopulate cities or serve other state purposes.
Waqf
A charitable endowment whose revenues supported mosques, schools, markets, and other institutions.
Patriarch
The head of an Orthodox or Armenian church; under Mehmed II the Orthodox patriarch became an interlocutor of the state.
Millet
A later term for Ottoman religious communities; fifteenth-century arrangements were less formal than the label implies.
Devshirme
The levy of Christian boys for service in the sultan's household and army.
Kapıkulu
The sultan's salaried household troops and officials, including Janissaries.
Janissary
A member of the sultan's elite infantry corps, increasingly armed with firearms.
Timar
A revenue grant to a cavalryman in exchange for military service.
Sipahi
A provincial cavalryman holding a timar.
Kanun
Sultanic law issued by the ruler, complementing sharia.
Tahrir
An Ottoman survey registering population, land, and revenues.
Cizye
The poll tax paid by non-Muslim subjects in exchange for protection and exemption from military service.
Divan
The imperial council that handled affairs of state.
Otlukbeli
The 1473 battle in which Mehmed II defeated Uzun Hasan of the Aq Qoyunlu.
Safaviyya
The Sufi order of Ardabil founded by Shaykh Safi al-Din that became the basis of the Safavid dynasty.
Safwat al-Safa
The hagiographic biography of Safi al-Din by Ibn Bazzaz, revised over time and a key but problematic source.
Murshid-i kamil
"Perfect spiritual guide," a title used by the Safavid leadership.
Qizilbash
Turkmen followers of the Safavids, named for their red cap, who formed the dynasty's military elite.
Taj-i Haydari
The red headgear of twelve folds introduced under Shaykh Haydar.
Turkmen
Turkish-speaking tribal groups of Anatolia, Azerbaijan, and northern Syria.
Twelver Shiism
The Shia tradition recognizing twelve imams; proclaimed the state religion by Ismail in 1501.
Confessionalization
The long process by which a state and its institutions shape a population's religious identity.
Ghulat
A label for groups attributing superhuman qualities to Ali or to leaders, often used polemically.
Khutba
The Friday sermon, in which the ruler's name is mentioned as a mark of sovereignty.
Fatwa
A legal opinion by a jurist; used to justify Ottoman war against the Qizilbash.
Münşeat
A compilation of chancery documents, such as Feridun Bey's collection preserving the 1514 letters.
Tüfek / tofang
Ottoman and Persian terms for a handheld firearm.
Gunpowder empires
A comparative label for the Ottomans, Safavids, and Mughals; its explanatory value is debated.
Suzerainty
Overlordship over a ruler who keeps local authority, as with Kurdish chiefs and Dulkadir under Ottoman policy.

Identification & Significance

Junayd and Haydar
Leaders who militarized the Safavid order.
Shah Ismail I

Şahkulu

Selim I

Idris Bidlisi
Kurdish scholar and negotiator with Kurdish chiefs.

Uzbek leader defeated and killed at Marv in 1510.

Tabriz

Ardabil
Home of the Safavid shrine and order.
Chaldiran
Site of the 1514 battle.
Trebizond

Comparative Documentary Study of Ottoman and Safavid Legitimacy

This project asks you to compare how the Ottoman and Safavid dynasties represented their authority in the decades around 1500.

  1. Select documents. Choose two or three sources, such as Tursun Beg, Kritoboulos, poems attributed to Ismail, or the 1514 letters.
  2. Establish provenance. For each, identify author, audience, date, edition, and known problems of transmission.
  3. Analyze claims. Identify the sources of legitimacy each text asserts, such as lineage, law, conquest, religion, or kingship.
  4. Compare with other evidence. Use scholarship, such as Allouche, Babayan, İnalcık, and Ágoston, to test the claims against material evidence.
  5. Write. Produce a 4–6 page essay that uses careful language about uncertainty and does not treat any single source as neutral.

Chapter Reflection

Chapter 70 matters because it marks the passage from the fragmented political world that followed the Mongol conquests to the large empires that dominated the Middle East for the next four centuries. The Ottoman state that took Constantinople and the Safavid state that took Tabriz were each the product of long processes described in earlier chapters, but their consolidation in the decades around 1500 created a new regional structure whose effects reached from the Balkans to Central Asia.

The chapter also shows how to think about historical causation. Gunpowder, religion, and individual ambition all mattered, but none sufficed. The more illuminating factors were organizational: how a state raised money, fed an army, recruited officials, and won the loyalty of populations at its edges. The same lesson applies to the later chapters of this work, in which technology, ideology, and politics repeatedly interact.

Continuing structures run forward from these events. The Ottoman-Safavid frontier shaped the history of Kurdish, Armenian, and Turkmen communities for centuries. The Safavid proclamation of Twelver Shiism began a long process that Chapters 76 and 77 examine in full. The Ottoman capital on the Bosphorus remained the center of an empire whose institutions Chapter 72 will describe. The strains between central government and frontier societies, evident in Şahkulu's revolt, recur throughout later Ottoman and Iranian history.

Chapter 71 turns south, to the Ottoman conquest of the Mamluk Sultanate and the incorporation of Syria, Egypt, and the Hijaz. With that conquest the Ottoman Empire became a Middle Eastern empire in the fullest sense, and Volume V begins its study of the empire and its Safavid rival at the height of their power.
