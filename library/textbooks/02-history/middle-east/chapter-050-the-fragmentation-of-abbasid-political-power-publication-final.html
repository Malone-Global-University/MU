Part XII — Society, Persianate Revival, and the Fragmentation of Caliphal Power

The Fragmentation of Abbasid Political Power

A History of the Middle East: From Ancient Civilizations to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

In 836 CE the caliph al-Mu'tasim left Baghdad and founded a new residence on the Tigris, roughly a hundred kilometers upstream, which he named Samarra. Within a few decades the city had palaces, barracks, markets, and mosques stretching along the river for many kilometers, and its ruins, still visible from the air, are among the largest abandoned urban sites of the early Islamic world. Samarra was built to house a caliph and his army. Yet on the night of 10–11 December 861, the caliph al-Mutawakkil was murdered inside its palace by members of that army, and in the following decade several successors were removed violently, while another died after a short reign. A city designed to secure caliphal power had become the stage on which that power was contested.

This chapter asks how an imperial government that, in the 780s and 790s, had collected revenue from Egypt to Khurasan and commanded armies that campaigned from the Byzantine frontier to Central Asia became, by the middle of the tenth century, a caliphate whose ruler lived in Baghdad under the supervision of a Daylamite military dynasty from the mountains south of the Caspian Sea. On 19 December 945, the commander Ahmad ibn Buya entered Baghdad. The caliph, al-Mustakfi, received him, conferred titles upon him and his brothers, and was within weeks deposed and blinded. Yet a caliph remained: al-Muti' was installed, his name was proclaimed in the Friday sermon, and the institution survived for more than three further centuries in Baghdad before the Mongol conquest examined in Chapter 63.

The central argument of the chapter is that this transformation was neither a single catastrophe nor a simple "decline." It was a prolonged reorganization of sovereignty. Four processes interacted. Civil war and the recruitment of new military forces altered the relationship between caliphs and the men who commanded their armies. Fiscal strain, produced partly by the cost of those armies and partly by the loss of control over provincial revenues, limited the caliphs' capacity to pay, appoint, and enforce. Provincial governors such as Ahmad ibn Tulun in Egypt and Syria turned delegated authority into hereditary rule while continuing to acknowledge the caliph. And in the early tenth century, senior military commanders assumed the practical powers of government, culminating in the Buyid conquest of Baghdad. None of these developments, taken alone, explains the outcome. Their interaction does.

The chapter also insists on a distinction that students will need throughout the remaining volumes: the difference between effective political control and recognized legitimacy. A Tulunid ruler in Fustat, a Samanid amir in Bukhara, and a Buyid amir in Baghdad all governed territories that the caliph in practice did not govern. Yet each, at least formally, sought recognition from the Abbasid caliph, whose name appeared on coins and in the Friday sermon, and whose investiture could confer standing upon a ruler's claim. The caliphate had lost much of its power to command. It retained, for a long time, the power to confer legitimacy, and regional rulers had reasons to want it preserved.

Finally, the chapter distinguishes political fragmentation from civilizational decline. The same decades that saw the Anarchy at Samarra, the Zanj Rebellion, and the Qarmatian raids also saw the work of historians such as al-Tabari, geographers and administrative theorists, physicians and philosophers, and a commercial economy that remained connected from the Mediterranean to the Indian Ocean (examined in Chapter 51). The chapter's subject is the political order, not the vitality of the society that order governed. Readers should keep that difference in view throughout.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Describe the structure of Abbasid government in the early ninth century and identify the structural difficulties of governing an extensive empire through delegated authority.
  2. Explain the causes, course, and consequences of the civil war between al-Amin and al-Ma'mun, distinguishing immediate destruction from longer-term institutional change.
  3. Analyze al-Mu'tasim's military recruitment and the foundation of Samarra, avoiding the treatment of "Turkic soldiers" as a single political faction.
  4. Reconstruct the sequence of the Anarchy at Samarra (861–870) and explain it as a struggle among identifiable interests.
  5. Evaluate the interaction of military expenditure, provincial revenue, and bureaucratic competition in the Abbasid fiscal crisis, including the partial recovery under al-Mu'tadid.
  6. Explain how Ahmad ibn Tulun and his successors built a hereditary regional state in Egypt and Syria while retaining connections to Abbasid legitimacy.
  7. Assess the Qarmatians as a movement with religious ideas, social constituencies, and political aims, rather than as simple religious deviance.
  8. Trace the emergence of the office of amir al-umara and distinguish its establishment in 936 from the Buyid entry into Baghdad in 945.
  9. Describe the rise of the Buyids from Daylamite military networks and explain why 945 marks an institutional turning point rather than the end of the caliphate.
  10. Distinguish nominal from effective authority by analyzing the khutba, coinage, investiture, and administrative continuity.
  11. Evaluate primary sources, especially the Abbasid-centered chronicles of al-Tabari and the retrospective narrative of Miskawayh, for their uses and limits.
  12. Argue for or against the claim that political decentralization in this period amounted to civilizational decline.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did the Abbasid government lose direct control over increasingly autonomous provinces?
  2. How did military recruitment and military factionalism change the relationship among caliphs, commanders, and bureaucrats?
  3. Why did fiscal pressure and declining access to provincial revenue weaken the central government, and why was al-Mu'tadid's recovery temporary?
  4. How did the Tulunids establish and maintain regional authority while retaining connections to Abbasid legitimacy?
  5. Why did military leadership eventually assume many of the caliph's practical governing responsibilities?
  6. How did the Buyids transform Abbasid political authority without abolishing the caliphal institution?
  7. What does it mean to say that the caliph kept legitimacy but lost power, and what evidence supports that claim?
  8. What are the strengths and biases of the Abbasid-centered chronicles on which this chapter relies, and how do historians compensate for them?

Historical Context

Chapters 44 through 49 established the Abbasid state at its height. The Abbasid revolution of 750 brought to power a dynasty claiming kinship with the Prophet through his uncle al-Abbas, supported by networks that were strongly Khurasani; al-Mansur founded Baghdad in 762; and under al-Mahdi, Harun al-Rashid, and al-Ma'mun the caliphate developed a bureaucratic government centered on the vizier and the revenue, army, and chancery bureaus, supported by an agricultural economy centered on the irrigated lands of Iraq (Chapters 44 and 45). Baghdad became a city of markets, scholars, translators, and households of every status (Chapters 46 through 48). This chapter does not retell that history. It asks what happened to the institutions described there.

Two earlier developments matter especially. First, the Abbasid caliphate had never governed all of the early Islamic world. Spain had been ruled since 756 by a surviving Umayyad prince, Morocco was dominated from the 780s by the Idrisids, and Ifriqiya (roughly modern Tunisia and eastern Algeria) had been governed from 800 by the Aghlabids, a dynasty of governors that retained formal allegiance to Baghdad. The Abbasids' own appointees, in other words, had already begun to found local lines. Second, the caliphate's army had changed. The Khurasani soldiers (abna al-dawla) who brought the dynasty to power aged into a privileged, increasingly expensive and politically demanding constituency, and caliphs from the early ninth century looked for other sources of military manpower. That search, examined in Section 50.4, shaped everything that followed.

The period also belongs to the wider story of the eastern Islamic world told in Chapter 49. The Tahirids (governing Khurasan from 821), the Saffarids (from the 860s), and the Samanids (recognized in Transoxiana from the 870s and 880s) each practiced varying degrees of autonomy and each retained some formal relationship with the caliph. They appear here only where they bear on the central political system. Chapter 51 will turn to the commercial networks that continued across these jurisdictions, and Chapter 53 will pick up the narrative where this chapter ends, examining the sustained governance of the Buyids and the relationship between their amirs and the caliphs.

Division I · The Abbasid Imperial System under Strain
Section 50.1

Abbasid Central Authority and the Problem of Imperial Government

At the beginning of the ninth century the Abbasid caliph was simultaneously the leader of the Muslim community, the commander of its armies, the highest source of justice, and the patron on whom courtiers, scholars, soldiers, and poets depended. He ruled through a set of bureaus (diwans) in Baghdad that handled land revenue, military pay rolls, correspondence, and the postal and intelligence service known as the barid, and through a vizier who coordinated them. Provinces were governed by appointed amirs, who collected taxes, commanded local troops, and, in principle, forwarded the surplus to the capital. The institutions are examined in Chapter 45. Here the important point is how much depended on the personal relationship between caliph and appointee.

Distance was one difficulty. A message and an army took weeks to travel between Baghdad and the major provincial capitals, and a governor who controlled the local tax bureau, the local army, and the loyalty of local elites held considerable leverage over the caliph. Provinces also differed profoundly. Iraq's irrigated plain, Egypt's Nile valley, the highland and oasis agriculture of Iran, and the Central Asian borderlands had distinct tax systems, social structures, and political traditions. In Iran, rural notables (dihqans) and local princely families remained powerful; in Egypt, Coptic scribes staffed the tax administration much as they had under earlier rulers; in Arabia, tribal alliances counted for more than any bureau. The caliphs governed by working through these intermediaries and not by replacing them.

Money was a second difficulty. The caliphate's army had to be paid, and its payment depended on a fiscal system in which provincial surplus arrived in Baghdad only if governors chose or were compelled to send it. A caliph who lacked funds could not pay soldiers, and unpaid soldiers might threaten him. A caliph who lacked loyal soldiers could not compel governors to remit revenue. The system could, therefore, slide into a vicious circle, and the political history of the ninth century is partly a history of how that circle tightened.

None of this implies that the Abbasid government was weak or inefficient in 800. By the standards of its day it was an impressive administrative machine, and its architecture of bureaus survived to be used by successors for centuries. The problem was structural rather than a matter of personal failings: a government that relied on delegation across vast distances, and on armies that required constant payment, could remain stable only while its center held the military and fiscal initiative. Sections 50.2 through 50.8 trace how that initiative slipped.

Section 50.2

The Fourth Fitna and the Civil War of al-Amin and al-Ma'mun, 809–813

Harun al-Rashid died in March 809 while on campaign in Khurasan, leaving a succession plan that was, in the later chroniclers' account, elaborate and unstable. He had named his son Muhammad, al-Amin, whose mother Zubayda was of Abbasid descent, as first heir; al-Ma'mun, whose mother was a Persian concubine, as second, with extensive authority in Khurasan; and a third son, al-Qasim al-Mu'taman, as third. The chronicle tradition, notably al-Tabari, reports that Harun had the arrangement witnessed in Mecca and deposited documents in the Ka'ba. Historians treat details of these accounts with caution, since the sources were written after the war and may reflect the claims of the victor, but there is general agreement that the plan established a divided inheritance in which one brother's autonomy in the east potentially limited the other's authority.

Tension grew soon after al-Amin's accession. His vizier, al-Fadl ibn al-Rabi', whose family's position depended on al-Amin, had reason to fear al-Ma'mun's accession. Al-Ma'mun's own advisers, notably al-Fadl ibn Sahl, a Persian-born convert who rose to dominate his government, had equal reason to resist al-Amin's. In 810 al-Amin sought to alter the line of succession in favor of his own son, and al-Ma'mun refused. An army sent from Baghdad under Ali ibn Isa ibn Mahan was defeated near Rayy in 811. Al-Ma'mun's commanders, above all Tahir ibn al-Husayn and Harthama ibn A'yan, advanced west, and by 812 they were besieging Baghdad.

The siege lasted more than a year and was a civilian catastrophe. Chroniclers describe bombardment by catapult, burning districts, and the destruction of large portions of the city's eastern and western quarters. They also describe the role of irregular, urban volunteers and street fighters who defended neighborhoods when the regular army was demoralized and unpaid. The war thus involved not only competing princes but a population that organized its own defense. Al-Amin was captured and killed in September 813, and al-Ma'mun became caliph, though he did not enter Baghdad until 819.

The war's consequences should be distinguished carefully. The physical damage to Baghdad was substantial, but the city recovered and remained the great metropolis of the Abbasid world for generations. The institutional consequence was more lasting: the victor's power came from commanders and administrators drawn from Khurasan and from a Persianate eastern political network, and the Baghdad-based military of the old regime, including many of those who had defended al-Amin, was defeated and discredited. A caliph's authority now rested visibly on the loyalty of particular commanders and regions. That did not cause fragmentation by itself, but it revealed how conditional caliphal power could be.

Section 50.3

Al-Ma'mun, Provincial Power, and the Reconstruction of Caliphal Authority

After his victory al-Ma'mun remained in Merv, in Khurasan, until 818, governing through al-Fadl ibn Sahl. In 817 he took the dramatic step of designating Ali al-Rida, a descendant of Ali ibn Abi Talib recognized as imam by many Shia, as his heir. The decision, which many historians interpret as an attempt to reconcile Alid and Abbasid claims and to bring pro-Alid constituencies into his coalition, was bitterly resisted in Baghdad, where a faction proclaimed an Abbasid prince, Ibrahim ibn al-Mahdi, as caliph. Al-Fadl ibn Sahl was killed in 818 and Ali al-Rida died the same year. Al-Ma'mun then moved west and entered Baghdad in 819, abandoning the Alid succession plan.

Reconstruction relied on delegation. Al-Ma'mun appointed Tahir ibn al-Husayn as governor of Khurasan in 821, and the office passed to Tahir's descendants. The Tahirids governed in the caliph's name, sent tribute, and had their governorship confirmed with each succession, but in practice they governed the east. Their position illustrates the logic of delegation: al-Ma'mun needed a trusted, capable deputy in a distant region, and the deputy's family acquired roots there. The Tahirids also held positions in Baghdad itself; members of the family commanded its security forces, serving the caliph at the center while their kinsmen governed the periphery.

Al-Ma'mun was an energetic caliph. He sponsored scholars and translations, the best-known features of his reign (Chapter 46), and attempted to impose doctrinal conformity through the inquisition known as the mihna, begun in 833 (Chapter 47). He campaigned against the Byzantines and suppressed revolts, including a Coptic rebellion in the Egyptian Delta that he himself visited the province to suppress. He died in 833 near Tarsus on the Byzantine frontier. His reign shows that the caliphate could be powerful and activist. It also shows how that power rested on negotiated relationships with provincial commanders and on a military whose composition was already being questioned, a problem his successor addressed in a different way.

Section 50.4

Al-Mu'tasim, Military Recruitment, and the Foundation of Samarra

Al-Mu'tasim, al-Ma'mun's brother, succeeded in 833. He had long commanded a force of Turkic soldiers that he had acquired, often by purchase, from Central Asia, and he enlarged it as caliph. The soldiers, known in the sources as ghilman or, loosely, atrak ("Turks"), were typically young men from the steppe and the Transoxianan frontier who were brought to the Abbasid lands as slaves or captives, converted, trained, and attached to the household or command of a patron. Alongside them stood other new contingents: soldiers from Farghana and Ushrusana in Central Asia and men from North Africa (Maghariba). The recruitment was a deliberate policy. A force loyal to the caliph personally, trained to his methods and dependent on his payment, could substitute for the older Khurasani abna al-dawla and for Arab tribal and urban forces whose political loyalties had proved uncertain in the civil war and beyond.

Historical Vocabulary
Ghulam, Abna al-Dawla, and Military Households

A ghulam (plural ghilman) was a young male servant or retainer, in the military context typically a slave soldier purchased or captured, converted to Islam, trained, and freed or kept in a bond of dependence to a patron. The status of a ghulam was not simply that of a menial. Many rose to command armies, hold governorships, and amass great wealth, and their legal slave origin coexisted with substantial political power. The abna al-dawla ("sons of the dynasty") were the descendants of the Khurasani soldiers who brought the Abbasids to power, a privileged military group in Baghdad. Both terms describe groups, not unified factions: each included commanders with competing interests, and individual soldiers shifted allegiance with pay, patronage, and circumstance. The system of military slavery would reappear, in a different form, in the Mamluk Sultanate treated in Chapter 65.

The new army created friction with Baghdad's population. The sources, with the usual caution about their later perspective, report incidents between soldiers and civilians, and al-Mu'tasim's decision to build a new city was explained in part by the need to separate his forces from the capital's crowded neighborhoods. In 836 he founded Samarra on the east bank of the Tigris, north of Baghdad. It was laid out as a palace complex surrounded by quarters allocated to different military groups, with their own markets and mosques, and, according to the geographer al-Ya'qubi, who wrote in the late ninth century, to individual commanders and groups of soldiers. The layout was itself a statement about the army's organization.

Al-Mu'tasim's reign also illustrates the capacities of the new system. His forces, combined with Khurasanian commanders, suppressed the long-running revolt of Babak al-Khurrami in Azerbaijan (837), and in 838 campaigned successfully against the Byzantine city of Amorium. But he also showed the system's potential for danger. The commander al-Afshin, a Central Asian prince who had been instrumental in the victory over Babak, was arrested and died in custody in 841, after what sources describe as a trial in which his loyalty was questioned. The case showed that powerful commanders could become rivals, and that caliphs saw them as such. The difficulty was not that Turkic troops were a monolithic danger, but that any large, expensive, specialized military force had the capacity to make demands, and a caliph who depended on it had to keep paying and managing it.

Section 50.5

The Samarra Caliphate and the Changing Balance of Political Power

For roughly fifty years, from 836 to 892, Samarra served as the principal seat of the caliphs (with an interruption of a few years under al-Mu'tamid's predecessors in the 860s when Baghdad was contested). Its monuments suggest an imperial ambition. The Great Mosque, built under al-Mutawakkil between 848 and 852, was among the largest mosques ever built and is known for its spiral minaret, the Malwiya. Palaces such as the Jawsaq al-Khaqani, with their courtyards, gardens, and decorated halls, introduced styles in stucco and glazed pottery that were exported to other regions; the mosque built by Ahmad ibn Tulun in Egypt, discussed in Section 50.11, shows their influence directly. In 859 al-Mutawakkil began an additional city, al-Ja'fariyya, a few kilometers to the north, demonstrating the continuing expansion of the complex.

Politically, Samarra concentrated the caliphate's military resources in a single location under the caliph's eye. This had administrative advantages: soldiers were paid and supplied through the court, commanders were close to the caliph, and the capital was removed from the pressures of Baghdad's urban politics. It also created vulnerabilities. A caliph lived next to the soldiers whose loyalty he needed, and could be deposed by them without the need for a distant army. The military was not unified: Turkic commanders, Maghribi and Farghani contingents, and the remnants of the Khurasani soldiers had separate leaderships, and rivalry among them could escalate. Moreover, civil functions such as tax collection and justice continued to be conducted at least partly from Baghdad, so the court at Samarra and the city of Baghdad stood in a complicated relationship, with Baghdad administered by a governor, often a member of the Tahirid family, who answered to the caliph but had his own base of support.

Al-Mutawakkil, caliph from 847, tried to manage this balance. He reversed the doctrinal policy of the mihna, favoring traditionalist scholars (Chapter 47), and seems to have sought to reduce the influence of the leading Turkic commanders by promoting his own sons and other personnel. He briefly took the court to Damascus in 858, an episode that some historians read as an attempt to establish an alternative base, though it was short-lived. His success was limited: his efforts to diminish the commanders' standing produced the plot that ended his life.

Section 50.6

The Anarchy at Samarra, 861–870

Al-Mutawakkil was murdered on the night of 10–11 December 861 by Turkic guards in the palace at al-Ja'fariyya, with, according to the chronicle tradition, the foreknowledge of his son and designated heir al-Muntasir, who had been alienated by the father's preference for a younger brother. Al-Muntasir became caliph but died after about six months, in 862. Later accounts sometimes allege poisoning or military involvement, but the circumstances of his death are not established with certainty. The leading commanders at the moment, including Wasif and Bugha, then selected a new caliph, al-Musta'in, a grandson of al-Mu'tasim, excluding the sons of al-Mutawakkil. This is the beginning of the period known as the Anarchy at Samarra.

A bare list of caliphs conveys the scale of upheaval. Al-Musta'in (862–866) fled to Baghdad in early 865 with some commanders, was besieged there by forces still at Samarra who had proclaimed al-Mu'tazz, and was forced to abdicate in 866; he was later killed. Al-Mu'tazz (866–869) was deposed in 869 and died in confinement. According to the chronicler al-Tabari, soldiers' demands for pay were a major cause: when pressed for funds, al-Mu'tazz allegedly asked his mother Qabiha to supply money and she refused. Al-Muhtadi (869–870), who attempted a reform of court life and relations with the military, was himself killed in 870. His successor, al-Mu'tamid (870–892), reigned for twenty-two years but was largely overshadowed by his brother al-Muwaffaq, who commanded the army and led the campaign against the Zanj.

Primary Source Window

Al-Tabari, Tarikh al-rusul wa'l-muluk (History of the Prophets and Kings), on the Samarra Crises

Creator: Abu Ja'far Muhammad ibn Jarir al-Tabari (d. 923), scholar resident in Baghdad · Date: completed c. 915 · Type: Universal chronicle, arranged year by year and compiled largely from earlier reports and eyewitness testimony

Context: Al-Tabari's history is the most detailed surviving narrative of the ninth-century caliphate. For the Samarra years he relied on accounts collected from participants and observers, often including court officials and members of the military establishment. He wrote in Baghdad within living memory of many of the events.

Paraphrase (not a direct quotation): Al-Tabari reports that the murder of al-Mutawakkil was carried out by a group of guards led by Turkic officers, that the caliph's son al-Muntasir was aware of the plot, and that the principal commanders subsequently directed the choice of his successors. In his account of al-Mu'tazz's deposition, he describes soldiers demanding payment, the caliph asking his mother for money, and her refusal, after which the soldiers turned against him.

Analysis: The narrative is rich in names, dates, and chains of events, and gives a sense of how soldiers, commanders, and court women pressed their interests. But historians note limits. Al-Tabari wrote from an Abbasid, Baghdad-centered perspective, often reproducing stories that blame particular individuals, and his informants had their own loyalties. The fiscal explanation, in which a caliph is killed over unpaid wages, is dramatic and may be partly literary. It should be treated as a plausible representation of a real problem, the difficulty of paying the army, rather than as a precise account of one event. The source also tells us little about provincial reactions or about the experience of ordinary residents of Samarra and Baghdad.

It is important to reconstruct the period as a political sequence rather than describe it as generalized disorder. The actors had identifiable interests: commanders sought to preserve their wealth and offices, soldiers to receive pay, court officials and princes to secure the succession, and the Tahirid governors of Baghdad to protect their own position and the city. Alliances shifted. Commanders who had supported one caliph turned against him; Turkic leaders fought each other; Maghribi and Farghani contingents sometimes opposed the Turkic leaders; and the caliphs themselves tried to play factions against one another. Nor was military control absolute. The commanders chose caliphs from the Abbasid family and did not attempt to replace the dynasty, an indication of how much the dynastic institution mattered even to those who commanded its armies.

Section 50.7

Military Expenditure, Provincial Revenue, and the Abbasid Fiscal Crisis

The caliphate's income came mainly from land taxes (kharaj) on agricultural districts, supplemented by other levies, customs, and the revenues of crown estates. Its heartland, the irrigated plain of southern and central Iraq known as the Sawad, produced much of the revenue, together with the remittances of provinces such as Egypt, Syria, Khuzestan, and Fars. Expenditure, in turn, was dominated by the army and the court. A professional army that was paid regularly, equipped, and sometimes granted gifts at accessions required a very large and reliable income. Historians cannot reconstruct the budget with confidence for most years: surviving figures come from later administrative handbooks and may reflect idealized or particular dates, and this chapter does not present precise totals.

Several pressures converged. The cost of the army grew as the caliphs enlarged it and as accessions, depositions, and rival claimants led to repeated bonuses. Provincial governors who controlled revenue had an incentive to keep more of it. The Tahirids in Khurasan sent tribute, but the regimes that followed, notably the Saffarids from the 860s, sent little. In Egypt, as the next sections show, the governor Ahmad ibn Tulun retained much of the revenue in the 870s and 880s. The Zanj rebellion in southern Iraq (869–883, with its social history in Chapter 48) disrupted the agricultural lands and the port of Basra, damaging the central revenue base at the same moment that al-Muwaffaq's campaigns increased costs. Competition among fiscal officials, and a growing reliance on tax farming and on assignments of revenue to military men (iqta'), changed how money flowed. The caliph's treasury paid out of a smaller and less certain income.

The fiscal story, however, is not one of uniform decline. Under al-Muwaffaq and then al-Mu'tadid (892–902) the government reorganized and recovered. Al-Mu'tadid is reported by chroniclers to have left a substantial treasury. He is also credited with a calendar reform of 895 that adjusted the start of the tax year to the agricultural season, which had drifted out of alignment with the lunar-based Islamic calendar, a measure that benefited cultivators and tax collection alike. This recovery matters for two reasons. It shows that the caliphate was capable of reform and had not been irreversibly undermined by 892. And it helps explain why the later crisis was more severe: the recovered system again came to depend on the personal ability of the caliph and his officials, and when a child came to the throne in 908 and court expenditure grew, the improved finances did not last.

Section 50.8

Viziers, Bureaucrats, Commanders, and the Contest for Government

The central government was never only a contest between caliph and army. A class of secretaries and fiscal officials, drawn from families of administrators, controlled the bureaus. Their expertise in accounts, tax law, and correspondence made them indispensable, and they competed with each other through patronage networks and family dynasties, such as the Banu'l-Jarrah and the Banu'l-Furat, who alternately dominated the vizierate in the late ninth and early tenth centuries. Viziers sometimes confiscated the property of fallen rivals (musadara), turning confiscation into a fiscal tool and a political weapon. Bankers and financiers, among them Jewish and Christian court bankers whose loans advanced funds against future revenues, formed an additional layer of influence.

Military commanders had their own relation to this civilian bureaucracy. They needed it to pay their troops and to supply the court with administrative continuity, but they also resented its control of money, and civilian officials resented the assignment of revenue to soldiers. Palace households, including the caliph's mother, wives, and senior servants, represented a third arena. Women of the court such as Zubayda, Qabiha, and later Shaghab, mother of al-Muqtadir, held property and patronage and could influence appointments.

It would be wrong to describe this as simple dysfunction. The competition among civilian and military elites was a normal feature of premodern government, and the bureaus continued to produce revenue accounts, appointments, and correspondence throughout the period. In some respects the contest was a sign of the state's resilience: groups fought over control of the government because the government was worth controlling. What changed was the balance. By the early tenth century the vizier depended increasingly on the support of the commanders, and the commanders on revenue the vizier could or could not supply, a mutual dependence that created repeated crises.

Historical Debate

Who Controlled Whom? The Turkic Soldiers and Abbasid Authority

The question: Did the recruitment of Turkic soldiers cause the loss of caliphal authority, or was the caliphs' dependence on a paid, specialized army a symptom of broader political and fiscal problems?

Interpretation A — The army undermined the caliphate: Older narratives, partly following medieval writers, treated the arrival of Turkic soldiers as a turning point after which caliphs became puppets. On this view, the concentration of military power in a force with no ties to the population of Iraq, and loyal chiefly to its commanders, ended the caliphs' independence.
Interpretation B — The army was an instrument of a changing state: More recent scholarship, including studies of the Samarra military by Matthew Gordon and the broader work of Hugh Kennedy, emphasizes that the soldiers were not a unified bloc, that they were recruited by caliphs for sound political reasons, and that the problem lay in financing and managing an expensive professional army across a large empire. The same system of military patronage appeared in many later Islamic states.

Evidence: Both views draw on the same chronicle narratives, which require careful reading: they were written by civilians and sometimes reflect hostility toward soldiers, and they tend to describe soldiers collectively when particular commanders were acting for particular reasons.

Division III · Provincial Autonomy and the Emergence of Regional States
Section 50.9

Egypt before the Tulunids: Governors, Revenue, and Imperial Control

Egypt held a special place in the Abbasid fiscal system. The Nile valley and Delta, whose geography is described in Chapter 2, produced large, predictable agricultural surpluses, and the province's land tax was among the most valuable sources of income outside Iraq. Fustat, the garrison-city founded after the Arab conquest, served as the administrative capital. The province's revenue depended on the annual flood, measured by officials at the Nile gauge on the island of Rawda, a Nilometer rebuilt in 861 under al-Mutawakkil's authority, and on a tax administration whose clerks were largely Coptic Christians with generations of expertise in surveying and accounting. Governors came from Baghdad or Samarra, often held office briefly, and sometimes governed through deputies without residing in the province.

The relationship between governor and caliph was strained by revenue extraction. Egyptian taxation, increasingly heavy, provoked discontent among Copts, several of whom rebelled in the Delta; the largest of these revolts in the early ninth century was suppressed in 832 with al-Ma'mun's personal presence. The military context was also changing. Under al-Mu'tasim the caliphate reportedly removed Arab soldiers in Egypt from the official pay rolls, replacing them with troops of the new type, a measure that weakened an older Arab military class and tied the province's defense to the central government's new army. Governors now had access to Turkic commanders, some of whom were appointed to rule. The result was a province ruled by men who were part of the ruling military elite, rather than local notables, but who were, through distance and the weakening of Samarra's authority, increasingly able to act independently.

Control of Egyptian revenue was thus contested between the governor, who needed funds for his troops, and a separate fiscal director appointed by the caliph's government. By the 860s that director was Ahmad ibn al-Mudabbir, a skilled administrator whose assertive tax policy created local resentment. This division of fiscal and military control was meant as a safeguard. It created an opening, as the next section shows, for a governor who could eventually unite both.

Section 50.10

Ahmad ibn Tulun and the Formation of the Tulunid State, 868–884

Ahmad ibn Tulun was born in 835, the son of a Turkic soldier of the Samarra army who had himself come to the Abbasid lands as a slave. He was educated in Samarra and Tarsus, a frontier city where he encountered religious scholarship as well as military practice. When the commander Bakbak, his stepfather according to the sources, received the governorship of Egypt in 868, he sent Ahmad to govern in his place. Ahmad arrived in Fustat that year as a deputy, with no independent standing.

Over the following years he gradually consolidated control. He built a loyal military force, purchasing and recruiting ghilman, including Turkic and Sudanese soldiers, and strengthened his position among local elites. Most importantly, he gained control of Egypt's fiscal administration. Ibn al-Mudabbir, the caliph's fiscal director, was transferred to Syria in the early 870s, and Ahmad took the revenue into his own hands. He sent remittances to Baghdad at first, but increasingly retained the surplus for his own army and building projects. The Abbasid government was by then preoccupied with the Zanj rebellion and the struggle for control at Samarra, and had little leverage.

In the late 870s Ahmad extended his authority into Syria, taking Damascus and other cities and, according to the sources, establishing control as far as the Cilician frontier. This brought him into direct conflict with al-Muwaffaq, who, after the suppression of the Zanj, turned his attention to Egypt and Syria. The confrontation reached a dramatic moment in 882, when, according to the chronicles, the caliph al-Mu'tamid, resentful of his brother's dominance, left Samarra with the apparent intention of joining Ahmad in Egypt and was intercepted and returned. Ahmad convened jurists in Damascus, where he is reported to have denounced al-Muwaffaq, though not the caliph himself. The detail is revealing: his challenge was directed at the commander who held real power, and he preserved the fiction of loyalty to the caliph.

Ahmad died in March 884, leaving a state in which his son Khumarawayh succeeded him without any new appointment from Baghdad. The hereditary transfer is the key institutional development. It meant that a provincial governorship had become, in practice, a family possession, and the Abbasid government had to decide whether to accept or fight it. That the state was not a modern independent nation-state should be emphasized: no one in Egypt claimed a distinct national sovereignty. Ahmad's authority was that of a governor who had become a regional ruler, and his legitimacy still derived from an Abbasid appointment.

Section 50.11

Tulunid Government, Economy, Urban Development, and Political Legitimacy

The Tulunid state financed itself from Egyptian taxation, and a considerable part of that revenue funded the army. The army included Turkic ghilman, Sudanese infantry, and Greek (Rumi) military slaves, and the state maintained a navy for the Mediterranean coast and a force able to campaign in Syria. Fiscal administration continued to employ Coptic and Muslim secretaries, and the government's income benefited from a strong agricultural economy and from trade through Fustat. Chroniclers, writing long after the dynasty's fall, describe Ahmad as a careful administrator who kept accounts and avoided waste, a reputation that may owe something to later contrast with his successors.

Ahmad's urban projects expressed his authority. In 870 he began a new official quarter, al-Qata'i, northeast of Fustat, a palace-and-barracks district modeled in part on Samarra, the city in which he had grown up. Its center was a large parade ground and palace, and the district had its own markets and housing for the military and officials. In 872 he founded a hospital (maristan) in Fustat, and he supported water supply works. Between 876 and 879 he built the congregational mosque that bears his name, an enormous brick-and-stucco building with a courtyard and a minaret, whose design and decorative vocabulary clearly reflect Samarra. The mosque survives, though the rest of al-Qata'i was largely destroyed or absorbed into later Cairo.

The caliph's name in the sermon and on coins preserved the province's place within the Abbasid system; the governor's practical control gave him the power to make decisions. Section 50.12

Khumarawayh, Tulunid Succession, and the Abbasid Reconquest of Egypt

Khumarawayh succeeded his father in 884 and faced an immediate military challenge from al-Muwaffaq's forces. A confrontation at al-Tawahin in Syria in 886 ended in a compromise rather than a decisive victory. Under the settlement, the caliphate recognized Khumarawayh as governor of Egypt and parts of Syria and the frontier districts for an extended term, reportedly thirty years, in return for tribute. The date and terms are important because they show the caliphate negotiating with a hereditary provincial ruler, and doing so from the logic of delegation: the arrangement was framed as an appointment, even as it recognized a ruling family.

Khumarawayh also pursued diplomacy. In 895 he arranged the marriage of his daughter Qatr al-Nada to the caliph al-Mu'tadid, accompanied, according to later accounts, by a famously lavish dowry and procession to Baghdad. The marriage tied the Tulunid family to the Abbasid house, and also illustrates the scale of the court's expenditure. Khumarawayh's rule has an unfavorable reputation in the sources, which stress extravagance in palace building and entertainment. This reputation should be treated cautiously, since it was recorded after the dynasty's fall and may overstate. Nevertheless, the cost of maintaining a large army and court did strain the dynasty's finances.

Khumarawayh was killed in 896 in Damascus, reportedly by members of his household. His successors were young and short-lived rulers dominated by military leaders, and the army fractured. The Abbasid government under al-Mu'tadid and then al-Muktafi took advantage. In 905 an Abbasid army under Muhammad ibn Sulayman al-Katib occupied Egypt, ended Tulunid rule, and, according to the sources, largely demolished the palace quarter of al-Qata'i. Direct Abbasid rule was restored.

The reconquest shows that the caliphate could still organize and send an army across Syria and Egypt. But it did not resolve the underlying problem. Egypt's new governors faced the same difficulty of controlling a distant province with its own fiscal and military resources, and within three decades the province was again under a semi-autonomous Turkic dynasty, the Ikhshidids, who took power in 935. The Tulunid precedent therefore mattered as an institutional model, and chapters 53 and 54 will consider how Egypt's later rulers, including the Fatimids, built on or broke from it.

Division IV · The Transformation of the Abbasid Political Order
Section 50.13

The Abbasid Recovery under al-Muwaffaq and al-Mu'tadid

From about 870, al-Muwaffaq, the caliph's brother, served as the dominant figure in the government. He commanded the army, directed fiscal affairs, and led the campaigns that defined the late ninth century. His principal task was the Zanj rebellion, a revolt in southern Iraq that threatened Basra and the lower Tigris and Euphrates; its social composition and the experience of its participants are discussed in Chapter 48. For the political history, its significance lies in what it required: a long, costly campaign, concluded in 883 with the capture of the rebel capital, al-Mukhtara. The effort produced a seasoned army and an experienced leadership, and the victory restored the south to central control.

Al-Muwaffaq also fought the Saffarids. The Saffarid ruler Ya'qub ibn al-Layth had advanced from Sistan, overthrown the Tahirids at Nishapur in 873, and marched toward Baghdad, only to be defeated near Dayr al-Aqul in 876. The defeat halted a major challenge, but it did not restore Abbasid authority in the east; Chapter 49 describes the Saffarids and then the Samanids, who defeated the Saffarids in 900 and governed Khurasan and Transoxiana with caliphal recognition.

Al-Muwaffaq's son, al-Mu'tadid, became caliph in 892 and moved the court back to Baghdad, ending the Samarra period. He was a vigorous military leader, campaigning against rebels and rivals in the Jazira, Jibal, and Syria, and a capable administrator, reorganizing the bureaus with the help of his vizier. He built a palace complex in Baghdad that renewed the capital's role. His reign, with its fiscal recovery and its calendar reform, was an attempt to rebuild the central government on firmer foundations. It was a real if partial success, and it demonstrates that the history of fragmentation is not one of unbroken descent. Yet the recovery rested on a few persons, and the problems that emerged after his death in 902 showed how dependent it was on them.

Section 50.14

The Qarmatians, Regional Revolts, and Challenges to Caliphal Authority

The Qarmatians emerged from the Isma'ili Shia movement, a branch of Shiism that held that the imamate descended through Isma'il, son of Ja'far al-Sadiq, and that awaited a hidden imam or his return (Chapter 47). In the 870s and 880s, a missionary organization spread through southern Iraq, with a base around Kufa, and its adherents were known by the name of one early leader, Hamdan Qarmat. The movement drew support from rural and peasant communities and from marginal groups discontented with taxation and with the existing order, while its leaders propagated a doctrine of imminent redemption. Later in the 890s, the Isma'ili mission divided over the claims of the leader who would found the Fatimid dynasty (Chapter 54), and the Qarmatians in Iraq and Arabia rejected those claims, so that the Qarmatians and the Fatimids became, in time, distinct and at times hostile movements.

In eastern Arabia the movement acquired a territorial base. Around 899–900 Abu Sa'id al-Jannabi established a state in al-Bahrayn, the coastal region of eastern Arabia, centered later on al-Ahsa. The Qarmatian state governed agricultural oases and coastal towns, fielded armies, and for several decades attacked the Abbasid pilgrimage routes and cities. In the early tenth century, Qarmatian forces defeated Abbasid armies, raided Basra in 923, and in 930, under Abu Tahir al-Jannabi, attacked Mecca during the pilgrimage and carried off the Black Stone. Later historical accounts date the Black Stone’s return to 339 AH (950–951 CE). Accounts differ over the circumstances, including whether offers of payment or diplomatic negotiation contributed; a specific ransom payment should not be asserted as established.

The Qarmatians cannot be explained by religious difference alone. The movement's religious ideas, particularly the expectation of a redeemer, gave it ideological coherence. Its social base reflected real economic and political grievances. Opportunity arose from the Abbasid government's distraction and fiscal strain. And its organization, with missionaries, military leaders, and an administrative system in al-Bahrayn, meant that it could sustain a state. The significance for Abbasid legitimacy was great. Protecting the pilgrimage and the holy cities was a central claim of Abbasid caliphs, and the Black Stone's seizure was a humiliating demonstration that the caliph could not perform that function. Regional revolts elsewhere, among them the Kharijites and Alid movements in the Caspian and Yemen, added to the pressure. The Qarmatian case thus belongs to the story of fragmentation as an opposing political system that arose from the same conditions.

Section 50.15

The Decline of Central Authority in the Early Tenth Century

Al-Mu'tadid's son al-Muktafi (902–908) continued the recovery, defeating the Qarmatians of Syria and reasserting control over Egypt in 905. But on his death a thirteen-year-old brother, al-Muqtadir, was chosen as caliph, partly because a child would be easier for viziers and court figures to direct. His reign (908–932) was long and unstable. A group of officials briefly proclaimed an adult Abbasid prince, Ibn al-Mu'tazz, in 908, and the coup collapsed within a day. In 929 commanders briefly deposed al-Muqtadir in favor of his brother al-Qahir, and restored him within days. These events showed how easily the caliph could be removed and replaced by those who controlled the army.

The court spent heavily. The caliph's mother, Shaghab, held great wealth and influence, and her trusted agent Thumal al-Qahramana presided over sessions of the court of complaints (mazalim) on the caliph's behalf, a remarkable example of women's public authority at the court. Viziers such as Ibn al-Furat and Ali ibn Isa represented competing fiscal approaches, one willing to borrow and confiscate, the other more cautious, and their alternating terms produced instability. Financiers, among them the Jewish bankers known as the Jahbadhs, lent money to the government against anticipated revenue.

Meanwhile the provinces drifted. The Samanids governed the northeast, the Qarmatians challenged the south, and Egypt and Syria passed through several hands. Within Iraq, local governors such as the Baridi family at Basra and Wasit, and, farther north, the Hamdanids of Mosul, became powerful. The caliph's revenue increasingly came from a smaller region, and the army's leadership, under the commander Mu'nis al-Muzaffar, became the central power. Al-Muqtadir was killed in October 932 in a battle with Mu'nis's forces. Mu'nis then installed al-Qahir (932–934), who was himself deposed and blinded; his successor al-Radi (934–940) inherited a government with a depleted treasury and few reliable instruments.

Section 50.16

The Amir al-Umara and the Transfer of Effective Political Power

In 936, facing a collapse of revenue and an unmanageable political situation, al-Radi appointed Muhammad ibn Ra'iq, a military governor who had become powerful in Wasit and Basra, to the new post of amir al-umara. The appointment placed command of the army, control of the fiscal bureaus, and the right to make government appointments under a single commander. The caliph retained the dignity of his office, and, according to later sources, the amir's name came to be mentioned in the sermon and on coins after the caliph's. Real direction of government passed to the amir.

Historical Vocabulary
Amir al-Umara ("Commander of Commanders")

The title amir al-umara designated a military officer who, by delegation from the caliph, exercised general authority over the army and, increasingly, over civil administration. It differed from the vizierate in being a military rather than bureaucratic office, and from earlier provincial governorships in covering the central government itself. Its creation did not abolish the caliphate. It created a division of function that later rulers, including the Buyids, would formalize: the caliph as the source of legitimacy and the amir as the holder of practical power. Historians sometimes describe this as a shift from caliphal government to government in the caliph's name.

The office was unstable at first. Ibn Ra'iq was succeeded by Bajkam, a Turkic commander, who governed from 938 until he was killed in 941. Other commanders held the title briefly, until Tuzun, a Turkic military leader, became amir al-umara in 943. Each tried to control the treasury and army, but the revenue base was too small and the competition among rivals too intense to produce stability. The Baridi family in southern Iraq, the Hamdanids in the north, and local commanders contested Baghdad. The caliphs of the period, al-Radi, al-Muttaqi (940–944), and al-Mustakfi (944–946), were increasingly confined to ceremonial roles, though they still tried to use their position to play commanders against one another, as al-Muttaqi's appeals to outside rulers illustrate.

Division V · The Buyids and the Reconfiguration of Caliphal Authority
Section 50.17

The Rise of the Buyids in Western Iran and Iraq

The Buyids take their name from Buya, a Daylamite from the mountainous region of Daylam, south of the Caspian Sea. Later tradition, which should be treated with caution, claimed that he was of humble origin. His three sons, Ali, Hasan, and Ahmad, entered the military service of local rulers in northern and western Iran. The Daylamites, known for their infantry, had long been recruited as soldiers; in the early tenth century the region was the setting for Alid (Zaydi) Shia political activity and for the rise of Iranian dynasties such as the Ziyarids. The brothers served commanders including Makan ibn Kaki and the Ziyarid Mardawij, and in the early 930s they turned their experience into independent power.

Ali, the eldest, took control of Karaj and then of Fars, entering Shiraz in 934 and establishing a base from which he would be known by the honorific Imad al-Dawla. Ahmad moved into Kirman and then toward Khuzestan, the rich agricultural province of southwestern Iran bordering Iraq, taking Ahwaz in the later 930s. Hasan, later Rukn al-Dawla, built a position in central Iran, including Rayy and Isfahan, while competing with the Samanids and Ziyarids. They acted as a family confederation, with the elder brother recognized as senior, but each governed and campaigned in his own territory.

Their military force combined Daylamite infantry with Turkic cavalry, an arrangement that gave them flexibility but also created an internal tension that would trouble their governance later. Their conquests were enabled by the weakness of rival regimes and by the collapse of Abbasid authority over the provinces adjoining Iraq. This chapter treats their rise only to the point of the entry into Baghdad. Their institutions, including the iqta' system, their dynastic politics, their patronage of Persian and Arabic culture, and the religious character of their rule, are examined in Chapter 53.

Section 50.18

The Buyid Entry into Baghdad in 945

By 944–945 Baghdad was politically exhausted. Tuzun died in 945, and the caliph al-Mustakfi relied on a secretary, Ibn Shirzad, to hold the office of amir al-umara. The army was unpaid and divided, and local rulers around Iraq, including the Hamdanids and the Baridis, contested control. Ahmad ibn Buya, who had by then taken Wasit and the lower Tigris region, marched on Baghdad. On 19 December 945 he entered the city and was received by the caliph. Al-Mustakfi conferred on him the title of amir al-umara and the honorific Mu'izz al-Dawla ("he who strengthens the dynasty"), and awarded his brothers the titles Imad al-Dawla and Rukn al-Dawla.

The next weeks displayed the shift in the relationship. Soldiers, many of them Daylamite, quartered themselves in Baghdad. The caliph's powers over appointments and revenue were exercised by Mu'izz al-Dawla. In early 946, following an alleged conspiracy by the caliph, Mu'izz al-Dawla had al-Mustakfi deposed and blinded, and installed al-Muti', a different member of the Abbasid family, as caliph. The new caliph's role was limited to the formal functions of the office, and his financial allowance came from the amir.

What is clear is that the Buyids chose to rule through the existing institution, and that the institution had value to them.

Section 50.19

Caliphal Legitimacy, Regional Sovereignty, and the Persistence of Abbasid Institutions

Historical Vocabulary
Khutba, Sikka, and Tiraz: The Signs of Sovereignty

In medieval Islamic political culture, sovereignty was publicly expressed in several ways. The khutba, the sermon at Friday congregational prayer, included a formal invocation of the ruler in whose name the community prayed; naming a ruler there was an act of recognition. The sikka, the right to issue coinage, put the ruler's name and titles on the currency that circulated. Tiraz were inscribed textiles, often produced in state workshops, that bore the ruler's name and were granted as robes of honor. A regional ruler who named the caliph in these ways acknowledged his sovereignty, while one who omitted him asserted independence or rival claims. Investiture, the caliph's formal grant of office, titles, and robes, completed the system.

The distinction between nominal and effective authority is central to this chapter, but "nominal" must not be misunderstood as "meaningless." The caliph's name in the khutba and on coins conferred recognized standing on rulers whose power was otherwise derived from arms. The caliph's investiture could validate a dynasty's claim to govern a territory, as the Tulunid and Samanid cases show. And the caliph remained the formal head of the community, the symbolic leader of Islamic law and ritual, and the legitimate recipient of oaths. In a world in which rulers competed for the loyalty of populations and scholars, a legitimacy that no single ruler could monopolize was useful to all of them.

Administrative continuity reinforced this. The bureaus, the training of secretaries, the practice of tax accounting, the court of complaints, and the legal system of judges and jurists persisted through the transition. Regional rulers adopted Abbasid administrative models, so that a Samanid, Tulunid, or Buyid chancery looked in structure like the Baghdad one. The caliphate's institutions were therefore a shared framework, not simply the apparatus of one ruler.

Rivals complicated the picture. In 909 the Fatimids, a dynasty claiming descent from the Prophet's daughter Fatima, established a rival caliphate in Ifriqiya (Chapter 54), and in 929 the Umayyad ruler of Cordoba adopted the title of caliph. The Abbasid claim to be the single legitimate caliph was therefore openly challenged, and that made the willingness of regional rulers in the eastern lands to continue to acknowledge Baghdad more significant. The Abbasid caliphate survived because many actors, with different motives, found it useful: rulers needed recognition, scholars valued a unifying institution, and the population looked to it for religious order. This is how a caliph who no longer governed independently remained a recognized institution.

Section 50.20

Review and Historical Synthesis: Fragmentation, Adaptation, and the Abbasid Legacy

The history traced in this chapter began with a civil war between two brothers and ended with a Daylamite amir in the caliph's palace. Between those points, a new army was recruited and housed at Samarra; its commanders came to determine the succession; fiscal strain and the cost of soldiers limited the central government; a governor in Egypt created a hereditary state; a recovery under al-Muwaffaq and al-Mu'tadid temporarily restored central capacity; the Qarmatians created a rival polity; and the office of amir al-umara, established in 936, moved practical authority from the caliph and his bureaus to military commanders, leading to the Buyid entry in 945.

These processes were interlinked. The army that the caliphs recruited in order to secure their authority required payment, and the difficulty of paying it deepened the fiscal crisis. Fiscal crisis made it harder to enforce provincial remittances, strengthening governors who retained revenue and so created hereditary dynasties. The loss of provincial revenue in turn reduced the central government's ability to pay the army. In that cycle, commanders became more important and viziers less. The Buyids, outsiders with their own army, stepped into a role that the previous decades had prepared.

The chapter's conclusion is that the Abbasid caliphate lost much of its centralized political power but not its institutional existence. Students should therefore distinguish political decentralization from civilizational decline. During the same period, Baghdad remained a major center of scholarship and commerce, historians such as al-Tabari and al-Mas'udi wrote their works, and the Mediterranean, Red Sea, and Indian Ocean trade that Chapter 51 describes continued to link regions whose rulers were separate. A divided political order did not automatically mean a diminished economy or culture. Indeed, some regional courts, such as those of the Samanids and the Buyids, were major patrons of learning.

Historical Debate

Decline or Decentralization?

The question: Should the political fragmentation of the caliphate be understood as decline, or as the reorganization of a large political system into regional states within a shared framework?

Interpretation A — Decline: The loss of central fiscal and military control, repeated depositions of caliphs, and the Buyid occupation of Baghdad represent a real collapse of Abbasid imperial power. Medieval writers, and many modern ones, used the language of decline and "eclipse," as in the title of the early English translation of Miskawayh, The Eclipse of the Abbasid Caliphate.
Interpretation B — Decentralization and adaptation: Many historians now stress that the political order changed form while the broader Islamic civilization continued to develop. Regional dynasties shared Abbasid administrative models, scholarship and trade flourished, and the caliphal institution endured. On this view, "decline" is a misleading label applied from the perspective of the capital, and it risks confusing political and cultural history.

Evidence: Both interpretations rely on the same political events but weigh differently the continuing vitality of scholarship, commerce, and urban life, and the evidence of regional dynasties' use of caliphal legitimacy.

Student task: Write a short argument on whether "decline" is an appropriate word for the period, distinguishing clearly between political power and cultural or economic activity.

The Abbasid legacy was lasting. The model of a caliph conferring legitimacy on military rulers who held practical power was inherited by the Seljuk sultans (Chapter 57), whose relationship with the caliph resembled and developed the Buyid one, and by the Mamluks, who later sheltered Abbasid caliphs in Cairo (Chapter 65). The pattern of military elites sustained by revenue assignments, the use of slave soldiers, and the combination of dynastic autonomy with caliphal recognition all structured the political history of the volumes that follow. Chapter 51 now turns from politics to commerce, showing how long-distance trade across the Mediterranean, the Red Sea, the Persian Gulf, and the Indian Ocean continued across these increasingly fragmented political jurisdictions, and Chapter 53 returns to Baghdad to examine how the Buyids governed.

From the perspective of a soldier in the Samarra army: Livelihood depended on regular pay and on the favor of a commander. Unpaid wages were a real grievance, and loyalty was tied to patrons who could secure payment more than to any caliph in particular.

Continuity and Change Over Time

The Caliphate from 800 to 950

What Changed

  • The caliph's control over appointments, revenue, and the army passed to commanders and then to a dynasty
  • Military recruitment shifted from Khurasani and Arab forces to purchased and recruited soldiers, and later to Daylamite and Turkic forces under regional dynasties
  • Provinces moved from appointed governors to hereditary regional rulers
  • The capital moved from Baghdad to Samarra and back, and political initiative moved to the military

What Stayed the Same

  • The caliph's name in the khutba and on coins as a mark of recognition
  • The bureaus, scribal traditions, and legal institutions that regional states adopted
  • Baghdad's importance as a center of scholarship, commerce, and religious authority
  • The Abbasid dynasty's claim to the caliphate

Why the pattern matters: Seeing both change and continuity prevents two errors: treating the caliphate as destroyed by 945, and treating it as unchanged.

Cause and Effect

Why Abbasid Central Power Fragmented

Long-term causes

Government by delegation across large distances; dependence on a professional army requiring constant payment; provincial elites with local roots.

Short-term causes

The civil war of 809–813; the recruitment of new armies; the murder of al-Mutawakkil in 861; the Zanj revolt; child rulers and factional court politics in the early tenth century.

Immediate effects

Commanders choose caliphs; Egypt and Syria come under hereditary rule; revenues shrink; the amirate is created in 936.

Long-term and regional consequences

The Buyid system in Baghdad; the model of rulers governing under caliphal recognition; the rise of rival caliphates; the pattern later seen in Seljuk and Mamluk rule.

Historical complexity: Military factionalism, fiscal pressure, and provincial autonomy reinforced one another, and no single factor explains the outcome.

Everyday Life and Social History

Living through Political Fragmentation

For most inhabitants of the Abbasid lands, political change was experienced through taxation, security, and the price and availability of goods. During the siege of Baghdad in 812–813, residents endured bombardment and the destruction of neighborhoods, and organized their own defense. In Samarra, markets, workshops, and households grew around the military quarters, and the abandonment of the city after 892 left a ruined landscape. Cultivators in the Sawad faced the disruption of the Zanj revolt, and Egyptian farmers paid land taxes that supported first the Abbasid governor, then the Tulunid state.

Households were also political arenas. Court women, such as Zubayda, Qabiha, and Shaghab, controlled property and influenced appointments; Qatr al-Nada's marriage to al-Mu'tadid was a diplomatic act. Pilgrims faced the risks of Qarmatian attacks on the route to Mecca, and trade continued despite the dangers. The surviving sources, written largely by officials and scholars, give limited access to ordinary lives, and historians use archaeology, papyri, and urban remains to reconstruct them.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced the reorganization of Abbasid sovereignty between roughly 800 and 945. It began from the observation that the Abbasid government, however impressive, governed through delegation across great distances and depended on an army that required constant payment. These structural conditions, examined in Section 50.1, did not cause fragmentation by themselves, but they set the terms within which crises developed.

The civil war of 809–813 between al-Amin and al-Ma'mun, examined in Sections 50.2 and 50.3, showed how conditional caliphal power was. Al-Ma'mun won through eastern commanders and administrators, and rebuilt authority by delegating governorships such as that of Khurasan to the Tahirids. The caliphate remained energetic, yet its stability rested on negotiated relations with regional and military elites.

Al-Mu'tasim's recruitment of Turkic and other soldiers and his foundation of Samarra in 836, treated in Sections 50.4 and 50.5, created a caliphate built around a new, expensive, and diverse army. The chapter emphasized that these soldiers were not a single faction. The Anarchy at Samarra (861–870), examined in Section 50.6, followed the murder of al-Mutawakkil and saw caliphs deposed by commanders and soldiers pursuing identifiable interests, including pay and security, in alliance and rivalry with court and Tahirid actors.

Fiscal pressure, examined in Section 50.7, tied these military developments to the caliphate's income. Army costs grew while provincial remittances fell, and the Zanj revolt damaged the Iraqi revenue base. Yet the story was not one of uniform decline: al-Muwaffaq and al-Mu'tadid achieved a real recovery, including a notable fiscal and calendar reform, that showed the state's resilience and the limits of reforms that depended on a few strong individuals. Section 50.8 added that civilian bureaucrats, commanders, and palace households competed for control of government, and the Historical Debate asked whether the army or deeper structural problems best explain the outcome.

The Tulunids, examined in Sections 50.9 through 50.12, illustrate provincial autonomy within a continuing framework of legitimacy. Ahmad ibn Tulun, arriving as a deputy in 868, took control of Egyptian revenue and the army and extended his power to Syria. His son Khumarawayh succeeded him, and the 886 settlement recognized hereditary rule in return for tribute. The dynasty built al-Qata'i and the Mosque of Ibn Tulun, expressing its cultural membership in the Abbasid world, and it ended in the Abbasid reconquest of 905, which restored direct rule but not a lasting solution.

Sections 50.13 through 50.15 examined the transformation of the central political order. Al-Muwaffaq and al-Mu'tadid restored military and fiscal capacity and returned the court to Baghdad. The Qarmatians, a movement combining religious ideology, social grievance, and political organization, established a rival state in eastern Arabia and challenged the caliph's role as protector of the pilgrimage, most dramatically in 930. After al-Mu'tadid and al-Muktafi, child-caliph al-Muqtadir's reign exposed court extravagance and factional competition.

Section 50.16 traced the amir al-umara. The office created in 936 gave a commander control of the army, treasury, and appointments, leaving the caliph the dignity of his position. Several commanders held it, none securely, until the Buyids arrived. Sections 50.17 and 50.18 showed how the Daylamite brothers Ali, Hasan, and Ahmad ibn Buya built power in Fars, Khuzestan, and central Iran, and how Ahmad entered Baghdad on 19 December 945, received the title Mu'izz al-Dawla, and shortly thereafter installed al-Muti' as caliph after deposing al-Mustakfi.

Section 50.19 explained why the caliphate survived: the khutba, coinage, investiture, and administrative continuity gave the caliph a form of recognition that rulers found useful, and rival caliphates in the Maghrib and Cordoba made the Abbasid claim more rather than less valuable to those who continued to honor it. Section 50.20 and the review features argued that political decentralization should not be mistaken for civilizational decline, and that the transformed Abbasid system, in which legitimacy and power were divided, became a model for the Seljuks, the Mamluks, and other regimes of later volumes.

Chapter Reflection

The fragmentation of Abbasid political power is one of the great transitions of the medieval Middle East, and it is easily misread. Told from the perspective of Baghdad, it is a story of loss. Told from the perspective of Fustat, Bukhara, Shiraz, or al-Ahsa, it is a story of new states, new elites, and new opportunities. This chapter has tried to hold both perspectives together and to show that they describe the same process.

The chapter's larger lesson concerns how political systems change. A government can lose practical control while its institutions and symbols persist, and the persisting institutions can matter. The caliph's survival as a source of legitimacy meant that the Islamic world did not divide into wholly separate political and religious universes. Regional rulers continued to belong to a shared framework of law, administration, and ritual, a fact that shaped everything from the Seljuk sultanate to the Mamluk court that would later shelter Abbasid caliphs.

The chapter also shows the importance of material foundations: the cost of armies, the flow of revenue, and the geography of provinces. These structures, more than any ruler's personality, explain much of what happened. And it shows the value of reading sources critically, since the chronicles on which we depend were written by people with their own positions in the story.

Chapter 51 will turn to commerce across the Mediterranean, Red Sea, Persian Gulf, and Indian Ocean, showing that economic and intellectual connection continued across divided jurisdictions, and Chapter 53 will return to Baghdad under the Buyids. Together with Chapters 44 through 49, they complete Volume III's account of the caliphal age, and prepare for the political world of Volume IV, in which sultans, rival caliphs, and regional dynasties would govern a region still linked by religion, trade, and learning.
