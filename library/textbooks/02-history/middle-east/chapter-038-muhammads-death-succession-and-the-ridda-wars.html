Muhammad's Death, Succession, and the Ridda Wars

A History of the Middle East: From Ancient Civilizations to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

On a Monday in June 632 CE, in the dwelling beside the mosque at Medina where he had lived for a decade, Muhammad died. Within hours, the community he had gathered faced a question that no revelation had settled in a form all could accept: who, if anyone, could now speak and act for a movement whose founder had been simultaneously a prophet, a judge, a war leader, a diplomat, and the chief negotiator of alliances stretching across much of Arabia? Within two years, that question would be answered by a combination of argument, improvisation, and force, and the answer would shape the political history of the Middle East for centuries.

The events of this chapter are among the most consequential and most difficult to reconstruct in the history of the region. They are consequential because the arrangements that emerged between 632 and 634 — a leader styled the "successor" of God's messenger, a principle that allegiance to Medina included payment of the alms-levy, and an army capable of imposing that principle across the peninsula — became the base from which the Arab-Islamic conquests, examined in Chapter 39, were launched. They are difficult to reconstruct because virtually every detailed account was written down generations afterward, in the ninth and tenth centuries, by scholars working within communities that had by then developed settled and competing views about who had been the rightful leader and what the early wars had meant.

Chapter 37 traced how Muhammad built in Medina a community held together by revelation, by his personal authority, by treaties and marriages, and by a network of agreements with tribes that ranged from sincere conversion to cautious political submission. When he died, that network had not yet existed in its full form for long. Mecca had been taken only in 630; many delegations had come to Medina in the year or two afterward; and large parts of Arabia, especially in the center, east, and south, were tied to the Prophet by personal agreement rather than by institutions. Those ties depended partly on the man who had made them.

This chapter therefore asks how Muhammad's followers responded to the loss of his personal and prophetic authority, why succession and allegiance became contested across Arabia, and how Abū Bakr's leadership and the so-called Ridda Wars established the foundations of a more enduring caliphal political order. It follows the argument in five movements: the death and the succession crisis at Medina; the first decisions of Abū Bakr and the nature of the challenge to his authority; the campaigns of central Arabia, including the confrontation with rival prophetic leaders at Yamāma; the separate and different conflicts in Yemen, Oman, Mahra, and eastern Arabia; and the consolidation that followed.

A caution governs the whole chapter. The name Ridda, usually translated "apostasy," comes from the later Muslim historical tradition and expresses its verdict on the conflicts. The evidence, read carefully, shows a more varied picture: refusals to pay the alms-levy to Medina, rejections of Abū Bakr's leadership, reassertions of tribal independence, regional struggles that predated Muhammad's death, and the movements of rival prophets whose followers regarded themselves as faithful monotheists. The chapter treats the traditional label as a source to be analyzed, not as an explanation.

Finally, the chapter treats the succession dispute with the care it requires. The disagreement between those who later became Sunni and Shiʿi communities about whether Muhammad had designated ʿAlī, and about the legitimacy of Abū Bakr's selection, grew out of events recounted here. But the doctrines of the fully developed Sunni and Shiʿi traditions, examined in Chapter 47, did not exist in 632. The task is to understand what the participants disputed at the time, and how later communities remembered and refashioned it.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Evaluate the sources for Muhammad's death, the Saqīfa meeting, and the Ridda campaigns, distinguishing early evidence from later transmission and sectarian reworking.
  2. Explain why no accepted procedure for succession existed in 632 and why the Muhājirūn, Anṣār, Quraysh, and Muhammad's family held different interests.
  3. Reconstruct the arguments made at Saqīfa and explain how Abū Bakr came to be recognized.
  4. Describe the early disagreements involving ʿAlī, Fāṭima, and the Prophet's household while distinguishing them from later Sunni and Shiʿi doctrine.
  5. Analyze the meaning and limits of the office of khalīfat rasūl Allāh in contrast with Muhammad's prophetic authority.
  6. Assess the Ridda conflicts as a set of regionally distinct movements rather than one uniform apostasy.
  7. Explain the political and religious roles of Ṭulayḥa, Sajāḥ, Musaylima, and al-Aswad al-ʿAnsī within their own communities.
  8. Compare campaigns in Najd and Yamāma with those in Yemen, Ḥaḍramawt, Oman, Mahra, and eastern Arabia.
  9. Evaluate competing historical interpretations of the Ridda Wars, weighing religious, fiscal, and political explanations.
  10. Assess what had and had not changed in Arabian politics between 632 and 634, including the limits of caliphal institutions at the threshold of the conquests.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Who possessed legitimate authority after Muhammad, and by what criteria did different groups judge legitimacy?
  2. How reliable are accounts of Saqīfa and the Ridda Wars, given that they were written down long after the events by communities with strong interests in the outcome?
  3. Why did different tribes and regions respond differently to Muhammad's death?
  4. To what extent did opposition to Medina represent a rejection of Islam, and to what extent a rejection of political and fiscal subordination?
  5. How did rival prophets such as Musaylima and Ṭulayḥa draw on existing religious and political traditions in Arabia?
  6. How did Abū Bakr's government defeat, divide, or negotiate with its opponents, and what role did commanders such as Khālid ibn al-Walīd play?
  7. How did the experience of civilians — herders, farmers, households, captives — shape and reflect the wars?
  8. What institutional foundations had been laid by 634, and how far did they resemble the later caliphal state?

Historical Context

By 632, Muhammad had moved from preaching in Mecca (Chapter 36) to governing in Medina (Chapter 37). After the Hijra of 622, he had created an inclusive community from emigrants (Muhājirūn) and Medinan helpers (Anṣār), fought Quraysh at Badr, Uḥud, and the Trench, concluded the truce of al-Ḥudaybiya in 628, and entered Mecca in 630. The following year, often called the Year of Delegations, brought envoys from many Arabian groups seeking agreements with Medina. The late-antique Arabia described in Chapters 34 and 35 — a land of oases, pastoral confederations, caravan towns, and southern kingdoms under shifting Byzantine, Sasanian, and Aksumite influence — was being drawn into a single political orbit more quickly than any previous power had managed.

Yet this integration was uneven. Arabian political life rested on kinship groups (tribes and clans), alliances (often called ḥilf), tribal leaders (sayyids), the protection of strangers (jiwār), and the payment of tribute or dues. Muhammad's agreements incorporated these forms. Some groups accepted Islam in full, prayed, and paid the alms-levy to Medina's collectors. Others made treaties of protection, sometimes preserving their own religious practices for a time. The southern regions, especially Yemen, were governed through local leaders and Persian-descended communities whom Muhammad had confirmed in office. Some of those arrangements were fragile even before his death: a self-proclaimed prophet, al-Aswad al-ʿAnsī, had seized power in Yemen, and other claimants — Musaylima in Yamāma and Ṭulayḥa among Asad — were reported to have begun to act during Muhammad's final year.

The wider environment mattered as well. The long Byzantine–Sasanian war of the early seventh century (Chapter 34) had ended in 628–630 with exhaustion on both sides, and Sasanian authority along the Gulf and in Yemen was weakening. Medina's Arabian rivals therefore could not easily look for a patron to the north or east, yet the empires still shaped the political horizon, since Muhammad had reportedly ordered a raid toward the Byzantine frontier shortly before his death. As Chapter 2 emphasized, the geography of Arabia — scattered oases, long distances, seasonal pastures — also made any central authority difficult to project; a force based in Medina had to cross hundreds of kilometers of steppe and desert to reach Yamāma, Bahrain, or Yemen.

Reconstructing the Succession Crisis: Sources, Traditions, and Historical Problems

No contemporary narrative of Muhammad's death survives. The Qurʾān contains almost no biographical detail, but it does preserve one verse, 3:144, which says that Muhammad was only a messenger before whom other messengers had passed away, and asks whether followers would turn back on their heels if he died or was killed. Later tradition connected the verse to the moment of his death, but the Qurʾān itself offers no account of succession and does not name a successor. Historians therefore depend on narratives compiled well after the events.

These narratives fall into several families. The biography of Muhammad by Ibn Isḥāq (d. c. 767), known mainly through the recension of Ibn Hishām (d. c. 833), covers the death and the Saqīfa meeting. The reports of al-Wāqidī (d. 823) and the biographical dictionary of Ibn Saʿd (d. 845) preserve additional material. Collections of ḥadīth, especially those of al-Bukhārī (d. 870) and Muslim (d. 875), contain reports about Muhammad's illness and about Abū Bakr's and ʿUmar's actions. Al-Balādhurī (d. c. 892) and above all al-Ṭabarī (d. 923), in his universal history, gathered competing reports into long compilations. Shiʿi historians and ḥadīth collectors preserved other traditions that emphasize ʿAlī's claim.

Each tradition has to be read with attention to its transmission. Reports typically come with a chain of transmitters (isnād), but the chain itself may have been constructed or improved later. Some accounts of Saqīfa, such as those transmitted from Abū Mikhnaf or Hishām al-Kalbī, reflect Iraqi, pro-ʿAlid or Abbasid-era perspectives; others, like the long address attributed to ʿUmar in the ḥadīth collections, reflect the perspective of the community that regarded Abū Bakr and ʿUmar as models. For the Ridda campaigns, much of the narrative detail comes from Sayf ibn ʿUmar (d. c. 796), whose reports are preserved chiefly in al-Ṭabarī. Medieval scholars of ḥadīth already doubted his reliability, and modern historians generally treat his vivid chronologies, commander lists, and speeches with caution while recognizing that he preserved some information otherwise lost.

Modern scholarship has therefore divided between approaches. Some historians, such as W. Montgomery Watt, accepted a broad outline from the traditional sources while criticizing details. Others, notably Wilferd Madelung in The Succession to Muhammad, reread the reports to emphasize political maneuvering by a coalition around Abū Bakr and ʿUmar and the exclusion of the Prophet's family. Still others, including Fred Donner, have emphasized the earliest community as a movement of "believers" whose political structures took shape in the first decades, and Patricia Crone and Michael Cook argued for much greater skepticism about the Islamic narrative. This chapter does not adopt any single view. It presents what the sources claim, notes where they disagree, and signals where the evidence is thin.

Non-Muslim sources add little for the succession itself. A few Christian texts of the 630s and 640s mention Arab invaders and, indirectly, a prophet, but none describes the Ridda in detail. The archaeology of Arabia, discussed again in Chapter 4's treatment of evidence, offers inscriptions and settlement evidence but not narratives of 632–634. The reader should therefore keep in mind that every sentence below concerning events at Medina and Yamāma rests on traditions with an interest.

Section 38.2

Muhammad's Final Illness, Death, and Burial in 632

According to the traditions, Muhammad fell ill after returning from his last pilgrimage to Mecca, performed in March 632 and later remembered as the Farewell Pilgrimage. He became feverish and was cared for in the apartment of his wife ʿĀʾisha, with the agreement of his other wives. Reports say that, as the illness worsened, he could not lead the prayers and asked that Abū Bakr do so — a report that Abū Bakr's later supporters treated as a sign of favor, and that others read less decisively. There are conflicting accounts of how long the illness lasted, commonly said to be several days to about two weeks.

Several episodes of the final days became central to later argument. In one tradition, preserved in the Sunni ḥadīth collections, Muhammad asked for writing materials to dictate something that would prevent his followers from going astray, but those present disagreed about whether to fulfil the request and he let the matter drop. Sunni scholars have explained the episode in various ways; Shiʿi scholars have often taken it as evidence that he intended to name ʿAlī. The sources do not say what he intended to write, and the historian cannot supply it.

Muhammad died on a Monday, traditionally 12 Rabīʿ al-awwal 11 AH, which corresponds to 8 June 632. Reports describe the stunned reaction of the community. In a widely repeated account, ʿUmar refused to believe he had died and threatened those who said so; Abū Bakr, who arrived from his residence in the outskirts of Medina, went to the body, then addressed the people, reportedly reminding them that those who worshipped Muhammad should know he had died, while those who worshipped God should know God was living, and citing the Qurʾānic verse mentioned above. Whether the scene occurred as described is uncertain, but its message — that the movement would continue after the man — is clear and became important to Abū Bakr's later standing.

Muhammad's burial took place, according to the tradition, in the room where he had died, after a delay in which political events intervened. Reports differ about who washed the body — ʿAlī and several relatives are named — and about the manner of the prayers, which were reportedly performed by groups of people rather than under a single prayer leader. The burial in the house, rather than in a public cemetery, would later become the nucleus of the Prophet's mosque's most revered site. The delay in burial, which several sources link to the political meeting at Saqīfa, is itself a point of disagreement: some narrators treat it as ordinary, others as a sign that ʿAlī and the family were absorbed in mourning while other leaders settled the leadership.

The political consequence of Muhammad's death was larger than any ordinary succession. He had been a messenger whose revelation closed with him, according to Muslim belief, yet he had also been the head of a community, a negotiator, a military commander, an arbiter of disputes, and the recipient of a share of the revenues. Which of these functions could be inherited, and by whom, was unclear. The Qurʾān enjoined consultation among the believers but did not provide a procedure. The tribal tradition of Arabia knew selection of chiefs by consensus among elders, not hereditary monarchy; but the Prophet's community was not a tribe.

Section 38.3

The Immediate Leadership Crisis: Muhājirūn, Anṣār, and the Question of Authority

The community at Medina in June 632 was not a single bloc. The Muhājirūn were Meccans, chiefly of Quraysh, who had emigrated with or after Muhammad and who lacked kinship ties in Medina. Many were poor when they arrived, and their prominence rested on early conversion, hardship, and closeness to the Prophet. The Anṣār ("helpers") were the Medinan families of the Aws and Khazraj, who had invited Muhammad, defended him, and provided the land and the manpower of his city. Their earlier history had been marked by feuds between the two groups, and rivalry between them did not vanish after Muhammad's arrival.

The Muhājirūn and Anṣār thus approached the question of leadership with different assumptions. For many of Quraysh, the new religion was tied to the prestige of Mecca and its sanctuary, and leadership by a Qurashī followed a long habit of Arabian deference to that tribe's guardianship of the Kaʿba. Many Anṣār, by contrast, felt that Medina had been the base of the movement and that they had borne a disproportionate share of its defense; they also feared that Quraysh, having been the Prophet's chief adversaries until 630, would dominate. A third group, the more recent converts of Mecca and the surrounding region, had Islam without long attachment to either camp. The Prophet's own kin among the Banū Hāshim, including ʿAlī, his cousin and son-in-law, and ʿAbbās, his uncle, represented a fourth set of expectations.

Nothing in the earlier practice of the community had created a mechanism for choosing a successor. Muhammad had designated deputies to govern Medina when he was away and had named commanders for expeditions, and he had appointed governors to regions. But he had no sons who survived to adulthood, and the Qurʾān does not link prophecy with hereditary rule. Some followers expected the movement to remain consultative; others thought the new order required a single leader. The silence of the sources about a clearly known procedure is probably genuine: the fact that the debate at Saqīfa took the form it did suggests that no one had a ready answer.

Equally important was the dispersed character of the Muslim polity. A leader in Medina would be responsible for a large area where authority rested on relationships Muhammad had made one by one, with chiefs, governors, and delegations. The risk that those relationships would lapse if the person died was well understood by those involved; reports that certain tribes quickly stopped sending alms or asserted independence reflect this. The crisis of leadership, in short, was both a question of who should lead the Muslims and a question of whether anyone could hold the network together at all.

Section 38.4

The Meeting at Saqīfa and the Selection of Abū Bakr

According to the principal accounts, while Muhammad's family prepared his body, a number of Anṣār gathered at the covered meeting place (saqīfa) of the Banū Sāʿida, a Khazraj clan, in Medina. Their candidate, reportedly, was Saʿd ibn ʿUbāda, the Khazrajī chief, who was said to be ill. When news reached ʿUmar, who was said to be at the Prophet's house, he and Abū Bakr, accompanied by Abū ʿUbayda ibn al-Jarrāḥ, hurried to the gathering. The story that they went uninvited, apparently fearing a seizure of leadership by the Anṣār, appears in several versions.

What was said at Saqīfa is the most disputed element. In the traditional accounts, Abū Bakr argued that the Arabs would accept leadership only from Quraysh, the Prophet's tribe and the people with the greatest standing among the Arabs, and proposed that the leaders be Quraysh while the Anṣār serve as ministers or advisers. A speaker from the Anṣār, usually named as al-Ḥubāb ibn al-Mundhir, is said to have proposed a division: one leader from the Anṣār and one from the Muhājirūn. ʿUmar and Abū Bakr rejected this as impractical; tradition says that ʿUmar offered to pay allegiance to Abū Bakr and was joined by Abū ʿUbayda. A Khazrajī leader, Bashīr ibn Saʿd, reportedly gave his allegiance, perhaps because of the rivalry between his own group and that of Saʿd ibn ʿUbāda; the Aws, who may have feared a Khazraj-led leadership, followed. The meeting is said to have turned tumultuous, with accusations and, in some narrations, physical jostling of Saʿd.

The next day, the traditions state, a public pledge of allegiance (bayʿa) was taken from the broader community in the mosque, and Abū Bakr delivered an inaugural address in which he asked for obedience as long as he obeyed God and His messenger. The account of the speech's wording varies across sources, and the Primary Source Window below treats it with caution.

Primary Source Window I

Context: Several overlapping versions of the Saqīfa meeting survive. One, attributed to ʿUmar and given in the collections of ḥadīth, appears in a long address in which he reportedly explained the circumstances of Abū Bakr's selection and described it as a hasty affair that had been fortunate. Others, in historical works, add dialogue and details of the speakers and incidents.

Paraphrase (not a quotation): In the traditions, the Anṣār assert that leadership should rest with them, as the people who sheltered and defended the Prophet; Abū Bakr answers that the Arabs will not defer to anyone except Quraysh, offers the Anṣār a respected advisory role, and puts forward ʿUmar or Abū ʿUbayda for the office. A speaker among the Anṣār proposes a joint arrangement. ʿUmar, followed by others, pledges allegiance to Abū Bakr, and the assembly follows, amid noisy dispute.

Analysis: The accounts agree on the outline: a meeting of Anṣār, the arrival of Abū Bakr and ʿUmar, an argument based on Qurashī primacy, and a rapid pledge. They differ over tone — whether the proceedings were consultative or coercive — and over the role of ʿAlī and the Banū Hāshim, who are absent. The framing as a consultation among the Muslim leaders fits the later Sunni understanding that the early community chose its leader; the emphasis in other accounts on haste and pressure fits the later Shiʿi argument that the family had been bypassed. Who speaks matters: the surviving speeches come almost entirely from the winners and their allies, and the voices of the Prophet's kin, of absent tribal leaders, and of ordinary Anṣār are largely missing.

Limitations: No account is contemporary; none can be verified independently. Direct speech in the sources is better read as the way later narrators understood the arguments than as a transcript.

Questions for students:

  1. Which arguments in the accounts appeal to precedent, which to merit, and which to expedience?
  2. Why might the Aws have been willing to support Abū Bakr rather than a Khazrajī leader?
  3. Whose perspective is absent from the accounts, and how might that absence affect the picture?
  4. How might a Sunni historian and a Shiʿi historian of the ninth century each use these accounts differently?

Historians offer several interpretations of the meeting. A traditional view sees it as a consultation in which the early community, faced with a crisis, rationally chose its most senior and trusted figure. A more skeptical view sees a pre-arranged coalition among Abū Bakr, ʿUmar, and a handful of allies who took advantage of the Anṣār's disunity. Both views may contain truth. The speed of the decision suggests that the leaders recognized that delay would expose the community to rivals; the fact that Saʿd ibn ʿUbāda is reported never to have given his allegiance, and that he later left Medina for Syria, suggests that the outcome was not accepted by all. What the sources do establish is that, within a day or two, Abū Bakr was recognized by most of the Medinan community, and that he took up the leadership without a prolonged crisis.

Section 38.5

ʿAlī, Fāṭima, the Prophet's Household, and Competing Claims to Leadership

ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib was Muhammad's cousin, one of the earliest converts, husband of Fāṭima, and father of the Prophet's only surviving grandsons, al-Ḥasan and al-Ḥusayn. In the traditions of the Banū Hāshim and of later Shiʿi communities, ʿAlī had the strongest claim, based on kinship, early service, and what they consider to have been explicit designation. Sunni tradition gives ʿAlī great honor as one of the foremost Companions and later the fourth caliph, but does not hold that he had been explicitly appointed.

The key textual issue is the event of Ghadīr Khumm. According to widely transmitted reports, on the return from the Farewell Pilgrimage, at a pond called Ghadīr Khumm, Muhammad took ʿAlī by the hand and said, in words that vary slightly in different chains, that whoever regarded him as mawlā should regard ʿAlī as mawlā. The word has a wide range of meanings — master, patron, friend, ally, kinsman — and the two traditions read it differently. Shiʿi scholars understood it as an appointment to authority over the community; many Sunni scholars understood it as an expression of affection and solidarity or of ʿAlī's standing. The historian cannot settle the exegesis, but can note that both communities preserved the report, which suggests the event had some basis, and that its significance was not universally understood as designation in 632, since neither ʿAlī nor his supporters were reported to have invoked it in the first days.

What the sources do report is that ʿAlī and some of the Banū Hāshim did not immediately pledge allegiance. Different traditions explain the delay differently. According to some Sunni reports, ʿAlī pledged allegiance later, after Fāṭima died some months after her father. According to other reports, a fuller reconciliation took place only then. Shiʿi sources describe pressure upon ʿAlī and his household in the early days, including a reported confrontation at Fāṭima's house; Sunni sources often deny or minimize such a confrontation. The accounts are polemically charged in both directions, and the evidence for the details is weak.

A further dispute concerned Muhammad's property. Fāṭima, in the traditions, claimed her inheritance, including the oasis estate of Fadak near Khaybar and shares of revenue from other lands. Abū Bakr reportedly refused, citing a saying attributed to the Prophet that prophets do not leave inheritance, and that what they leave is charity. The question mattered less for the value of the property than for its implications: whether the Prophet's family had a distinct claim on the community's resources, and whether the head of the community was the custodian of the Prophet's public resources. Fāṭima is reported to have been angry, and tradition holds that she died about six months after her father; the date and the circumstances are disputed.

It is anachronistic to speak of "Sunni" and "Shiʿi" parties in 632. The word shīʿa in its early sense meant partisans, and those who favored ʿAlī in these years were a small group of close relatives and companions. The doctrines of the imamate — that leadership of the community belongs by divine designation to a line descending from ʿAlī — developed gradually over the following century and a half, in the context of the civil wars and dynastic conflicts described in Chapters 40 and 43, and received their mature formulations in the period covered by Chapter 47. Equally, the Sunni conception of the first four caliphs as "rightly guided" and the doctrine of the community's consensus took shape later. What can be said of 632 is that a group of influential companions around Abū Bakr and ʿUmar gained recognition from most of the community, that some close to the Prophet's family dissented or delayed, and that the questions raised — whether authority belonged to the family, to Quraysh, or to the community as a whole — would never cease to be debated.

Division II — Abū Bakr's Leadership and the Origins of the Ridda Wars · Section 38.6

Abū Bakr's Accession and the Meaning of the Caliphate

Abū Bakr (ʿAbd Allāh ibn Abī Quḥāfa), a Meccan merchant of the clan of Taym, had been among Muhammad's earliest followers, had accompanied him on the Hijra, had led the pilgrimage of 631, and was the father of ʿĀʾisha. He was probably in his early sixties in 632. His authority rested less on tribal power — Taym was a modest clan — than on early adherence, personal closeness to Muhammad, a reputation for judgment, and the support of other leading Companions.

The title tradition assigns him is khalīfat rasūl Allāh, "successor" or "deputy" of the messenger of God. The Arabic khalīfa (see the vocabulary box) has a range of meanings, including one who comes after another and one who stands in another's place. The Qurʾān also uses the root for humanity's vicegerency and for David. How the early community understood the title in Abū Bakr's lifetime is uncertain, and later usage, in which the caliph is "deputy of God," seems to be a development under the Umayyads. For Abū Bakr's reign, the sources indicate that he claimed to succeed Muhammad as head of the community, not as a prophet, and that he conceived of authority as limited by the Qurʾān and the Prophet's example.

Historical Vocabulary
Khalīfa, Khilāfa, and Bayʿa

Khalīfa (English "caliph") means "one who succeeds" or "one who stands in place of another." Khilāfa (caliphate) is the office or the rule of a khalīfa. The first leaders after Muhammad were remembered as khalīfat rasūl Allāh; ʿUmar, according to tradition, preferred the title amīr al-muʾminīn, "commander of the believers." The bayʿa was a pledge of allegiance, often sealed with a handclasp, by which individuals or groups acknowledged a leader; it could be given or withheld, and in the early period functioned as a political and personal contract. Historians stress that the office of the 630s was far simpler than the caliphates of later centuries: there was no standing bureaucracy, no capital apart from Medina, no fixed income, and no settled doctrine of succession.

Abū Bakr's reported inaugural address, whose exact wording varies, asked for obedience conditional on his own obedience to God and the Prophet and acknowledged that he was not the best of the community. Whether or not it was said as reported, it expresses the model of leadership that the later tradition attributed to him: a first-among-believers constrained by scripture. In practice, he seems to have consulted other senior Companions, among whom ʿUmar was the most influential, along with ʿUthmān, ʿAlī (when available), Ṭalḥa, al-Zubayr, ʿAbd al-Raḥmān ibn ʿAwf, and others. Later writers describe this circle as an informal consultative council (shūrā), but there is no sign of a formal institution.

The difference between Abū Bakr's authority and Muhammad's was therefore fundamental. Muhammad had been the bearer of revelation, and his decisions could be justified by it; Abū Bakr could not claim revelation, and his decisions had to be defended by argument from precedent, from the Qurʾān, and from the advice of respected men. This limited his authority in principle but, as the Ridda showed, did not prevent him from acting decisively. He was probably helped by his reputation for steadfastness and by the support of a core of men who had much to lose from the collapse of the movement.

Section 38.7

The Expedition of Usāma ibn Zayd and the Defense of Medinan Authority

In the weeks before his death, tradition says, Muhammad had commissioned an expedition toward the Byzantine frontier in the region of Balqāʾ, east of the Jordan, under Usāma ibn Zayd, a young man still in his late teens or early twenties, son of Zayd ibn Ḥāritha who had fallen at Muʾta in 629. The assignment may have been meant to avenge Muʾta, to display Medinan strength to the Arab tribes of the Syrian steppe who were clients of the Byzantine Empire, or to bring those tribes into the Medinan alliance. Sources differ on the force's size; some say about three thousand men, but numbers in these accounts are not reliable.

When Muhammad died, the army had assembled but had not departed. The new caliph faced a dilemma. Several leading Companions advised delay: Medina was exposed, tribes in the vicinity were restless, and Usāma was young and untested. ʿUmar is reported to have urged that the army remain, and some of the Anṣār asked for an older commander. Abū Bakr, the traditions say, refused to rescind a decision made by the Prophet and insisted that the expedition go, though he allowed ʿUmar to remain in Medina. He accompanied the army for a distance on foot, according to the story, and made Usāma's father's memory a reason for honor.

Historians have asked why Abū Bakr insisted. Defenders see principle: he considered himself bound to carry out the Prophet's orders. Others see strategy. Sending a force north would have discouraged Byzantine-aligned tribes, demonstrated continuity of leadership, and perhaps drawn off men whose loyalty was uncertain. The risk was that the departure left Medina thinly defended just as hostile groups were gathering, and in the event, some tribes in the vicinity of Medina, including groups of Ghaṭafān, Ṭayyiʾ, and Asad, did threaten it. The expedition returned, by the traditional account, after roughly forty days, having met little resistance, and brought back booty. Its military significance was slight; its political significance, in the memory of the community, was large, because it represented a decision of Abū Bakr to proceed with Muhammad's plans even in a crisis. Chapter 39 examines the later campaigns on the Syrian frontier.

Section 38.8

Tribal Allegiance, Zakāt, and the Fragility of Political Integration

To understand why Muhammad's death produced a crisis in the tribes, one must understand the arrangements through which the tribes had joined Medina. Muhammad's agreements, preserved in texts that later authors copied or summarized, varied. Some tribes had accepted Islam fully, had a mosque, prayed, and paid the alms-levy; others made a treaty promising peace and perhaps a payment, but kept their own forms of religious practice for a time. Delegations, some in the year 9 and 10 AH, brought pledges from groups from Najd, Yamāma, Bahrain, Oman, Yemen, and Ḥaḍramawt. Agents of Medina — collectors, teachers, and governors — were stationed in many areas.

Several terms need distinction. Zakāt in the Qurʾān is a charitable obligation, associated with prayer; ṣadaqa is a related word for alms, and in the early period the two overlapped. By the time of Muhammad's death, zakāt had apparently become an organized levy on livestock, crops, and certain wealth, collected by agents and distributed to the poor, to the collectors, and to the needs of the community. For tribes that had submitted, payment was the practical sign of acceptance of Medinan authority, and to Muhammad's followers it was also a religious duty. For tribal leaders accustomed to independence, paying a levy to a distant capital looked like tribute, and with the Prophet gone, many questioned whether the obligation continued.

Their reasoning is reconstructed from the sources, which record a number of positions. Some groups appear to have said they would pray but would not send zakāt to Abū Bakr; some reportedly offered to distribute it themselves; others rejected Medina's collectors; others went further and followed rival leaders. The Qurʾān, they might have argued, had addressed the payment to Muhammad, whose prayers purified the donor; since the Prophet had died, the obligation, it was argued, was no longer binding on them. Abū Bakr and his advisers rejected this view.

Primary Source Window II

Abū Bakr and the Linking of Prayer and Zakāt

Creator: Transmitted reports preserved in the ḥadīth collections of al-Bukhārī and Muslim and echoed in historical works · Date of events: 632 · Date of compilation: ninth century · Type: Ḥadīth narratives of a dialogue between Abū Bakr and ʿUmar

Context: The reports describe a conversation in which ʿUmar questioned Abū Bakr's decision to fight those who withheld zakāt, reasoning that people who professed faith were protected. The collections are Sunni, compiled over two centuries after the event, and meant to establish models of juristic reasoning.

Paraphrase (not a quotation): ʿUmar objects that the Prophet had said people who profess the faith are protected in life and property unless a right of Islam applies. Abū Bakr replies that zakāt is a right attached to property and that he will fight anyone who separates prayer from zakāt, adding that he would fight even over the smallest item that had been paid in the Prophet's time. ʿUmar later says he came to recognize that God had opened Abū Bakr's mind to the matter.

Analysis: In the transmitted form, the exchange sets out a legal argument: payment of zakāt is part of what it means to profess Islam, and refusing it removes the immunity of the believer. It also records a disagreement between the two leading figures, which later scholars used to show that even the greatest Companions could differ and be persuaded by reasoning. For the historian, the report indicates what the later tradition believed was at stake — the inseparability of ritual and fiscal obligation — and suggests that the legal definition of the Ridda as apostasy was shaped partly by this argument.

Limitations: The report offers the justification for Abū Bakr's decision, not the motivations of the people who refused, and it belongs to a legal genre. It does not tell us what the tribes believed they were doing.

Section 38.9

What Was the Ridda? Apostasy, Rebellion, or Political Separation?

The Arabic ridda derives from a root meaning to turn back, and in Islamic law refers to apostasy, the abandonment of Islam by one who had professed it. The campaigns of 632–633 became known as ḥurūb al-ridda, "the wars of apostasy," and those who fought Medina as ahl al-ridda. This name, which appears in the historical literature composed generations later, embodies the verdict of the winners that those groups had rejected the faith.

Historical Vocabulary
Ridda and Zakāt

Ridda in later Islamic legal usage means apostasy; as a name for the events of 632–633 it is a label applied by the later tradition. Modern historians use it as a convenient label but vary in whether they accept its implications. Zakāt is the obligatory alms-levy of Islam; in Muhammad's last years it appears to have been collected by Medinan agents from tribes that had joined the community, which made it both a religious duty and a mark of political submission. Nabī means prophet; the sources call the rival claimants mutanabbī ("one who claims prophecy") or kadhdhāb ("liar"), terms that reflect their opponents' judgment and do not describe how their followers saw them.

What can be said, based on the sources, about the actual motives? Four overlapping elements stand out. First, some tribes refused to pay zakāt to Medina but did not necessarily renounce Islamic prayer or belief. Second, some tribes or leaders rejected Abū Bakr's leadership as a personal relationship — many pledges had been made to Muhammad as a person — without necessarily rejecting the religion. Third, some groups followed leaders who claimed prophecy or revelation of their own, often in language and ritual similar to that of Muhammad; their followers might regard them as legitimate monotheists. Fourth, in several regions, local political rivalries, preexisting disputes, and the desire to recover independence drove opposition, with religion supplying the vocabulary.

The terms of the argument depend on what one counts as "Islam" in 632. The community then lacked a fixed body of law, and the distinction between religious affiliation and political allegiance, which later jurisprudence drew sharply, was blurred. A tribe that continued to pray while refusing to send revenue to Medina was in a legally ambiguous position that jurists later resolved by treating the refusal as grounds for war. A group that accepted a rival prophet had stepped outside the Prophet's community in any case. The Ridda thus includes both movements that look like apostasy to later Muslim jurists and movements that look like secession.

The point for historians is to resist a single explanation. The sources, especially Sayf ibn ʿUmar, tend to flatten the variety into a single movement of apostasy, with a consistent narrative of coordinated campaigns by eleven columns. Detailed reading shows that the regions involved had very different histories, that the movements were not coordinated, and that several outbreaks were purely local. The chapter continues with an analysis of the regions in turn; the Historical Debate in Section 38.22 returns to the interpretive question.

Section 38.10

The Geography of Revolt and the Mobilization of the Caliphal Armies

The conflicts of 632–633 spread across much of Arabia, but they should be mapped by region. Near Medina, tribes from the northern Najd, including Asad, Ghaṭafān (with its branches Fazāra and ʿAbs and Dhubyān), and parts of Ṭayyiʾ, posed the most immediate threat and were the first to be confronted. In central Najd, Banū Tamīm was divided, and the oasis region of Yamāma, in the east of the central plateau, was dominated by Banū Ḥanīfa under Musaylima. Eastern Arabia, including al-Baḥrayn on the Gulf coast, was a patchwork of tribal groups, local rulers, and Persian-descended garrisons. Oman, in the southeast, had its own political structures; Mahra lay to its southwest along the southern coast; Ḥaḍramawt and Yemen occupied the southern highlands and coast.

The mobilization described by the sources is considerable. According to al-Ṭabarī's narrative based on Sayf, Abū Bakr first fought defensive actions near Medina, then organized the Muslim forces into a main army under Khālid ibn al-Walīd and a series of columns, each with a commander, a banner, and a destination. The standard list names eleven. Some historians doubt the neatness of this scheme, which suits a later narrative interest in a coordinated campaign, but accept that Medina sent several forces in different directions, and that the commanders included veterans of Muhammad's campaigns and Meccan aristocrats like Khālid and ʿIkrima who had joined only after 630.

The forces consisted largely of Muhājirūn and Anṣār along with loyal tribal contingents; as the campaigns proceeded, defeated or reconciled tribes were added as auxiliaries. Command relationships were informal: Abū Bakr issued instructions through letters and messengers, and commanders had substantial discretion. This setting created friction, notably in the dispute over Khālid's conduct described in Section 38.13. Logistical constraints were severe. Armies moved with camels and horses, depending on wells, local supplies, and the cooperation of tribes along the route. Distances were long: Medina to Yamāma is roughly a thousand kilometers by caravan route, and Medina to Yemen further still. Coordination across such distances, with messages carried by riders, was possible only in the loosest sense.

Division III — Central Arabia, Rival Prophetic Claims, and the Battle of Yamāma · Section 38.11

Ṭulayḥa ibn Khuwaylid and the Tribal Coalitions of Northern Najd

Ṭulayḥa ibn Khuwaylid, of the Banū Asad, was a tribal leader and reputed warrior who had, according to the traditions, visited Medina and accepted Islam in 631 before returning to his people. The sources report that he later claimed to receive revelations, perhaps already before Muhammad's death, and attracted support from Asad and neighboring groups, notably parts of Ghaṭafān, including the Fazāra under ʿUyayna ibn Ḥiṣn, and elements of Ṭayyiʾ. His revelations, as preserved by hostile reporters, are said to have been rhymed utterances of the kind associated with Arabian seers (kāhin), and some are said to have modified ritual practice. The surviving wording is scanty and should not be treated as a reliable text.

The political basis of the movement is more apparent than its theology. Asad and Ghaṭafān had histories of alliance and rivalry with Quraysh and Medina; Ghaṭafān had fought Muhammad at the Trench. Many of their leaders had joined Medina in the Year of Delegations without deep commitment. A leader who could assert religious authority independently of Medina offered a means to preserve autonomy. Ṭulayḥa was thus both a prophetic claimant and the head of a tribal coalition. The Muslim sources emphasize his prophetic claim; a historian might equally stress that the coalition was a defense against Medinan taxation and subordination.

The coalition quickly moved against Medina. Groups from Asad, Ghaṭafān, and others attacked the outskirts, probably in the summer of 632, and clashed with Medinan defenders at places such as Dhū Ḥusā and Dhū al-Qaṣṣa near Medina. Some traditions have Abū Bakr personally leading a night sortie and ʿAlī, Ṭalḥa, and al-Zubayr guarding the approaches while Usāma's army was away. Whether or not the details are accurate, they illustrate that the crisis was acute and that Medina was for a time on the defensive.

Section 38.12

The Battle of Buzākha and Khālid ibn al-Walīd's Central Arabian Campaign

After Usāma's return and the repulse of the first attacks, Abū Bakr organized a stronger force under Khālid ibn al-Walīd. Khālid, of the Meccan clan of Makhzūm, had commanded Qurashī cavalry against Muhammad at Uḥud, converted around 629, and distinguished himself at Muʾta, where, as tradition says, he extricated the army after the death of its commanders. His reputation for tactical skill made him the natural choice for the central campaign.

According to the narrative preserved in al-Ṭabarī, Khālid first neutralized Ṭayyiʾ by diplomacy. ʿAdī ibn Ḥātim, a leading figure of that tribe who had remained loyal to Muhammad, persuaded much of Ṭayyiʾ to stay out of the conflict or return to Medina's side, thereby weakening Ṭulayḥa's coalition. Khālid then confronted Ṭulayḥa near the well of Buzākha, in the territory of Asad. The battle, traditionally dated to autumn 632, is described in terms of a hard fight in which the coalition collapsed after the defection of ʿUyayna ibn Ḥiṣn, who is said to have concluded that Ṭulayḥa had been proved false. Ṭulayḥa fled, reportedly to Syria.

The sources add a number of details — speeches, duels, the precise numbers of dead — that are likely embellished. What can be said with some confidence is that the coalition of Asad, Ghaṭafān, and allies was broken in a relatively short campaign; that submission followed, with the defeated tribes being required to renew their allegiance, hand over certain individuals, and resume payment; and that ʿUyayna was taken to Medina as a captive and later pardoned. Ṭulayḥa himself is reported to have returned after Abū Bakr's death, accepted Islam again, and later served in the Iraqi campaigns, an example of the political reintegration that the caliphate permitted to many former opponents.

The campaign illustrates the methods that would recur. Khālid combined military threat with diplomacy, exploited divisions within and among tribes, and offered a path back to the community for those who submitted. The cost of defeat was not extermination for the coalition as a whole, though particular leaders were punished. Medina's goals, in other words, were political subordination and the resumption of tribute, expressed in religious terms.

Section 38.13

Banū Tamīm, Mālik ibn Nuwayra, and the Controversy over Khālid's Conduct

Banū Tamīm, a large and politically fragmented confederation of eastern and central Najd, responded to Muhammad's death in different ways. Some of its clans remained loyal; others, notably elements of the Yarbūʿ led by Mālik ibn Nuwayra, withheld zakāt or hesitated. Mālik had been appointed by Muhammad as a collector, according to some reports, which made his position as a local intermediary delicate: a Medinan agent who ceased forwarding the levy, or who held it back, while deliberating about the new leadership. Tamīm was also riven by internal rivalries, and some clans were willing to fight their neighbors under the banner of Medina.

Khālid, after Buzākha, moved against the Tamīm area. According to the sources, he found the Tamīmī groups dispersed and unwilling to resist, and Mālik's people at al-Buṭāḥ were said to have disbanded. What followed is the subject of competing reports. Mālik was seized and brought before Khālid, and was executed. Different narrators gave different reasons. In one version, Mālik had been in rebellion, had admitted withholding zakāt, and had spoken disrespectfully of Muhammad; in another, he and his men had said they were Muslims, had given the call to prayer, and were killed because of Khālid's desire for Mālik's wife. The report that Khālid then married Mālik's widow, Umm Tamīm (Laylā), is widely transmitted, and her marriage immediately after the death, rather than after the required waiting period, scandalized contemporaries in the telling.

The affair provoked controversy within the community. ʿUmar, according to the traditions, urged Abū Bakr to remove Khālid, arguing that he had killed a Muslim and violated norms, and one of Khālid's own officers, Abū Qatāda, is said to have protested and left the army in disapproval. Mālik's brother, Mutammim, was reported to have lamented him in verses that circulated widely. Abū Bakr, the sources say, summoned Khālid to Medina, heard his explanation, accepted that he had made an error of judgment in acting on a misunderstanding, paid blood-money, and returned him to command, saying that he would not sheathe a sword that God had drawn.

Historians cannot determine what actually happened. The Mālik episode is a case study in the character of the sources. The pro-Khālid tradition defends the execution as a justified response to rebellion; the opposing tradition, which gained force in later times, used it to criticize Khālid and, by extension, Abū Bakr's tolerance of him. Whether Mālik was an apostate, a tribal chief who had not yet submitted, or a Muslim killed by mistake, depends on which strand is credited. The episode also reveals the structural problem of the early caliphate: commanders operated far from Medina and had wide authority over life and death, while the caliph had limited means of control other than reprimand and removal. The question of what legal protection individuals who professed Islam should have in a context of war and withdrawal of allegiance would be debated by jurists for centuries (see Chapter 47).

Section 38.14

Sajāḥ bint al-Ḥārith and Competing Religious Leadership

The sources report that a woman named Sajāḥ bint al-Ḥārith claimed prophetic status among Tamīm and neighboring groups. She is described as being of Tamīmī descent through her father and connected through her mother's side to the Banū Taghlib, a largely Christian Arab confederation of the Jazīra and northern Arabia. Some traditions say she was a Christian; others describe her as a diviner (kāhina). She is said to have gathered a following that included Tamīmī clans and to have contemplated attacking Medina, but to have turned instead to Yamāma and reached an arrangement with Musaylima.

The accounts of that arrangement are conspicuously colored by their authors' interests. In a widely reported story, Sajāḥ and Musaylima met, concluded an agreement, and married; the narrators often mock or sexualize the encounter, citing rhymed verses attributed to them with explicit content. Modern historians treat the story with suspicion: the humor and the gendered framing suggest hostile invention, and the marriage may have been a political alliance in a tribal setting, or may not have happened. The picture of Sajāḥ as a seer who led a coalition of Tamīmī and Taghlibī groups, whatever its historicity, shows that female religious leadership of some kind was imaginable in the region and was remembered, even if in demeaning form.

After Musaylima's defeat, tradition says, Sajāḥ withdrew to the Jazīra among her maternal relatives, later accepted Islam, and moved to Kūfa during the conquests. The limits of the evidence are plain. The narratives about her are late, derived from a tradition hostile to women claiming prophetic leadership, and give no independent evidence of her teachings. A responsible account notes her existence in the tradition, regards the story of her alliance with Musaylima as uncertain, and avoids repeating its more lurid details.

Section 38.15

Musaylima, Banū Ḥanīfa, and the Challenge from Yamāma

Yamāma is a region of oases and agricultural settlements in eastern Najd, known for grain and date production. Its principal population was Banū Ḥanīfa, a settled branch of the Bakr ibn Wāʾil confederation, whose towns lay in the valley of Wādī Ḥanīfa near the later site of Riyadh. Unlike pastoral nomads, the Ḥanīfa were farmers with fortified settlements and a substantial population, and Yamāma had long been oriented toward the Gulf and the Persian-influenced eastern coast. Because Yamāma supplied grain to Arabia, its political position mattered to Mecca and Medina.

Musaylima ibn Ḥabīb, whom the Muslim sources call by the diminutive Musaylima ("little Maslama"), also known as Rahmān al-Yamāma ("the Merciful One of Yamāma") in the tradition, was a leader of Ḥanīfa who claimed prophethood. According to reports, a delegation of Ḥanīfa had visited Muhammad in 631, and Musaylima, who stayed behind, later claimed to have been associated with him in prophecy. A letter reportedly sent to Muhammad, proposing a division of authority, and Muhammad's reported reply, are preserved in the tradition, but their authenticity is doubtful. The Qurʾān-like rhymed utterances attributed to Musaylima survive in fragments in hostile sources, and are probably distorted.

Why did Musaylima win a following? The sources emphasize the personal charisma of the prophet, but the political context is crucial. Ḥanīfa were proud of their independence, had been aligned with the Persian-influenced eastern Arabian order, and had little connection to Quraysh. A prophet of their own offered religious legitimacy and a means to refuse subordination to a Qurashī-dominated Medina — reportedly some said that if prophecy was to be Qurashī, they preferred a prophet from Rabīʿa, to which Bakr ibn Wāʾil belonged. Musaylima's followers appear to have prayed and recognized one God; their break with Medina was a matter of which prophet and which capital.

The evidence concerning Musaylima's teachings is limited by the fact that nothing survives that his followers themselves wrote. Later authors dismissed him as a liar; some modern scholars, noting that his utterances are said to resemble the Qurʾān's style, have asked whether he was part of a broader late-antique Arabian monotheism in which several prophets appeared. In any case, the movement in Yamāma was substantial: by the accounts, tens of thousands of fighters supported him, and it was the single most serious military challenge Abū Bakr faced.

Section 38.16

The Battle of Yamāma: Defeat of Musaylima and the Cost of Victory

Abū Bakr's first attempts to subdue Yamāma failed. A force under ʿIkrima ibn Abī Jahl, sent in advance, was defeated or withdrew; another under Shuraḥbīl ibn Ḥasana was sent in support, and Abū Bakr is reported to have rebuked ʿIkrima for fighting before reinforcements arrived. Only after the campaign in Najd did Khālid, reinforced with fresh troops from Medina, advance on Yamāma, probably in late 632 or early 633. The year is disputed in the sources: some date the battle to 11 AH, others to 12.

Traditions describe an army of several thousand Muslims against a larger Ḥanīfa force, perhaps twenty thousand or more; such figures are doubtful. The confrontation took place at ʿAqrabāʾ in the plain near Musaylima's settlement. The first clash went badly for the Muslims, who were pressed back, according to reports, almost to Khālid's tent. The tide turned when Khālid reorganized his forces, separating contingents by tribal or origin groups so that each would fight with reputation at stake, and when Muslim reciters and veterans rallied the line. The Ḥanīfa then withdrew into a walled garden enclosure, later remembered as the "Garden of Death" (ḥadīqat al-mawt), where Musaylima was killed. The killer is named in tradition as Waḥshī, the Abyssinian who had earlier killed Muhammad's uncle Ḥamza at Uḥud, but accounts attribute the death to several men.

The terms of the settlement afterward are told in a story featuring Mujjāʿa ibn Murāra, a Ḥanīfa leader, who is said to have tricked Khālid by presenting women and children dressed as warriors on the walls of the fortresses, thereby gaining better terms. The story explains why the Ḥanīfa received relatively lenient treatment: their surviving community submitted to Medina, accepted Islam under its authority, and paid the levy. Whether or not the stratagem occurred, the outcome is consistent with Khālid's pattern of negotiating after decisive victory.

The cost was high. Sources report heavy Muslim losses, commonly numbered at around twelve hundred, among them many early converts of the Muhājirūn and Anṣār and a number of those who had memorized portions of the Qurʾān, including Sālim, the freedman of Abū Ḥudhayfa, and Zayd ibn al-Khaṭṭāb, brother of ʿUmar. Ḥanīfa losses are given as several thousand. These numbers cannot be verified; modern historians accept that casualties were exceptionally heavy by Arabian standards. The defeat destroyed organized opposition in central Arabia, removed the principal prophetic rival, and brought the rich oasis region under Medina. The loss of so many Companions also shaped the memory of Yamāma and, as Section 38.24 explains, linked it to the tradition of the first written compilation of the Qurʾān.

Division IV — The Ridda Wars beyond Central Arabia · Section 38.17

Yemen, al-Aswad al-ʿAnsī, and the Crisis before Muhammad's Death

Yemen in 632 was a very different political world from Najd. The southwestern highlands, a region of terraced agriculture, towns, and fortified settlements, had been the center of the Himyarite kingdom, which Aksumite and then Sasanian intervention had undermined over the previous century (Chapter 34). After about 570, a Persian expedition had established Sasanian influence, and a community of Persian descendants — known in Arabic sources as the abnāʾ, "the sons" — held power in Ṣanʿāʾ under a governor, in alliance with local Yemeni notables. When the Sasanian state weakened in the late 620s, its Yemeni agent, Bādhām, reportedly accepted Muhammad's authority and Islam, and Muhammad confirmed him and appointed Muslims, among them Muʿādh ibn Jabal and Abū Mūsā al-Ashʿarī, as teachers and officials.

Al-Aswad al-ʿAnsī, called Dhū al-Khimār ("the veiled one"), a leader from the ʿAns tribe in the Yemeni mountains, led a movement against this arrangement in the last months of Muhammad's life. He claimed prophecy, took Najrān and then Ṣanʿāʾ, and, according to the accounts, killed or expelled Muhammad's representatives, married the widow of the Persian governor, and ruled briefly. His movement drew on resentment of the Persian-descended elite and on Yemeni local pride, as well as on his religious claim. Tradition says that he was killed by a plot involving Fayrūz al-Daylamī and other leaders of the abnāʾ, and that Muhammad learned by revelation of his death on the eve of his own passing, though news reached Medina only after Muhammad had died.

The al-Aswad episode demonstrates that the Yemeni crisis began before the succession question arose. By June 632 the region was unstable, with competition among the abnāʾ, Yemeni chiefs, and Muhammad's appointees. Muhammad's death added a new layer of uncertainty about whom Yemen's leaders owed allegiance to, and the Yemeni conflicts that followed reflected both these older rivalries and the new question of loyalty to Medina.

Section 38.18

Yemen and Ḥaḍramawt after 632: Kinda, al-Ashʿath ibn Qays, and the Restoration of Authority

Following al-Aswad's death, Qays ibn Makshūḥ al-Murādī, a local commander who had been involved in the plot but resented the Persian-descended leaders, rose against the abnāʾ. Fayrūz and others resisted him. Reports indicate that the conflict in Ṣanʿāʾ became a three-sided struggle among local Yemeni leaders, the abnāʾ who had accepted Islam, and challengers. Abū Bakr sent al-Muhājir ibn Abī Umayya, a Qurashī, and later ʿIkrima ibn Abī Jahl, with forces to support the Muslim party. Medina's intervention thus took the form of backing one side in an existing local struggle, which helps to explain why Yemen's reintegration did not follow the pattern of Najd.

Further east, in Ḥaḍramawt, the leading tribal confederation was Kinda, whose leaders had a long history of power in central Arabia and had submitted to Muhammad through delegations. Its chief, al-Ashʿath ibn Qays, had come to Medina in 631 and was said to have married into Abū Bakr's circle. After Muhammad's death, parts of Kinda refused to pay zakāt to the collector and resisted; in the accounts, Ziyād ibn Labīd al-Anṣārī, the Medinan governor, clashed with them. Medinan forces, with al-Muhājir, besieged the fortress of al-Nujayr, where Kinda's resistance was centered. The fortress fell, according to the sources, after a siege in which many were killed.

The best-known story concerns al-Ashʿath's surrender. In the narrative, he negotiated terms for the safety of a small number of people in the fortress and, by trickery or misunderstanding, omitted his own name, so that he was taken prisoner and brought to Abū Bakr in chains. The caliph, however, pardoned him and married him to his sister Umm Farwa. The tale, whatever its historicity, demonstrates the tradition's interest in explaining how a leader who had rebelled came to be reintegrated as a distinguished commander; al-Ashʿath later fought in Iraq and Syria and played a prominent role in the first civil war (Chapter 40).

Yemen's political history therefore differs strongly from that of Najd and Yamāma. The southern campaigns were fought by smaller forces; they involved sieges of fortresses and contests among local elites; and they ended not with the defeat of one great rival prophet but with a series of local settlements. The Yemeni highlands, with their old urban and agricultural traditions, would remain a complex part of the caliphate, discussed again in Chapters 41 and 87.

Section 38.19

Oman and Mahra: Regional Resistance and the Eastern and Southern Campaigns

Oman on the southeastern coast was ruled by two brothers, Jayfar and ʿAbbād, sons of al-Julandā, from the Azd tribal confederation, who had accepted Islam during Muhammad's lifetime after receiving a letter and envoy, ʿAmr ibn al-ʿĀṣ. Their authority was challenged by Laqīṭ ibn Mālik, called Dhū al-Tāj ("the crowned one"), who, in the sources, claimed prophethood and attracted support among the Azd and others in the coastal region. The tradition describes him as a powerful figure with royal pretensions, and the crown title suggests a connection to Persian-influenced kingship in the Gulf.

Abū Bakr sent Ḥudhayfa ibn Miḥṣan and ʿArfaja ibn Harthama, with ʿIkrima's forces later joining, to support Jayfar and ʿAbbād. The decisive engagement, according to the sources, took place near Dibbā, a port on the eastern coast, where Laqīṭ's forces were defeated after hard fighting and heavy casualties, followed by the enslavement or deportation of captives. Accounts of the numbers killed are exaggerated and inconsistent. The significance of the battle lay in confirming Medinan-backed control over the Omani coast and the trade routes of the Gulf of Oman, as well as in the extent to which the Muslim side relied on local allies.

Mahra, a region of southern Arabia known for its distinctive language and camel breeding, lay along the coast to the southwest of Oman. The sources state that Mahra was split between two leaders, one of whom, Shakhrīt, was inclined toward Medina and the other, al-Muṣabbaḥ, in revolt, and that ʿIkrima exploited the rivalry to defeat the rebels and bring the region back into the fold. The details are thin and mixed with stories, but they confirm that Mahra's politics were separate from those of Oman, and that treating the two as one movement would be misleading.

Section 38.20

Eastern Arabia and al-Baḥrayn: Rebellion, Alliances, and the Gulf Communities

In the early seventh century, al-Baḥrayn designated the whole eastern coast of Arabia, a region of date oases, fishing and pearling settlements, and trading ports linked to the Persian Gulf. Its population included Arab tribes — notably ʿAbd al-Qays and various branches of Bakr ibn Wāʾil and Tamīm — alongside Persian-descended garrisons, Christian communities, Jews, and Zoroastrian groups. The Sasanian empire had maintained a presence there, and Muhammad had sent an envoy, al-ʿAlāʾ ibn al-Ḥaḍramī, to the local ruler, al-Mundhir ibn Sāwā of Tamīm, who had accepted Islam. Al-Mundhir died shortly after Muhammad did, and the political order began to fragment.

According to the sources, parts of Bakr ibn Wāʾil, led by al-Ḥuṭam (Shurayḥ ibn Ḍubayʿa), returned to the old Persian-linked order and supported a member of the former Lakhmid royal line, al-Mundhir ibn al-Nuʿmān, called al-Gharūr, as a king. The ʿAbd al-Qays, by contrast, were divided; one leader, al-Jārūd ibn al-Muʿallā, is reported to have remained loyal to Islam and persuaded part of the tribe to do likewise. The rebels gathered at the fortress of Jawāthā, held by loyalists, and the Medinan commander al-ʿAlāʾ, sent by Abū Bakr, defeated them after a siege and a night attack. Further fighting followed at Hajar and along the coast, where rebels who had fled were pursued.

The conflict in al-Baḥrayn shows that, like Yemen, the east had its own dynamics. The revolt involved tribal rivalries, a claim to restore a Lakhmid-type kingship, and the interests of Persian-affiliated elites, who may have preferred the older arrangement. Religious language was used on both sides, but the contest was about political authority and the future of the Gulf coast. After the settlement, al-Baḥrayn became part of the caliphate's territory and a base for later campaigns toward Iraq and Fārs, as Chapter 39 shows. The prominence of miracle stories in the traditions — such as accounts of wells appearing or of armies crossing shallow water — is a reminder to read this narrative with caution.

Section 38.21

Coordinating the Campaigns: Commanders, Logistics, Diplomacy, and Civilian Consequences

Comparing the campaigns reveals both a pattern and wide variation. In each region, Medina relied on a small core of Muhājirūn and Anṣār as officers and fighters, supplemented by loyal tribal contingents. In Najd and Yamāma, campaigns were conducted by Khālid's mobile army and ended in battles; in Yemen, Ḥaḍramawt, Oman, and al-Baḥrayn, forces were smaller and operated with local allies against fortresses and coastal strongholds. Command arrangements were flexible, with letters from Abū Bakr urging commanders to call people to Islam first, to give the call to prayer as a sign of submission, to spare those who answered it, and to fight those who did not, as reported in the sources. These instructions, if authentic in form, show an attempt to set a standard of conduct while leaving discretion to commanders.

Logistics determined much. Armies moved only where water was available, and the loyalty of tribes along the route could decide whether the force had access to wells and pasture. Spoils and booty provided incentives, and loyal contingents were paid or rewarded from them. Diplomacy was constant: commanders sent letters, offered amnesty, and made agreements, and the sources report that many groups submitted without fighting. The mixture of coercion and negotiation shaped the outcome as much as battlefield victories.

The wars had serious effects on civilians. Defeated groups were sometimes required to hand over leaders, return captured property, or relinquish animals; in some regions, women and children were taken as captives, and Abū Bakr is reported to have ordered the release of some; and in others, populations suffered from the loss of livestock and crops, displacement, and the killing of men. The sources on these points are sparse and often glossed over by later writers who wished to present the wars as righteous. The scale of enslavement is disputed: some reports suggest the practice was widespread, others that Abū Bakr or ʿUmar later freed many Arab captives, and the legal principle, usually associated with ʿUmar, that Arab Muslims should not be enslaved developed later. The human cost of the Ridda needs to be understood as a real and painful experience for many households, even if the sources give only glimpses.

Division V — Consolidation, Institutional Consequences, and the Transition to Conquest · Section 38.22

The Consequences of the Ridda Wars: Defeat, Reintegration, and Social Disruption

By the end of 633, Medina had reasserted its authority over most of the peninsula. The terms of reintegration varied. Many tribes returned to the community on condition of resuming prayer and paying zakāt; leaders of the principal revolts were killed, as with Musaylima, or captured and later pardoned, as with ʿUyayna and al-Ashʿath; and some groups were punished by confiscation of property or the taking of captives. Within a short time, former opponents, including Ṭulayḥa, Sajāḥ, and many Tamīmī and Kindī leaders, are reported to have joined the armies that left Arabia. The wars thus functioned as a process of selection and absorption.

The wars also redefined the relationship between religious affiliation and political allegiance. After 633, to be a Muslim in Arabia meant, in practice, to acknowledge Medina and to pay the levy, and to refuse was to risk military action. The legal rule that apostasy was punishable and that refusing zakāt could justify war was grounded in the outcome. The tribes' acceptance, it seems, was tempered by the expectation of a share in the spoils of future expansion, which opened an avenue of upward mobility.

The costs were substantial. Heavy casualties at Yamāma and elsewhere removed experienced early Muslims; livestock and fields were damaged by campaigns and by the movement of armies; and the economic life of oasis communities like Yamāma and of the Gulf coast was disrupted. The effect on households is hard to quantify: widows and orphans, captured individuals, and families displaced from their lands appear in the sources only incidentally. Caliphal control also remained thin: Medina relied on local leaders, delegated governors, and tribal contingents, and the reintegration of regions like Yemen and Oman depended on local elites who retained substantial power.

Historical Debate

Apostasy, Taxation, Sovereignty, or Something Else? Interpreting the Ridda

The question: Were the Ridda Wars primarily a response to religious apostasy, resistance to centralized taxation, a struggle over political sovereignty, or a different combination of causes across Arabia?

Interpretation A — Religious apostasy: The classical Islamic tradition, followed by many modern Muslim and some Western historians, holds that large groups abandoned Islam or its obligations after Muhammad's death, that Abū Bakr's campaigns saved the religion, and that rival prophets exploited that defection. The tradition's consistent use of the term ridda and the legal treatment of the wars as precedent for apostasy law support this reading.
Interpretation B — Political and fiscal secession: Many modern historians, including Watt and scholars such as Elias Shoufani and Hugh Kennedy in different formulations, emphasize that the agreements of Muhammad's last years had been personal and political, that many groups refused to pay Medina rather than abandon prayer, and that the revolts were reassertions of tribal autonomy. On this view, "apostasy" was a retrospective religious description of a political conflict.
Interpretation C — Regionally varied and multi-causal: A third approach, which this textbook finds most consistent with the evidence, holds that the Ridda should be broken into regional conflicts with different causes: religious rivalry in Yamāma, tribal coalition-building in northern Najd, succession of local rulers and Persian-linked politics in Yemen, Oman, and al-Baḥrayn, and fiscal refusal in many groups. Sayf ibn ʿUmar's unified narrative may reflect later schematization.

Evidence: All interpretations rely on the same limited traditions, chiefly al-Ṭabarī, al-Balādhurī, and ḥadīth collections. Disagreement concerns how far to trust Sayf, how to read the fragments of rival prophets' sayings, and how to interpret the Muslims' own legal characterization of the events.

Why it matters: The answer shapes understanding of the early state: whether it was defending a religion, imposing an empire-in-embryo, or doing both. It also illustrates how a later label can be mistaken for an explanation.

Section 38.23

The Consolidation of Early Caliphal Authority

The wars strengthened the caliph's position in several ways. Abū Bakr's decisions at critical moments — to send Usāma, to refuse concessions on zakāt, to continue the campaigns, and to retain Khālid — became part of his reputation as a leader of resolve. The military successes also demonstrated that Medina could mobilize forces across Arabia, a message that discouraged further opposition.

Yet the institutions of the caliphate remained rudimentary. Abū Bakr lived modestly in Medina, according to tradition, in a house near the mosque, and the sources report that he received a small stipend for his service, after giving up trade. The treasury, to the extent it existed, was a simple store of revenues, distributed fairly quickly among the community, so that there was no standing fiscal apparatus. Governors and collectors were appointed from among Companions or from loyal local chiefs; they reported to Medina by messenger, and much depended on personal relationships. Military organization rested on tribal contingents and volunteers, with no standing army. The later registry (dīwān) and stipend system that ʿUmar is credited with creating, discussed in Chapter 41, had not yet been introduced.

The relationship between religion and politics was also clarified. Muhammad's prophetic authority could not be inherited, but his role as head of the community, collector of alms, and war leader could be. Abū Bakr's decision to treat the payment of zakāt to the caliph as a requirement of Islam defined the caliph as the legitimate recipient of that obligation. This precedent shaped later fiscal theory. At the same time, Medina's position changed: from the base of a prophetic movement it became the capital of a polity with claims across Arabia, and a destination for delegations, governors, and tribal leaders. The distribution of captives and spoils, and the settlement of loyal commanders and allies, created networks of patronage that would soon underlie the garrison towns of the conquests.

Section 38.24

Yamāma and the Early Preservation of the Qurʾān

A well-known tradition connects the losses at Yamāma to the early history of the Qurʾān. According to the report preserved in the collection of al-Bukhārī, ʿUmar came to Abū Bakr shortly after the battle and warned that heavy casualties among the reciters (qurrāʾ) might cause parts of the Qurʾān to be lost, urging that it be gathered. Abū Bakr at first hesitated, saying that he could not do what the Prophet had not done, but was persuaded and asked Zayd ibn Thābit, a young Medinan who had served as a scribe for Muhammad, to undertake the task.

Primary Source Window III

Zayd ibn Thābit and the Collection of Qurʾānic Material after Yamāma

Creator: Report attributed to Zayd ibn Thābit, transmitted in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī (ḥadīth 4986 in the Sunnah.com numbering) · Date of events: c. 633 · Date of compilation: ninth century · Type: First-person ḥadīth narrative

Context: The report is presented as Zayd's own account. It is widely cited in Muslim discussions of the history of the Qurʾān and in Western scholarship on the formation of the text.

Paraphrase (not a quotation): Zayd relates that, after the battle of Yamāma, Abū Bakr summoned him and told him that ʿUmar had urged that the Qurʾān be collected because of the deaths of many reciters. Zayd says he found the task heavy and questioned doing what the Prophet had not done, but he was persuaded and began to search for the material among sheets, palm stalks, stones, and the memories of men, until he had gathered it. The pages were then kept by Abū Bakr, afterward by ʿUmar, and then by ʿUmar's daughter Ḥafṣa.

Analysis: The report shows that, in the tradition, Qurʾānic material existed in written and memorized form during Muhammad's lifetime, that Yamāma's casualties prompted a systematic collection, and that the collected sheets passed from caliph to caliph. It ties the Qurʾān's preservation to the authority of the early caliphs.

Limitations: The report is late and belongs to a body of competing traditions; other accounts attribute a compilation to different people or to ʿUthmān's later standardization (Chapter 40). Modern scholars differ on whether a complete collection was made under Abū Bakr, and manuscript evidence is discussed in later volumes. The historian should treat the account as the community's later memory, not as proof of an event's details.

Section 38.25

Abū Bakr's Final Years, the Succession of ʿUmar, and the Threshold of Imperial Expansion

By 633, as the Ridda campaigns wound down, Abū Bakr sent forces beyond Arabia. Traditions report that Khālid, after Yamāma, was sent toward southern Iraq, where the Arab tribes of the lower Euphrates and the Persian frontier region of al-Ḥīra were drawn into conflict, and that forces were sent toward Syria and Palestine under commanders such as ʿAmr ibn al-ʿĀṣ, Yazīd ibn Abī Sufyān, and Abū ʿUbayda. Whether these campaigns were planned expansion, a continuation of the pursuit of rebels who had fled to the frontier regions, an attempt to channel the energies of newly reintegrated tribes, or a combination is debated. The conquests themselves, their chronology, and their consequences belong to Chapter 39.

Abū Bakr fell ill in the summer of 634 and died, according to the traditional date, on 22 Jumādā II, 13 AH, corresponding to about 23 August 634, after a reign of roughly two years and three months. Before his death, the sources say, he consulted leading Companions — ʿUthmān, ʿAbd al-Raḥmān ibn ʿAwf, and others — about designating a successor, then dictated a statement naming ʿUmar. Some Companions are reported to have worried about ʿUmar's severity, but accepted the choice, and the community pledged allegiance. The use of designation, rather than open selection, set a precedent for succession by the incumbent's choice, which would be followed in different forms by later dynasties and criticized by others, including the later supporters of ʿAlī.

ʿUmar took the title amīr al-muʾminīn, which became the standard caliphal title, and built on Abū Bakr's foundations. When Abū Bakr died, the polity he left was larger and more secure than in 632, but it had not become a bureaucratic state. Its central features were the personal authority of the caliph, the cooperation of a small circle of Companions, the military capacity of loyal tribal contingents, and the incorporation of Arabia's tribes by a mixture of force and agreement. Its institutions — taxation beyond zakāt and the spoils, a register of fighters, garrison towns, provincial governors, and a monetary system — were developed in the decades that followed, as Chapters 39 and 41 explain.

Multiple Perspectives

Authority after Muhammad: Six Vantage Points

The Muhājirūn: Many emigrants saw leadership as belonging to Quraysh, the Prophet's tribe and the group the Arabs would accept, and valued early adherence and sacrifice as grounds for authority. They also feared disorder if the question remained open.
The Anṣār: Medinans stressed that their city had sheltered and defended the movement. Some sought a share in leadership; the division between Aws and Khazraj shaped whether they could act together.
Muhammad's family and close kin: ʿAlī, Fāṭima, and the Banū Hāshim, according to different traditions, expected a role for the household, whether through designation or kinship, and were affected by disputes over inheritance and allegiance.
Rebel tribal leaders and prophets: Chiefs such as Mālik, ʿUyayna, and Laqīṭ, and claimants such as Musaylima, weighed continued independence against the benefits of alliance with Medina; their followers often saw no contradiction between worshipping one God and rejecting Abū Bakr.
Ordinary communities in the path of the campaigns: Herders and farmers in Najd, Yamāma, and the coasts faced requisition, loss of animals, displacement, or captivity, with little say in the decisions of leaders.
Muslim commanders: Figures like Khālid and ʿIkrima, recent converts of aristocratic Quraysh, sought to prove loyalty and advance status, operating far from Medina with broad discretion.
Continuity and Change Over Time

From Prophetic Leadership to Caliphal Authority

What Changed

  • Authority passed from a prophet who received revelation to a deputy who could not, requiring justification by argument and precedent
  • The payment of zakāt to Medina was affirmed as a binding obligation tied to membership in the community
  • The Muslim polity expanded its practical reach across Arabia and began to look beyond it
  • Former opponents were absorbed into the military and political structure

What Stayed the Same

  • Tribal and kinship structures remained the basis of social and military organization
  • Medina remained a small city with few formal institutions
  • Consultation among respected men, alliances, and pledges remained the means of governing
  • Regional leaders in Yemen, Oman, and the Gulf kept substantial local power

Why the pattern matters: Understanding the mix of rupture and continuity prevents two errors: imagining that Muhammad's death produced either an immediate fully formed caliphate or complete collapse.

Cause and Effect

Succession, Allegiance, and the Ridda

Long-term causes

Arabia's tribal autonomy, uneven integration under Muhammad, preexisting rivalries in Yemen and the Gulf, and the dependence of many agreements on personal ties.

Short-term causes

The death of Muhammad without an agreed succession procedure; the claims of rival prophets; the demand that zakāt continue to be paid to Medina.

Immediate effects

Recognition of Abū Bakr amid dissent; attacks on Medina; campaigns in Najd, Yamāma, and the periphery; heavy casualties and enslavement.

Long-term consequences

A precedent linking religious obligation and fiscal allegiance; a legal template for apostasy; the military mobilization that fed the conquests; continuing disputes over legitimate leadership.

Historical complexity: The Ridda was neither one movement nor inevitable; outcomes depended on local politics, commanders' choices, and the thin evidence we have.

Everyday Life and Social History

War, Obligation, and Household in 632–634

For most people, the events of these years appeared as changes in obligations and risks. A pastoral family in Najd might have faced the question of whether to send a share of its livestock to Medina's collector, a decision with consequences for relations with neighbors and chiefs. When armies passed, wells and pastures were requisitioned, animals taken, and men called to fight; where battles occurred, as at Yamāma, entire agricultural communities suffered the loss of crops, date groves, and adult men. Widows and orphans, among both Muslim and Ḥanīfa families, had to be supported by kin.

Captivity was a distinct experience. Women and children taken in the campaigns were treated as property, sold, or settled in households; the sources record the existence of captives but seldom their voices. Enslavement also shifted families' social positions and obligations. In Medina, the community's daily life continued around the mosque and the household of the caliph, whose simple style of living became a model in later memory. Because nearly all surviving sources were written by educated men of later generations, the experiences of herders, farmers, and women appear only indirectly, and historians must read the sources against the grain.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how the Muslim community responded to the death of Muhammad in June 632, how Abū Bakr emerged as its leader, and how the conflicts of 632–633 shaped the political order that would carry the conquests. The central argument is that the loss of Muhammad's personal and prophetic authority created a crisis over leadership and allegiance, and that its resolution involved improvised arrangements rather than settled institutions.

Sources for these events are late, written within communities with interests in the outcome, and sometimes contradictory. The Saqīfa accounts, the narratives of the Ridda, and the tradition about the Qurʾān all must be read critically, with attention to isnāds, genres, and the special problem of Sayf ibn ʿUmar.

At Medina, the Muhājirūn, Anṣār, Quraysh, and Muhammad's family had different interests, and no procedure for succession existed. At Saqīfa, an argument for Qurashī primacy, supported by ʿUmar and Abū Bakr and aided by the rivalry between Aws and Khazraj, led to the recognition of Abū Bakr. ʿAlī, Fāṭima, and some of the Banū Hāshim delayed or withheld allegiance, and disputes over Fadak and the meaning of Ghadīr Khumm planted the seeds of later Shiʿi and Sunni disagreements, though the doctrines themselves developed later.

Abū Bakr's office of khalīfat rasūl Allāh was a deputyship of a limited kind. He lacked revelation and relied on precedent, consultation, and the support of leading Companions. His decisions to send Usāma's expedition, to insist that zakāt remain obligatory, and to prosecute the wars with determination defined the early caliphate.

The Ridda, as the sources call it, was not one movement. Refusals of zakāt, rejections of Abū Bakr's leadership, rival prophetic claims, and local political rivalries combined in different proportions. In northern Najd, Ṭulayḥa led a coalition of Asad and others; among Tamīm, Mālik ibn Nuwayra's death raised questions about command and accountability; Sajāḥ and Musaylima represented rival religious leadership; and in Yamāma, Banū Ḥanīfa mounted the most formidable challenge.

Khālid ibn al-Walīd's campaigns defeated Ṭulayḥa at Buzākha and Musaylima at Yamāma, the latter at heavy cost. Beyond central Arabia, conflicts in Yemen, Ḥaḍramawt, Oman, Mahra, and al-Baḥrayn followed different logics, shaped by older rivalries involving the Persian-descended elite, local rulers, and coastal communities, and were resolved by smaller forces and negotiated settlements.

The consequences included the reintegration of most tribes, the pardoning and reuse of former opponents, the enslavement and displacement of many civilians, economic damage, and the establishment of the principle that payment of zakāt to the caliph was an obligation of membership. The tradition that Yamāma's casualties prompted the first collection of Qurʾānic material under Zayd ibn Thābit illustrates how memory linked battle and scripture.

When Abū Bakr died in August 634, he left a polity larger and more cohesive than in 632 but still dependent on personal authority, tribal contingents, and a small circle of Companions. ʿUmar's designation as successor set a precedent, and the first campaigns beyond Arabia opened the conquests examined in Chapter 39.

The chapter's historical significance lies in its demonstration that the early caliphate was made through contested, contingent decisions rather than from a fixed blueprint. The disputes of these two years — over who could lead, what allegiance required, and who counted as part of the community — remained live in the first civil war of Chapter 40 and in the doctrinal developments of Chapter 47.

Chapter Reflection

The two years between Muhammad's death and Abū Bakr's death are short, but they decided questions that shaped much of later Middle Eastern history. A community founded on prophecy had to find a way to continue without its prophet, and a polity formed through personal agreements had to determine whether those agreements survived. The answers given in these years — leadership by a deputy bound by scripture and precedent, a linkage between religious obligation and political allegiance, and the willingness to use force to maintain it — became foundations for the caliphate.

The chapter also shows how the writing of history shapes its meaning. The labels "apostasy," "rebel," and "liar" come from the victors' tradition, and the disputes over Saqīfa were later taken up by Sunni and Shiʿi communities as foundations of competing doctrines. A historian's task is to recover the variety of motives and experiences beneath those labels without denying the real religious convictions of those involved.

These events also connect to larger patterns across the region: the interaction between settled and pastoral societies, the role of frontier regions like Yemen and the Gulf that had been shaped by Persian and Aksumite influence, and the capacity of a small group of leaders to mobilize tribal contingents. The wars also prepared the military and social ground for the conquests of Chapter 39, in which many former opponents fought, and for the debates over legitimacy that erupted in Chapter 40.

Understanding this transition therefore requires holding together continuity and rupture. The caliphate of 634 was not the empire of the Umayyads, but it was no longer simply Muhammad's Medina. It was a fragile and improvised political order, tested by crisis and strengthened by war, whose later development the rest of this volume traces.
