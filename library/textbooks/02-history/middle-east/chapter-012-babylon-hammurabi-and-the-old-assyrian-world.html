Babylon, Hammurabi, and the Old Assyrian World

A History of the Middle East: From Ancient Civilizations to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

In the city of Assur, a woman named Lamassi sent a clay-tablet letter to her husband, Pushu-ken, more than a thousand kilometers away in the Assyrian merchant community at Kanesh in central Anatolia. Lamassi and other women of her household produced textiles for the family business and sent them north with the caravans that carried Assyrian goods into Anatolia. In one surviving letter, now catalogued as YPM BC 006791 / NBC 3816, she presses her husband for the silver proceeds from textiles already sent for sale and for the wool she needs to keep production going. The tablet was written at Assur but was recovered by archaeologists at Kültepe, ancient Kanesh, among the thousands of documents left behind by the Old Assyrian merchant community there.

That merchant community at Kanesh has yielded more private commercial and family documents than almost any other site of its period anywhere in the ancient Middle East, and it is one of two great archival worlds this chapter uses to reconstruct the early second millennium BCE. The other is the royal palace of Mari on the middle Euphrates, whose thousands of administrative and diplomatic tablets record the correspondence of kings, governors, and ambassadors negotiating alliances, tracking troop movements, exchanging gifts, and managing a political landscape that stretched from the Persian Gulf to the Mediterranean coast and into Anatolia. Between these two archives — one dominated by private merchant households, the other by royal diplomacy — stands the figure most readers will already associate with this period: Hammurabi of Babylon, whose famous stone monument of legal judgments has made him, for many students, a symbol of ancient law itself.

Chapter 11 left Mesopotamia in a state of transition. The centralized bureaucratic state of the Third Dynasty of Ur had collapsed under military pressure, administrative strain, and economic crisis, and Amorite-speaking populations, already present within Mesopotamian society for generations, had become prominent among the rulers of the successor states that emerged from that collapse. This chapter picks up that story directly, but it resists a temptation that later tradition, and Hammurabi's own inscriptions, encourage: the temptation to read the early second millennium BCE as a mere prelude to Babylonian greatness. For roughly two centuries after the fall of Ur, Babylon was one competitor among many — smaller and less consequential, for long stretches, than Isin, Larsa, Eshnunna, Mari, or the short-lived northern kingdom of Shamshi-Adad I. Hammurabi's eventual prominence, which arrived only in the second half of his own reign, was the product of a genuinely open and unpredictable political system, not the fulfillment of an obvious destiny.

This chapter's central claim, therefore, is that the early second millennium BCE Middle East — bounded loosely by southern Mesopotamia, the Diyala region, the middle Euphrates, Upper Mesopotamia, and central Anatolia — should be understood as a single interconnected but genuinely multipolar system. Kings corresponded with kings through professional diplomats. Merchant households in Ashur financed commercial expeditions into Anatolia without direct royal control over most of the transactions involved. Family property, marriage, and inheritance operated according to customary and written legal practice that existed both before and independently of any single ruler's proclamations. Understanding Hammurabi's Babylon, in other words, requires understanding the wider system he temporarily dominated, not treating that system as background scenery for his biography.

The chapter also confronts directly one of the most famous and most frequently misunderstood monuments to survive from the ancient Middle East: the tall dark-stone stele inscribed with the judgments that modern scholarship calls, with some care about the term, the Laws of Hammurabi. Later chapters in world history classrooms have sometimes presented this monument as the first law code in human history, a claim this textbook has already shown to be inaccurate in Chapter 11's discussion of earlier Mesopotamian legal traditions, and as a straightforward statute book that Old Babylonian judges consulted directly in court, a claim this chapter will show to be considerably more complicated. Examining the stele carefully, alongside actual Old Babylonian contracts, court records, and the Kanesh merchant archive, allows students to ask a more sophisticated question than "what did the laws say": namely, what kind of document this was, what work it was meant to perform, and how it related to the actual legal and commercial life documented elsewhere in the period's abundant written record.

Finally, this chapter closes Part III of the textbook. Chapters 9 through 11 traced three distinct Mesopotamian political forms: the competitive city-state world of Sumer, the short-lived territorial empire built by Sargon of Akkad, and the elaborate bureaucratic monarchy of the Ur III state. Chapter 12 introduces a fourth form, one defined less by any single institutional model than by intense interconnection itself — a competitive interstate and commercial system in which royal diplomacy, private commerce, and household legal practice operated simultaneously and in tension with one another, and within which Hammurabi's Babylon achieved a temporary, real, but ultimately unstable supremacy.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Describe the political fragmentation of Mesopotamia following the collapse of the Third Dynasty of Ur and identify the major competing kingdoms of the Isin-Larsa and early Old Babylonian periods.
  2. Explain that Babylon was a secondary regional center before the rise of its First Dynasty, and trace the gradual and historiographically debated emergence of that dynasty.
  3. Distinguish Old Assyrian commercial activity in the early second millennium BCE from the much later Neo-Assyrian territorial empire, and avoid projecting later Assyrian military institutions backward.
  4. Describe the karum trading settlements at Kanesh and other Anatolian sites, including the goods exchanged, the caravan system, and the private merchant families who organized them.
  5. Analyze Old Assyrian merchant archives as evidence for family firms, credit, contract, partnership, risk, and household life independent of direct state administration.
  6. Explain the political and diplomatic significance of Mari under Zimri-Lim, including its royal correspondence, intelligence networks, and alliance negotiations.
  7. Describe Shamshi-Adad I's temporary kingdom in Upper Mesopotamia and explain why its rapid rise and fall illustrates the instability of regional supremacy in this period.
  8. Explain Hammurabi's rise to regional dominance as the product of a shifting system of alliances and rivalries rather than a single continuous campaign of conquest.
  9. Describe Old Babylonian systems of agricultural administration, land tenure, taxation, and royal government, and connect them to the practical challenges of ruling newly conquered territory.
  10. Analyze Old Babylonian family, property, and inheritance practices using both everyday legal documents and the Laws of Hammurabi.
  11. Evaluate the Laws of Hammurabi as a historical document, distinguishing its structure, function, and royal ideology from the operation of a modern statutory legal code.
  12. Explain why Hammurabi's territorial dominance proved historically unstable and describe the eventual end of the Old Babylonian period.

  2. How did Old Assyrian merchant networks operate without direct royal administration, and what does this suggest about the relationship between states and commerce in the ancient Middle East?

Historical Context

Chapter 11 treated Ur III's collapse as multicausal: contraction of provincial control and resource flows, severe food-supply stress, political fragmentation and frontier tensions, and ultimately decisive Elamite/Simashkian military action. It rejected a simple Amorite-invasion explanation, emphasizing that people identified as Martu/Amorites were already integrated into Ur III society. That chapter also traced the gradual settlement and political integration of Amorite-speaking groups into Mesopotamian urban society, a process this chapter assumes rather than re-explains. Students should recall that Amorite background does not describe a unified ethnic bloc with a single political program; Amorite-associated dynasties ruled a wide range of competing Mesopotamian city-states in this period, often in conflict with one another, and Amorite identity operated primarily as a linguistic and social category rather than as a coherent political movement.

Out of Ur's collapse emerged a genuinely fragmented political landscape. Rival dynasties at Isin and Larsa in southern Mesopotamia competed for the prestige and ritual authority once associated with Ur and Uruk, sometimes described in modern scholarship as the Isin-Larsa period. Further north, along the Diyala River east of the Tigris, the kingdom of Eshnunna emerged as a significant regional power with its own scribal and legal traditions. Eshnunna also preserved an important Old Babylonian legal collection conventionally known as the Laws of Eshnunna, earlier than Hammurabi's monument and introduced as new material in this chapter. Babylon itself, a secondary city on the Euphrates with no particular claim to ancient prestige compared with Nippur, Ur, or Uruk, began to accumulate territory in central Mesopotamia under a dynasty of Amorite background beginning in the early second millennium BCE.

Simultaneously, but in a very different register, the northern Mesopotamian city of Ashur on the upper Tigris developed into the hub of an extensive private commercial network extending deep into central Anatolia. This Old Assyrian trading system, radically different in character from the much later Neo-Assyrian military empire examined in Chapter 19, depended on family merchant houses, donkey caravans, and a system of Old Assyrian trading settlements, the most thoroughly documented of which is Kanesh, modern Kültepe, near Kayseri in Turkey. Further west, on the middle Euphrates, the kingdom of Mari maintained close and often tense relationships with Mesopotamian and Syrian powers alike, functioning as a strategic node connecting southern Mesopotamia, the Syrian steppe, and the wider eastern Mediterranean world.

This chapter examines how these several worlds — southern Mesopotamian dynastic competition, Old Assyrian commerce, Mari's diplomacy, and a brief but significant northern Mesopotamian kingdom under Shamshi-Adad I — together produced the conditions within which Hammurabi of Babylon, reigning conventionally from around 1792 to 1750 BCE according to the widely used Middle Chronology, achieved a temporary but historically significant regional supremacy. The chapter's final sections examine his famous legal monument, the everyday social and legal life it partially reflects, and the limits of the political order he constructed, which did not survive intact for more than a few generations after his death.

Section 12.1

After Ur III: A Multipolar Mesopotamian World

The collapse of the Third Dynasty of Ur did not produce a power vacuum so much as a rapid redistribution of political authority among cities and dynasties that had previously operated as provinces within a single bureaucratic state. In southern Mesopotamia, the cities of Isin and Larsa emerged as the principal claimants to the prestige once concentrated at Ur, each dynasty at various points controlling Ur, Uruk, Nippur, and other ancient cult centers and each presenting itself, through royal inscriptions and year-names, as the legitimate heir to Sumerian kingship traditions stretching back through the material examined in Chapters 9 through 11. Modern scholars often label the roughly two centuries following Ur's fall the Isin-Larsa period in recognition of this southern Mesopotamian rivalry, though the label should not obscure the fact that many other kingdoms were simultaneously significant elsewhere in the region.

East of the Tigris, in the Diyala River region, the kingdom of Eshnunna developed into a considerable regional power under its own line of rulers, maintaining an important scribal and legal tradition — including the Laws of Eshnunna, an early collection of legal judgments predating Hammurabi's own monument and introduced in this chapter — and participating actively in the diplomatic and military maneuvering examined later in this chapter. Further north and west, the city of Mari on the middle Euphrates occupied a strategically vital position controlling trade and communication between southern Mesopotamia, the Syrian interior, and the eastern Mediterranean coast, while Ashur on the upper Tigris developed, in this same period, into the center of an extensive private commercial network extending into Anatolia, examined beginning in Section 12.3.

What unified this politically fragmented landscape was not shared government but constant contact. Royal inscriptions, treaties, and, above all, the diplomatic correspondence preserved at Mari reveal rulers from Elam in the east to Aleppo and the Mediterranean coast in the west exchanging messengers, negotiating marriages, forming military coalitions, and monitoring one another's activities on a near-continuous basis. This interconnection means that no single kingdom's history in this period can be understood in isolation. A conflict between Eshnunna and Larsa might draw in Babylon, Mari, and Elam as interested parties; a succession crisis in one kingdom might create an opportunity for expansion by a rival hundreds of miles away. Students should resist any narrative that treats this period as merely a waiting room before Hammurabi's arrival. For the better part of a century after Babylon's dynasty was founded, Isin, Larsa, and Eshnunna were, at various points, considerably more powerful and more prominent than Babylon itself.

Section 12.2

Babylon before Hammurabi

Babylon possessed no particular ancient religious or political prestige comparable to Nippur, the traditional cultic center of the god Enlil, or Ur and Uruk, the great cities of Sumerian tradition examined in Chapter 9. Before the early second millennium BCE, Babylon appears in the historical record as a secondary Mesopotamian city rather than a capital of regional significance. Later Babylonian king-list tradition places a ruler named Sumu-abum at the head of the First Dynasty of Babylon around 1894 BCE. This textbook presents that succession as a historiographical problem rather than a settled fact: one influential recent reconstruction, proposed by Rients de Boer, distinguishes Sumu-abum as a wider Amorite military and tribal leader whose activities were not confined to Babylon itself, and identifies Sumu-la-El, reigning conventionally from about 1880 to 1845 BCE, as the more likely founder of the specific dynastic line that later produced Hammurabi. This reconstruction has not become universal scholarly consensus, and students should understand that the earliest history of Babylon's ruling house remains actively debated among specialists rather than securely fixed by the later king lists that ancient scribes themselves compiled generations afterward.

For approximately a century after these beginnings, the First Dynasty of Babylon expanded cautiously, consolidating control over Babylon itself and a modest surrounding territory in central Mesopotamia rather than pursuing the kind of dramatic conquest later associated with Hammurabi. A sequence of rulers following Sumu-la-El — including Sabium, Apil-Sin, and Sin-muballit, Hammurabi's immediate predecessor and father — gradually strengthened Babylon's position through fortification, canal construction, temple building, and careful diplomacy with neighboring powers, but none of them achieved regional dominance. Sin-muballit, for example, fought campaigns against Larsa and other neighbors with mixed success, leaving Hammurabi, upon his accession around 1792 BCE, in control of a moderately strong but still regionally subordinate kingdom, surrounded by more powerful neighbors including Larsa to the south, Eshnunna to the northeast, and, for a time, the northern Mesopotamian kingdom of Shamshi-Adad I.

This gradual, multigenerational buildup matters historically because it corrects a common misconception: Hammurabi did not inherit an already-dominant Babylon and simply extend its power outward. He inherited a competitive middle-tier kingdom whose eventual supremacy over its neighbors was neither inherited nor guaranteed, and much of his own reign — roughly its first three decades — was spent consolidating this inherited position, building infrastructure, maintaining diplomatic relationships, and waiting for opportunities rather than pursuing continuous territorial expansion. The dramatic conquests for which Hammurabi is remembered occurred disproportionately late in his reign, especially during regnal years 30 through 38, a chronological fact this chapter returns to in Section 12.8.

Section 12.3

Ashur and the Old Assyrian Merchant World

While southern and central Mesopotamian dynasties competed for territorial dominance, the city of Ashur on the upper Tigris developed a strikingly different kind of regional power, one built on long-distance private commerce rather than territorial conquest. Modern scholars refer to this phase of Assyrian history as the Old Assyrian period, and it is essential that students distinguish it sharply from the Neo-Assyrian Empire examined many centuries later in Chapter 19. The Old Assyrian city-state of the early second millennium BCE possessed no standing imperial army comparable to later Assyrian military institutions, no extensive network of conquered provinces, and no ideology of universal military dominance. Its influence instead radiated outward through commercial networks organized by private family firms operating with considerable independence from direct royal control.

Ashur's political structure in this period combined a hereditary ruling office with significant participation by a city assembly and by the leading merchant families whose commercial activities generated much of the city's wealth. The ruler of Ashur in this period is often described in scholarship as holding a position closer to a chief magistrate or overseer of the city's commercial and religious affairs than to a later Mesopotamian king exercising unchecked personal authority, though the precise balance of royal and civic power remains a subject of ongoing scholarly discussion given the limitations of the surviving evidence, which derives disproportionately from private commercial archives rather than from royal administrative records.

The wealth generated by Ashur's merchant families derived from a specific and lucrative trade: the export of tin and textiles into central Anatolia, in exchange for Anatolian silver and gold, which merchants then carried back to Ashur to repay loans, satisfy investors, and fund further commercial expeditions. Tin, essential for bronze production, reached Assur through long-distance eastern trade networks whose ultimate sources remain debated before being carried onward to Anatolia, where it commanded a substantial price differential, as did finely woven Mesopotamian textiles, while Anatolian silver was correspondingly valuable when carried south. This price differential, sustained over generations by the practical difficulty and expense of the journey itself, created the economic foundation for an extensive and well-documented commercial network examined in the following sections.

Section 12.4

Kanesh and the Anatolian Trade Colonies

The most thoroughly documented node in the Old Assyrian commercial network is Kanesh, identified with the modern archaeological site of Kültepe near Kayseri in central Turkey. Excavations at Kültepe have recovered more than twenty thousand cuneiform tablets, the overwhelming majority of them private commercial and family documents rather than royal inscriptions, making Kanesh one of the richest sources anywhere in the ancient Middle East for reconstructing everyday economic and household life rather than royal ideology.

Assyrian merchants at Kanesh and similar sites organized their settlements according to a distinctive institutional pattern. The main Assyrian merchant quarter at a large center such as Kanesh was called a karum, a term that literally referred to a quay or harbor district but came to designate an organized Assyrian merchant quarter and community with its own corporate institutions and legal jurisdiction, operating within an independent Anatolian polity, while smaller subordinate trading settlements were called wabartum. These communities operated under a body of commercial law and custom recognized by both the Assyrian merchant community and the local Anatolian rulers who permitted and taxed their activity, illustrating that Old Assyrian trade depended on negotiated arrangements with independent Anatolian city-states rather than on Assyrian territorial control. The karum system was not maintained by an Assyrian occupation army or territorial provincial government; it was a self-governing merchant community operating by treaty and custom within an Anatolian political landscape it did not rule.

Old Assyrian long-distance trade relied heavily on donkey caravans, which were particularly well suited to moving relatively high-value cargoes over the roughly one-thousand-kilometer route between Assur and central Anatolia. Caravan records preserved at Kanesh itemize the goods carried — tin, textiles, and, on the return journey, silver and gold — alongside the practical expenses of the journey: donkeys, drivers, tolls paid to local rulers along the route, and fees for guides through difficult terrain. A single well-documented caravan account, for example, itemizes silver invested in tin and textiles alongside the costs of hired transport, donkey purchase, and road tolls, giving historians an unusually granular view of the actual logistics and expense structure underlying long-distance ancient trade, rather than the more abstract references to tribute and gift exchange that dominate many royal inscriptions from other periods examined elsewhere in this textbook.

Section 12.5

Merchants, Households, Credit, and Contract

The Kanesh archive allows historians to move beyond the mechanics of trade routes into the human and institutional fabric of the merchant families who conducted this commerce. Old Assyrian trade was organized predominantly through family firms, often involving a senior merchant based in Ashur who financed and directed operations, sons or junior partners who traveled to and resided in Anatolia to manage the karum end of the business, and wives and other household members who remained in Ashur managing property, producing textiles for export, and handling correspondence and family affairs during years-long separations.

These firms operated through sophisticated financial instruments. Investors, sometimes numbering a dozen or more individuals pooling resources into a single commercial venture, entered into partnership agreements specifying the division of eventual profits, often calculated over multi-year terms. Loans carried specified interest rates and repayment terms, sometimes secured against future caravan proceeds. Disputes over unpaid debts, damaged or substituted goods, and disputed partnership shares generated lawsuits heard before merchant assemblies at Kanesh or, in more serious cases, before authorities in Ashur itself, and the surviving legal documents show merchants invoking prior contracts, witness testimony, and established commercial custom in pursuing these claims.

Family correspondence preserved in this same archive humanizes the commercial system in ways few other ancient sources allow. Letters exchanged between merchants abroad and their wives in Ashur discuss textile production quotas, complaints about inadequate wool supplies, negotiations over household expenses, anxieties about long absences, arrangements for children's upbringing and eventual marriage, and, in some cases, evident marital tension arising from years of physical separation combined with financial pressure. Women in these merchant households were not passive dependents; many managed substantial household economic operations independently, produced the textiles that formed a major share of the goods carried to Anatolia, held and disposed of property in their own names, and, in some documented cases, initiated legal action to protect their economic interests. This evidence provides one of this textbook's clearest windows onto non-royal household and commercial life, drawn chiefly from prosperous merchant families rather than the full social spectrum of Old Assyrian society, deliberately contrasted here with the centralized state archive of Ur III examined in Chapter 11.

Historical Vocabulary
Karum and the Old Assyrian Trading World

The term karum originally meant a quay or riverbank landing area but came, in the Old Assyrian period, to designate an organized Assyrian merchant quarter and community, with its own corporate institutions and legal jurisdiction, operating within an independent foreign city, most famously at Kanesh. A karum functioned as a self-governing commercial community with its own assembly, officials, and recognized body of commercial law, operating under negotiated arrangements with the local ruling authority rather than under Assyrian political control. Smaller, subordinate trading settlements along the caravan routes were termed wabartum.

Students should take particular care not to confuse this Old Assyrian commercial system with the much later Neo-Assyrian Empire examined in Chapter 19, which was a militarized territorial empire built on conquest, deportation, and provincial administration many centuries afterward. The Old Assyrian karum system was not maintained by an Assyrian occupation army or a territorial provincial government, and it involved no Assyrian governors ruling Anatolian territory and no ideology of universal conquest; it was fundamentally a commercial and legal arrangement between an Assyrian merchant community and independent Anatolian political authorities, sustained by mutual economic benefit rather than by military coercion.

Section 12.6

Mari and the Politics of the Middle Euphrates

Mari, located on the middle Euphrates near the border of modern Syria and Iraq, occupied a position of exceptional strategic importance in this period, controlling trade and communication routes linking southern Mesopotamia, the Syrian interior, the Mediterranean coast, and, indirectly, Anatolia. Excavations at Mari's royal palace recovered one of the richest administrative and diplomatic archives surviving from the entire ancient Middle East, comprising more than twenty thousand tablets that document not merely royal decrees but the day-to-day operation of interstate diplomacy in unusual and vivid detail.

The archive's most historically significant portion dates to the reign of Zimri-Lim, who ruled Mari for about fifteen years before Hammurabi's conquest of the city. Zimri-Lim's correspondence reveals a world of professional ambassadors traveling between courts, intelligence reports assessing the military strength and political intentions of neighboring rulers, negotiations over marriage alliances between royal families, elaborate gift exchange used to cement diplomatic relationships, and detailed logistical coordination of troop movements when military cooperation was required. Letters from Mari's ambassadors at other courts, including at Babylon itself, describe court protocol, rumors, and shifting political calculations with a specificity that allows historians to reconstruct the texture of ancient diplomacy far more concretely than most royal inscriptions permit.

Mari should not be understood merely as "the city Hammurabi eventually destroyed." For most of the period covered by its surviving archive, Mari operated as an independent and diplomatically active kingdom in its own right, allied at various points with Babylon against common rivals, and Zimri-Lim's correspondence documents a ruler actively managing relationships with Babylon, Aleppo, Eshnunna, and numerous smaller polities across the Syrian steppe. The eventual destruction of Mari by Hammurabi, examined in Section 12.9, represents the endpoint of this diplomatic history rather than its defining feature, and treating Mari primarily as Hammurabi's eventual victim risks obscuring the richness of the evidence its archive provides for the mechanics of ancient interstate politics.

Section 12.7

Shamshi-Adad I and the Contest for Upper Mesopotamia

Before Zimri-Lim's reign at Mari, a very different kind of political formation had briefly dominated Upper Mesopotamia: the personal kingdom constructed by Shamshi-Adad I, a ruler whose exact origins remain debated but who established control over Ashur, much of the upper Tigris and Khabur regions, and, for a period, Mari itself, installing his son Yasmah-Addu there as a subordinate ruler while placing another son, Ishme-Dagan, at Ekallatum on the Tigris. Shamshi-Adad's kingdom, sometimes referred to in modern scholarship as the kingdom of Upper Mesopotamia, represented a significant departure from the earlier commercial character of the Old Assyrian world examined in Section 12.3, since it involved direct territorial conquest and dynastic administration rather than negotiated commercial arrangements.

Shamshi-Adad's correspondence, much of it preserved in the Mari archive because his son governed the city, reveals an energetic and demanding ruler closely monitoring the administration of his sons' territories, offering detailed and often critical advice on military and administrative matters, and evidently anxious about his sons' competence relative to his own. This correspondence provides valuable evidence for how a rapidly assembled territorial kingdom, dependent heavily on the personal authority and military reputation of its founder, attempted to function through delegated family rule rather than through the kind of institutionalized bureaucracy examined in the Ur III state in Chapter 11.

The fragility of this arrangement became apparent immediately upon Shamshi-Adad's death, conventionally dated to around 1776 BCE. Without the personal authority that had held the kingdom together, Yasmah-Addu was quickly overthrown at Mari, and Zimri-Lim, a member of Mari's earlier royal line who had spent the intervening years in exile, returned to reclaim the throne. Shamshi-Adad's broader kingdom of Upper Mesopotamia likewise fragmented rapidly, with Ishme-Dagan struggling to maintain even his own reduced territory around Ekallatum. This rapid collapse is historically significant precisely because it illustrates, in miniature, the pattern that this chapter argues characterizes the entire early second millennium BCE: military and territorial supremacy in this period was frequently personal, dependent on the individual ruler who assembled it, and vulnerable to rapid unraveling upon that ruler's death, a pattern students should keep in mind when they encounter Hammurabi's own conquests in the sections that follow.

Section 12.8

Hammurabi in a World of Alliances

Hammurabi's reign, conventionally dated to approximately 1792 to 1750 BCE under the Middle Chronology commonly used in this textbook, should be understood in two distinct phases. For roughly its first three decades, Hammurabi ruled a moderately strong but regionally subordinate kingdom, engaging in defensive warfare, careful diplomacy, and infrastructural development rather than sustained territorial expansion. Mari's diplomatic archive, examined in Section 12.6, documents Babylon during this period as one significant power among several, allied at various points with Mari against Elam and against other rivals, but by no means the dominant force in the region.

The decisive transformation began late in Hammurabi's reign, especially during regnal years 30 through 38, and the year names by which Babylonian scribes dated administrative documents allow historians to reconstruct its sequence with more precision, and more complexity, than a simple south-to-west-to-northeast narrative would suggest. In his thirtieth regnal year, Hammurabi claims victory over a coalition led by Elam that included forces from Eshnunna and other allies. In his thirty-first year he turned against his longtime rival Rim-Sin of Larsa, whose extraordinarily long reign — Rim-Sin had ruled Larsa for well over five decades by this point — had made his kingdom the dominant power in southern Mesopotamia; Hammurabi's defeat and conquest of Larsa brought the whole of southern Mesopotamia, including the ancient cult cities of Ur, Uruk, and Nippur, under Babylonian control for the first time. Hammurabi's attack on Larsa emerged from changing alliances and military circumstances late in his reign; surviving evidence does not establish a single long-planned strategy or a securely identifiable private motive.

The campaigns that followed complicate any tidy sequence. Hammurabi's year names record a further victory involving Eshnunna, Subartu, and Gutium in his thirty-second year, before he turned against his former ally Mari in his thirty-third year, defeating Mari and the nearby kingdom of Malgium; a separate year name associated with his thirty-fifth year records the destruction of Mari's walls, some years after the city's initial defeat. Still later year names, including one from his thirty-eighth year, record further conflict involving Eshnunna. Converting these regnal years into absolute BCE dates depends on the chronological system used, and this textbook does not present more precise absolute dates than the evidence supports. What the year-name sequence makes clear is that Eshnunna was not simply Hammurabi's final campaign after Mari; it appears as an opponent, and in some years apparently as an ally against a common threat, both before and after the destruction of Mari. Students should resist the common simplification that presents these campaigns as a single continuous forty-year war of conquest, or as a clean progression from Larsa to Mari to Eshnunna. They represent, instead, a genuinely tangled sequence of calculated moves against former allies and rivals within a fluid balance-of-power system, executed disproportionately during the concentrated late-reign campaigns of years 30 through 38, following three earlier decades that had largely consolidated rather than expanded Babylonian power.

Section 12.9

Conquest, Administration, Agriculture, and the Babylonian Kingdom

Hammurabi's rapid territorial expansion during his concentrated late-reign campaigns created an immediate and practical administrative challenge: governing newly conquered cities and regions, each with its own local elites, temple institutions, and administrative traditions, under a coherent Babylonian framework. Royal correspondence from this period, along with administrative and legal documents from conquered cities such as Larsa, reveals Hammurabi's government working to integrate these territories through a combination of appointed royal officials, continued reliance on local administrative personnel and institutions where practical, and direct royal correspondence addressing specific local disputes, tax assessments, and irrigation matters.

Agriculture remained the foundation of the kingdom's revenue and administrative concern, as it had been for every Mesopotamian state examined in this Part. Royal letters preserved from Hammurabi's reign address canal maintenance and dredging, water allocation disputes between communities, the timing of irrigation relative to the flood cycle discussed in Chapter 2, and the assessment of agricultural dues owed to the crown or to temple estates. Landholding in Old Babylonian society existed along a spectrum ranging from land directly administered by the crown or by major temple institutions, to land held by intermediary officials and soldiers in exchange for service obligations, to privately owned family land bought, sold, leased, and inherited according to documented legal procedures examined further in Section 12.10.

A distinctive feature of Old Babylonian administration was ilku, a term referring broadly to service obligations owed to the crown that could be associated with service land or other institutional arrangements. The precise form of the obligation varied and should not be reduced to one uniform system in which every ilku holder simply received a landholding in exchange for fixed military or labor service; surviving documents show considerable local and individual variation in how the obligation was structured and discharged. Even so, the institution as a whole allowed the state to draw on military manpower and labor for public works without necessarily supporting a permanent salaried bureaucracy on the scale of the Ur III state examined in Chapter 11. Royal officials known by titles such as the shakkanakku, a provincial governor or military commander, and various classes of scribes, tax collectors, and judges staffed the practical apparatus of Hammurabi's expanded kingdom, though the degree of centralized uniformity across such a rapidly assembled territory should not be overstated; considerable local variation in administrative practice persisted, particularly in recently conquered southern cities that retained strong independent institutional traditions of their own.

Section 12.10

Family, Property, Status, and Everyday Law

Alongside the political narrative of conquest and administration, the Old Babylonian period offers an unusually rich body of evidence for everyday family and economic life, drawn from thousands of surviving contracts, court records, letters, and administrative documents produced independently of the famous royal monument examined in the next section. Marriage in Old Babylonian society was typically arranged through negotiation between families and formalized through written contracts. A dowry originated in the bride's family and constituted property associated with her marriage and future support; surviving contracts and legal collections preserve important rights concerning its return and inheritance, although practical control and disposition varied by circumstance. In some cases a bride price was also paid by the groom's family, though practices varied by region, period, and social status.

Inheritance practices show sons generally dividing a father's estate, with documented provisions for favoring an eldest son in certain contexts, for legitimizing children born to secondary wives or enslaved women under specified conditions, and for adopted children to inherit on terms established at the time of adoption. Women could, in documented cases, own, buy, sell, and bequeath property independently, particularly widows managing a deceased husband's estate on behalf of minor children. At Sippar, the especially well-documented naditum women of Shamash did not bear children and frequently managed substantial property and commercial affairs, engaging in transactions and appearing frequently as parties to surviving legal documents. Naditum institutions varied among cities, so the Sippar evidence should not automatically be generalized to Babylon, Nippur, or every Old Babylonian community.

Old Babylonian society is often described using a threefold status terminology found in both everyday documents and the Laws of Hammurabi: the awilum, the muskenum, and the wardum. Students should approach this terminology with considerable caution rather than treating it as a simple three-tier class system directly translatable into modern categories of "upper class," "middle class," and "slave." The precise legal and social meaning of awilum and muskenum remains debated among specialists; awilum appears to designate a person of full legal status, often but not necessarily of higher social standing, while muskenum designated a person of more restricted legal status whose precise relationship to royal service, dependency, or property holding is not fully settled in current scholarship. The wardum, most straightforwardly rendered as an enslaved person, occupied a distinct and clearly subordinate legal category, though even here, documented evidence shows enslaved individuals in Old Babylonian society possessing limited legal capacities, including the ability to hold some property and, in certain circumstances, to purchase their own freedom, that complicate any simple assumption about the uniform character of ancient slavery across different times and places.

Everyday Life and Social History

Households, Work, and Daily Life in the Old Babylonian World

Everyday Old Babylonian life, as reconstructed from tens of thousands of surviving administrative and private documents, centered on the household as the basic economic and social unit. A typical household combined agricultural labor on family or leased land, craft production including the textile weaving so central to the Old Assyrian trade examined in Section 12.5, and, for households connected to temple or palace institutions, service obligations tied to landholding under the ilku system discussed in Section 12.9. Children were frequently apprenticed to learn a trade. Scribal education took place in household, professional, and other instructional settings, producing the scribes whose exercises and documents preserve much of the period's written culture.

Diet centered on barley bread and beer, supplemented by dates, onions, fish, and, less frequently for most households, meat, while clothing was predominantly woven wool, the same commodity that anchored much of the era's long-distance commerce. Religious practice combined household worship of personal or family deities with participation in the major temple cults of cities such as Babylon, Nippur, Ur, and Sippar, and family archives sometimes preserve records of individuals seeking oracular guidance or making votive offerings in connection with illness, business ventures, or family disputes. Because most surviving documents were produced or preserved by literate households with sufficient property to generate legal records, historians must read this evidence carefully for what it reveals, and fails to reveal, about the larger population of agricultural laborers, tenant farmers, and dependent workers whose lives generated comparatively few surviving written traces of their own.

Section 12.11

The Laws of Hammurabi: Justice, Kingship, and Social Order

Late in his reign, around 1755 to 1750 BCE, Hammurabi commissioned a tall, dark stone stele, inscribed with a lengthy royal prologue, a collection of roughly 282 legal judgments, and a lengthy epilogue. The stone itself has been the subject of long-running scientific disagreement: the Louvre, which holds the monument, currently catalogues it as basalt, while much earlier and some ongoing scholarship has described it as diorite, and a recent scientific review concludes that the basalt-versus-diorite question remains unresolved. The epilogue states that Hammurabi set up a stele of his judgments before his own image in Esagila, Marduk's temple at Babylon, where it could be seen by the god and, presumably, by petitioners and officials. Whether the surviving stele recovered at Susa is that particular monument is not certain; other copies or versions of the text are known to have existed, and some scholars have proposed other original locations for the specific stele now in the Louvre. The monument, wherever it originally stood, later traveled a long distance from its setting: it was carried off as war booty by Elamite forces centuries after Hammurabi's death and was ultimately discovered by archaeologists at Susa in Iran in 1901 and 1902, where it had been re-erected in antiquity, missing several columns of text that had apparently been deliberately erased, likely for reuse or reinscription by the Elamite king who had taken it.

The document's structure repays careful attention. The prologue presents Hammurabi as a king chosen and empowered by the gods Anu and Enlil, and specifically commissioned by Marduk, to establish justice in the land, protect the weak from the strong, and ensure the proper worship of the gods in each of the cities whose local temples and traditions the prologue lists at considerable length — a list that itself provides valuable evidence for the extent of Hammurabi's kingdom and his effort to present his rule as legitimately encompassing each city's particular religious traditions rather than imposing a uniform Babylonian identity upon them. The epilogue similarly emphasizes Hammurabi's role as a shepherd of his people and a source of justice, invoking elaborate curses upon any future ruler who might deface the monument or ignore its provisions, and blessings upon those who respect it.

Between prologue and epilogue lies the collection of legal judgments itself, organized in a distinctive style scholars call casuistic: each provision is framed as a conditional case, typically beginning "if a man..." and specifying a particular circumstance followed by its legal consequence, rather than as an abstract general rule. The provisions address an extraordinarily wide range of subjects: false accusation and perjury, theft and receiving stolen goods, military service and the obligations attached to land grants, agricultural tenancy and irrigation disputes, commercial partnerships and loans at interest, marriage, divorce, and inheritance, professional liability including the famous and severe provisions concerning a surgeon whose patient dies or a builder whose house collapses, and personal injury, where the text applies varying penalties, including in some cases the principle of proportional retaliation often called by the Latin phrase lex talionis, "an eye for an eye," though the actual provisions apply this principle selectively and, in cases involving parties of differing social status, apply markedly different penalties for what modern readers would consider comparable injuries.

Historians today, following recent curatorial and scholarly reassessment including that of the Louvre, which holds the monument, generally describe the Laws of Hammurabi as a work of jurisprudence — a royal collection of judicial precedents and ideals presented to demonstrate the king's commitment to justice — rather than as a legal code in the modern statutory sense that judges directly applied. This distinction matters enormously for how students should interpret the document. Surviving Old Babylonian court records do not normally cite the Hammurabi collection explicitly as an authoritative statute, and documented judicial outcomes do not always match the cases preserved on the stele, suggesting that local custom, prior precedent, and judicial discretion continued to govern actual legal practice alongside, rather than strictly beneath, the monument's more symbolic and ideological function as a statement of royal justice.

Historical Vocabulary
Casuistic Law and Royal Jurisprudence

Casuistic law refers to legal reasoning organized around specific hypothetical cases — "if a man does X, then Y shall happen" — as opposed to apodictic law, which states general moral or legal commands without reference to particular circumstances, such as "you shall not steal." The Laws of Hammurabi is written almost entirely in casuistic form, a style shared with the earlier Sumerian and Eshnunna legal collections examined in Chapter 11, suggesting a long-standing Mesopotamian scribal and legal tradition rather than an innovation unique to Hammurabi.

Scholars increasingly describe such monuments as works of royal jurisprudence — collections of judicial wisdom and precedent compiled to demonstrate a king's commitment to justice — rather than as codes in the modern sense of comprehensive, exclusively binding statutory law that a court is obligated to apply directly. The collection may also have had jurisprudential or didactic functions, but its precise relationship to everyday judicial procedure remains debated. This distinction does not mean the Hammurabi monument was purely symbolic or without any practical legal influence; rather, it means its relationship to actual courtroom practice was almost certainly more complex and more mediated by custom, precedent, and judicial discretion than a modern statute book.

Paraphrase, Laws of Hammurabi (case concerning a builder): The text specifies that if a builder constructs a house that later collapses and causes the death of the house's owner, the builder himself shall be put to death; if the collapse instead causes the death of the owner's son, then the builder's son shall be put to death — a provision illustrating both the casuistic case-based structure of the collection and its application, in some provisions, of penalties calculated by analogy to the victim rather than directly to the responsible party.
Paraphrase, letter from Lamassi to Pushu-ken: Lamassi, writing from Assur, presses her husband for the silver proceeds she believes are owed to her from textiles she had already sent to him for sale, and asks him to send wool or resolve the outstanding obligation, reflecting her ongoing role in producing and marketing the textiles that financed the family's Anatolian trade.

Analysis: Read together, these sources illustrate the central interpretive problem of this section: the Hammurabi monument presents an idealized, royally sponsored vision of justice addressed to the gods and to posterity, expressed through severe and sometimes startling hypothetical cases, while Lamassi's letter reflects the actual, unglamorous texture of contractual and family obligation as lived by a commercial household operating largely outside direct royal oversight. Neither source alone provides a complete picture of Old Babylonian-period legal and economic life; historians must read monumental and documentary evidence together and remain alert to the different purposes each type of source was created to serve.

Section 12.12

The Limits of Hammurabi's Achievement and the End of Part III

Hammurabi died around 1750 BCE and was succeeded by his son Samsu-iluna, who inherited a kingdom considerably larger than the one Hammurabi himself had inherited from his father but one whose recently conquered territories had not had time to develop deep institutional integration with Babylon. Within a few years of his accession, Samsu-iluna faced serious revolts in the recently conquered south, including a significant uprising associated with a rebel figure remembered in later tradition as Rim-Sin II, apparently invoking the memory of the former Larsa dynasty Hammurabi had overthrown. Although Samsu-iluna suppressed these revolts, southern Mesopotamia in subsequent decades experienced gradual political and economic contraction, including the apparent abandonment or decline of several ancient southern cities, a development some historians have connected to long-term environmental pressures, including salinization patterns building on trends already discussed in Chapter 2's treatment of Mesopotamian hydrology, though the precise causes of this southern decline remain debated and were almost certainly multiple rather than singular.

Over the following century and a half, the First Dynasty of Babylon retained control of a reduced kingdom centered on central Mesopotamia, but the vast territorial reach Hammurabi had briefly achieved was not sustained by his successors. The Old Babylonian period conventionally ends around 1595 BCE, when a Hittite king, Mursili I, launched a long-distance raid down the Euphrates and sacked Babylon itself before withdrawing — an episode examined in relation to the wider Hittite state in Chapter 15. The Hittite sack conventionally marks the end of Babylon's First Dynasty. The political transition that followed is poorly documented. The First Sealand Dynasty already controlled much of the deep south, while rulers bearing Kassite names, a population whose origins and language remain incompletely understood, subsequently established power around Babylon and eventually incorporated the southern Sealand territories, a process examined further in later chapters concerning the Late Bronze Age international system.

This chapter closes, then, not with a triumphant image of permanent Babylonian empire but with a more historically accurate and more analytically useful conclusion: Hammurabi's achievement was real and consequential — he permanently elevated Babylon's status as a Mesopotamian political and cultic center, and later Mesopotamian kings for well over a thousand years would continue to treat control of Babylon as a marker of legitimate regional kingship — but the specific territorial unification he constructed late in his reign proved no more durable, in the end, than the northern Mesopotamian kingdom Shamshi-Adad I had assembled a generation earlier. Personal military and diplomatic achievement, however impressive, did not automatically translate into permanent institutional integration across a large and recently conquered territory, a lesson this textbook will find repeated, in different forms, across the empires examined in every subsequent volume.

With this chapter, Part III of the textbook comes to a close. Chapters 9 through 12 have traced four distinct Mesopotamian political forms operating across roughly a millennium and a half: the competitive city-state world of early Sumer, examined in Chapter 9; the short-lived but historically pioneering territorial empire constructed by Sargon of Akkad, examined in Chapter 10; the elaborate bureaucratic monarchy of the Ur III state, examined in Chapter 11; and, in this chapter, a competitive interstate and commercial system encompassing royal diplomacy at Mari, private commerce at Ashur and Kanesh, and temporary Babylonian territorial supremacy under Hammurabi. Part IV now turns outward from Mesopotamia to examine Egypt, Anatolia, and the Levant, and the broader Bronze Age international system these regions, together with Mesopotamia, constructed across the middle centuries of the second millennium BCE.

Historical Debate

Was the Laws of Hammurabi a Functioning Legal Code?

The question: Did the Laws of Hammurabi function as an operative legal code that Old Babylonian judges directly applied in court, or was it primarily a work of royal ideology and jurisprudential ideal whose relationship to actual legal practice was more indirect?

Interpretation A — The monument reflects genuine and influential legal practice: Some historians emphasize that the collection's provisions correspond closely, in subject matter and often in substance, to categories addressed in actual Old Babylonian contracts and court records — marriage, inheritance, commercial partnership, agricultural tenancy, and personal injury — and argue that even if surviving judges did not cite the Hammurabi collection explicitly as an authoritative text, its content likely reflected and reinforced widely shared legal norms that did directly shape everyday judicial outcomes across Hammurabi's kingdom.
Interpretation B — The monument is primarily ideological and only indirectly practical: Other historians, drawing on the fact that surviving Old Babylonian court records do not normally cite the Hammurabi collection explicitly as an authoritative statute and on documented cases in which actual legal outcomes diverge from the stele's provisions, argue that the collection functioned chiefly as a public statement of royal justice addressed to the gods and to posterity — closer to a monument of jurisprudential ideals, similar to the earlier Sumerian and Eshnunna collections examined in this chapter, than to a binding statute book that structured actual courtroom procedure.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the same body of evidence — the stele's own text, the thousands of surviving Old Babylonian contracts and court records, and comparison with earlier Mesopotamian legal collections — but weigh the significance of the absence of direct citation differently, and disagree about how much practical judicial authority a document could carry without being formally cited.

Why it matters: This textbook, consistent with recent curatorial and scholarly reassessment, treats the Laws of Hammurabi as a work of royal jurisprudence whose practical relationship to courtroom practice was real but mediated, rather than as a modern-style comprehensive and exclusively binding code — a position that should shape how students read every subsequent ancient legal document encountered in this textbook.

Student task: Using the evidence described above, construct the strongest possible version of each interpretation, and then explain which position you find more persuasive and why, being explicit about what kind of additional evidence might strengthen or weaken your conclusion.

Multiple Perspectives

Living in the Old Babylonian World

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns historians have identified across many kinds of evidence. They are not quotations from any single named historical actor.

From the perspective of a tenant farmer on royal or temple land near Babylon: Daily life is organized around canal maintenance schedules, planting and harvest labor, and the agricultural dues owed under the terms of one's tenancy, with concerns focused on water allocation disputes with neighboring communities and the burden of ilku service obligations attached to certain landholdings.
From the perspective of a woman managing property in Sippar, possibly of naditum status: Economic life involves actively buying, leasing, and managing land and other property, entering into loan agreements, and, when necessary, pursuing legal claims to protect one's interests, in a social role that does not fit neatly into simple assumptions about the economic dependency of women in ancient Mesopotamian society.
From the perspective of an Assyrian merchant's wife in Ashur: Household management includes overseeing textile production for shipment to Anatolia, managing family finances and property during a husband's multi-year absences, and conducting an ongoing, sometimes contentious correspondence over quotas, quality, and money that reveals both economic partnership and real domestic strain.
From the perspective of a scribe or diplomat at the court of Mari: Professional life involves drafting correspondence, managing intelligence about rival courts, and helping coordinate marriage alliances, gift exchange, and troop movements within a diplomatic system whose stability depends on continuous negotiation rather than fixed treaties alone.
From the perspective of a resident of a recently conquered southern city such as Larsa after Hammurabi's victory: Political life involves adjusting to new royal officials and altered tax and administrative arrangements imposed from Babylon, while continuing to rely, wherever possible, on familiar local institutions, temple administration, and legal custom that predate the conquest.
Continuity and Change Over Time

From Ur III to Old Babylonian Order

What Changed

  • Political authority shifted from a single centralized bureaucratic state under Ur to a multipolar system of competing kingdoms, and eventually to Hammurabi's temporary regional supremacy
  • Long-distance commerce came to be organized substantially through private Assyrian family firms operating semi-independently of direct royal administration, rather than through state-directed trade expeditions comparable to those documented under Ur III
  • Royal legal monuments shifted in emphasis, with Hammurabi's collection presenting an especially elaborate ideological prologue and epilogue framing the king's relationship to the gods and to justice
  • Territorial control over southern Mesopotamian cities such as Ur, Uruk, and Nippur passed among several successive dynasties within a relatively short span of generations

What Stayed the Same

  • Agricultural administration, canal maintenance, and irrigation-based land tenure remained the fundamental economic basis of every Mesopotamian state examined in this Part, regardless of its political form
  • Casuistic legal reasoning, expressed in conditional case form, continued as a shared Mesopotamian scribal tradition connecting Ur III-era law, the Eshnunna collection, and Hammurabi's monument
  • Cities such as Nippur retained their religious prestige as cult centers even as political dominance shifted repeatedly among Ur, Isin, Larsa, and Babylon
  • Family, marriage, and inheritance practices documented in everyday contracts show substantial continuity across the political transitions this chapter examines, reflecting the deep persistence of household-level social and economic custom beneath shifting dynastic politics

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both the dramatic political changes and the underlying institutional and social continuities of this period helps students understand that the same Mesopotamian society could produce radically different political superstructures — city-states, territorial empire, bureaucratic monarchy, and competitive interstate systems — while its agricultural, legal, and household foundations changed far more gradually.

Cause and Effect

How a Competitive System Produced Temporary Supremacy

Long-term causes

The fragmentation following Ur III's collapse created a genuinely multipolar Mesopotamian system in which no single kingdom initially possessed overwhelming advantage, allowing Babylon's dynasty a century of gradual, low-profile accumulation of territory and resources.

Short-term causes

Changing military and diplomatic circumstances surrounding Hammurabi's conflict with Rim-Sin's long-dominant kingdom of Larsa, combined with Hammurabi's calculated decisions to abandon his alliance with Mari and to move against Eshnunna at various points, created a specific sequence of opportunities during the concentrated late-reign campaigns of Hammurabi's reign.

Immediate effects

Babylon briefly controlled the whole of southern and much of central and northern Mesopotamia, absorbing the ancient cult cities of the south and ending Mari's independent political existence along with the active accumulation of its diplomatic archive.

Longer-term consequences

Newly conquered territories lacked deep institutional integration with Babylon, contributing to the revolts and gradual southern contraction under Samsu-iluna and to the eventual limitation of the First Dynasty of Babylon to a reduced central Mesopotamian kingdom well before the dynasty's final end in 1595 BCE.

Historical complexity: Hammurabi's supremacy resulted from a specific, contingent sequence of alliance decisions within a competitive system rather than from any structural inevitability, and its limited durability after his death illustrates that swift territorial conquest and lasting institutional integration are historically distinct achievements, a distinction this textbook will revisit when examining later Mesopotamian, Egyptian, and Near Eastern empires.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the early second millennium BCE Mesopotamian and Anatolian world as a genuinely multipolar and intensely interconnected system, resisting any narrative that treats the period merely as background to Hammurabi's eventual rise. Section 12.1 established that the collapse of the Third Dynasty of Ur, examined in Chapter 11, produced not a power vacuum but a competitive landscape of rival kingdoms — Isin, Larsa, Eshnunna, Mari, and, eventually, Babylon — bound together by constant diplomatic and military contact rather than by shared government.

Section 12.2 traced Babylon's gradual and unremarkable rise across roughly a century of the First Dynasty's early rulers, correcting the misconception that Hammurabi inherited an already-dominant capital. Sections 12.3 through 12.5 turned to a strikingly different Mesopotamian world, the Old Assyrian commercial network centered on Ashur and extending, through the karum system, into central Anatolia, examined through the extraordinarily rich private archive recovered at Kanesh. This archive revealed family merchant firms, credit and partnership arrangements, caravan logistics, and household life largely independent of direct royal administration, offering a deliberate contrast to the centralized state economy of Ur III examined in the previous chapter.

Sections 12.6 and 12.7 examined two contrasting northern Mesopotamian political formations: Mari under Zimri-Lim, whose diplomatic archive illuminates the mechanics of interstate alliance, intelligence, and negotiation, and the short-lived personal kingdom of Shamshi-Adad I, whose rapid collapse upon its founder's death illustrated the instability of territorial supremacy built on personal military authority rather than durable institutions.

Sections 12.8 and 12.9 traced Hammurabi's own rise to regional dominance, emphasizing that his most dramatic conquests — against Larsa, Mari, and Eshnunna — occurred during the concentrated late-reign campaigns of regnal years 30 through 38, following three earlier decades devoted largely to consolidation, and examining the practical administrative and agricultural challenges of governing the suddenly expanded kingdom that resulted. Section 12.10 turned to everyday social and legal life, examining marriage, inheritance, property, and the complex and much-debated status terminology of awilum, muskenum, and wardum documented in thousands of ordinary Old Babylonian contracts and court records.

Section 12.11 examined the Laws of Hammurabi in detail, tracing its structure of royal prologue, casuistic legal judgments, and epilogue, and presenting the central historiographical debate over whether the monument functioned as an operative legal code or as a work of royal jurisprudential ideology whose relationship to actual courtroom practice was real but indirect. This textbook adopted the latter, more historically cautious position, consistent with recent curatorial and scholarly reassessment of the monument.

Section 12.12 closed the chapter, and Part III as a whole, by examining the limits of Hammurabi's achievement: the revolts and gradual southern contraction faced by his successor Samsu-iluna, the eventual reduction of the First Dynasty of Babylon to a smaller central Mesopotamian kingdom, and the dynasty's final end with the Hittite sack of Babylon around 1595 BCE. Hammurabi's territorial unification, like Shamshi-Adad's kingdom of Upper Mesopotamia a generation earlier, proved that swift conquest and durable institutional integration were historically distinct achievements.

Taken together, Chapters 9 through 12 have now traced four distinct Mesopotamian political forms across roughly a millennium and a half of documented history: the competitive city-state world of early Sumer, the pioneering territorial empire of Sargon of Akkad, the elaborate bureaucratic monarchy of Ur III, and, in this chapter, a competitive interstate and commercial system encompassing royal diplomacy, private long-distance commerce, and Hammurabi's temporary supremacy. This progression demonstrates that Mesopotamian civilization did not develop toward any single inevitable political form, but continually reorganized itself among city-state, empire, bureaucratic monarchy, and competitive multipolar systems in response to specific historical circumstances.

This chapter's emphasis on multiple kinds of evidence — royal monumental inscriptions, diplomatic correspondence, private commercial archives, and everyday legal contracts — models an interpretive method this textbook will apply repeatedly in later chapters: no single category of source, however famous or well preserved, provides a complete picture of an ancient society, and historians must read monumental, administrative, and private documentary evidence together, attentive to the different purposes each was created to serve.

Part IV, beginning with Chapter 13, now turns outward from Mesopotamia to examine Egypt's own political and religious development, before bringing Egypt, the Levant, Anatolia, and Mesopotamia together within the broader Bronze Age international system that dominates the middle centuries of the second millennium BCE — a system whose diplomatic and commercial patterns, students will notice, echo in important ways the interconnected world examined in this chapter.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Old Babylonian period
The era of Mesopotamian history, roughly the twentieth through sixteenth centuries BCE, encompassing the First Dynasty of Babylon and its neighbors, examined throughout this chapter.
Old Assyrian period
The era of Assyrian history centered on Ashur's commercial network with Anatolia, sharply distinct from the later Neo-Assyrian territorial empire, discussed in Section 12.3.
Karum
A self-governing Assyrian merchant colony operating within a foreign city, most famously at Kanesh, discussed in Section 12.4.
Wabartum
A smaller, subordinate Old Assyrian trading post along caravan routes, distinguished from the larger karum settlements.
Amorite
A linguistic and social category describing West Semitic-speaking populations increasingly integrated into Mesopotamian political life from the late third millennium BCE, not a unified ethnic or political bloc, discussed in Chapter 11 and referenced in Section 12.1.
Casuistic law
Legal reasoning expressed through specific hypothetical cases ("if a man does X, then Y"), the dominant style of the Laws of Hammurabi and earlier Mesopotamian legal collections, discussed in Section 12.11.
Apodictic law
Legal or moral reasoning expressed as an unconditional general command, contrasted with casuistic law in Section 12.11.
Lex talionis
The principle of proportional retaliation, "an eye for an eye," applied selectively and unevenly within the Laws of Hammurabi, discussed in Section 12.11.
Awilum
A term denoting a person of full legal status in Old Babylonian society, whose precise social meaning remains debated among specialists, discussed in Section 12.10.
Muskenum
A term denoting a person of more restricted legal status in Old Babylonian society, whose exact relationship to royal service or dependency remains unsettled in scholarship, discussed in Section 12.10.
Wardum
An enslaved person under Old Babylonian law, a distinct legal category that nonetheless permitted certain limited legal capacities, discussed in Section 12.10.
Dowry
Property originating in a bride's family and associated with her marriage and future support; surviving contracts preserve rights concerning its return and inheritance, though practical control varied by circumstance, discussed in Section 12.10.
Naditum
A class of women, especially well documented at Sippar, associated with religious institutions and often not bearing children, who actively managed property and engaged in commercial and legal transactions; naditum institutions varied by city and should not be generalized uncritically, discussed in Section 12.10.
Ilku
Service obligations owed to the crown that could be associated with service land or other institutional arrangements; their form varied by place, office, and circumstance, discussed in Section 12.9.
Tamkarum
A Mesopotamian merchant or trading agent, a term applied to figures engaged in both state-associated and private commerce across several periods examined in this textbook.
Stele
An upright stone monument inscribed with text or relief carving, such as the dark-stone monument bearing the Laws of Hammurabi, discussed in Section 12.11.
Middle Chronology
One of several competing scholarly systems for dating second-millennium BCE Mesopotamian history, used in this textbook to date Hammurabi's reign to approximately 1792–1750 BCE.
Shakkanakku
A title used for a provincial governor or military commander within Old Babylonian administration, discussed in Section 12.9.
Prologue and epilogue
The framing sections of the Laws of Hammurabi that present Hammurabi's divine commission and his role as a just and protective king, distinct from the casuistic legal judgments they surround, discussed in Section 12.11.
Kingdom of Upper Mesopotamia
The short-lived territorial state assembled by Shamshi-Adad I across the upper Tigris and Khabur regions, discussed in Section 12.7.
Isin-Larsa period
The era of southern Mesopotamian history in which the rival dynasties of Isin and Larsa competed for the political and cultic prestige formerly associated with Ur, discussed in Section 12.1.
Stele material debate
The Louvre currently catalogues Hammurabi's monument as basalt, while older and some ongoing scholarship has described it as diorite; its precise geological identification remains scientifically debated, discussed in Section 12.11.
Susa
City in southwestern Iran, capital of ancient Elam, where the Laws of Hammurabi stele was rediscovered by archaeologists in 1901–1902 after having been carried there as war booty in antiquity, discussed in Section 12.11.
Kassite dynasty
Rulers bearing Kassite names who gradually established durable control over Babylonia after the poorly documented transition following the First Dynasty; they later absorbed the southern Sealand kingdom, referenced in Section 12.12 and examined further in later chapters.
Suzerainty
A relationship in which a stronger power exercises overlordship over a nominally autonomous ruler or state, relevant to understanding the fluid alliance system among Mesopotamian kingdoms in this period.
Salinization
The gradual buildup of mineral salts in irrigated soil, a long-term environmental pressure on southern Mesopotamian agriculture discussed in Chapter 2 and referenced in connection with the Old Babylonian period's southern contraction in Section 12.12.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has completed Part III's examination of the Mesopotamian state tradition by showing that the political history of the early second millennium BCE cannot be reduced to a simple story of Babylonian rise. It was, instead, a genuinely open and interconnected system in which private commerce, royal diplomacy, and territorial ambition operated simultaneously, sometimes reinforcing one another and sometimes in tension, and within which Hammurabi's eventual supremacy emerged from contingent decisions within a competitive balance of power rather than from any predetermined trajectory.

The chapter has also modeled an interpretive habit that this textbook will return to repeatedly: reading multiple, very different kinds of evidence together. A royal monument commissioned to declare a king's justice before the gods, a private letter about unwoven textiles between a merchant and his wife, and a diplomatic report assessing a rival king's intentions each preserve a different slice of ancient reality, and none of them alone would allow historians to reconstruct the richly textured society this chapter has surveyed. Students who learn to ask what kind of document they are reading, and for what purpose it was created, before asking what it tells them about the past, are well prepared for the far more numerous and more contested bodies of evidence they will encounter in later volumes of this textbook.

The chapter's central caution about the Laws of Hammurabi — that a document's fame and its function are not the same thing, and that a work of royal jurisprudence is not automatically identical to an operative legal code — is a caution students should carry forward into every subsequent encounter with monumental inscriptions, royal proclamations, and official ideology throughout this textbook's remaining volumes, many of which will present rulers announcing their own justice, piety, or military success in terms that require exactly this kind of careful, source-critical reading.

Finally, this chapter's account of Hammurabi's swift but ultimately unstable territorial achievement, following directly upon the similarly unstable kingdom of Shamshi-Adad I a generation earlier, previews a pattern this textbook will encounter again and again across the centuries and empires that follow: military conquest and durable institutional integration are related but distinct historical accomplishments, and the difference between them will shape the fortunes of the Akkadian successors, the Hittites, the Assyrians, the Persians, and every later empire this multi-volume history examines.

Part IV now opens with Chapter 13, turning from Mesopotamia to Egypt's own long process of state formation along the Nile, before Chapters 14 through 16 bring Egypt, the Levant, Anatolia, and Mesopotamia together within the interconnected diplomatic and commercial world of the Late Bronze Age — a world whose royal correspondence and long-distance exchange, students will find, echo in important ways the interconnected, multipolar Mesopotamian and Anatolian world this chapter has surveyed.
