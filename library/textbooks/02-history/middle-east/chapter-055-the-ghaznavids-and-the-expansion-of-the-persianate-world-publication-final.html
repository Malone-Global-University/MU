Part XIII — The Post-Abbasid Regional Order

The Ghaznavids and the Expansion of the Persianate World

A History of the Middle East: From Ancient Civilizations to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

Abu'l-Fazl Bayhaqi spent much of his career as a secretary in the chancery of the Ghaznavid court, and in his old age he set down a long history of the reign of Sultan Masʿud I, whose officials, generals, and quarrels he had watched from inside the bureaucracy. What he wrote was not a battle chronicle or a royal panegyric. It is a prose narrative, in Persian, of how an empire was run: who was appointed and who was dismissed, how armies were paid, how letters were drafted, and how a court that governed lands from the Caspian highlands to the Indus failed to hold its western half against Turkmen horsemen. That a Turkic-led military dynasty should be remembered through one of the masterpieces of Persian prose is the subject of this chapter in miniature.

At the height of its power, around 1030, the Ghaznavid state ruled a territory that stretched from the edges of the Iranian plateau at Rayy in the west, through Khurasan, Sistan, and what is now Afghanistan, to the Indus basin and the Punjab in the east, and it held Khwarazm on the Oxus delta as a dependency. Its capital, Ghazni, stood on a high plateau in the eastern uplands of the Hindu Kush system, far from the great Abbasid and Iranian cities of earlier centuries. Its rulers, Sebuktigin, Mahmud, and Masʿud, were of Turkic military origin. Their government used Persian-speaking secretaries, Arabic as a language of religion, scholarship, and diplomacy, and a court culture in which Persian poetry became an instrument of kingship.

The dynasty did not appear from nowhere. As earlier chapters showed, the Samanids of Bukhara had built a durable eastern Islamic state that combined Iranian bureaucratic tradition with an army increasingly recruited from Turkic slave soldiers (Chapter 49), and the supply of such soldiers from the steppe frontier was part of the wider Turkic military migration surveyed in Chapter 52. Ghaznavid power grew from within this system, first as the personal following of a Samanid commander who withdrew to Ghazni in 962, then as a principality under his successors, and finally, under Mahmud, as an empire that outlived and absorbed much of the Samanid inheritance. This chapter therefore begins with the Samanid frontier rather than with the conquests for which Mahmud is best remembered.

Describing the Ghaznavids is difficult because every simple label misleads. They were Turkic in military origin, but they governed through Iranian institutions and patronized Persian letters. They were Sunni rulers who sought and received recognition from the Abbasid caliph, but they were effectively independent of him. They were patrons of poets and scholars, and they were also commanders of armies that financed themselves through taxation, tribute, and plunder. This chapter uses the term Persianate in an institutional and cultural sense, for a world of courts, offices, texts, schools, and networks in which Persian was a prestigious language of administration and literature. It does not mean a world of Persian-speaking ethnic Iranians alone, and the chapter's argument depends on keeping the two ideas apart.

The Ghaznavid expedition into South Asia is the most contested part of the story. Persian and Arabic court writers presented Mahmud's campaigns as triumphs of Islam over idolatry. Later European, South Asian, and nationalist writers used the same accounts to support narratives of permanent conflict between religious communities. Modern specialists have moved away from both. They distinguish raids from tribute relationships and tribute relationships from permanent government, they compare Persian chronicles with Sanskrit inscriptions and other evidence, and they ask what happened to the communities the campaigns affected. The chapter takes that approach. It records documented violence, including the plunder of temples, without turning a complicated eleventh-century political history into a timeless contest between two homogeneous groups.

The Ghaznavid court was also a center of intellectual life. Ferdowsi completed the Shahnameh, the great Persian epic of kings, in this period, and Persian court poets developed the panegyric forms that would shape later literature. Al-Biruni, a scholar from Khwarazm who came into Ghaznavid service after Mahmud's conquest of his homeland, produced the most careful medieval Muslim study of Indian learning. These achievements belong to the same political world as the campaigns and the tax registers, and the chapter treats them as intertwined without reducing scholarship to a by-product of conquest.

The chapter proceeds in five divisions of four sections each. Division I traces the Samanid inheritance and the rise of Ghaznavid kingship. Division II examines the army, the administration, the fiscal system, and urban society. Division III follows the expansion into South Asia and the societies it affected. Division IV turns to Persian literature, scholarship, and the geography of Persianate culture. Division V follows western expansion, the reign of Masʿud, the Battle of Dandanqan in 1040, and the dynasty's long afterlife in Afghanistan and the Punjab until 1186. The mature formation of the Seljuk state belongs to Chapter 57, the northern frontier worlds to Chapter 56, and the administrative system of Nizam al-Mulk to Chapter 58. Buyid history, which Chapter 53 narrates, enters here only where it touched Ghaznavid policy.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how the Ghaznavid dynasty emerged from the Samanid military and political system rather than as a state without predecessors.
  2. Describe the political geography of eastern Iran, Afghanistan, and the Indus approaches, and why Ghazni was a valuable political center.
  3. Trace the succession from Alptigin to Sebuktigin to Mahmud, and the relationship of each to Samanid authority.
  4. Distinguish Abbasid recognition of Mahmud from direct Abbasid control, and analyze the language of Sunni kingship used by the dynasty.
  5. Analyze the organization of the Ghaznavid army, differentiating enslaved soldiers, free retainers, and allied troops.
  6. Evaluate the role of viziers, diwans, and Persian secretaries in Ghaznavid government, and the place of Persian and Arabic within it.
  7. Assess the fiscal basis of the empire and the relationship between military success and fiscal vulnerability.
  8. Differentiate raiding, tribute, occupation, and durable government in the Ghaznavid campaigns in South Asia.
  9. Compare Persian and Arabic court narratives with other evidence for the campaigns, including the Somnath expedition.
  10. Interpret the contributions of Persian court poetry, Ferdowsi's Shahnameh, and al-Biruni's Kitab al-Hind to Persianate culture and intellectual exchange.
  11. Explain why the Battle of Dandanqan in 1040 transformed the regional balance without ending the dynasty.
  12. Evaluate competing explanations for the expansion of the Persianate world, avoiding an ethnic or purely conquest-based account.

Historical Context

By the middle of the tenth century the Abbasid caliphate had ceased to govern most of its former territory directly. As Chapter 50 explained, provincial dynasties and military commanders held effective power, while the caliph retained religious and ceremonial standing. In western Iran and Iraq, the Buyids controlled Baghdad (Chapter 53). In Egypt and North Africa, the Fatimids had founded a rival Ismaili caliphate (Chapter 54). In the east, the Samanid amirs ruled from Bukhara over Transoxiana and Khurasan as nominal servants of the Abbasid caliph, and they presided over a flourishing Persian-language literary and administrative culture, treated in Chapter 49.

The Samanid state rested on a partnership between a Persian-speaking bureaucracy and a military establishment increasingly composed of Turkic ghulams, slave soldiers purchased or captured on the steppe frontier and trained in the households of amirs and commanders. Chapter 45 described the general development of military slavery under the Abbasids, and Chapter 52 followed the movement of Turkic soldiers and migrants into the Islamic world. In Samanid Khurasan these soldiers became powerful. They held provincial governorships, sometimes manipulated succession, and in the 960s and 970s some used their positions to found their own principalities. The Ghaznavids are the most successful of those principalities.

The eastern frontier of the Islamic world was also changing in other ways. North of the Oxus, the Qarakhanids, a Turkic dynasty that had converted to Islam, were expanding toward the Samanid lands. In Sistan, Saffarid successors held a local principality. To the south and east of Ghazni lay the Hindu Shahi kingdoms, a politically organized state controlling the Kabul region, the Khyber approaches, and parts of the Indus basin, along with the mountain communities of Ghur and Zabulistan. In Multan, an Ismaili ruling house with ties to the Fatimid movement controlled an important commercial city. These powers shaped the possibilities open to the dynasty that settled at Ghazni, and they must be understood as actors with their own interests.

The chronological focus of this chapter is roughly 950 to 1050, concentrating on Sebuktigin (r. 977–997), Mahmud (r. 998–1030), and Masʿud I (r. 1030–1041). The later dynasty, including its Punjab-based government and its end at Ghurid hands in 1186, appears in a concise epilogue at the end of the chapter.

Division I — The Samanid Inheritance · Section 55.1

Eastern Iran, Afghanistan, and the Political Geography of the Samanid Frontier

The Samanid amirs ruled from Bukhara over a state whose heartland lay in Transoxiana, the lands beyond the Oxus River, and in Khurasan, the great province of northeastern Iran with its cities of Nishapur, Merv, Herat, and Balkh. Khurasan was governed by military commanders, usually called sipahsalars, who were appointed from Bukhara but often held enough local power to challenge the amir. The distinction between a loyal provincial commander and an independent ruler was therefore thin, and it frequently depended on who controlled the army and the revenues. The Ghaznavids emerged from precisely this ambiguity.

South and east of Khurasan the terrain rises into the Hindu Kush and its southern extensions, a mountainous region of high plateaus, river valleys, and passes that link the Iranian plateau to the Indus basin. Modern borders do not describe this landscape. Its principal districts in the tenth century included Zabulistan, around Ghazni; Bust and the Helmand valley to the southwest; Kabul and its surrounding valleys; Ghur, a highland region of fortified valleys to the west; and the plains of Sistan. Chapter 2 introduced the Iranian plateau and the geographic logic of mountains as both barriers and corridors; the Hindu Kush region is a striking example, because the same ranges that separated societies also contained the passes through which merchants, armies, and migrants moved.

Ghazni was not simply a convenient military camp. It lay on a high plateau, more than two thousand meters above sea level, where agriculture depended on rivers and irrigation channels fed by mountain snowmelt, and its position controlled the roads between Kabul, Bust, and the southern routes toward the Indus. The region had long been a frontier of Islamic expansion, ruled in the ninth and early tenth centuries by local dynasties, and it retained a mixed population of Muslims, Buddhists, and followers of other traditions. Silver mines in the Hindu Kush, which medieval writers associate with the Panjshir region, supplied metal for coinage in Samanid and later Ghaznavid mints. Trade moved across the same passes, including slaves, horses, textiles, and spices, connecting Central Asia to South Asia (the wider commercial world is treated in Chapter 51).

The frontier setting mattered politically. A commander who held Ghazni could draw on local mountain populations for infantry, on the plains of Khurasan and Sistan for taxation and supplies, and on the steppe and Turkic markets for slave soldiers, while commanding access to the wealthy and politically fragmented lands beyond the Indus. Local rulers, including the families of Zabulistan and Ghur, were neither passive nor uniformly hostile; they negotiated, intermarried, and fought, and the Ghaznavids became rulers in part by incorporating them. Historians must also keep in mind that the sources for this region are overwhelmingly written from the viewpoint of the courts and chanceries of Bukhara and Ghazni, and that the voices of mountain communities, merchants, and cultivators survive mainly through incidental references.

Section 55.2

Alptigin, Sebuktigin, and the Establishment of Ghaznavid Rule

The founder of the Ghaznavid line, Alptigin, was a Turkic military commander who rose in the Samanid establishment and became a leading figure at the court of Bukhara and in the governance of Khurasan. When the Samanid amir ʿAbd al-Malik I died in 961, a dispute arose over the succession. Alptigin, according to the sources, backed a candidate who lost, and he withdrew with his household and troops to the southeast. In 962–963 he took Ghazni from its local ruler. Alptigin died soon afterward, so he established a base rather than a state.

A series of commanders ruled Ghazni in the following years, apparently chosen from the pool of Alptigin's military household and acting as Samanid servants in name while behaving as local lords. Among them was Bilkatigin, who ruled for more than a decade, and Piri (or Pirey), whose rule is reported in the sources as harsh. In 977 the military leaders of Ghazni chose Sebuktigin, a former slave soldier of Alptigin who had risen through the ranks and, by tradition, had married into Alptigin's family. The sources agree that he was purchased as a young man from the steppe frontier. The details of his origins come largely from later accounts that emphasize his rise from slavery to rule, a narrative the Ghaznavid court had reasons to elaborate.

Sebuktigin's reign shows that the dynasty's later power had an institutional foundation. He extended control over Bust and neighboring territories in the south and southwest, took forts in the highlands, and built an army of ghulams and local troops. He fought the Hindu Shahi ruler Jayapala, whose kingdom controlled the routes from Kabul to the Indus; the sources report that conflict in the 980s and early 990s, that Jayapala was forced to accept terms, and that Sebuktigin gained territory and revenue in the border districts. The details of the campaigns, including numbers and the precise sequence, come from later Persian and Arabic histories and should be treated with caution.

Sebuktigin also remained a servant of the Samanids when it suited him, and the relationship was profitable. In 994 he supported the Samanid amir Nuh II against rebellious commanders in Khurasan, and in return the amir conferred honorific titles on him and appointed his son Mahmud to command in Khurasan from Nishapur. The service demonstrated the dynasty's value to Bukhara, increased its prestige and resources, and gave Mahmud administrative experience in the Samanid heartland. It also illustrated the distinction between nominal subordination and growing independence. The Samanid amir needed Ghazni's army, and the commander at Ghazni needed Samanid legitimacy, until the Samanid state weakened and the need became one-sided.

Section 55.3

Mahmud's Accession, the Samanid Collapse, and the New Eastern Political Order

Sebuktigin died in 997. He had apparently designated his younger son Ismaʿil as heir, a choice that Mahmud, then commanding in Khurasan, did not accept. The brothers fought, and in 998 Mahmud defeated Ismaʿil at Ghazni, took the city, and became ruler. The episode shows the principle that would trouble the dynasty repeatedly: a father's designation counted for less than control of the army and the treasury, and sons competed for the loyalty of commanders. Mahmud's victory also demonstrated that his prior command in Khurasan gave him resources his brother could not match.

The Samanid state meanwhile collapsed from within. A succession of weak amirs, factional struggles among military commanders in Khurasan, and pressure from the Qarakhanids north of the Oxus combined to destroy the dynasty. In 999 the Qarakhanid ruler took Bukhara, ending effective Samanid rule in Transoxiana; a Samanid prince, Ismaʿil al-Muntasir, later tried to recover the lands with the help of Oghuz Turkic groups, and was killed in 1005. Mahmud meanwhile took control of Khurasan, defeating Samanid commanders who resisted, and acknowledged the new situation by recognizing the Qarakhanids as rulers north of the Oxus. The river became the practical boundary between the two states.

It is important not to overstate Mahmud's role. He did not conquer the entire Samanid inheritance. The Qarakhanid takeover of Bukhara and Transoxiana was a separate development, driven by different leaders with different resources, and the settlement between the two powers was negotiated and unstable. Relations between Ghazni and the Qarakhanids combined marriage alliances, diplomacy, and warfare. In the following years Qarakhanid armies invaded Khurasan, and Mahmud repelled them; he later also expanded into Sistan, where he ended the local Saffarid-descended ruler's autonomy in about 1003.

What Mahmud inherited from the Samanids was not only territory. He acquired, with Khurasan, the Samanid provincial administration, its fiscal records, its experienced secretaries, and the prestige of governing the central Islamic east. These assets mattered more than any single battle, because they allowed a military dynasty of slave origin to claim continuity with the older Iranian-Islamic order. The Ghaznavids did not merely replace the Samanids; they absorbed their personnel and institutions, and in doing so became something different from the military principality Sebuktigin had ruled.

Section 55.4

Mahmud of Ghazni, Abbasid Recognition, and the Language of Sunni Kingship

A ruler who had seized power by arms needed a vocabulary of legitimacy, and Mahmud found one in the Abbasid caliphate. Soon after taking power, he sought recognition from the caliph al-Qadir in Baghdad. The caliph responded with a diploma of investiture, robes of honor, and honorific titles, among them Yamin al-Dawla ("Right Hand of the State") and Amin al-Milla ("Trustee of the Faith"). The first title became part of the name by which the dynasty's earliest historian, al-Utbi, called his chronicle, the Tarikh al-Yamini. Such recognition was symbolic and political: it did not make Mahmud the caliph's agent, but it supported his claim to rule as a legitimate Muslim sovereign rather than a usurping slave commander.

The relationship was one of mutual interest. The caliph, whose capital was controlled by the Shiʿi Buyids (Chapter 53) and who faced the rival Fatimid caliphate in Cairo (Chapter 54), gained an energetic Sunni ally with an army. Mahmud gained a public endorsement and, through the caliph's name on coins and in the Friday sermon, an affirmation of his place in the Islamic order. The relationship also shaped policy. The caliph al-Qadir promoted a creed hostile to Muʿtazilite rationalism and to Shiʿi movements, and Mahmud, particularly in later years, associated himself with suppression of those he labeled heretics. Reports of such measures after the capture of Rayy in 1029 appear in later sources and should be read in light of their sectarian vantage points, and the wider relationship of religious policy to political interest requires caution.

Mahmud's titles and the title sultan deserve attention. Contemporaries and later writers increasingly used "sultan" for Mahmud, but the term's meaning in this period was still developing. It indicated power and authority rather than an office defined by law, and it later acquired a more fixed meaning in Seljuk usage (Chapter 57). Coins and inscriptions show Mahmud using the caliph's name and a set of titles that stressed his service to the faith and the state while asserting his own rank. The effect was to place a military ruler within a framework in which the caliph conferred legitimacy and the ruler provided protection.

Public religion also gave Mahmud's wars a vocabulary. Court writers described campaigns against non-Muslims as ghaza, warfare for the faith, and gave their patron the standing of a ghazi, a fighter for Islam. The language was real and had real audiences: it appealed to religious scholars, to volunteers, and to the caliph. But it did not explain the full range of Ghaznavid military action. Mahmud also fought Muslim rulers, including the Qarakhanids, the Buyids, and the rulers of Multan, and the Muslim population of Khurasan paid for his armies. Religious rhetoric was a means of legitimation, one among several motives, and historians must examine it alongside finance, security, prestige, and strategy.

Historical Vocabulary
Ghaza and Ghazi

Ghaza refers to raiding or warfare on the frontier of Islam, and a ghazi is a warrior who takes part in it. In many medieval frontier societies the terms carried religious prestige and implied that fighting non-Muslims was meritorious. Courts such as the Ghaznavid applied the vocabulary to rulers as part of a claim to piety and leadership. The terms were rhetorically powerful, but their use in a chronicle does not tell the historian what actually motivated a campaign, who participated in it, or how it was financed. Ghazi language could coexist with the pursuit of tribute, territory, and prestige.

Primary Source Window

Al-Utbi, Kitab al-Yamini (Tarikh al-Yamini): Mahmud's Campaigns and Royal Legitimation

Creator: Abu Naṣr Muḥammad al-ʿUtbi, a secretary associated with the Ghaznavid court · Date: composed in Arabic in the early eleventh century, covering the reigns of Sebuktigin and Mahmud into the 1020s approximately · Type: Court history in ornate Arabic prose

Context: Al-Utbi wrote in a rhetorical style meant to display the author's skill as much as to record events. The Arabic original was later adapted into Persian, notably in the early thirteenth century, and the English translation by James Reynolds (published in 1858) was made from that Persian version rather than from the Arabic. Students therefore encounter at least three layers: the Arabic text, the Persian adaptation, and the English rendering.

Editorial paraphrase (not a quotation): The work presents Mahmud as a victorious servant of the faith whose triumphs over unbelievers and rebels fulfil a religious duty and earn the favor of the caliph. It treats the caliph's honors as validation of the ruler's piety and describes campaigns in elevated language emphasizing divine assistance, large numbers of enemy dead, and abundant spoil.

Analysis and limitations: Al-Utbi is a valuable witness to how the court wished to present its ruler, and he preserves names, places, and sequences that other sources lack. He is not a neutral reporter. His account is panegyrical, its numerical claims are rhetorical, its geography is sometimes vague, and it gives no voice to the societies attacked. Historians use it by comparing it with Persian prose histories, coins, inscriptions, and sources from the regions affected.

  2. What audience did the author address, and how might it have shaped his emphasis on religious victory?
  3. What kinds of evidence could confirm or challenge his claims about booty and enemy casualties?
Division II — Military Institutions, Administration, and Economy · Section 55.5

Military Slavery, Turkic Commanders, and the Organization of the Ghaznavid Army

Chapter 45 explained how Abbasid caliphs came to rely on slave soldiers whose loyalty was to their patron rather than to a tribe or province. The Ghaznavids inherited and adapted that system through the Samanids. Their core forces included ghulams, young men purchased or captured, converted to Islam, trained as cavalrymen, and raised within the households of the ruler and of high commanders. The best of them, especially those of the ruler's own household, could rise to command armies and govern provinces. Sebuktigin's own career is the clearest example, since a slave soldier founded a ruling line.

But the army was not made solely of slaves, and it is a mistake to equate Ghaznavid military power with a single institution. The sources distinguish several categories. The household ghulams of the court formed an elite, paid directly by the ruler. Commanders with their own followings, some of free status, brought retainers. Local troops, including mountain infantry from Ghur, Zabulistan, and other highland districts, served as infantry and garrison forces. Indian contingents, under their own commanders, appear in the sources, and Bayhaqi describes a Hindu commander named Tilak who held important responsibility under Masʿud. Allied or subordinate rulers supplied troops on demand. Finally, volunteers motivated by religion or booty joined some campaigns. The army was a coalition rather than a monolith.

The Ghaznavid army included heavy and light cavalry, infantry, archers, and siege specialists capable of using catapults and incendiary materials against fortified places. Its most famous distinctive feature was war elephants, obtained from India, which served as shock weapons and as symbols of royal power. Maintaining such forces on long-distance campaigns required organization. Fodder, water, and food had to be assembled; baggage trains had to cross mountains; and the administration of the army, including inspections and pay lists, formed one of the major departments of government. Hot-weather campaigns in the plains of the Indus, winter campaigns in the mountains, and expeditions across deserts, such as the march toward Somnath, demonstrated considerable logistical capacity.

The system produced stability and instability together. Personal ties between rulers and ghulams created loyal cadres, and the ruler's ability to reward them with salaries, gifts, and booty maintained their allegiance. But the same dependence on military households meant that commanders, who often controlled provinces and their own followings, could challenge the ruler at moments of weakness, and that succession disputes became contests for the army's support. Mahmud's accession in 998 and Masʿud's in 1030 both turned on the army's loyalty. Unlike the Abbasid pattern, where slave soldiers eventually dominated the caliph, the Ghaznavid ruler remained a military leader himself, which gave the dynasty a more direct relationship with its army but exposed its stability to the ruler's personal success. Defeat could therefore have swift and drastic political consequences, as 1040 demonstrated.

Historical Vocabulary
Ghulam

In this period ghulam (plural ghilman) usually denotes a military slave or a young retainer trained in a patron's household, often of Turkic origin. The word is not a precise legal category in every source; a ghulam could be legally enslaved, manumitted but still bound by loyalty, or a free client using the term for a patron's follower. Historians therefore distinguish the legal status of an individual from the social and political function of the system. Many ghulams achieved wealth and power, which complicates a simple picture of slavery as only subordination, while slavery itself remained a coercive institution that involved capture, sale, and the severing of family ties.

Section 55.6

Viziers, Diwans, Secretaries, and Persian Administrative Government

While military commanders won the empire, secretaries ran it. The Ghaznavids adopted the administrative structure of the Samanid and wider Islamic world, in which a vizier headed a set of bureaus, or diwans. The principal ones included the diwan of the vizier or of finance, responsible for taxation and expenditure; the army bureau, which kept registers of soldiers and managed pay; the chancery, which drafted official correspondence; and the intelligence and postal service, whose agents reported on officials and provinces. Provincial governors, judges, and tax officials extended the system to the provinces, with courts of law staffed by judges trained in religious law.

The people who staffed these offices were mostly educated Iranians from families of secretaries, landowners, and scholars, many of them from Khurasan and Sistan. Their skills were rare and valuable: they understood accounting, tax assessment, official style, and the conventions of correspondence. They also tended to look down on the military leaders they served and to compete with one another through patronage networks. Viziers such as Fadl b. Ahmad al-Isfara'ini, Ahmad b. Hasan Maymandi, and, under Masʿud, Hasanak, held great power and suffered disgrace. The Ghaznavid practice of confiscating the wealth of fallen officials, known as musadara, made office-holding profitable and dangerous. Bayhaqi's history describes the imprisonment and execution of Hasanak early in Masʿud’s reign, approximately 1031–1032, with a depth that shows how political intrigue, personal rivalry, and charges of religious deviance converged.

Language is central. Official letters to the caliph and foreign courts, and much formal correspondence, were written in Arabic, the language of religion, law, and scholarship across the Islamic world. Persian developed as the language of internal administration and of prose and verse in the court. The chancery tradition inherited from the Samanids, where Persian prose writing was already cultivated (Chapter 49), made it natural for Ghaznavid secretaries to work in both languages. Bayhaqi, who served in the chancery, wrote in Persian, and his prose became a model. The relationship between the two languages was not competitive in a simple sense. Arabic remained the language of high scholarship, theology, and law, and Persian grew in domains of kingship, literature, and bureaucratic narration.

A dynasty of Turkic military origin therefore governed through institutions it had not invented and officials who were not of its own background. The arrangement was uneasy. Rulers distrusted ministers who accumulated wealth, commanders resented the influence of civilians, and secretaries criticized rulers who disregarded established procedure. Bayhaqi's pages are full of such tensions. Yet the partnership worked because each side supplied what the other lacked: the army protected and extended the state, and the bureaucracy raised the revenues, produced the records, and wrote the letters that made it function as a state.

Historical Vocabulary
Persianate

Persianate describes societies, institutions, and cultural practices shaped by the Persian language and the Iranian literary and administrative tradition, without implying that the people involved were ethnically Persian. A Turkic-descended ruler, an Arab or Indian scholar, or a Central Asian poet could be part of a Persianate world by participating in its court culture, using its language, and adopting its models of government and literature. The term is therefore institutional and cultural, and this chapter uses it in that sense. Related ideas include "Iranian," which may refer to the plateau and its peoples, and "Persian-speaking," which refers to language use alone.

Section 55.7

Taxation, Agricultural Revenue, Tribute, and the Financing of Empire

An army of cavalry, elephants, and garrison troops, together with a court famous for gifts to poets and scholars, required large and reliable revenue. The Ghaznavid state drew on several sources, and the historian's first task is to distinguish them. The most regular was the land tax, the kharaj, levied on the agricultural production of Khurasan, Sistan, and the districts around Ghazni. Khurasan, with its irrigated oases and qanat-fed fields (the technology described in Chapter 2), was the richest tax base. Commercial taxes and customs on goods moving through cities and across the passes added revenue, and mines, including the silver of the Hindu Kush, supported the mint.

A second category was tribute, payments extracted from subordinate rulers in exchange for the continuation of their rule. These payments might be annual, negotiated, or imposed after defeat, and they were as much political as fiscal because they placed the payer in a relationship of subordination. A third category was booty: movable wealth, weapons, animals, and captives taken in war. The sources repeatedly give very large figures for booty from Indian campaigns, and those figures cannot be treated as exact. They do show that treasure, including coin, precious metals, and temple valuables, was important, and that its distribution to soldiers and courtiers helped sustain loyalty. Extraordinary levies and confiscations from officials and wealthy individuals supplemented regular income.

The distinction between regular revenue and campaign proceeds matters. A state that depends on plunder must keep winning; a state that depends on taxation must keep its cultivators and merchants productive. The Ghaznavids needed both. Maintaining the army, building fortifications, funding the court, and paying governors and officials cost very large sums, while the elephants and long-distance expeditions added heavy costs. Revenue demands fell on cultivators and merchants, and the sources, written by officials and court writers, record complaints, tax disputes, and the severity of some collectors only incidentally.

The relation between success and vulnerability is the key point. Under Mahmud, victories brought treasure and prestige, which funded further campaigns. Under Masʿud, defeats and loss of provinces cut revenue at the same moment that the army needed more pay, and Bayhaqi records repeated disputes over how to finance campaigns. When the Seljuks took Khurasan, the Ghaznavids lost not only a territory but their principal agricultural tax base, and the compensating revenues from India and the eastern provinces proved smaller. The fiscal structure that had supported empire could not simply be transferred eastward.

Section 55.8

Cities, Routes, Merchants, and Social Life across the Ghaznavid Domains

The Ghaznavid state rested on a network of cities. Ghazni was the capital, with its palaces, mint, and bazaars. Balkh, an ancient city of the northern Hindu Kush foothills and a Khurasan center, and Herat, with its fruit-growing countryside, were major provincial capitals. Bust, in the Helmand valley, served as a military and administrative base, while Nishapur was among the greatest cities of the eastern Islamic world, a center of religious scholarship, textile production, and commerce. After the conquest of the Punjab, Lahore became the principal Ghaznavid city in South Asia. These cities were more than military centers; they were markets, centers of craft production, and sites of learning, and they supported the state through taxes and services.

Trade moved along roads linking Khurasan with Central Asia and with the Indus basin. Merchants exchanged horses, textiles, metals, spices, and slaves, and caravans carried goods and information across the Hindu Kush. The commercial networks and ports discussed in Chapter 51 connected these overland routes with the Indian Ocean. Merchants of different religions and regions moved through Ghaznavid cities, among them Muslims, Jews, Hindus, and Buddhists; the Ghaznavids taxed them but also depended on them for supplies, credit, and luxury goods. Commerce supported the state independently of campaigns, a point often lost when the dynasty is described only through its battles.

Social life was diverse. The rural population cultivated grain, fruit, and cotton in irrigated oases and grazed herds on highland pastures. Craftsmen worked in metal, textiles, leather, and ceramics. Scholars and jurists studied and taught in mosques and private circles. Soldiers lived in barracks and households, enslaved people worked as domestic servants, craftsmen, and soldiers, and women appear in the sources mainly as royal and elite women, whose households and charitable foundations sometimes shaped patronage, and as members of enslaved and working populations whose experiences are recorded only fleetingly. Evidence for ordinary women and rural households is thin and mostly indirect, drawn from legal texts, archaeology, and passing remarks in chronicles.

Understanding this social world prevents the Ghaznavid story from shrinking to kings, soldiers, and court poets. The state's armies came from households and villages, its revenue from fields and markets, and its culture from schools and workshops as well as palaces. The same geography that made Ghazni a base for conquest also made it a crossroads, and its prosperity depended on the many people who moved goods and ideas across the mountains.

Everyday Life and Social History

Living in the Ghaznavid World

A cultivator in an irrigated Khurasan village lived by the rhythm of water allocation and the tax collector's visits, with the burden of the land tax and occasional extraordinary levies, and with the risk that marching armies would requisition supplies or damage fields. A highland family in Zabulistan or Ghur combined farming with herding and sometimes sent young men into military service as infantry. A merchant at Balkh or Lahore dealt in goods carried across the mountains, paid transit taxes, and relied on local rulers for security. An enslaved young man bought on the steppe frontier might be trained as a soldier and, with luck, rise in a commander's household; an enslaved woman might work in a domestic household with few rights and little chance of recorded notice.

The surviving sources are mostly written by officials, court writers, and scholars, so the daily lives of ordinary people must be reconstructed from legal rulings, archaeological remains, coins, and scattered remarks. Historians treat such reconstruction carefully, aware that silence about a group does not mean it did not matter.

Division III — The Expansion into South Asia · Section 55.9

The Hindu Shahi Kingdoms and the Northwestern Indian Frontier

The opponents Sebuktigin and Mahmud met in the northwest were not a disorganized collection of petty chiefs. The Hindu Shahis, sometimes called the Kabul Shahis in earlier phases, ruled a kingdom whose territory in the tenth century included the Kabul region, the approaches to the Khyber Pass, Peshawar and the plain of Gandhara, and parts of the Indus basin, with a later center at Udabhandapura (Hund) on the Indus. The dynasty had a long history and a bureaucratic and military system, minted coins, patronized temples, and ruled a population of Hindus, Buddhists, and Muslims. Its coin type, featuring a horseman and a bull, circulated widely and was imitated in the region.

The Hindu Shahi ruler Jayapala fought Sebuktigin in the 980s and 990s, and the sources report that he formed an alliance with neighboring rulers and lost territory in the border districts. When Mahmud took power, the conflict resumed. In 1001 Mahmud defeated Jayapala near Peshawar. According to Persian sources, Jayapala, humiliated and unable to rebuild his position, abdicated in favor of his son Anandapala and ended his life by self-immolation, a report that must be read as reflecting later literary framing as well as possible fact. Whatever its details, the outcome opened the Peshawar plain and the Indus approaches to Ghaznavid control.

The military and fiscal importance of this frontier was considerable. The mountain passes from the Kabul valley to the Indus allowed armies to move and tolls to be collected. Fortified settlements guarded the roads, and control of the plain beyond the Khyber gave access to the Punjab, one of the richest agricultural regions in the subcontinent. For the Hindu Shahis, losing these districts meant losing their economic base and their buffer; for the Ghaznavids, gaining them offered revenue, access to Indian commerce and elephants, and a staging ground for later campaigns.

It is important to see the Hindu Shahis as active historical agents. They sought allies, adjusted their strategy, rebuilt armies, and negotiated, and they were defeated through a combination of Ghaznavid military superiority, their own internal pressures, and the limited resources of a frontier kingdom. Their defeat was not a foregone conclusion. Equally, the conquest of frontier territory differed from the subsequent consolidation of government; seizing Peshawar did not create an administration in the Punjab, a transition that took years and depended on arrangements with local rulers and officials, as the next section explains.

Section 55.10

The Punjab, Multan, and the Development of Ghaznavid Rule in India

After Jayapala's defeat, his son Anandapala led continued resistance. Later sources, including accounts written centuries afterward, describe a large coalition of Indian rulers assembled to meet Mahmud in a major battle conventionally dated to 1008–1009, in which the Ghaznavid army prevailed. The size and composition of the coalition rest on late and rhetorical sources, and students should regard the famous narrative details, such as a dramatic elephant stampede, as uncertain. What is clearer is that the Hindu Shahis lost their main strength in these years, retained a diminished presence in the Salt Range and the Punjab foothills under later rulers such as Trilochanapala and Bhimapala, and disappeared as a political force by about 1026.

Multan was a separate problem. This commercial city in the southern Punjab was ruled by an Ismaili dynasty with ties to the Fatimid movement (Chapter 54), a Muslim state that Mahmud, as a Sunni ruler, regarded as heretical. Mahmud attacked Multan, probably in 1005–1006 and again in 1010, ending Ismaili rule in the city. The episode is a reminder that Ghaznavid warfare in South Asia targeted Muslims as well as non-Muslims, and that political and sectarian motives were interwoven with the control of trade and territory.

Lahore became the center of Ghaznavid government in the Punjab. By the early 1020s the region was incorporated under governors appointed from Ghazni, who collected taxes, kept garrisons, and supervised local rulers. Governors such as Ahmad Niyaltigin, who also raided the Ganges valley in the 1030s, held considerable autonomy. The Ghaznavids minted coins at Lahore, including silver coins with Arabic legends accompanied by a Sanskrit translation of the Muslim profession of faith, a striking example of adaptation to a local audience. Hindu commanders and soldiers served in Ghaznavid armies, and local rulers who accepted subordination often kept their positions.

The relationship between military occupation, subordinate rulers, governors, tax collection, and trade therefore varied by place and time. Some districts experienced a single campaign followed by tribute; others were incorporated under governors; others, like Multan, passed from independent rule to Ghaznavid control. This pattern challenges the older view that Ghaznavid activity in South Asia consisted only of raids that returned to Ghazni with plunder. Modern scholars, among them Ali Anooshahr, emphasize networks of tributary and political relationships and the development of a durable Ghaznavid presence in the Punjab, while recognizing that the dynasty's authority beyond the Punjab was intermittent.

Section 55.11

Campaigns into Northern India, Temple Raids, and the Expedition to Somnath

Beyond the Punjab, Mahmud led a series of expeditions into northern and western India in the second decade of the eleventh century and later. In 1014 he attacked Thanesar; in 1018–1019 he campaigned to Mathura and Kannauj in the Ganges plain, where the ruler of Kannauj withdrew or submitted; and he also moved against fortresses in central India such as Gwalior and Kalinjar. These expeditions struck wealthy cities and major temples, and the sources emphasize the booty, the captives, and the destruction of images. They did not result in lasting Ghaznavid government in the Ganges plain, and the regions affected returned to local rulers after the army departed, sometimes after tribute payments.

The expedition to Somnath in 1025–1026 is the best known and the most debated. Somnath, on the coast of Gujarat, was a prominent Shaiva temple and a place of pilgrimage in a region active in Indian Ocean trade. Mahmud marched from Ghazni by way of Multan across the desert, reached Somnath, defeated its defenders, and plundered and damaged the temple. He returned by a difficult route, apparently harassed on the way. The sources give large figures for the number of defenders killed and for the booty, but such numbers are not reliable. That the temple was attacked, that wealth and captives were taken, and that people were killed is well supported by sources including Persian writers; the dimensions and consequences are less certain.

Why were temples targeted? Temples in medieval India were not only religious sites. They were centers of wealth and patronage, holding endowments, treasure, and images donated by kings, and they were often connected to royal legitimacy. Attacking a major temple could deliver booty, undermine a rival ruler's prestige, and demonstrate victory. Indian kings had themselves sometimes seized and moved images from defeated rivals' temples, as historians of medieval India have noted. The Ghaznavid attacks differed in the religious rhetoric attached to them and in the sustained use of iconoclasm as a mark of Islamic victory. The destruction caused real loss to the communities who worshipped and served in these temples, and recognizing this does not require ignoring the material and political motives involved.

The evidence demands comparison. The main accounts of Somnath available to Muslim readers came from Persian and Arabic writers, notably Gardizi, who wrote around 1050, the poet Farrukhi Sistani, and later historians such as Ibn al-Athir in the thirteenth century. They emphasized the religious meaning of the victory. Sanskrit inscriptions and Jain sources from Gujarat and the surrounding regions tell a different story: they give the event little prominence in the decades that followed, and the temple was rebuilt in subsequent generations. Romila Thapar's study Somanatha: The Many Voices of a History uses this comparison to show how the episode acquired layers of meaning in Persian chronicles, in Indian memory, and in colonial and nationalist politics. A famous example of later reinterpretation occurred in 1842, when the British governor-general proclaimed the return of supposed Somnath gates from Ghazni, a political gesture that rested on a doubtful identification.

The result should neither minimize nor exaggerate. The destruction of temples and the killing and enslavement of people were documented parts of the Ghaznavid expeditions. But the campaigns were not evidence of an unchanging contest between two monolithic religious communities. Hindu soldiers fought for the Ghaznavids, Muslim states were also attacked, and Indian rulers fought each other. Those later narratives that portray the expeditions as the origin of eternal conflict reflect later politics more than eleventh-century realities.

Section 55.12

Local Rulers, Religious Communities, Resistance, and the Indian Frontier

Turning from the Ghaznavid campaigns to the societies they affected changes the picture. After the defeat of the Hindu Shahis, the Punjab and Indus regions contained a variety of ruling families, communities, and institutions: remnant Hindu Shahi princes, regional dynasties of the Ganges plain and Rajasthan, Buddhist monasteries in decline in the northwest, Hindu temples and brahmanical communities, Muslim communities with roots in the Arab conquest of Sind in the eighth century, merchants, and peasants. The responses of these groups were varied. Some resisted by force; some submitted and paid tribute; some served the new rulers as soldiers, officials, and intermediaries; others fled or were displaced.

Some evidence points to collaboration and adaptation. Hindu commanders, including Tilak, led Ghaznavid troops. Local officials continued to operate in the Punjab under Ghaznavid governors. Bilingual coinage addressed Indian audiences. Merchants of various religions continued to trade across the frontier. Captives taken in the campaigns were brought to Ghazni, where, according to sources, they worked in construction and as servants, and some enslaved people were sold in markets, which meant that conquest also produced a flow of people and skills to the west. Scholars, craftsmen, and soldiers moved in both directions.

Political incorporation did not equal religious transformation. Ghaznavid conquest did not produce uniform or immediate conversion of local populations. Conversion to Islam in South Asia was a gradual and regionally varied process, involving trade, Sufi networks, politics, and social mobility over centuries rather than a single event. In the Punjab, Muslim communities grew over time, but the eleventh-century population remained largely non-Muslim. Al-Biruni, discussed in Section 55.15, remarked that the Ghaznavid raids left a legacy of bitterness among Indian scholars, a warning against assuming that political incorporation brought acceptance.

Ghaznavid expansion created enduring political and cultural connections between Iran, Afghanistan, and northern India, and these connections later facilitated the movement of soldiers, scholars, poets, and Sufis into the subcontinent. It also produced warfare, plunder, coercion, and unequal relationships, and historians have to hold both aspects in view. The next division turns to the culture that traveled along those routes.

The question: What best explains the pattern of Ghaznavid campaigns in South Asia?

Division IV — Persianate Culture, Scholarship, and the Court · Section 55.13

Persian Court Poetry, Literary Patronage, and the Language of Kingship

The Ghaznavid court was a center of Persian poetry. Building on the literary revival associated with the Samanid court (Chapter 49), poets in Mahmud's circle wrote in the qasida, a formal ode in praise of a patron, and in other forms. Among the best known were Unsuri, who, according to later tradition, held the leading position among court poets; Farrukhi Sistani, from Sistan, whose verse is rich in descriptions of nature and of court ceremonies; and Manuchehri Damghani, noted for his lively imagery. These poets lived by patronage: a successful poem could bring gifts of money, robes, and favor, and the court's generosity was a theme of the poetry itself.

Panegyric poetry served the state. It praised the ruler's justice, courage, and generosity, celebrated victories, described festivals, and rendered political claims in memorable form. When a poet described a campaign, he gave it an interpretation: the ruler's triumph was divine will, his enemies were wicked or foolish, and his generosity was boundless. Such verse helped constitute sovereignty by giving a ruler of slave origin a language in which kingship appeared natural. At the same time, panegyric is not documentary evidence. Its exaggerations are conventional, and historians use it to understand ideals and self-presentation while checking factual claims against other sources.

The Ghaznavids were major patrons of Persian letters despite the Turkic origins of their dynasty, and specialists emphasize the exceptional prominence of Persian in their court culture. This fact illustrates a central point of the chapter: language and culture are not the same as ancestry. Poets writing in Persian came from Khurasan, Sistan, and other regions; the patrons were Turkic soldiers and Iranian officials; and the audience included people of many backgrounds. The pattern shows that Persian could function as a language of prestige beyond any single ethnic group.

Persian was not alone. Arabic remained the language of religious scholarship, theology, law, philosophy, and much formal historical writing, as the careers of al-Utbi and al-Biruni show. Writers working in Arabic also enjoyed Ghaznavid patronage, and the Arabic literary scholar al-Thaʿalibi, working in Nishapur, is reported to have dedicated a work to a member of the dynasty. The Ghaznavid court was therefore bilingual in function. It used Arabic for religion, scholarship, and international correspondence, and Persian increasingly for poetry, narrative prose, and the culture of the court.

Section 55.14

Ferdowsi, the Shahnameh, and the Reinterpretation of Iran's Pre-Islamic Past

Abu'l-Qasim Ferdowsi, from the Khurasan town of Tus, composed the Shahnameh ("Book of Kings"), a Persian epic of roughly fifty thousand couplets that tells the history of Iranian kingship from the mythical first kings through the heroic age of Rostam and the legendary dynasties to the fall of the Sasanian Empire (Chapters 32 and 39). The poem draws on older prose traditions, including a Persian prose Shahnameh compiled in Tus in the mid-tenth century and the work of the poet Daqiqi, who began a verse version and died before completing it. Ferdowsi began his work in the Samanid period and, according to the poem, completed a version around 1010. The conventional date of 1010 for completion is widely used but, like many details of Ferdowsi's life, rests on his own statements and later tradition.

The poem's themes include the nature of just kingship, the moral costs of power, the tragedies of fathers and sons, the heroism of warriors, and the fall of dynasties. It treats ancient Iran with a mixture of admiration and elegy, and it frames the Arab-Islamic conquest as a catastrophe while composing in a language and a cultural world that had become Islamic. Its literary Persian, with relatively few Arabic words, became a model. The epic therefore offers a way for a Muslim society to remember a pre-Islamic past without rejecting Islam, a process of preservation and transformation. It also shows that Iranian cultural memory was alive and adaptable in the eastern Islamic world, as the outline of this textbook requires.

Ferdowsi's relationship with the Ghaznavid court is often told as a story. In the best-known tradition, which appears in later sources such as the twelfth-century Chahar Maqala of Nizami ʿArudi, Ferdowsi presented his poem to Mahmud, expected a lavish reward, received less than promised, wrote a satire against the sultan, and died in disappointment. Modern literary scholarship treats these stories cautiously. The satire attributed to Ferdowsi is of doubtful authenticity, the account is attested only generations after the events, and it fits the pattern of legends about poets and patrons. What the poem itself shows is that Ferdowsi dedicated it to Mahmud, that he wrote in a world in which patronage mattered, and that he was a poet of Tus whose roots lay in the landed class of eastern Iran rather than in the Ghazni court. How closely Mahmud engaged with the poem, and what Ferdowsi received, cannot be established securely.

Equally uncertain is Ferdowsi's own religious identity, which has been debated. The poem contains Islamic piety and, for some readers, hints of Shiʿi sympathy, but no simple label is proven. Historians should read the Shahnameh as a literary work expressing ideals of kingship and memory, not as a reliable narrative of ancient events and not as a political manifesto of Iranian nationalism in the modern sense. The poem's own audience was a Persian-speaking, Muslim, courtly and landed society, and its meaning shifted across later centuries.

As a source, the epic tells historians about values, memory, and literary culture, not about what happened in ancient Iran. A methodological comparison with Ghaznavid panegyric is helpful: the court poets praised the living ruler and described current events in conventional terms, while the epic told a long story of earlier kings and let its moral lessons emerge through narrative. Both used poetry to articulate royal ideals, but for different purposes.

Section 55.15

Al-Biruni, Sanskrit Learning, and Intellectual Exchange with India

Abu Rayhan al-Biruni was born in 973 in Kath, in Khwarazm, and trained as a mathematician, astronomer, and scholar in the intellectual milieu of the eastern Islamic world. After Mahmud's conquest of Khwarazm in 1017, he came into the Ghaznavid orbit, as did other scholars, whether voluntarily or under pressure, and for years he worked under Ghaznavid patronage. Sources report that Mahmud also sought other scholars who preferred not to join the court, a reminder that patronage could shade into coercion. Al-Biruni spent time in the Ghaznavid domains in the northwestern Indian frontier and in Ghazni; his exact movements, and whether he accompanied armies, are uncertain.

His interests were vast. He wrote on chronology and calendars, astronomy, mathematics, geography, mineralogy, pharmacology, and comparative religion. A major astronomical handbook, the Qanun al-Masʿudi, was dedicated to Mahmud's son Masʿud. In the Punjab region he is credited with a measurement of the earth's size using methods of trigonometry, and he studied Sanskrit well enough to read Indian astronomical and philosophical texts, to work with Indian scholars, and, as he reports, to translate in both directions between Arabic and Sanskrit. His surviving study of India, the Kitab al-Hind (completed around 1030), is a systematic account of Indian religion, philosophy, science, geography, customs, and social institutions.

Al-Biruni's method was distinctive. He sought to describe Indian beliefs in terms of Indian sources and to explain unfamiliar ideas by comparison with Greek, Islamic, and other traditions, and he reported when he could not understand or verify something. He acknowledged linguistic difficulty and the cultural barriers between communities. He did not suppose that he was an impartial outsider: he wrote as a Muslim scholar, he categorized Indian ideas through frameworks familiar to his readers, and he relied on informants whose views were partial and whose willingness to share knowledge may have been limited by the political context.

The relation between conquest and knowledge is the heart of the matter. Al-Biruni's work would hardly have been possible without the political conditions created by Ghaznavid expansion, which gave him access to the Punjab and to Indian scholars. Yet his scholarship cannot be reduced to a by-product of conquest. It reflects his own curiosity, his training, and a longer tradition of Islamic scientific and comparative inquiry (Chapter 46). He also recognized the damage the campaigns had done. The history of imperial expansion and the history of knowledge transmission intersected without being identical, and a responsible account keeps them distinct.

Section 55.16

Ghazni, Palaces, Architecture, Scholarship, and the Geography of Persianate Culture

Ghazni was an imperial capital designed to impress. Written sources describe palaces, gardens, mosques, and ceremonial spaces where the ruler received envoys, distributed robes, and presided over court festivals. The archaeological record is more limited. Much of what survives dates from the later Ghaznavid period, especially the early twelfth century, including a palace complex of Masʿud III with carved marble panels and inscriptions, and two minarets associated with Masʿud III and Bahram Shah. The traditional tomb of Mahmud at Ghazni has been rebuilt repeatedly, so the present structure should not be taken as an eleventh-century building. Excavations at Lashkari Bazar near Bust revealed a large palace and bazaar complex of the Ghaznavid period, and they are among the most important sources for the dynasty's architecture. Students should distinguish securely dated structures from remains attributed to later Ghaznavid phases.

Provincial centers shared in the culture. Herat, Balkh, and Bust had mosques, schools, and workshops, and Nishapur was a major seat of religious learning. Metalwork, textiles, ceramics, and carved stone show the craft traditions of the region. Inscriptions in Arabic and Persian appear on buildings and objects, and the use of Persian verse in the decoration of a later palace is an early example of Persian in monumental inscription. These remains support the written evidence that Ghazni was a place where political representation, scholarship, and craft production met.

Arabic and Persian both flourished. Theology, law, philosophy, and the sciences were largely studied in Arabic, and Ghaznavid rulers supported religious scholars of different schools. Rulers promoted Hanafi and, according to some sources, favored the Karramiyya, a pietist group influential in the east, while engaging with Shafiʿi scholars, and religious policy changed with circumstances. The expansion of Persian as a literary and administrative language did not eliminate Arabic intellectual life; both languages occupied overlapping and complementary domains.

Persianate institutions extended beyond the plateau through networks of officials, poets, religious scholars, Sufis, artisans, and military patrons. Ghaznavid Lahore, as the epilogue will show, became a center of Persian poetry and Sufi learning, and the movement of people along the roads from Ghazni to the Punjab carried the language and its institutions into northern India. This process prepared the way for the wider spread of Persian as an administrative and literary language across Central and South Asia in later centuries, a development that lies beyond the scope of this volume but whose roots are visible here.

Historical Debate

How Did the Ghaznavids Expand the Persianate World?

The question: Was the diffusion of Persianate culture a result of royal patronage and bureaucracy, of military conquest and mobility, or of adaptation by local elites?

Interpretation A — Patronage and bureaucracy: The Ghaznavid court's support for Persian poets, historians, and secretaries, and the use of Persian in administration, created institutions that attracted talented people and set models for other courts.
Interpretation B — Conquest and mobility: Military expansion carried officials, soldiers, and scholars into new territories, and conquest created the political space in which Persianate institutions could take root in Afghanistan and the Punjab.
Interpretation C — Adaptation by local elites: Local rulers, scribes, and merchants adopted Persianate practices because they brought prestige, administrative utility, or access to wider networks, so the diffusion depended on choices made within the receiving societies.

Evidence: Court histories, administrative records, bilingual coinage, the careers of officials and poets, and the later history of Lahore all bear on the question. Conquest may have been important without being sufficient: Persian culture also spread through trade, religion, and the prestige of established models.

Student task: Argue whether military conquest was necessary, sufficient, both, or neither to explain the spread of Persianate culture, using at least three pieces of evidence.

Mahmud's Western Campaigns, Khwarazm, and the Changing Balance in Iran

The Ghaznavid state was not oriented solely toward India. Mahmud's western policy was equally consequential, and it set the stage for the later confrontation with the Seljuks. In 1017 he extended Ghaznavid control to Khwarazm, the fertile delta of the Oxus, then ruled by the Maʾmunid dynasty, which had ties to Mahmud by marriage. After the Maʾmunid ruler was killed by his own army, Mahmud intervened, installed a governor from his own commanders, and annexed the region. The conquest brought scholars such as al-Biruni into the Ghaznavid orbit and gave the dynasty a strategic position on the Oxus.

Relations with the Qarakhanids shaped these priorities. The Oxus boundary agreed after 999 was never fully stable, and Mahmud's strategic concern for the north constrained how much of his army could be committed elsewhere. Diplomacy, marriage, and the occasional meeting between rulers kept the two states from continuous war, but each watched the other. The Qarakhanids also faced their own succession struggles, which sometimes weakened them and at other times pushed ambitious rulers toward Khurasan.

Mahmud's most prominent western conquest came in 1029, when he took Rayy, the major city in the northern Iranian plateau near modern Tehran, from the Buyid ruler Majd al-Dawla. Buyid history is treated in Chapter 53, and it is necessary here only to note that the Buyid state in the Jibal region had been weakened by rivalry among family members and by the death of the long-time regent, Sayyida (Shirin). Mahmud captured the Buyid prince, and his forces moved into adjoining territories, including parts of the Jibal. Sources report that he suppressed groups regarded as heretical and destroyed books in Rayy, accounts that reflect both the religious policy of the time and the vantage of sources hostile or sympathetic to him.

Sunni political claims helped justify western expansion, since Mahmud could present himself as the caliph's defender against Shiʿi Buyid dominance. Yet theology did not exhaust the matter. The Jibal were wealthy, strategically located, and tied to routes toward Baghdad and Azerbaijan, and control of them strengthened the Ghaznavid position against both the Buyids and the rising Turkmen groups. The expansion also stretched the empire. Governing a state extending from Rayy to Lahore, with communications running over mountains and deserts, would test any administration, and it left Mahmud's successor with a very wide frontier and a limited reserve of loyal troops.

Section 55.18

The Succession of Masʿud I and the Problems of Imperial Overextension

Mahmud died in 1030. As in 998, the succession was contested. Mahmud had apparently preferred his son Muhammad, who was at Ghazni, while Masʿud, the abler and better-known commander, was in the west at Isfahan, which he had taken from the local Kakuyid ruler. Officers at Ghazni raised Muhammad to the throne, but Masʿud marched east, and within months had overthrown his brother and taken power. The episode again showed the importance of the army's loyalty, and Masʿud's rewards to his followers, and his resentments against officials who had supported his brother, shaped the politics of his reign. The dismissal and execution of the vizier Hasanak, approximately 1031–1032, belongs to this atmosphere.

Masʿud inherited a very large empire. Khwarazm was governed by a powerful commander, Altuntash, whose son Harun later rebelled; Rayy and the Jibal needed a strong garrison; the Punjab governors, such as Ahmad Niyaltigin, acted with considerable independence and later fell out with the sultan; and the mountain frontiers of Ghur needed attention. Masʿud continued the campaigns in India, including the capture of Hansi in 1037, but his main concern was Khurasan, where a new challenge had emerged.

That challenge came from Oghuz Turkic groups, the Seljuks and their followers, who had been moving into northern Khurasan. As Chapter 52 noted, steppe peoples had long moved into Islamic lands as soldiers and migrants. The Seljuks, led by Tughril and Chaghri, were pastoralists with mobile cavalry who sought pasture and a political position. Mahmud himself had earlier taken action against one Seljuk leader, Arslan Isra'il, and resettled Oghuz groups in Khurasan, a measure that arguably created problems rather than solving them. During the 1030s, Oghuz and Seljuk raiders attacked Khurasan, and Ghaznavid governors proved unable to contain them. The Seljuks occupied Merv and Nishapur in 1037–1038, and Tughril was proclaimed ruler in Nishapur. The mature Seljuk political formation belongs to Chapter 57.

It would be a mistake to describe the Seljuk advance as inevitable or to blame the Ghaznavid defeat only on Masʿud's personal decisions. The empire faced fronts in several directions, its army was stretched, its fiscal system depended on Khurasan, and the Seljuks possessed mobility and local support among some Turkmen and, at times, disaffected local populations. Bayhaqi's history, written from inside the court, shows the interplay of leadership, administrative judgment, military finance, and the difficulties of governing widely separated territories. It also shows that contemporaries debated strategy, and that those debates, including whether to fight a decisive battle or to wear down the enemy, were genuinely open.

Section 55.19

The Battle of Dandanqan, 1040, and the Reorientation of Ghaznavid Power

By 1039–1040 the strategic situation in Khurasan had become critical. The Seljuks held Nishapur and Merv, Ghaznavid recovery attempts had failed, and Masʿud decided to lead a large army from Herat into the northwest. The Seljuk leaders employed a strategy suited to their strengths: they avoided a pitched battle at first, used their mobile cavalry to harass the Ghaznavid columns, and exploited the heat and the scarcity of water and supplies on the road. The Ghaznavid army, with its elephants, baggage, and heavy organization, suffered from supply difficulties and exhaustion.

The decisive confrontation took place in May 1040 near Dandanqan, a fortified place between Sarakhs and Merv in the desert fringe of Khurasan. Accounts agree that the Ghaznavid army, weakened by thirst and strain, fought the Seljuks and collapsed. Details of the battle, including its sequence, the role of individual commanders, and the behavior of particular units, vary among the sources, and Bayhaqi's version, though detailed, is shaped by his view of the sultan's decisions. What is clear is that Masʿud's army was routed, and that the Seljuks, under Tughril, Chaghri, and their kinsmen, took control of Khurasan.

The political consequences were swift. Masʿud retreated to Ghazni, abandoned thoughts of recovering the west, and in late 1040 attempted to move his treasury and household toward India, probably intending to continue the dynasty from the Punjab. In early 1041 his troops mutinied during the journey, deposed him, and restored his brother Muhammad; Masʿud was killed shortly afterward. His son Mawdud avenged his father and took power, reigning until 1049. The speed of these events illustrates the vulnerability of a state whose stability rested on the personal success of the ruler and the loyalty of the army.

Dandanqan marked a transformation of the regional balance, not the extinction of the dynasty. The Ghaznavids lost Khurasan, Khwarazm, and their western territories, and with them the agricultural and fiscal core of their empire. They kept Ghazni, eastern Afghanistan, Bust, parts of Sistan for a time, and the Punjab. The new frontier with the Seljuks hardened along the mountains and plains of Khurasan and Tokharistan, and later Ghaznavid rulers made peace with the Seljuks and recognized their superiority. The Seljuks, rather than the Ghaznavids, would now dominate the Iranian plateau, a transformation traced in Chapter 57.

Section 55.20

Historical Synthesis: The Ghaznavids and the Expansion of the Persianate World

The Ghaznavids transformed a Turkic-led military principality into an empire whose political institutions, administrative practices, literary patronage, and scholarly networks expanded the influence of Persianate civilization across eastern Iran, Afghanistan, and northern India. Their achievements were inseparable from military coercion, regional collaboration, fiscal extraction, and the adaptation of earlier Islamic and Iranian institutions. The dynasty's military ancestry lay in the system of slave soldiers that the Samanids and Abbasids had developed, but the institutions through which it governed were Persian-speaking bureaucracy, Islamic law, and a court culture of poets and scholars. The distinction between where a dynasty comes from and how it rules is one of the chapter's central lessons.

The Ghaznavid empire cannot be described as exclusively Turkic or exclusively Persian. It combined military households, Iranian administrators, Islamic political claims, local rulers, and the diverse societies they governed. The campaigns in South Asia involved raiding, plunder, tribute, and, in the Punjab, durable government, and they produced violence and loss as well as cultural contact. The patronage of Ferdowsi's epic tradition, court poets, and al-Biruni's scholarship coexisted with fiscal pressure and warfare. Persianate expansion was therefore not the migration of Persian-speaking populations or the imposition of one ethnic identity. It was a historical process involving courts, military institutions, written administration, literature, education, religious networks, commerce, and local adaptation.

Epilogue: The later Ghaznavids. After 1040 the dynasty continued in Ghazni and the Punjab. Mawdud's successors, and especially Ibrahim (r. 1059–1099), made peace with the Seljuks, and the dynasty entered a long phase of reduced but stable rule. Ghazni remained a center of culture. Under later rulers, including Masʿud III and Bahram Shah, the court patronized poets such as Sanaʾi, and Lahore grew in importance as a second capital, producing the poet Masʿud-i Saʿd-i Salman and hosting the Sufi writer Hujwiri, author of an early Persian treatise on Sufism. In 1150–1151 the Ghurid ruler ʿAla al-Din Husayn sacked Ghazni, and the dynasty withdrew to Lahore, where Khusraw Malik, the last Ghaznavid, was overthrown by the Ghurid Muʿizz al-Din Muhammad in 1186. The loss of Khurasan in 1040 and the end of the dynasty in 1186 are separated by almost a century and a half and should not be conflated. The later South Asian sultanates, including the Delhi Sultanate, were different political formations and cannot be treated as simple continuations of the Ghaznavid state.

The chapters that follow take up the consequences. Chapter 56 turns to the Armenian, Kurdish, Byzantine, and northern frontier worlds, whose political diversity preceded Seljuk expansion. Chapter 57 narrates the Seljuk migrations and the formation of the Great Seljuk Empire, in which the victors of Dandanqan reorganized the eastern Islamic world. Chapter 58 examines the Persianate government and Sunni revival associated with Nizam al-Mulk, where the administrative precedents established under the Samanids and Ghaznavids will be recognizable.

Cause and Effect

The Loss of Khurasan, 1040

Long-term causes

A military system dependent on a successful ruler and booty, a fiscal base concentrated in Khurasan, and a vast territory with long lines of communication.

Short-term causes

Succession disputes in 1030, wars on multiple fronts, mounting Seljuk and Oghuz pressure in the 1030s, and supply and strategic failures in 1039–1040.

Defeat at Dandanqan, the loss of Khurasan and Khwarazm, the overthrow and death of Masʿud, and a political crisis at Ghazni.

Chapter Summary

The Ghaznavids emerged from the Samanid military and political environment. Alptigin's withdrawal to Ghazni in 962–963 after a succession dispute created a base, and Sebuktigin, a former slave soldier, turned it into a principality that served the Samanids while expanding on its own account. The geography of the Hindu Kush and the Ghazni plateau, with its mountain passes, silver, and trade routes, gave the dynasty resources and strategic options that a provincial commander elsewhere would have lacked.

Mahmud's accession in 998 and the collapse of Samanid government in 999 created a new eastern order divided between the Ghaznavids and the Qarakhanids along the Oxus. Mahmud inherited not only Khurasan but the Samanid administration and its personnel. He sought and received recognition from the Abbasid caliph, whose honors supported his legitimacy without giving the caliph control, and he used the language of Sunni kingship and ghaza to present his rule, though religious rhetoric was only one element in his policies.

The Ghaznavid army combined slave soldiers, free retainers, local infantry, Indian contingents, and allied troops, supported by cavalry, siege weapons, and elephants. The system gave the ruler loyal cadres but made succession dependent on the army. Administration followed Islamic and Samanid models, with viziers, diwans, secretaries, and judges, and Persian-speaking officials played a central role while Arabic retained its place in religion, scholarship, and diplomacy.

The state was financed by land taxes, commercial revenue, tribute, booty, and confiscation. Military success brought treasure, but the fiscal system was vulnerable to defeat and loss of the Khurasan tax base. Cities, trade routes, and the diverse population of cultivators, craftsmen, merchants, soldiers, and enslaved people underpinned the state, though sources for ordinary lives are limited.

In South Asia, the Ghaznavids defeated the Hindu Shahis, who were capable political opponents, and gradually established durable government in the Punjab centered on Lahore, while launching expeditions to northern and western India that included the plunder of temples. Historians distinguish raiding, tribute, occupation, and durable government, and they compare Persian and Arabic chronicles with Sanskrit and other evidence. The Somnath expedition illustrates how events acquire layers of meaning in sources and later politics.

Persian court poetry, Ferdowsi's Shahnameh, and al-Biruni's scholarship show the cultural vitality of the Ghaznavid world. Patronage mattered, but legends about it, such as the story of Ferdowsi and Mahmud, must be treated with caution. Al-Biruni's study of India was possible because of the political context but cannot be reduced to it. The spread of Persianate institutions through officials, poets, scholars, and military patrons reached beyond the Iranian plateau.

Mahmud's western expansion to Khwarazm and Rayy showed that the empire was not oriented solely to India, but it also overextended a state with a narrow fiscal base. Masʿud's succession, wars on multiple fronts, and the Seljuk challenge in Khurasan culminated in the defeat at Dandanqan in 1040, which cost the dynasty its western territories and ultimately Masʿud's throne and life.

The dynasty did not end in 1040. It continued in Ghazni and the Punjab, with Lahore growing in importance, until the Ghurids sacked Ghazni in 1150–1151 and took Lahore in 1186. The distinction between the loss of Khurasan and the end of the dynasty is essential to understanding the Ghaznavid legacy.

The chapter's central argument is that the Ghaznavids transformed a Turkic-led military principality into an empire whose institutions, patronage, and scholarly networks extended Persianate civilization into Afghanistan and northern India. Persianate expansion was a process involving courts, armies, writing, religion, commerce, and local adaptation, not an ethnic migration. Its achievements and its coercion belong to the same history, and the chapters that follow trace what the Seljuks and their successors made of this inheritance.

MGU Library Research Project

Reading the Ghaznavids through Competing Sources

This project asks you to compare how different sources present a single Ghaznavid episode and to evaluate what each can and cannot tell historians.

  1. Choose an episode. For example, the defeat of Jayapala in 1001, the Somnath expedition, the execution of Hasanak, or the battle of Dandanqan.
  2. Collect sources. Locate accounts by at least three kinds of sources, such as an Arabic court history, a Persian history, a poet, an inscription or coin, and a modern specialist study.
  3. Analyze each source. Identify its author, audience, language, date, and purpose, and note what it emphasizes and omits.
  4. Compare and evaluate. Where do the sources agree? Where do they differ? Which claims can be corroborated, and which depend on a single source?
  5. Write your findings. In a 3–5 page essay, present a reasoned account of the episode that distinguishes evidence, inference, and later tradition.

Library resources to use: translated primary sources, Bosworth's studies, the Encyclopaedia Iranica, and Thapar's Somanatha.

Chapter Reflection

The Ghaznavid story matters because it shows how a regional order could be reshaped without being replaced. A dynasty born from the military slave system of the Samanids became the patron of Persian poetry, the employer of Persian secretaries, and the sponsor of one of the great scholars of the age. Cultural identity, in other words, proved more flexible than ancestry, and institutions proved more durable than the rulers who inherited them.

The chapter also shows that expansion and culture were bound together without being the same thing. The campaigns in South Asia brought treasure, tribute, and government to the Ghaznavids and brought violence and loss to the communities they struck, while the same routes carried scholars, poets, and officials. Honest history holds both facts in view and resists narratives that turn one dimension into the whole story.

The defeat at Dandanqan in 1040 marks the transition to a new phase. The Seljuks, whom Chapter 57 will examine, took over the Iranian heartland and adopted much of the Persianate administrative and cultural inheritance that the Ghaznavids and Samanids had developed. The continuing life of the Ghaznavids in Ghazni and Lahore, and the later use of Persian across Central and South Asia, show that Persianate civilization did not depend on any single dynasty.

For the larger history of the Middle East, the lesson is that the region's states were not simply ethnic or religious blocs. They were composite systems of soldiers, officials, scholars, merchants, and cultivators, linked by languages, institutions, and routes that crossed political frontiers. The Ghaznavids are a vivid example, and the chapters ahead will return to similar patterns under the Seljuks, the Mongols, and the early modern empires.
