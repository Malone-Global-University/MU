Part X — Conquest, Civil War, and the Umayyad Empire

Uthman, Ali, and the First Fitna

A History of the Middle East: From Ancient Civilizations to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

In June 656, in a house in Medina, the third caliph of the Muslim community was killed by fellow Muslims. Within months, companions of the Prophet were leading armies against one another.

The event had no precedent. Medina was no ordinary city: it had been Muḥammad’s refuge and the center of the community, and the caliph was the successor to its first leaders. His killing by a group that included men from the new garrison cities of Egypt and Iraq showed how far the center’s authority had been eroded by the empire’s own growth.

The story is usually told as a drama of personalities: the pious but weak ʿUthmān, the principled ʿAlī, the shrewd Muʿāwiya. This chapter instead asks what institutions failed or were strained. Chapter 39 traced the conquests. Their results included revenue, stipends, new cities, and armies whose leaders expected a voice. Medina’s small circle of early Companions could not easily satisfy these expectations.

The sources complicate every step. They were written after the war, in an age of competing doctrines, and they often disagree about who did what. The chapter therefore treats narration itself as evidence.

It follows the sequence from ʿUthmān’s selection in 644 to the settlement of 661, with attention to Kūfa, Baṣra, Egypt, and Syria. It ends where Chapter 41 begins, with Muʿāwiya’s government. Karbalāʾ, in 680, belongs to Chapter 43, and later legal and theological developments to Chapter 47.

The chapter also takes the sources seriously as historical objects. The Arabic narratives of al-Balādhurī and al-Ṭabarī were written long after the events, while the Armenian Sebeos and the Syriac Maronite Chronicle give brief near-contemporary glimpses from outside the Muslim community. Placing these side by side shows both how much is known and how much remains uncertain.

Finally, the chapter argues that the First Fitna should not be read as an early version of later divisions. The followers of ʿAlī, the supporters of ʿUthmān, the Syrians, and the dissenters at Ḥarūrāʾ were political and religious coalitions in formation. Their disputes would be reinterpreted by later Sunni, Shiʿi, and Khārijite traditions, but in 656 those traditions did not yet exist in settled form.

Historical Context

Muḥammad’s death in 632 left the community without a universally agreed succession procedure; reports about earlier indications of leadership were interpreted differently. Abū Bakr (632–634) and ʿUmar (634–644) preserved its unity and directed the conquests described in Chapter 39, which brought Syria, Iraq, Egypt, and much of Iran under Muslim rule within about two decades and ended the Byzantine-Sasanian regional order. Chapter 38 examined the first succession and the Ridda wars, in which Abū Bakr reasserted the authority of Medina over Arabian tribes.

The conquests created new institutions. ʿUmar organized the dīwān, a register of Muslim fighters who received stipends from the conquered lands’ revenue, and settled them in garrison cities. Provincial governors, many with their own armies and connections, ran the provinces, often through the existing local administrations. The arrangement worked while the conquests continued and while ʿUmar’s personal authority held it together.

By 644 the community that had once fit inside Medina directed several provinces with different populations and resources. Tribal contingents from Arabia, who had settled in Iraq, Egypt, and Syria, had developed their own leaders and interests, and the questions of how revenue should be divided, who should govern, and what limited a caliph’s authority had no settled answers. The Qurʾān and the Prophet’s example offered principles, but no procedure for selecting or removing a successor.

This chapter begins with the selection of ʿUthmān in 644 and ends in 661, when Muʿāwiya became the single caliph. The Umayyad government that followed is the subject of Chapter 41, the long-term social transformation of the caliphate is treated in Chapter 42, the renewed civil war after 680 in Chapter 43, and the doctrinal development of Sunni, Shiʿi, and Khārijite traditions in Chapter 47.

Division I

ʿUthmān’s Accession and the Government of an Expanding Empire

40.1 Reconstructing the First Fitna: Sources, Historical Memory, and Competing Traditions

No continuous narrative of the First Fitna survives from the years 656–661. The detailed account depends on Arabic works compiled in the ninth and early tenth centuries, above all al-Balādhurī’s Ansāb al-ashrāf and al-Ṭabarī’s History of the Prophets and Kings. Both authors gather reports from earlier transmitters and frequently preserve conflicting versions rather than choosing among them. Their reports are generally introduced by chains of narrators (isnāds), and the identity of a transmitter is itself evidence: it points to a region, a school of storytelling, and often a political loyalty.

Several transmitters matter especially for this chapter. Abū Mikhnaf (d. c. 774), from Kūfa, collected the memory of Iraqi partisans of ʿAlī. Sayf ibn ʿUmar (d. c. 796) produced a version favorable to the Companions generally and to the Medinan establishment, and many modern historians regard his distinctive material with caution. Medinan scholars, among them al-Wāqidī (d. 823), preserved other traditions. Naṣr ibn Muzāḥim’s Waqʿat Ṣiffīn is a pro-ʿAlid, Kūfan account of the battle and its documents. Later Sunni and Shiʿi writers read all this through the doctrinal debates of their own time, and the war itself became a source of argument over who had been right.

Evidence from outside the Muslim tradition is brief but valuable. The Armenian history attributed to Sebeos was written within a generation of the events and treats the civil war from an outside viewpoint. The Syriac Maronite Chronicle, compiled after 664, probably in the later seventh century, and surviving in a damaged manuscript, offers a perspective generally favorable to Muʿāwiya and offers a date for his acclamation at Jerusalem. Its chronology does not match the standard Arabic sequence. It places that acclamation under the year AG 971 (660/61), and scholars have noted that it appears to date ʿAlī’s death earlier than the Arabic tradition does and to place his killing at Ḥīra rather than Kūfa, which is not necessarily an error as Ḥīra lay near Kūfa. Historians such as Marsham have observed that chronological problems beset the Arabic tradition too. This chapter therefore does not harmonize the two timelines silently; where they differ, the difference is noted.

Documentary evidence is thin. Papyri, early coins, and Qurʾān manuscripts help with questions of administration and text, but not with the narrative of the war. For most episodes the historian must therefore weigh a report by its transmitter, its likely audience, whether independent traditions agree, and whether it serves an obvious argument.

40.2 The Death of ʿUmar, the Consultative Council, and ʿUthmān’s Selection in 644

In November 644 ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb was stabbed during dawn prayer in Medina by Abū Luʾluʾa, a slave of Persian origin, and died several days later. He had not named a successor. The tradition says that he appointed a council (shūrā) of six senior Companions from Quraysh to choose among themselves: ʿUthmān ibn ʿAffān, ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib, Ṭalḥa ibn ʿUbayd Allāh, al-Zubayr ibn al-ʿAwwām, Saʿd ibn Abī Waqqāṣ, and ʿAbd al-Raḥmān ibn ʿAwf. These men were early converts and men of standing, and several were wealthy.

According to the usual account, ʿAbd al-Raḥmān withdrew from the candidacy and spent days consulting the council’s members and the city’s residents, including the commanders and tribal leaders who were in Medina. He then asked ʿAlī and ʿUthmān whether they would rule according to the Qurʾān, the practice of the Prophet, and the precedent of Abū Bakr and ʿUmar. In the familiar story ʿUthmān agreed, while ʿAlī answered that he would follow his own judgment, and ʿAbd al-Raḥmān gave his pledge to ʿUthmān.

The details of this episode were told later by people who knew that ʿUthmān’s rule ended in disaster and who had views about ʿAlī’s claims, so they should not be read as a neutral record. What is more securely established is that a small circle of senior Meccan Companions decided the matter in Medina, that ʿUthmān, then aged about seventy, a rich member of the Umayya clan and an early convert, was accepted by the community in Medina, and that the provinces then accepted him as well. The procedure did not itself produce a rule for future successions, a point that Chapter 38 raised for the earlier transitions and that this crisis would make acute.

40.3 Caliphal Government after the Conquests: Authority, Revenue, and Imperial Responsibilities

ʿUthmān took charge of an empire very different from the one that Abū Bakr had inherited. The conquests of Syria, Iraq, Egypt, and much of Iran, described in Chapter 39, were still advancing in his early years, and they added revenue and responsibilities faster than institutions could grow. The caliph ruled through governors (ʿummāl, singular ʿāmil) whom he appointed, who commanded provincial armies, supervised prayer and justice, and in many cases controlled revenue. Beneath them the administration frequently continued in the languages and with the officials of the Byzantine and Sasanian systems, for example Greek and Coptic in Egypt and Middle Persian in Iran, while Arab governors added military oversight.

Revenue came from tribute, land taxes, personal levies, and a share of booty. Later sources commonly describe some of these revenues with the terms kharāj and jizya, although practices and terminology were not yet uniform across the conquered provinces. ʿUmar had organized the distribution of income through the dīwān, a register of Muslim fighters, with annual stipends (ʿaṭāʾ) graded according to early service in the Prophet’s cause or in the conquests. He had also, according to the tradition, kept much conquered land in the hands of the local cultivators under taxation, holding the income as the common property of the community, rather than dividing the land among the conquerors.

Distance mattered. Letters and couriers took days or weeks between Medina and the garrison cities, and the caliph relied on a small circle of advisers drawn from the early Companions. ʿUmar’s personal authority had held this arrangement together. ʿUthmān’s authority was different. He was a respected early Muslim, but without the same reputation for stern supervision, and his government faced provinces whose military leaders had developed their own interests.

40.4 The New Provincial Societies: Tribal Settlement, Military Stipends, and Political Competition

The conquered territories were organized around garrison cities (amṣār, singular miṣr). Kūfa and Baṣra in Iraq were founded in the late 630s as camps for tribal contingents who had fought in the conquest, Fusṭāṭ in Egypt arose near the old fortress of Babylon after 641, and in Syria troops were settled in existing towns, including Damascus, Ḥimṣ, and Palestine’s military districts. Placing soldiers in separate cities, rather than among the conquered populations, kept them under their commanders’ supervision and allowed the state to pay them from the dīwān.

Within these cities, tribal solidarity (ʿaṣabiyya) and leadership by tribal nobles (ashrāf) shaped politics. Early arrivals and veterans of the first campaigns claimed precedence, while later migrants, younger men, and clients (mawālī) saw it differently. Rank in the register decided pay, and a governor who altered it could create enemies. Land and the revenue of the surrounding districts were a further source of competition, because tribal leaders could receive grants of estates or revenue rights.

The provinces also differed. Syria, a frontier against the Byzantines, kept its armies on campaign and under one governor, Muʿāwiya ibn Abī Sufyān, who had governed since about 639. Iraq’s two garrisons were more turbulent and had governors replaced frequently. Egypt’s military was smaller and more closely tied to a rich agricultural province whose revenue interested Medina. These differences would shape how each province responded to the crisis.

Historical Vocabulary
Fitna

The Arabic word fitna means trial, testing, or temptation, and in later usage came to denote strife and civil war within the community. Naming the war “the First Fitna” reflects later reflection that Muslim armies had fought one another. The word carries moral judgment, and modern historians use it as a convenient label rather than as a neutral description.

Bayʿa

A pledge of allegiance, often performed by a handclasp, by which a leader’s authority was recognized. Its voluntariness was a central issue in disputes over ʿUthmān and ʿAlī.

Division II

ʿUthmān’s Government, Provincial Grievances, and the Growth of Opposition

40.5 ʿUthmān’s Governors, the Umayyad Family, and the Politics of Appointment

Appointment of governors was the caliph’s most important political tool, and it became the main point of criticism of ʿUthmān. According to the tradition, he replaced several governors who had served under ʿUmar with men from his own clan or with associates. Muʿāwiya, already in place in Syria and himself of the Umayya clan, received wider authority. ʿAbd Allāh ibn ʿĀmir, a young maternal cousin, was put in charge of Baṣra in the late 640s, often dated to 649–650. ʿAbd Allāh ibn Saʿd ibn Abī Sarḥ, ʿUthmān’s foster brother, took over Egypt. Al-Walīd ibn ʿUqba, a maternal half-brother, and Saʿīd ibn al-ʿĀṣ, an Umayyad, governed Kūfa in turn. Marwān ibn al-Ḥakam, a cousin, became his close secretary.

Later critics, especially in Iraq, called this nepotism, and the charge became a central theme in accounts of the opposition. Yet the word may mislead. Rulers in this period commonly relied on kin whom they trusted, and the caliph could argue that his relatives were competent and loyal. Some, such as ʿAbd Allāh ibn ʿĀmir and ʿAbd Allāh ibn Saʿd, led successful campaigns, and Muʿāwiya’s record in Syria was strong. The question was less whether the appointees were able than whether the pattern gave one Qurashī clan a monopoly on provincial power at the expense of other early Muslim families and the tribal leaders of the garrisons, and whether the caliph kept enough independence from his relatives.

The sources add another dimension. Many of the Umayyads had opposed Muḥammad until the conquest of Mecca, and some of the early Companions viewed their rise with resentment. Modern historians are cautious about reading this as a fixed rivalry between clans, because the sources are retrospective and the political alignments were fluid, but the resentment is real in the narratives.

40.6 Kūfa: Land, Military Precedence, Governors, and Provincial Opposition

Kūfa was founded about 638 as a garrison for tribal contingents who had fought in Iraq, and it quickly became the most important center of military and political life there. Its wealth was tied to the Sawād, the fertile lowland of southern Iraq, whose tax revenue was distributed through the dīwān. Soldiers’ pay, the status of tribal leaders, and the control of land and revenue therefore depended on who governed.

The reported sequence of governors illustrates the instability. Under ʿUmar the city had been governed by Saʿd ibn Abī Waqqāṣ and later by others; ʿUthmān reappointed Saʿd at the start of his reign, an appointment that was separate from his earlier service under ʿUmar. Saʿd was soon removed, in the tradition after a dispute with ʿAbd Allāh ibn Masʿūd, the keeper of the Kūfan treasury, over a loan from public funds; the details of this explanation come from transmitted accounts and cannot be independently confirmed. Al-Walīd ibn ʿUqba, the caliph’s half-brother, governed from about 645–646 until about 649–650. He was accused of drunkenness and, according to the reports, was brought to Medina and punished, a story that opponents used against ʿUthmān.

Saʿīd ibn al-ʿĀṣ then governed until 655. According to the Kūfan tradition, he lost the support of the city’s leaders, among them Mālik al-Ashtar, who prevented him from re-entering and wrote to the caliph asking for Abū Mūsā al-Ashʿarī, a veteran who had governed Baṣra earlier. ʿUthmān agreed. Whatever the accuracy of individual details, the episode suggests that Kūfa’s notables could bargain with Medina and could take political action. A recent study of the appointments and their kinship connections stresses how closely governors’ careers were tied to networks of relatives and clients.

Kūfan opposition also had religious dimensions. Kūfa held a large community of reciters of the Qurʾān and the memory of Ibn Masʿūd, whose version of the text would be displaced by the standardization described in §40.10. Some Kūfans objected to Qurashī precedence, to the loss of control over local revenue, and to what they considered an unjust distribution of resources. These strands did not always coincide, but together they made Kūfa the center of the provincial opposition.

40.7 Baṣra: Tribal Politics, Expansion, and the Distribution of Imperial Resources

Baṣra, founded about 636 near the head of the Gulf, differed from Kūfa. It drew contingents from other tribal groups, was closer to the Persian frontier, and acted as the base for the campaigns in Fārs, Kirmān, Sijistān, and Khurāsān. Abū Mūsā al-Ashʿarī governed it into the late 640s, and the young ʿAbd Allāh ibn ʿĀmir took over around 649–650 in one influential reconstruction, though transmitted chronologies differ. The tradition credits him with leading successful advances in Iran, including the capture of Iranian cities and the final pursuit of the last Sasanian king Yazdgird III, who was killed in 651.

The result for Baṣra was a prosperity based on expansion. Conquests brought booty, land, and new tax revenue, and gave ambitious tribal leaders opportunities for command. Baṣrans also developed a rival sense of precedence and some of their leaders were prominent in later politics. Opposition to ʿUthmān is less evident in the sources than in Kūfa, and some Baṣran leaders were willing to support his cause, or to demand vengeance after his death. This difference helps explain why Baṣra became the destination of the coalition led by ʿĀʾisha, Ṭalḥa, and al-Zubayr in 656: it offered a potential base of supporters who would not necessarily back ʿAlī.

40.8 Egypt: Provincial Government, ʿAbd Allāh ibn Saʿd, and the Growth of Dissent

Egypt’s Greek and Coptic administration continued after the conquest, with Fusṭāṭ as the Muslim garrison. The conqueror, ʿAmr ibn al-ʿĀṣ, governed under ʿUmar. In ʿUthmān’s reign his authority was divided: ʿAbd Allāh ibn Saʿd ibn Abī Sarḥ, who had held command in Upper Egypt, was given responsibility for fiscal matters and subsequently the whole province. The traditions say a quarrel over revenue between the two preceded the change. The reports also say that ʿAmr was reinstated briefly to deal with the Byzantine attempt to retake Alexandria in about 645–646 and then removed again. The sequence is known from differing traditions and the exact details remain uncertain.

ʿAbd Allāh ibn Saʿd commanded campaigns against Nubia, which ended in a treaty traditionally dated to the early 650s, and at sea, notably at the Battle of the Masts in 655, in which the Egyptian fleet faced a Byzantine force. A recent argument based on the Armenian history attributed to Sebeos and on an Arabic poem suggests that a failed expedition against Constantinople around 654 may have angered Egyptian soldiers. This hypothesis remains debated, since Arabic prose sources hardly mention such an event.

Complaints against the governor concerned the revenue of the province and the treatment of soldiers. Among those who opposed him were Muḥammad ibn Abī Ḥudhayfa, who had been raised in ʿUthmān’s household, and Muḥammad ibn Abī Bakr, a son of the first caliph. As Martin Hinds emphasized, interaction between provincial opposition and the discontent in Medina is the key to understanding the assassination, and the Egyptian movement was central to it. Fusṭāṭ’s soldiers sent letters to Medina and then a delegation, and in 656 they provided the largest contingent in the siege.

40.9 Land, Treasury, Stipends, and the Political Economy of Discontent

Grievances against ʿUthmān’s government shared a common structure: they were disputes over who controlled public resources. The first concerned conquered land. ʿUmar’s policy, as the tradition describes it, had kept the land under local cultivators and treated its tax as income of the community. The reports say that under ʿUthmān grants were made from conquered lands, especially in Iraq, to prominent Companions and tribal leaders, and that estates came into private hands. How extensive these grants were is uncertain, and later narrators had a motive to exaggerate them.

The second concerned revenue. Soldiers expected their stipends, and provinces expected a share of their own revenue to be spent locally. A caliph who sent funds to Medina, or who gave gifts to his relatives, could be accused of diverting community income. The traditions accuse ʿUthmān of generosity to his kin, including grants from the treasury, and of reserving pasture (ḥimā) for public use in a way that disadvantaged herders. His defenders replied that a caliph could use his discretion, that he drew on his own wealth, and that his gifts were within customary limits.

The third concerned precedence. Veterans of the earliest conquests, later arrivals, tribal leaders, and the Medinan Companions each had different claims. Religious language gave these disputes force. In a community that believed its resources belonged to God and its leader should be bound by precedent, an accusation of unfair distribution became an accusation of injustice before God. That transformation, from fiscal complaint to moral and religious indictment, is one of the most important developments in the opposition’s history.

40.10 ʿUthmān and the Standardization of the Qurʾānic Text

According to the tradition preserved in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4987, the Qurʾān had been collected in written form after the Battle of Yamāma under Abū Bakr, and the pages were kept by ʿUmar’s daughter Ḥafṣa. When the commander Ḥudhayfa ibn al-Yamān, serving on the northern frontier in campaigns toward Armenia and Azerbaijan, saw that soldiers from Syria and Iraq were disputing over the recitation, he urged ʿUthmān to act. The caliph is said to have borrowed Ḥafṣa’s pages, charged Zayd ibn Thābit, ʿAbd Allāh ibn al-Zubayr, Saʿīd ibn al-ʿĀṣ, and ʿAbd al-Raḥmān ibn al-Ḥārith to copy them, and instructed them to follow the Qurashī dialect when they disagreed. Copies were sent to the major cities, and other collections were ordered withdrawn.

The measure had consequences. Regional collections, such as that of ʿAbd Allāh ibn Masʿūd in Kūfa, differed from the standard text in arrangement and some readings, and Ibn Masʿūd is reported to have resisted surrendering his copy. Some Kūfans saw the decision as an attack on a local religious authority and a use of caliphal power over the text. Supporters of ʿUthmān later emphasized that the standardization preserved unity.

Manuscript scholarship offers a check on the tradition. Studies, including the work of Marijn van Putten on shared orthographic features, support an early common written archetype for the standard consonantal text, which is consistent with a standardization in the period of ʿUthmān or earlier, but the precise date, the process, and the role of the caliph remain debated. The case shows that textual authority and caliphal authority had become entangled by 650.

Primary Source Window I

Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4987 on ʿUthmān’s Qurʾānic Recension

Type: Hadith report in a collection compiled in the ninth century by Muḥammad al-Bukhārī (d. 870) · Related evidence: early Qurʾān manuscripts and modern studies of their spelling

Context: The report is the best-known account of the standardization of the Qurʾān under ʿUthmān. It is told on the authority of Anas ibn Mālik, a Companion, and was recorded more than two centuries after the events. It appears in the book of the excellences of the Qurʾān, where its purpose is to explain how the community came to possess a single authoritative text.

Paraphrase of the report: Ḥudhayfa ibn al-Yamān, who had fought with Syrian and Iraqi troops on the northern frontier, was alarmed by their disputes over how the Qurʾān was recited and urged ʿUthmān to act before the community differed as earlier scriptural communities had. ʿUthmān asked Ḥafṣa for the pages kept with her, and had Zayd ibn Thābit and three men of Quraysh copy them into volumes, instructing the Qurashīs that where they disagreed with Zayd they should write in the dialect of Quraysh, since the Qurʾān had been revealed in it. Copies were sent to the provinces, and every other Qurʾānic document, whether in single sheets or volumes, was ordered burned.

Analysis: The report presents the measure as a response to a threat of division, and as the act of a caliph acting to protect the community’s unity. That emphasis matches the interest of later Sunni scholars in ʿUthmān’s service to the Qurʾān. The report also shows the entanglement of text and political authority: the caliph decides which text is authoritative and orders others destroyed. Kūfan reports about Ibn Masʿūd’s resistance suggest that not everyone welcomed the decision, which may be one reason the episode appears in accounts of opposition.

Limitations: The report was written down long after the event, and its details, including the number of copyists, the exact date, and the role of Ḥafṣa’s pages, cannot be confirmed independently. Manuscript scholarship, for example work by Marijn van Putten on shared spelling features in the early codices, supports a common written archetype for the standard consonantal text, which is consistent with a standardization in this period. It does not prove each detail of the transmitted account, and the date and mechanism remain debated.

Division III

Opposition, Siege, and the Assassination of ʿUthmān

40.11 Religious Criticism, Companions, and Opposition in Medina

Opposition was not confined to the provinces. In Medina, and among the Companions generally, there were reports of criticism of the caliph, and these reports matter because they gave moral weight to complaints that might otherwise have appeared merely provincial. The tradition preserves a small set of stories about prominent early Muslims who objected to specific policies.

Abū Dharr al-Ghifārī, an early convert known for his austerity, is the best-known example. The reports say that he came into conflict in Syria with Muʿāwiya over the use of wealth, citing Qurʾānic warnings against those who hoard gold and silver, and that he was summoned to Medina and then sent to live in al-Rabadha, east of the city, where he died around 652. Later narrators, especially pro-ʿAlid ones, made him a symbol of the poor Companion punished for criticizing the rich; others stressed that his departure to al-Rabadha was at his own choice. ʿAmmār ibn Yāsir, another early convert, is reported to have criticized the caliph and, in some versions, to have been beaten on his orders, injuries that are said to have angered several Companions. ʿAbd Allāh ibn Masʿūd, keeper of the Kūfan treasury and a respected reciter, quarreled with governors and with the caliph over the Qurʾānic codex and over money, and the tradition reports that he returned to Medina and died there. These stories vary in detail, and each appears in versions told by people with a stake in how the opposition should be remembered.

Several of the men who criticized or distanced themselves from ʿUthmān, including Ṭalḥa, al-Zubayr, and ʿĀʾisha, later took up the demand for vengeance for his death. The sources explain this reversal in different ways. Some say that they were horrified at the killing, which went far beyond what they had wanted; others suggest that they changed positions for reasons of ambition. Historians cannot decide between these explanations from the surviving evidence.

The sources support the conclusion that some distinguished early Muslims criticized the caliph’s policies and that this criticism strengthened provincial complaints. They do not reliably show that senior Companions planned or intended his killing. Attributions of responsibility to named Companions mostly appear in the traditions of later partisans, and modern studies, including those of Hinds and Madelung, treat such lists with caution.

40.12 Provincial Opposition Networks and the Movement toward Medina

By 655–656 complaints from the provinces had reached Medina, and the sources describe a network of contacts among the garrison cities. They report letters circulating among Kūfa, Baṣra, and Fusṭāṭ, and the arrival of delegations in Medina. The opposition was not a single organization. Its parts had different leaders, grievances, and goals, which the sources’ shorthand for “the rebels” tends to conceal.

The Kūfan group is the best documented, because Kūfan narrators preserved it. Its leaders included Mālik al-Ashtar and other tribal figures who had been at odds with the governors. According to the tradition, ʿUthmān had earlier ordered some Kūfan critics to be sent away to Syria, where Muʿāwiya kept them under watch, and they later returned to Iraq with their grievances intact. These details come from Kūfan reports and may have been shaped to emphasize the caliph’s harshness.

The Egyptians formed the largest and best-organized contingent. Their leaders included Muḥammad ibn Abī Ḥudhayfa, who had been raised in ʿUthmān’s household and had turned against the governor of Egypt, and Muḥammad ibn Abī Bakr, a son of the first caliph. The tradition says that they left Fusṭāṭ in the spring of 656 (about Shawwāl 35 AH), in several columns and, in some versions, claiming to go on a minor pilgrimage. Baṣran participation is reported as smaller, with leaders such as Ḥukaym ibn Jabala and Ḥurqūṣ ibn Zuhayr, the latter later associated with the Khārijites, a connection that deserves caution since it comes from later sources.

Medina itself was not a passive stage. Many residents sympathized with some of the complaints, and Companions such as ʿAlī, Ṭalḥa, and al-Zubayr dealt with the visitors. Martin Hinds’s study of the murder emphasized that the Medinan dissatisfaction and the provincial opposition interacted, so that the killing cannot be explained either as a plot of provincial rebels alone or as the act of a Medinan faction. The caliph’s unwillingness or inability to hold together a coalition in his own capital was part of the crisis.

40.13 The Siege of ʿUthmān: Mediation, Demands, and the Breakdown of Authority

The sources treat the confrontation in Medina as having phases. The first began when the delegations arrived and presented their grievances. According to the tradition, ʿAlī and other Companions mediated, and ʿUthmān agreed to a settlement, which included replacing the officials the visitors objected to. In the Egyptian case, the reports say that he agreed to appoint Muḥammad ibn Abī Bakr as governor of Egypt in place of ʿAbd Allāh ibn Saʿd, and that the Egyptians set out for home with a letter confirming the appointment.

The second phase begins with the best-known episode of the siege. On the road, the Egyptians are said to have intercepted a messenger carrying a letter under the caliph’s seal ordering their punishment, in some versions through the governor ʿAbd Allāh ibn Saʿd. They turned back and confronted ʿUthmān in Medina. He is reported to have denied any knowledge of the letter, and some versions blame Marwān ibn al-Ḥakam, who had access to the seal, while others treat the letter as a fabrication by the caliph’s enemies. The episode cannot be verified. Modern historians treat it as important because of how narrators used it, and doubtful as an event.

The siege of the house then began and lasted, according to the traditions, several weeks; the figure of about forty days is common but conventional. The protesters surrounded the house and controlled access to it. The tradition says that the water supply was cut, that supporters sent in water and supplies at times, and that ʿUthmān addressed the crowd from the house, recalling his service to Islam. During this period the pilgrimage season came, and the reports say that ʿUthmān appointed ʿAbd Allāh ibn ʿAbbās to lead the pilgrims, which took a number of potential defenders away from the city. ʿĀʾisha also left for Mecca.

The protesters’ demand moved from reform to abdication. ʿUthmān refused, reportedly saying that he would not remove a garment that God had put on him. The reports say that he also appealed to the governors for help, and that forces from Syria and elsewhere were sent but did not arrive in time. Whether Muʿāwiya delayed deliberately is debated, and the reports on this are shaped by later arguments about his responsibility. Accounts of ʿAlī’s role differ. Some portray him as sending his sons to defend the house and arguing with the protesters, others as hesitant or as sympathetic to some of their complaints. These differences reflect the argument over responsibility that followed.

40.14 The Assassination of ʿUthmān in 656

The sources agree that ʿUthmān was killed in his house in Medina in Dhū l-Ḥijja 35 AH, in the traditional dating on the eighteenth day, which corresponds to about 17 June 656, though conversions of such dates should be treated as approximate. According to the reports, a group of attackers entered, in some versions by climbing a neighboring wall while the defenders were at the gate, and killed him as he was reading the Qurʾān. His wife Nāʾila is said to have tried to protect him and to have lost fingers. The house was looted, and the treasury was plundered in some accounts.

Who struck him is disputed. Reports name different individuals, among them Egyptians such as Kināna ibn Bishr al-Tujībī and Sūdān ibn Ḥumrān, and some say that Muḥammad ibn Abī Bakr seized the caliph by the beard and then withdrew after a reproach, while others deny that he took part. These attributions are inconsistent, are tied to the sympathies of the narrators, and are not confirmed by independent evidence. The question of ʿAlī’s responsibility was also contested. Pro-ʿUthmānid narrators implied that ʿAlī either encouraged or failed to prevent the killing; pro-ʿAlid narrators emphasized his sons’ efforts to defend the house, and modern scholars such as Madelung have concluded that the evidence does not support the charge of complicity. This chapter does not identify the killers as an established fact. It does identify the principal facts: that the caliph was killed in Medina in 656 by Muslims, most of them from the provinces, and that no previous ruler of the community had died in this way.

Medina’s reaction was shock and division. The tradition says that ʿUthmān’s body lay for some days before a small group buried it at night, outside the main cemetery, without the usual ceremony, a detail that supporters later cited as a deliberate insult, and some versions say that the delay reflected the fear of the besiegers. The city was without a leader and in the hands of the protesters. The crisis had moved from a dispute over government to a struggle over who would govern and how the killing would be answered.

Every report reaches the compilers through narrators with regional and political commitments, and the compilers’ choices of what to include are themselves evidence. Names of participants differ and cannot be verified. The chronology of the siege and the number of days are conventional. These reports allow a reconstruction of the main outline, but not a confident attribution of the blows.

40.15 The Crisis of Legitimacy: Justice, Rebellion, and Responsibility for the Caliph’s Death

The assassination created a legitimacy crisis on several levels. The first concerned the act. For ʿUthmān’s supporters he was a rightful caliph unjustly killed, and his death required retaliation (qiṣāṣ). They appealed to the Qurʾānic provision that gives the heir of a person killed unjustly the authority to seek vengeance, and Muʿāwiya, as a close kinsman, claimed that right. Others agreed that the killing was a grave act but held that ʿUthmān’s own conduct had made the protest legitimate, and a smaller number approved of it.

The second level concerned procedure. Who could decide who was guilty and what the penalty should be? The community had no court that could try people from several provinces and tribes, some allied with powerful leaders and some present in the armies. To punish them risked war with their tribes. For ʿAlī this was the core practical difficulty, and he argued that order must come before punishment. His critics said that this meant the killers would be protected.

The third level concerned the caliphate. The question who should govern was inseparable from the question how to answer the killing. ʿAlī, Ṭalḥa, al-Zubayr, ʿĀʾisha, and Muʿāwiya each took a position that combined principle with interest, and the combination differs in each case. These positions should be seen as competing political programs, not as simple expressions of loyalty or hostility. Women also took part in the argument. ʿĀʾisha’s public role is the best known, and Nāʾila’s injured hand, which Syrian accounts say was displayed with ʿUthmān’s bloodstained shirt, shows how the killing was turned into a symbol, a story that may have been enhanced by later retelling.

Historical Debate

What Caused the First Fitna?

The question: Why did a community that had expanded so quickly divide into warring camps within a generation? Historians have proposed several explanations, which differ mainly in emphasis.

Provincial and economic grievances. Some stress control over land, revenue, and stipends. The garrison cities depended on the dīwān, and a caliph who diverted revenue or favored relatives could provoke the soldiers whose pay was at stake. The movement of Kūfans and Egyptians to Medina in 656 supports this view, as does the prominence of revenue complaints in the sources.
Failure of caliphal government. Others point to the inadequacy of a small Medinan elite governing an empire through governors with large autonomy. On this view ʿUthmān’s choices, and ʿUmar’s dominating personality that had masked the weakness of institutions, matter more than any particular grievance.
Competing ideals of justice and religious authority. A third approach emphasizes that the participants argued in religious terms: precedent, the Qurʾān, equity, consultation, and vengeance. The Khārijite slogan and the demand for retaliation suggest that ideas were not merely cover for interest.
Rival elite coalitions. A fourth emphasizes competition among Companions and clans, with Ṭalḥa, al-Zubayr, ʿAlī, and the Umayyads each seeking influence in a system with no accepted rule of succession.

Evidence and caution: All draw on the same later sources, which were shaped by the war’s own arguments, and this limits how conclusively any interpretation can be tested. Most scholars, including Martin Hinds, favor an explanation that combines provincial opposition and Medinan dissatisfaction, while differing about the weights.

Division IV

ʿAlī’s Caliphate and the Battle of the Camel

40.16 The Selection of ʿAlī and the Problem of Political Recognition

For several days after the killing Medina was in the hands of the protesters and of its residents. The tradition says that crowds, including some of the besiegers and many Medinans, pressed ʿAlī to accept the caliphate and that he at first refused, saying that he preferred to be a counselor rather than a ruler, and then accepted. The public pledge (bayʿa) took place in the mosque, traditionally a few days after the killing, in late Dhū l-Ḥijja 35 AH. The prominent role of Mālik al-Ashtar and others from the provinces in the sources suggests that ʿAlī’s supporters in the city included the same groups who had opposed ʿUthmān.

Accounts of how Ṭalḥa and al-Zubayr gave their pledges differ. Their later supporters said they had acted under pressure, while ʿAlī’s side held that they had pledged freely and then broken the oath. Other prominent Companions, including Saʿd ibn Abī Waqqāṣ and ʿAbd Allāh ibn ʿUmar, held back from the conflict without opposing ʿAlī, and some Umayyads, including Marwān, left Medina. Muʿāwiya in Syria did not pledge.

The circumstances of the selection differed from earlier accessions. Abū Bakr had been chosen through a rapid consultation among Medina’s leaders, ʿUmar had been designated by his predecessor, and ʿUthmān had been selected by a council after consultation. ʿAlī was chosen in a city under the shadow of a killing, without senior Companions’ consensus, and with backing from some who had opposed his predecessor. This did not make him an illegitimate ruler in the eyes of most contemporaries, because he was a senior Companion and kinsman of the Prophet, but it weakened his claim to universal recognition and made him dependent on groups who had their own interests.

40.17 ʿAlī’s Early Policies: Governors, Treasury, and the Demand for Justice

ʿAlī’s early decisions concerned governors and revenue. He chose to replace most of ʿUthmān’s appointees. The traditions say that advisers such as al-Mughīra ibn Shuʿba and ʿAbd Allāh ibn ʿAbbās urged him to keep them in place until he was secure, while he held that he could not leave them in office. He sent new governors, including ʿUthmān ibn Ḥunayf to Baṣra, Qays ibn Saʿd to Egypt, Sahl ibn Ḥunayf to Syria, and ʿUbayd Allāh ibn ʿAbbās to Yemen. The results varied. ʿUthmān ibn Ḥunayf took up his post, and Qays ibn Saʿd reached Egypt, where he governed with the help of a divided population. Muʿāwiya’s forces turned back the Syrian appointee, and in Kūfa the reports say that the governor sent was turned away by local leaders, while Abū Mūsā al-Ashʿarī remained in office and pledged to ʿAlī.

He also emphasized equal treatment in the distribution of revenue. The reports say that he distributed the treasury’s funds among Muslims without regard to precedence, and that he declared that grants made from public funds by his predecessor would be reclaimed, though how far he did so is uncertain. These policies won support among those who had felt neglected but angered holders of grants and the families of the earlier elite.

The demand to punish ʿUthmān’s killers posed the hardest problem. Some of the killers, or those accused of being so, belonged to groups whose support he needed, and many in Medina were unwilling to enforce punishment against tribes from the provinces. ʿAlī reportedly argued that order must come first and that punishment should follow a legal process with proof of individual guilt. His critics said that this was an excuse. The disagreement over priorities, vengeance first or stability first, would drive the coming war. The difficulty of governing through factions implicated in the previous crisis was real, and it left him with few options that did not alienate someone.

40.18 ʿĀʾisha, Ṭalḥa, al-Zubayr, and the Mobilization of Opposition at Baṣra

ʿĀʾisha, Muḥammad’s widow and the daughter of Abū Bakr, was in Mecca on pilgrimage when ʿUthmān was killed. She was a person of exceptional prestige, and had criticized aspects of ʿUthmān’s rule, but she regarded his killing as unjust and demanded that the killers be punished. In Mecca she was joined by Ṭalḥa and al-Zubayr, both members of the old council, who had left Medina after pledging to ʿAlī. Her stated aim, according to the sources, was reconciliation through justice for ʿUthmān and a consultation to decide on the leadership. The tradition says that other widows of the Prophet, including Umm Salama, advised against her course, and that Ḥafṣa at first supported her and then withdrew.

The three leaders had different interests. Ṭalḥa and al-Zubayr, the traditions suggest, hoped for influence, and each perhaps for the caliphate itself, while ʿĀʾisha’s own motive is described differently in different sources. They gathered support from some of the Umayyads and from former officials of ʿUthmān, including Yaʿlā ibn Munya, the former governor of Yemen, and ʿAbd Allāh ibn ʿĀmir, the former governor of Baṣra, who are reported to have given funds and advice. They decided to move to Baṣra, which promised troops and funds and where ʿAbd Allāh ibn ʿĀmir’s connections were strong.

At Baṣra they met resistance from the governor appointed by ʿAlī, ʿUthmān ibn Ḥunayf. After negotiation and some fighting, in which several people were killed, they took the city and held ʿUthmān ibn Ḥunayf, whom the reports say they humiliated by plucking his beard before releasing him. The city’s population was divided: some notables joined them, others stayed neutral or supported ʿAlī. ʿĀʾisha wrote to Kūfa and elsewhere asking for support. Motives differed among participants and should not be reduced to a single explanation; the coalition combined demand for justice, personal ambition, and the interests of former officeholders.

40.19 The Battle of the Camel, 656: Civil War among the Companions

ʿAlī left Medina with a small force and, after gathering support at Dhū Qār, received troops from Kūfa. The Kūfan governor, Abū Mūsā al-Ashʿarī, had urged neutrality, but ʿAlī’s envoys, including his son al-Ḥasan and ʿAmmār ibn Yāsir, mobilized Kūfan contingents, many of whom came out in support. In late 656, in Jumādā II 36 AH, the armies camped outside Baṣra. The traditions describe several days of negotiation in which envoys went between the camps.

Some reports say that an agreement was close the night before the battle, and that fighting began when extremists on one or both sides provoked it, possibly because they feared a settlement that would expose them to punishment for ʿUthmān’s death. This explanation appears in particular traditions, including some sympathetic to ʿAlī, and it also helped later writers excuse the Companions from responsibility for starting the war. It should be treated as one narrative, not as established fact.

The fighting centered on ʿĀʾisha’s camel, around which the heaviest combat took place, and ended after the camel was hamstrung. Ṭalḥa is said to have been struck by an arrow, with some traditions saying that Marwān ibn al-Ḥakam, fighting on his side, shot him. Al-Zubayr, according to the tradition, left the field and was killed by an attacker as he withdrew at Wādī al-Sibāʿ. ʿAlī treated ʿĀʾisha with respect and sent her back to Medina with an escort, and he reportedly distributed the funds in the Baṣran treasury among his soldiers. Casualty figures in the sources vary greatly and are not reliable. The battle’s significance lies in the fact that Companions fought one another, and its effect on the geography of power was lasting.

40.20 The Aftermath of the Camel and the Establishment of ʿAlī’s Base in Kūfa

After the battle ʿAlī remained at Baṣra for some time. He pardoned most of the defeated, including Marwān ibn al-Ḥakam and other prominent men on the opposing side, distributed funds from the treasury, and appointed ʿAbd Allāh ibn ʿAbbās as governor of the city. ʿĀʾisha was sent back to Medina with an escort, and the sources say that a group of women accompanied her. The treatment of the defeated reflects ʿAlī’s need to limit the damage of the war, and it later became part of the arguments about how Muslims should treat rebels.

He then moved to Kūfa, traditionally in Rajab 36 AH (about January 657), and made it his base. The decision is one of the chapter’s turning points. Kūfa was the largest garrison, had supported him during the campaign, and offered a larger and more reliable body of troops than the Hijaz. It also lay closer to Syria, which he knew he would have to confront.

The move changed the political geography of the caliphate. Medina, which had been the center of authority since the Hijra, ceased to be the seat of government. The Companions of the Hijaz, who had provided the leaders of the early caliphate, were no longer at its center. ʿAlī’s power now depended on the tribal leaders of Kūfa, such as Mālik al-Ashtar, Ḥujr ibn ʿAdī, ʿAdī ibn Ḥātim, and al-Ashʿath ibn Qays, who had their own expectations of rank, pay, and influence, and on the continued willingness of Iraq’s soldiers to fight. The reasons for supporting him varied from personal loyalty to resentment of Syrian power.

The move also left the Hijaz, Egypt, and Syria less firmly under his authority. The sources say that Mecca and Medina were weakly held, and Muʿāwiya’s defiance continued.

Division V

Muʿāwiya, Ṣiffīn, Arbitration, and the Khārijites

40.21 Muʿāwiya in Syria: Provincial Power, Umayyad Networks, and the Demand for Vengeance

Muʿāwiya ibn Abī Sufyān, a member of the Umayya clan and a kinsman of ʿUthmān, became governor of Syria around 639 after the death of his brother Yazīd in the plague of ʿAmwās, according to the tradition. By 656 he had governed for some two decades, a period of continuity unusual in the empire. Syria was organized in military districts (ajnād), including Damascus, Ḥimṣ, Jordan, Palestine, and Qinnasrīn, where tribal contingents were settled and paid. Muʿāwiya led operations against the Byzantines, including naval raids and the expedition to Cyprus in 649, undertaken with ʿUthmān’s permission, and developed ties with tribal leaders and garrison commanders that gave him great local authority.

When ʿAlī was chosen, Muʿāwiya refused to pledge allegiance until ʿUthmān’s killers were punished. The reports say that ʿAlī sent an envoy, Jarīr ibn ʿAbd Allāh, who was kept waiting, and that Syrian delegations replied demanding the surrender of the killers. Syrian accounts report that ʿUthmān’s bloodstained shirt and the severed fingers of Nāʾila were displayed in the Damascus mosque to rally support. This story comes from later sources and may have been shaped for effect, but it shows how the killing was used. Muʿāwiya gained the support of ʿAmr ibn al-ʿĀṣ, the former governor of Egypt, who had resented his removal, and of Syrian tribal leaders who saw the war as a defense of their province.

His claim rested on a combination of kinship, the duty of vengeance, and effective power. It was not at first a claim to be caliph, and he did not take the title at this stage. Syria’s resources, its army, and its unity made it a rival center of political authority. The contest between Kūfa and Damascus was one between two provincial systems as much as between two men, and the stability of Syria under a single governor contrasted sharply with Iraq’s turnover.

40.22 The Battle of Ṣiffīn, 657: Armies, Negotiations, and the Struggle for the Caliphate

After the Camel, ʿAlī called on Muʿāwiya to recognize him, and exchanges of letters and envoys failed. ʿAlī marched north from Kūfa, traditionally in Shawwāl 36 AH, and in the summer of 657 (Ṣafar 37 AH) the armies met at Ṣiffīn on the upper Euphrates, near the ancient city of Raqqa. The Syrian army had taken position first. The traditions describe a struggle for control of the river, in which Muʿāwiya’s forces first held the watering place and ʿAlī’s then took it, followed by weeks of skirmishing and attempted negotiation, including a pause in the sacred month of Muḥarram.

The commanders on each side included, on ʿAlī’s, Mālik al-Ashtar, Ḥujr ibn ʿAdī, and ʿAmmār ibn Yāsir, and on Muʿāwiya’s Syrian leaders such as Ḥabīb ibn Maslama and ʿAbd al-Raḥmān ibn Khālid ibn al-Walīd, together with ʿAmr ibn al-ʿĀṣ. The traditions give the sizes of the armies in tens of thousands, but these numbers are not reliable and cannot be treated as estimates. The main fighting in Ṣafar lasted several days, with cavalry engagements and duels, and reached a peak in a night remembered as laylat al-harīr, the night of clamor. ʿAmmār ibn Yāsir, an elderly Companion on ʿAlī’s side, was killed, and later traditions attributed to the Prophet a saying that a rebellious party would kill him, which supporters of ʿAlī used against Muʿāwiya. Whether this saying was circulated at the time is uncertain.

As ʿAlī’s forces appeared to be gaining, the tradition says that Syrian soldiers raised copies of the Qurʾān on their lances and called for the Book to settle the dispute. Al-Ashʿath ibn Qays and others favored a truce, while Mālik al-Ashtar is said to have wished to press on. Modern historians accept that a truce was agreed but consider the lance story a dramatized account, and they note that speeches and casualty figures were embroidered by later narrators such as Naṣr ibn Muzāḥim. Militarily Ṣiffīn was inconclusive. Politically it exposed a division within ʿAlī’s army between those who wanted to fight and those who accepted negotiation, and it demonstrated that Muʿāwiya could resist ʿAlī’s army on equal terms.

40.23 The Arbitration Agreement and the Breakdown of ʿAlī’s Coalition

The truce led to an arbitration agreement (taḥkīm) that survives in several versions, including those transmitted by Naṣr ibn Muzāḥim and al-Ṭabarī. Martin Hinds’s study showed that these versions differ, so the document must be reconstructed with care and cannot be quoted as a single text. The traditional date for the written agreement is Ṣafar 37 AH (summer 657). In outline, each side was to appoint an arbitrator, and they were to judge according to the Qurʾān and the community’s shared practice, with a truce in place until a decision.

The choice of arbitrators was itself contested. ʿAlī’s side named Abū Mūsā al-Ashʿarī, reportedly at the insistence of al-Ashʿath ibn Qays and others who wanted peace, over ʿAlī’s own preference for ʿAbd Allāh ibn ʿAbbās or Mālik al-Ashtar. Muʿāwiya’s side named ʿAmr ibn al-ʿĀṣ. The reports say that Muʿāwiya’s side objected to the title amīr al-muʾminīn being used for ʿAlī, and that ʿAlī agreed to omit it, an episode later compared with the wording of the Prophet’s treaty at al-Ḥudaybiya. How far the report is historical is debated.

The agreement left basic questions open. What were the arbitrators to decide: the circumstances of ʿUthmān’s death, the validity of ʿAlī’s caliphate, or the dispute between the two leaders? Who would enforce the decision? The arbitrators reportedly met in 658, with the sites given as Dūmat al-Jandal or Adhruḥ, and in the presence of witnesses from several groups. The popular story is that ʿAmr tricked Abū Mūsā into announcing the removal of both leaders, and then confirmed Muʿāwiya. It is probably a later dramatization and cannot be accepted as fact. What the sources agree on is that the meeting produced no accepted settlement.

The agreement weakened ʿAlī’s authority among those who believed that it submitted a matter settled by God to human judgment, and it allowed Muʿāwiya to treat the dispute as a contest between equals. Both effects arose from the agreement’s ambiguity, which made it acceptable to the armies and dangerous to ʿAlī’s coalition.

Paraphrase of the main terms: The parties agree that each side will accept the judgment of two arbitrators, one named by each, who are to judge according to the Qurʾān and the shared practice of the community, and that the armies will observe a truce while they do so. Versions differ on how the leaders are named, on details of the arbitrators’ mandate, and on the place and date of their meeting.

The agreement’s ambiguity is itself significant: it did not say clearly whether the arbitrators were to decide the question of ʿUthmān’s killing, ʿAlī’s legitimacy, or the dispute between the parties. Those who rejected it argued that it treated a settled matter as open to human judgment, while Muʿāwiya’s side gained recognition as an equal party.

40.24 The Emergence of the Khārijites: Ḥarūrāʾ, Divine Judgment, and Political Dissent

Many of ʿAlī’s soldiers, especially from Kūfan tribal groups, rejected the arbitration even as they marched back from Ṣiffīn. Some are reported to have refused to enter Kūfa with the army and to have camped separately at Ḥarūrāʾ, a village nearby. They are said to have used a slogan commonly rendered “judgment belongs to God alone.” The tradition reports that they considered the war against Muʿāwiya’s party required by God’s command to fight those who rebel, that submitting the matter to human arbiters was an error or a sin, and that ʿAlī should repent. They were later called Ḥarūriyya and Khawārij, “those who went out.” The numbers given in the sources, several thousand, are not reliable.

Among their leaders were ʿAbd Allāh ibn Wahb al-Rāsibī, Shabath ibn Ribʿī, and ʿAbd Allāh ibn al-Kawwāʾ, men who had been prominent in ʿAlī’s army, and some had urged the acceptance of arbitration before changing their minds. Many came from tribal groups, such as Tamīm and Bakr ibn Wāʾil, that were prominent in Kūfa, and many were Qurʾān reciters. The movement therefore began not as an outside sect but as a religious and political dispute within ʿAlī’s coalition, about who could judge, and about whether a leader who accepted arbitration with a rebel remained legitimate. It combined piety, tribal politics, and resentment of the leadership of Kūfa’s elites.

The tradition reports debates in which ʿAlī and his envoys, including Ibn ʿAbbās, argued with them. ʿAlī’s side cited passages of the Qurʾān that allowed arbitration, such as the provision for arbitration in a marital dispute, while the dissenters replied that arbitration of the matter at issue was a different case. ʿAlī is reported to have said that the slogan was a true statement used for a wrong purpose. Some returned to the army. Others did not.

Doctrines associated with later Khārijite groups, including strict views on grave sin, on declaring other Muslims unbelievers, and on leadership by the most pious regardless of descent, developed afterward and varied among communities. The early dissenters should not be assumed to have held them.

40.25 The Battle of Nahrawān, 658: Confrontation with the Seceders

ʿAlī first tried persuasion. According to reports, he and his envoys debated with the dissenters, and some returned to the army. The seceders, in turn, moved to the Nahrawān canal district east of the Tigris, and the sources say that they killed ʿAbd Allāh ibn Khabbāb, the son of a Companion, and others whom they met on the road who would not agree with them, though the details of these stories come from hostile narrators. ʿAlī, who was preparing to resume the Syrian campaign, demanded the surrender of the killers. The traditions say that the dissenters replied that they were all responsible, which ʿAlī regarded as a refusal.

He then turned against them and fought them at Nahrawān in 658. The traditions say that he offered safety to those who left the group, that many did, and that the rest were defeated, with very few survivors. Casualty numbers are uncertain. The episode raised a question for Muslims: how should the community treat other Muslims who rebel against a legitimate leader? ʿAlī’s conduct, including the offer of safety and the limited retaliation, became a precedent in later legal discussion of rebellion, though the legal tradition developed later.

The outcome removed an immediate threat but weakened ʿAlī’s position. Many Kūfans were reluctant to resume the war against the Syrians after fighting fellow Muslims. The sources report that ʿAlī camped at Nukhayla outside Kūfa to prepare the march, and that his soldiers drifted away and refused to go. Nahrawān also became a founding memory for later Khārijites, who regarded it as the unjust slaughter of pious believers and justified later violence by reference to it. The movement survived in Iraq and elsewhere, and its argument that an unjust leader could be rejected would shape political conflict for generations.

40.26 The Political Consequences of Ṣiffīn and Arbitration

By late 658 ʿAlī’s coalition had fractured. Some of its Kūfan tribal leaders favored peace, some blamed him for the arbitration, and the dissenters had been defeated but not reconciled. His committed supporters remained loyal, but the army that had come out for the Camel and Ṣiffīn would not readily march again, and the revenues from Iraq were insufficient to restore morale. The sources describe delays, reluctance, and defections.

Muʿāwiya’s position grew stronger. His Syrian army held together, he had the support of ʿAmr ibn al-ʿĀṣ, and he could choose when to act. The reports describe Syrian raids on the Iraqi frontier, such as one on towns along the Euphrates led by Sufyān ibn ʿAwf al-Ghāmidī, and expeditions toward the Hijaz and Yemen led by Busr ibn Abī Arṭāt, which the sources place in the last years of ʿAlī’s rule. The details vary, and some are probably exaggerated by later narrators, but they indicate a pattern in which Muʿāwiya’s forces tested ʿAlī’s authority at the edges. Some reports also speak of an agreement between ʿAlī and Muʿāwiya near the end of ʿAlī’s life to stop hostilities, with each ruling his own region, though this is uncertain. The war thus shifted from a confrontation between two coalitions to the erosion of one of them.

The arbitration also left an unresolved question of authority. Who had the right to decide whether a caliph was legitimate? Could a leader’s status be decided by negotiation with an opponent? Were participants in the conflict believers, unbelievers, or something else? These questions entered later theology, but in 658–659 they were practical political problems.

Division VI

Fragmentation, the Death of ʿAlī, and the Legacy of the First Fitna

40.27 The Struggle for Egypt and the Fragmentation of Provincial Authority, 658–660

Egypt’s allegiance mattered because of its revenue and its grain. ʿAlī’s first governor there, Qays ibn Saʿd, was a skilled politician who kept the peace in difficult circumstances. Reports say that he was recalled, partly because of suspicions at court and partly because of disputes over how to treat local factions, and Muḥammad ibn Abī Bakr replaced him. ʿAlī also named Mālik al-Ashtar to the post, but he is said to have died on the way, possibly by poison, an account that rests on tradition.

In 658 ʿAmr ibn al-ʿĀṣ, with Syrian support, marched into Egypt. Muḥammad ibn Abī Bakr’s forces were defeated, and he was killed. ʿAmr took control of Fusṭāṭ and the province’s revenues passed to Muʿāwiya. For ʿAlī, the loss of a wealthy province was a major blow and a visible sign of the shift in the balance of power.

Around the same time Syrian raiding parties struck at Iraq’s frontier regions and at districts in northern Arabia and the Hijaz, and some regions slipped from ʿAlī’s control. Muʿāwiya’s acclamation at Jerusalem is dated differently in the sources. The Maronite Chronicle places a ceremonial acclamation at Jerusalem in AG 971 (660/61), but Arabic accounts also report recognition of Muʿāwiya as caliph by his Syrian supporters before ʿAlī’s death, commonly placed around 658–659. They distinguish this from the wider recognition that followed al-Ḥasan’s surrender in Iraq in 661. The fragmentary Chronicle itself preserves multiple notices of allegiance that scholars have interpreted differently. These reports should not be collapsed into a single uncontroversial date or ceremony; what Muʿāwiya’s recognition meant in Syria was not identical to his subsequent acceptance across the caliphate.

40.28 Society and Government during Civil War: Armies, Revenue, Tribes, and Everyday Life

The war lasted several years and affected people well beyond the armies. For soldiers’ families in the garrison cities, campaigning meant absent husbands and sons, and stipends that depended on a functioning treasury. In Kūfa and Baṣra, years of conflict strained these payments, and the revenue from the Sawād had to be collected during disruption. Rival claims to collect taxes, raids on frontier districts, and uncertainty about which authority would prevail burdened farmers and landholders, who could be required to supply provisions to passing armies. The tax payments of non-Muslim populations in Iraq, Egypt, and Syria continued to flow to whichever authority held the province, and in Egypt the transfer of control to Muʿāwiya meant a change in who collected them.

Merchants and travelers were affected by insecurity on the roads, and pilgrimage routes were uncertain. Booty from the Camel, funds distributed from the Baṣran treasury, and the costs of long marches also changed the balance of resources among the tribes. Enslaved people, who served in households and campaigns, bore the costs of mobilization, and the sources rarely record them. Religious communities felt the war’s effects too: Christian and Jewish communities generally lived under whichever government ruled their district, and a source like the Maronite Chronicle shows that Christians in Syria viewed these years from a distinct perspective, concerned with taxes, church affairs, and relations with the Byzantines.

The evidence about ordinary life is thin, because the sources follow leaders and battles. Yet the strain on soldiers’ pay and the exhaustion of Iraq’s tribes were important political facts. The condition of the army helps explain why ʿAlī could not mount a decisive campaign after 658, while Muʿāwiya’s stable base allowed him to wait.

Everyday Life and Social History

For soldiers’ families in Kūfa and Baṣra, war meant delayed or uncertain stipends, and absent husbands and sons. Farmers in the Sawād faced demands from armies and tax collectors, and merchants faced insecure roads. Dependents and enslaved people within garrison households carried the burdens of mobilization. These experiences are mostly inferred from the structure of the garrison system, since the sources focus on leaders.

40.29 The Assassination of ʿAlī in 661 and the Succession of al-Ḥasan

The traditions say that three men from the Khārijite milieu agreed in Mecca to kill ʿAlī, Muʿāwiya, and ʿAmr ibn al-ʿĀṣ on the same day, on the grounds that the three had caused the war, and that only the attack on ʿAlī succeeded. The assassin, ʿAbd al-Raḥmān ibn Muljam, of the tribe of Murād, struck ʿAlī with a poisoned sword in the mosque at Kūfa in Ramaḍān 40 AH (January 661). The traditions give dates for the attack and for ʿAlī’s death between the seventeenth and twenty-first of the month. Ibn Muljam was executed. The place of ʿAlī’s burial is the subject of tradition, and later Shiʿi tradition identified it with Najaf, while other traditions give different places. The Maronite Chronicle’s notice of ʿAlī’s death does not match the Arabic dating, a difference that this chapter has noted but not resolved.

After ʿAlī’s death the people of Kūfa pledged to his son al-Ḥasan, who had little means to continue the war. The traditions distinguish al-Ḥasan’s vanguard, sent ahead toward Maskīn in northern Iraq to confront Muʿāwiya’s forces, from al-Ḥasan and his main army at al-Madāʾin. Accounts describe desertions and divisions among the vanguard and a mutiny and attack on al-Ḥasan near Sabāṭ, close to al-Madāʾin; their details and sequence differ. Negotiations followed through envoys, and the reports say that Muʿāwiya sent proposals in writing.

According to the accounts, al-Ḥasan agreed to yield the caliphate to Muʿāwiya in 661. The reported terms, which include financial arrangements, protections for his supporters, and in some versions a provision about the succession, differ among sources and were later shaped by partisans on both sides, so they are treated as contested. Al-Ḥasan withdrew to Medina, and Muʿāwiya entered Kūfa, where he received pledges. Muʿāwiya’s recognition came in stages: acknowledgment by his Syrian supporters earlier, and the broader acceptance that followed al-Ḥasan’s surrender in Iraq. The year was later remembered as the “year of the community” (ʿām al-jamāʿa) because the community was once more under a single leader, although the unity was one of submission to a stronger party, not of agreement.

40.30 The First Fitna and the Transformation of Legitimate Leadership

The war changed what Muslims argued about when they talked about leadership. ʿUthmān’s supporters stressed the sanctity of the caliph’s person and the duty to avenge him. Their position developed into a view, strong in later Sunni thought, that a caliph should not be removed by force. ʿAlī’s supporters stressed his kinship with the Prophet, his early merit, and the need for justice and fidelity to the Book. The group known as shīʿat ʿAlī, partisans of ʿAlī, was at this stage a political following; the doctrines of Shiʿi imams and designated succession developed later, as examined in Chapter 47. Muʿāwiya based his claim on kinship, the duty of vengeance, and actual power, and the Syrian army’s loyalty. The early Khārijites argued that no leader could escape judgment by God’s Book.

Institutionally, the war established that provincial armies and governors held power that a caliph could not easily override, and that control of resources such as Egypt and Iraq could decide the outcome. It moved the center of the caliphate away from Medina, first to Kūfa and then to Damascus. It also left questions without agreed answers: how a caliph should be selected, who could depose him, whether rebellion against him was ever permitted, and how to judge the Muslims who fought in the war.

These questions were not settled by Muʿāwiya’s accession, and the Second Fitna of 680 onward, discussed in Chapter 43, would reopen them. Meanwhile the structure of the new government, from Damascus, is the subject of Chapter 41. Later Sunni, Shiʿi, and Khārijite traditions each remembered the First Fitna in their own way, but in 661 none of these traditions existed in the form that later centuries gave them.

Chapter Summary

The First Fitna was a crisis of an empire that had grown faster than its institutions. The conquests of Chapter 39 brought wealth, garrison cities, and provincial elites, while the government remained a small circle of Companions in Medina. ʿUmar’s personal authority had masked the difficulty. After his death in 644 the community selected ʿUthmān through a small council, and the new caliph inherited a system that depended on governors, stipends, and consent.

Under ʿUthmān the provinces developed distinct politics. Syria under Muʿāwiya was stable and militarily active. Baṣra, under ʿAbd Allāh ibn ʿĀmir, was occupied with expansion in Iran. Kūfa, with its frequent changes of governors, and Egypt, where ʿAbd Allāh ibn Saʿd replaced ʿAmr ibn al-ʿĀṣ, saw growing dissent. Disputes over land, revenue, stipends, and patronage became religious arguments about justice, and ʿUthmān’s reliance on kinsmen gave critics a target.

The standardization of the Qurʾānic text illustrates the intertwining of religious and caliphal authority. The tradition in al-Bukhārī 4987 presents it as a measure for unity, and manuscript evidence supports an early common archetype, but the date and details remain debated. Some Kūfans resented the loss of local codices.

In 656 delegations from Egypt and Iraq came to Medina, besieged the caliph, and killed him. The sources disagree about the letter, the mediation, and the identity of the killers, and the chapter has treated those accounts as contested. The killing was unprecedented and produced a legitimacy crisis involving vengeance, justice, and who could rule.

ʿAlī’s accession was contested. His attempt to replace governors and his priority of order over immediate punishment met resistance. ʿĀʾisha, Ṭalḥa, and al-Zubayr, with different motives, raised opposition in Baṣra, and at the Battle of the Camel, Companions fought one another. ʿAlī then made Kūfa his base, which shifted the caliphate’s center from the Hijaz.

Muʿāwiya, backed by Syria’s army, refused to recognize ʿAlī without justice for ʿUthmān. At Ṣiffīn in 657 the armies fought without result, and the arbitration that followed, whose transmitted versions differ, split ʿAlī’s coalition. The early Khārijites emerged from within it, objecting that judgment belongs to God, and were defeated at Nahrawān in 658 but not eliminated.

The loss of Egypt in 658, the exhaustion of Iraq’s soldiers, and Syrian pressure weakened ʿAlī’s position. The Maronite Chronicle and the Arabic tradition differ over the date of Muʿāwiya’s acclamation at Jerusalem and of ʿAlī’s death, and the chapter has noted the discrepancy rather than harmonizing it. ʿAlī was assassinated at Kūfa in January 661, and al-Ḥasan, under pressure, yielded to Muʿāwiya.

The war had lasting institutional effects. Provincial armies and governors proved capable of resisting or deciding the caliph’s fate, and control of provinces such as Egypt and Iraq shaped outcomes. The center of government moved from Medina to Damascus.

It also opened questions about legitimacy: whether a caliph could be removed, who could judge him, how he should be chosen, and how to regard Muslims who fought each other. Later Sunni, Shiʿi, and Khārijite traditions developed from these questions, but they did not exist in settled form in 656–661.

The chapter’s approach has been to treat sources critically, to explain the crisis institutionally, and to avoid reading later doctrines into early coalitions. Muʿāwiya’s government, which followed, is the subject of Chapter 41.

Chapter Reflection

The First Fitna matters first because it showed how a successful conquering movement could be endangered by its own success. Revenue, garrison cities, and provincial elites created interests that a small council in Medina could not easily manage. The crisis was therefore not merely a quarrel among Companions but a test of how a new empire would be governed.

Its effects reached far beyond 661. The caliphate’s center moved from Medina to Kūfa and then to Damascus, and the capacity of provincial armies and governors to decide political outcomes became a continuing feature of caliphal politics. Chapter 41 shows how Muʿāwiya built a government on the ruins of the civil war, drawing on Syria’s military and administrative resources, and Chapter 43 examines how the questions left open in 661 returned with the Second Fitna.

The war also gave Muslims a vocabulary for arguing about legitimate leadership: consultation, precedent, kinship, justice, vengeance, and the authority of God’s Book. Later Sunni, Shiʿi, and Khārijite traditions developed their positions from these arguments and remembered the war in different ways. Students should keep in view both the events and the layers of memory through which they are known, and avoid assuming that the later traditions existed in finished form in 656.

Finally, the chapter illustrates the problem of evidence that runs through early Islamic history. Sources written generations later, brief outside accounts such as Sebeos and the Maronite Chronicle, and scattered documents must be weighed together. A careful history of the First Fitna acknowledges what is secure, what is probable, and what remains unknown. That discipline will serve in the chapters that follow, where the empire’s institutions, its peoples, and its disputes become increasingly visible.
