Part VIII — Rome, Sasanian Iran, Christianity, and Late Antiquity
32

Sasanian Iran and the Restoration of Persian Imperial Power

A History of the Middle East: From Ancient Civilizations to the Twenty-First Century

Chapter Opening

On a cliff face at Naqsh-e Rustam, near Persepolis, Achaemenid royal tombs overlook a later carving in which a Sasanian king on horseback receives a ring of authority from a god. Beneath the king's horse lies the defeated Arsacid ruler Artabanus, and beneath the god's horse lies the evil spirit Ahriman. Nearby reliefs of Shapur I show Roman emperors submitting to him. Together these images capture how the Sasanians wished to be seen: heirs to Iran's imperial past, divinely sanctioned, and rivals who could humble Rome.

In 224 CE Ardashir, a ruler from the province of Persis (Fars), defeated the Arsacid king Artabanus IV and began a dynasty that governed an empire from Mesopotamia to the edges of Central Asia for more than four centuries. This chapter asks how the Sasanians transformed an Arsacid inheritance into a durable late-antique imperial system, and how kingship, aristocratic power, religion, Mesopotamian wealth, and rivalry with Rome sustained and constrained it.

Two extremes should be avoided. The Sasanians were not simply Parthians under another name: they developed distinctive royal ideology, fiscal practice, religious patronage, and imperial imagery. Nor did they build a centralized "national Persian state": their empire rested on great noble houses, regional elites, local communities, and negotiated institutions. That tension between royal centralization and aristocratic and regional power runs through every section.

The chapter's limits follow the Authoritative Outline. Chapter 33 treats Christianity as a regional historical problem, and Chapter 34 treats Arabia, the Ghassanids and Lakhmids, and the Byzantine-Sasanian wars of the early seventh century. This chapter ends in 591, with an imposing but strained empire.

  4. Evaluate how centralized Sasanian government actually was.
  5. Describe the social hierarchy of nobles, priests, cultivators, and artisans.
  6. Explain why Mesopotamia and Ctesiphon were indispensable to the empire.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. What changed, and what stayed the same, between Arsacid and Sasanian rule?
  2. How did Sasanian kings legitimate their rule, and for which audiences?
  3. Why did strong monarchs repeatedly struggle with powerful noble houses?
  4. Why was Mesopotamia more important to the empire than a plateau-centered view suggests?
  5. How reliable are our sources, many of them Roman, Syriac, Armenian, or later Islamic-era Persian?
  6. Why did rivalry with Rome recur for centuries without becoming continuous warfare?
  7. How far did the Sasanians support a Zoroastrian "state church"?
  8. Why did an apparently formidable empire carry structural tensions by 591?

Historical Context

Volume II has followed the Achaemenid Empire (Chapters 21–25), Alexander and his successors (26–28), and Parthia with its Roman frontier (Chapter 30). The Arsacid Parthians ruled through a looser system in which regional kings and great families held substantial power. By the early third century, civil conflict among Arsacid claimants and repeated Roman invasions of Mesopotamia had weakened the dynasty. Chapter 31 has traced the Roman East from the Jewish side; Chapter 32 now turns to the Iranian side of the same late-antique world.

Evidence is uneven. Sasanian royal inscriptions, rock reliefs, coins, seals, and archaeological sites are contemporary but speak for the monarchy. Roman and Byzantine historians, Syriac Christian chronicles, Armenian histories, and the Babylonian Talmud supply outside views. The Middle Persian royal chronicle tradition survives mainly in later Arabic and Persian adaptations, so reconstructions of early Sasanian ideology must be handled with caution.

Section 32.1

The Fall of the Arsacids and the Rise of Ardashir I

By the early third century the Arsacid dynasty was weakened by rival claimants, notably Vologases VI and Artabanus IV, and by Roman invasions. Septimius Severus had sacked Ctesiphon in 198, and after Caracalla's campaign of 216–217 his successor Macrinus was defeated near Nisibis and agreed a settlement favorable to the Parthians, but the repeated strain left the monarchy with limited prestige. Arsacid kings had always ruled through subordinate kings and great families, and the wars gave those regional power-holders more room to act independently.

Persis (Fars), the Achaemenid heartland, was one such region. Local rulers there issued coins with Persian imagery, including fire altars, and served the Arsacids as subordinate kings. Later tradition names Sasan, then Papak (Pabag), as ancestors of Ardashir, with Sasan sometimes described as a priest of the Anahita temple at Istakhr. These accounts come largely from later Arabic and Persian histories and the Middle Persian romance Kārnāmag ī Ardashīr ī Pābagān, which mix history with legend, so the family's early history is uncertain. What is secure is that Ardashir extended his authority from Persis over neighboring Kirman and Khuzestan.

He defeated Artabanus IV at Hormozdgan in 224 (the exact site is debated), and the king was killed. Ardashir took Ctesiphon around 226 and was crowned king of kings, but consolidation took years: Hatra, a Parthian ally, fell only around 240, Armenia remained under an Arsacid line, and Ardashir campaigned in the east and north. Continuity was substantial. Great families associated with the Parthian period, among them the Suren and Karen, continued to serve, and Arsacid administrative practice and cavalry warfare survived. The change was a dynasty with a new claim to legitimacy, not a new civilization.

Section 32.2

Ardashir I and the Restoration of Iranian Kingship

Ardashir presented himself as a divinely favored ruler. His coins carry a royal portrait and, on the reverse, a fire altar, and his reliefs at Naqsh-e Rustam, Firuzabad, and Naqsh-e Rajab show a god, identified as Ahura Mazda, investing the king. Sasanian inscriptions speak of the king's xwarrah, the divine glory or fortune that legitimated rule, and describe the king's lineage as of divine origin. This imagery tied kingship to Zoroastrian divinity, though how developed Zoroastrian institutions were in Ardashir's time is discussed in Section 32.9.

Royal titles also changed. Ardashir styled himself king of kings of the Iranians (Ērān), and his son Shapur added "and non-Iranians" (Anērān), a formula that acknowledged a multilingual empire in which Iranian rule was claimed over non-Iranian subjects. Ērānšahr therefore described the king's realm and its sacred and political order, not an ethnic nation.

The question of "restoration" is debated. Roman writers such as Herodian reported that Ardashir claimed the lands of his Persian ancestors as far as the Aegean, which would fit an Achaemenid claim. Yet Sasanian-era tradition, preserved in later sources, appears to have remembered Persepolis through legendary figures rather than as the Achaemenids' capital, and many Sasanian practices continued Arsacid ones. Some historians therefore see restoration as a Roman interpretation or as selective dynastic rhetoric, while others see a real Persian regional memory from Persis. The safest conclusion is that the Sasanians were conscious of Persis as a homeland and of earlier Iranian kingship, without a detailed, accurate knowledge of Achaemenid history.

Historical Vocabulary

Ērānšahr (Middle Persian, "the Iranian realm") named the empire as a political and cultural space. It included non-Iranian-speaking populations, and the Sasanian court also distinguished Anērān, lands beyond. It did not mean a nation-state.

Section 32.3

Shapur I and the Expansion of Sasanian Power

Shapur I ruled with his father from about 240 and alone from 242 until 270. Early in his reign he turned against Rome. In 243–244 the emperor Gordian III campaigned in Mesopotamia and died near Misiche; Roman sources imply that his own soldiers killed him, while Shapur's inscription claims a Roman defeat and renames Misiche Peroz-Shapur. Gordian's successor Philip the Arab made peace and, according to Shapur, paid for it, with consequences for Roman influence in Armenia.

The conflict resumed in the 250s. Shapur's forces invaded Syria, captured Antioch (the date is debated), and took cities including Dura-Europos, whose fall is documented by archaeology at the site. Emperor Valerian marched east in 260 and was defeated near Edessa. Shapur claimed that he captured Valerian personally; Roman sources suggest the emperor was seized during negotiations or after defeat, and his fate in captivity is obscure and surrounded by legend.

Shapur deported captives from Syria and Anatolia into Iran, settling them in Khuzestan, Fars, and other regions, where they worked on royal projects. Traditions credit Roman prisoners with building works such as the dam at Shushtar, but these are later stories. Bishapur, a new city, with its reliefs showing Roman defeat and mosaics in Roman style, shows deportees and Roman craft influence. Roman recovery came soon: Odaenathus of Palmyra attacked Sasanian forces after 260, so Shapur's victories were not a permanent conquest of Syria.

Shapur also commemorated his victories in a trilingual inscription (Middle Persian, Parthian, and Greek) on the Ka'ba-ye Zartosht at Naqsh-e Rustam, beside the Achaemenid royal tombs; it is examined below as a source. Reliefs show Philip kneeling and Valerian held by the wrist. These monuments created an image of a Sasanian king equal or superior to Rome's emperor, a claim every later king had to match or answer.

Section 32.4

King of Kings: Court, Monarchy, and Imperial Ideology

Sasanian kingship was exercised through a court (dar, literally "the gate") of royal household, officials, priests, commanders, and the heads of noble houses. Later sources describe a chief minister (wuzurg framadār) who headed the administration under some fifth-century kings, but the evidence for court offices is uneven, and titles and duties changed from reign to reign. Silver plates and bowls showing kings hunting, feasting, or enthroned, together with crowns that differ by reign, show how the monarchy was made visible, and later sources describe festivals such as Nowruz and Mehregan as occasions of ceremony and gift-giving.

No single rule governed succession. Shapur I was followed by Hormizd I and Bahram I (both short reigns), Bahram II (274–293), and the brief Bahram III, until Shapur's son Narseh took the throne in 293. His own inscription at Paikuli, in Iraqi Kurdistan, justifies the accession and lists the nobles who supported him, a contemporary record of how much a claimant depended on the great houses. Later sources report that nobles murdered Shapur III in 388, opposed the sons of Yazdegerd I after his death in 420, and deposed Kavadh in 496.

The result was a persistent tension. Kings claimed divine sanction and a unique place above all subjects, yet needed noble armies, estates, and consent. Strong rulers curbed nobles through appointments, marriage, and new revenue, while weak or contested rulers were constrained by them. Understanding this tension is essential for the reforms of Section 32.15.

Section 32.5

Governing the Empire: Provinces, Officials, and Local Power

The empire was divided into provinces (shahr) under governors, with revenue officials, judges, and a scribal service (dibīr) keeping records. Early Sasanian government also relied on subordinate kings, such as the rulers of Armenia, Iberia, and the Kushan domains, who owed tribute and military service to the king of kings. Over time the crown replaced several of these rulers with its own officials: the Arsacid kingdom of Armenia was ended around 428 and placed under a Sasanian frontier governor (marzbān). Sasanian government therefore moved toward direct administration but did so unevenly, which is why historians describe it as more integrated than Arsacid government without being a modern centralized state.

Evidence for administration comes from seals and clay sealings (bullae), which record officials' names and titles, from coins issued at many mints, and from late legal texts, notably the Middle Persian Mādayān ī hazār dādestān ("Book of a Thousand Judgments"), compiled near the end of the dynasty and revealing a legal culture of judges, contracts, and property. Narrative sources add detail but are shaped by later memory, and most Sasanian archives have not survived.

Taxation was one of the principal points of contact between the state and its subjects, but the mechanisms are unevenly documented before the sixth-century reforms. Provincial fiscal officials worked through local intermediaries, including landholders, urban and occupational authorities, and the heads of some religious communities. This reliance on locally powerful people could make royal service and hereditary regional influence overlap.

Section 32.6

Nobility, Social Hierarchy, Land, and Military Power

Later Zoroastrian and Islamic-period sources describe four estates: priests, warriors, scribes, and commoners. This scheme drew on an old Iranian idea of social order and expressed an ideal, not a precise legal system, and real society was more varied. At its summit stood the royal family and the great noble houses. Sasanian and later traditions preserve a conventional group of seven great houses, including the Suren, Karen, and Mihran; the exact lists and precedence vary, and only some, especially the Suren and Karen, are securely attested as major houses already under the Arsacids.

Military power was bound up with landholding. The core of the army was the heavily armored cavalry (aswārān), supplied largely by nobles and lesser landholders, supported by archers, infantry levies drawn from the peasantry, elephants, and siege specialists, some of them captives or foreigners. Estates were worked by cultivators, tenants, and dependent laborers, along with slaves and deported captives, while artisans and merchants lived chiefly in cities. Fire temples and priests also held endowed land.

Lesser landed notables, later called dehqāns, managed estates and village affairs and were crucial in tax collection. Their importance grew in the sixth century when Khosrow I relied on them, and they remained significant after the Arab conquests (Chapter 39). This hierarchy supported royal power by supplying soldiers and revenue, and it also supplied recurring resistance, as the succession crises of Section 32.4 show.

Section 32.7

Mesopotamia and Ctesiphon: The Imperial Heartland

The Tigris plain was the empire's center of gravity. Ctesiphon, on the east bank, had been an Arsacid winter capital; across the river lay Seleucia, the old Hellenistic city, and Ardashir founded Veh-Ardashir nearby. Together these cities formed a metropolitan complex later known to Arabic sources as al-Mada'in. The surrounding province, Asōristān, was described in later Middle Persian tradition as the heart of the Iranian realm, and the arch known as the Taq-i Kisra, often attributed to Khosrow I, although its date and patron remain disputed, survives as a monument of the royal city.

Its wealth rested on irrigated farmland between the two rivers (see Chapter 2). Surveys indicate dense settlement and expanded irrigation in the Sasanian period, and the revenue of this land helped pay the armies. The population grew also by deportation: after the Antioch campaign of 540, Khosrow I settled captives in a new city near Ctesiphon, Veh-Antiok-Khosrow, which later sources also call Rumagan.

The region was religiously mixed. Babylonia became the setting in which rabbinic circles produced and transmitted the traditions that eventually formed the Babylonian Talmud; the institutional form of the later "academies" in Sasanian times remains debated. The leadership of the Christian Church of the East also centered at Seleucia-Ctesiphon (Chapter 33). Its position was a strength and a weakness. Carus captured Ctesiphon in 283, and Julian approached the capital during his invasion of 363 without taking it. Roman campaigns elsewhere in Mesopotamia and Armenia repeatedly threatened the imperial heartland even when they did not reach the city itself.

Section 32.8

Agriculture, Cities, Commerce, and the Sasanian Economy

Agriculture depended on canal systems drawn from the Tigris and Euphrates, such as the Nahrawan, traditionally dated to the Sasanian period, and on maintaining them against silt and flooding. Khuzestan, around Susa and Shushtar, produced cereals and, according to later sources, sugarcane and textiles. The crown held large estates, and kings founded cities, such as Bishapur, Gundeshapur, and Veh-Ardashir, partly to settle deportees and extend taxation.

The standard coin was the silver drachm, struck at many mints and carrying the king's portrait and a fire altar, while gold was used more for prestige and gifts, and Roman gold coins circulated in trade. Trade ran through the Persian Gulf to India and Sri Lanka and overland to Central Asia, where Sogdian merchants were prominent, and Sasanian silver and coins have been found as far as China. "Silk Road" is a nineteenth-century term for what was really a set of overlapping networks, and silk reached Rome partly through Sasanian territory. A Roman law of 408/409, implementing or reaffirming an agreement with Persia, restricted official cross-border trade to designated markets at Nisibis, Callinicum, and Artaxata. The measure shows that both states sought to channel commerce through places where customs and movement could be monitored.

Section 32.9

Zoroastrian Institutions and the Sasanian Monarchy

Sasanian kings patronized fire temples, which housed sacred fires ranked in a hierarchy. Later Zoroastrian tradition names three great fires: Adur Farnbag, associated with Fars, Adur Gushnasp, associated with Azerbaijan and the site of Takht-i Sulaiman, and Adur Burzen-Mihr, associated with the northeast. The priesthood (mobeds and higher ranks) performed ritual, taught, and judged, and held endowed property for the fires.

The best contemporary evidence for priestly ambition is Kartir, who left inscriptions at Naqsh-e Rustam, Naqsh-e Rajab, Sar Mashhad, and the Ka'ba-ye Zartosht. He served from Shapur I to Bahram II and rose to high judicial and religious office. He claimed to have established fires and suppressed other groups, among them Jews, Christians, Buddhists, and Manichaeans. His claims are self-presentation, and they do not prove systematic persecution; Mani died in imprisonment under Bahram I, while Kartir's influence and public self-presentation reached a high point under Bahram II (Section 32.10).

The Avesta was transmitted orally for centuries, and an alphabet to record it was probably devised in the Sasanian period; the Middle Persian "Pahlavi" books that describe ritual and law reached their present form mainly after the empire's fall, in the Islamic period. This late compilation is why historians hesitate to read ninth-century priestly texts back into the third century. Local cults and variant doctrines also existed, and the term "state church" is an analytical problem, taken up in the debate below, rather than a settled fact.

Historical Debate

Was Zoroastrianism a Sasanian State Religion?

Interpretation A — an organized state church: Older scholarship, relying on later priestly texts and Kartir, saw a unified, hierarchical Zoroastrian church allied with the crown from the start.
Interpretation B — a more fluid, evolving relationship: Recent work stresses regional variation, patchy royal enforcement, texts written down later, and kings who sometimes tolerated or used other religions.

Most historians now accept strong royal patronage of Zoroastrian institutions while doubting a fixed, uniform "state church" from 224. Student task: explain what evidence would settle the question, and why Kartir's inscriptions alone cannot.

Section 32.10

Religious Diversity under Sasanian Rule

Mani, born in Babylonia in 216, preached a dualist religion that drew on Christian, Zoroastrian, and Buddhist ideas and spread rapidly east and west. He enjoyed the favor of Shapur I, but under Bahram I he was imprisoned and died, conventionally around 274–277. Manichaean communities nevertheless survived across Iran and Central Asia and into the Roman world. For Sasanian history the key point is that royal religious policy changed with the king and the political moment, favor in one reign being followed by repression in another.

Jewish communities in Babylonia lived under their own leaders and legal traditions, with a communal head, the exilarch, attested in rabbinic sources. The Babylonian Talmud attributes to the third-century master Samuel the principle that the law of the kingdom is binding, which suggests how Jewish leaders negotiated their position under Persian rule. Later Jewish traditions report restrictions in the fifth century under Yazdegerd II and Peroz, but the evidence is thin, and most of the time communal life continued with considerable autonomy.

Christians were a large and growing community, enlarged by deportees from Roman Syria. As Roman emperors increasingly patronized Christianity and Roman-Sasanian war resumed, Persian Christians could be suspected of divided loyalty. Under Shapur II, especially from the 330s, severe persecutions followed amid wartime taxation, political suspicion, and conflict with Christian leaders, as recorded principally in Syriac martyr acts. Under Yazdegerd I the church received official recognition at the synod of 410, though persecutions returned later. These swings illustrate the pattern: policy followed political circumstance rather than a single religious program. The theology, councils, and Syriac traditions of the Church of the East belong to Chapter 33.

Section 32.11

Rome and Iran: A Frontier of War and Diplomacy

The northern Mesopotamian frontier was a zone of fortified cities. Nisibis was Roman until 363, when Jovian surrendered it, and thereafter Persian. Earlier, after Galerius defeated Narseh in Armenia, the peace of 298 had given Rome districts east of the Tigris and a stronger position in Armenia. In 507, after the war of 502–506, Anastasius founded and fortified Dara close to the Persian frontier; the Sasanians protested its existence as contrary to earlier understandings limiting frontier fortification. Beyond Mesopotamia, both empires cared about the Caucasus: Persian kings repeatedly asked Rome to contribute to the defense of the Caucasian passes against nomadic raiders, a recurring grievance in the sources.

Diplomacy was institutionalized. Embassies carried letters in which the two monarchs addressed each other as "brother," treaties provided for tribute or subsidies, ransom, and the return or exchange of prisoners, and trade was restricted to designated markets (Section 32.8). The Eternal Peace of 532 included a large Roman payment in gold. Population transfers continued as well: captives from conquered cities were resettled as labor and as symbols of victory.

Rivalry was therefore intermittent. After 387 there were only brief wars, in 421–422 and 440–441, followed by decades of peace until Kavadh's war with Anastasius in 502, and even then wars ended in settlements. Each side had other frontiers, in the Balkans and the Danube for Rome and in Central Asia for Iran, and the costs of sieges were high. The two empires were rivals who recognized each other as the only equals.

Cause and Effect

Why Roman-Iranian Rivalry Kept Returning

Long-term causes

Contested Mesopotamian and Armenian borderlands, wealth of frontier cities, and royal prestige tied to victory.

Short-term causes

Succession crises, tribute disputes, and opportunities when the other side faced trouble elsewhere.

Why not constant war

Costly sieges, eastern and northern threats on both sides, and diplomatic institutions that allowed settlement.

Consequences

Fortified frontiers, shifting borders, population transfers, and mutual cultural exposure.
Section 32.12

Armenia, the Caucasus, and the Politics of Buffer Kingdoms

Armenia was an active polity, not simply a prize. Its Arsacid kings were a branch of the Parthian dynasty, and its powerful noble houses (nakharars) held land and armies and could shift allegiance between Rome and Iran. Christianity entered royal politics in the early fourth century, traditionally around 301, which gave the kingdom a religion distinct from Persian state religion, a development whose religious significance is treated in Chapter 33.

Around 387 Rome and Iran partitioned Armenia, with Iran receiving the larger, eastern part. The Armenian Arsacid monarchy in the Persian zone lasted until about 428, when nobles asked Bahram V to remove their king, and Persarmenia was then governed by a Sasanian marzbān. The Armenian revolt of 450–451 under Vardan Mamikonian, remembered at the battle of Avarayr, resisted pressure on Armenia's religion and noble privileges. Armenian sources give the dominant account. Avarayr was a Sasanian battlefield victory, not the end of the conflict; renewed Armenian resistance eventually contributed to the Treaty of Nvarsak in 484, which granted significant concessions and greater freedom for Christian practice.

Elsewhere in the Caucasus, Iberia (Kartli) and Caucasian Albania also balanced between the empires. Their kings and aristocracies pursued their own interests, accepting Sasanian overlordship at some moments and resisting at others. Late in the sixth century the Iberian monarchy ended under Sasanian rule. These societies were active political communities, and their choices shaped the frontier as much as the policies of the two great powers.

Section 32.13

Shapur II and the Fourth-Century Imperial Contest

Shapur II came to the throne as an infant in 309 (later tradition elaborated this into a legend that he was crowned before birth), and the nobles governed during his minority. As an adult he campaigned against Arab groups on the Gulf coast and, from the 350s, against the Chionites on the eastern frontier, whose king later fought alongside him at Amida. His reign shows that Sasanian military attention was divided between east and west.

The wars with Rome resumed after Constantine's death in 337. Shapur besieged Nisibis three times, in 337/338, 346, and 350, without taking it, and the night battle of Singara, usually dated to 344 though 348 has also been proposed, was inconclusive. In 359, after a siege of about seventy-three days, Amida fell, followed by Singara and Bezabde. The wars also reached Armenia, where Shapur seized and imprisoned King Arsaces around 368.

In 363 the emperor Julian led a large army down the Euphrates and approached Ctesiphon but could not take the city, burned his boats, and retreated up the Tigris, where he was killed in a skirmish in June. His successor Jovian concluded a thirty-year peace that ceded Nisibis, Singara, and several districts east of the Tigris, and Nisibis's population was removed. The settlement strengthened Iran's position in Mesopotamia and Armenia and helped create the conditions for the later partition of Armenia around 387 (Section 32.12).

Section 32.14

Fifth-Century Pressures: Succession, Eastern Enemies, and Imperial Resilience

Yazdegerd I (399–420) pursued peace with Rome; Byzantine tradition even claimed that Arcadius entrusted his young son Theodosius II to the Persian king's protection, a story historians treat cautiously. The synod of 410 gave the Church of the East official recognition, though persecution returned late in his reign. Later Persian tradition remembered him as "the Sinner," a hostile verdict from noble and priestly sources, and his death in 420 was followed by a succession struggle in which nobles opposed his sons and Bahram V (420–438) took the throne with support from the Lakhmids of Hira (Chapter 34).

A short war with Rome in 421–422 over Christian refugees ended with mutual commitments on religious toleration. Bahram V and Yazdegerd II (438–457) also faced Hephthalite and related groups in the east, and a further brief war with Rome followed in 440–441. Pressure on Armenia in these years is described in Section 32.12.

The crisis came under Peroz (459–484). Enthroned with Hephthalite help, he suffered famine at home and then fought the Hephthalites. He suffered a major defeat and was captured, paying a heavy ransom that strained the treasury and leaving his son Kavadh as a hostage; one later source describes two separate captures, but this may duplicate the same episode. He was defeated and killed in 484 on a later campaign. The empire survived the setback: his successor Balash made the settlement with Armenia at Nvarsak, and tribute and fiscal strain increased the weight of the great nobles. That the state recovered shows the resilience of its institutions.

Section 32.15

Kavadh I, Social Conflict, and the Reforms of Khosrow I

Kavadh I (488–531) came to the throne amid noble power and fiscal strain. In his reign appeared Mazdak, whose movement is known mainly from hostile sources. Zoroastrian and Islamic-period accounts describe a heresy preaching sharing of property and women, while Greek writers such as Procopius report Kavadh's association with it. Historians debate whether it was a social revolution, a religious reform, or a movement Kavadh used against the great houses. The sources also disagree about Mazdak's fate, some placing his destruction at the end of Kavadh's reign and others under Khosrow I.

In 496 nobles and priests deposed Kavadh and imprisoned him. He escaped to the Hephthalites and returned with their army around 498–499. He then fought the Roman war of 502–506, capturing Amida, and a later war of 526–531. Kavadh also began the work of fiscal reorganization, though how much belongs to him and how much to his son is disputed.

Khosrow I (531–579) completed it, according to later sources. A land survey fixed assessments on the basis of area and crop, and a poll tax was collected in installments, with nobles, priests, soldiers, and scribes exempt. The cavalry was increasingly paid and equipped by the crown, and command was reorganized, later sources say into four regions under generals (spahbeds). Lesser landed notables, the dehqāns, were favored as a counterweight to great houses. Khosrow concluded the Eternal Peace with Justinian in 532, with a Roman payment of gold. Later tradition remembered him as Anushirvan, "of the immortal soul," and idealized his justice, so the reforms must be reconstructed from fragmentary evidence and not from this reputation.

Continuity and Change

Arsacid and Sasanian Government

Changed: a more assertive royal ideology, closer ties to a priestly establishment, more regular taxation and, under Khosrow I, a stronger royal army and land assessment. Persisted: dependence on great noble houses, subordinate regional rulers, Mesopotamian wealth, and heavy cavalry.

Section 32.16

The Sasanian Empire at Its Late-Sixth-Century Height

In 540 Khosrow invaded Syria and sacked Antioch, deporting its people (Section 32.7). A long war over Lazica on the Black Sea followed from 541, ending in the Fifty-Year Peace of 562, under which Rome paid an annual subsidy and Persia accepted toleration for Christians. In the east, Khosrow allied with the Western Turks and the Hephthalite state was destroyed around the 560s, making the Oxus the frontier, although the alliance with the Turks did not last. Around 570 a Sasanian expedition helped expel the Aksumite regime from Yemen and established Sasanian influence there (Chapter 34 treats South Arabia). War with Byzantium resumed in 572, and Khosrow died in 579.

His son Hormizd IV (579–590) continued the Roman war and faced noble resentment. The general Bahram Chobin, of the Mihran house, defeated an eastern invasion around 588 (sources call the invaders Turks, though Hephthalite forces were involved) but was dismissed after a defeat in the Caucasus and revolted in 589. Nobles deposed and killed Hormizd in 590 and raised his son Khosrow II, but Bahram took Ctesiphon and ruled briefly as king.

Khosrow II fled to Roman territory, where the emperor Maurice supplied troops, and returned in 591 to defeat Bahram, who fled to the Turks. As the price of help, Khosrow ceded Dara, Martyropolis, and much of Armenia to Rome. The episode shows both the strength of the dynasty, which survived a general's usurpation, and its dependence on noble factions and foreign help. The empire in 591 remained a wealthy and formidable great power with a strengthened fiscal and military structure, but its succession politics remained fragile and its religious and frontier relations were strained. Chapter 33 turns to Christianity across the region, and Chapter 34 takes up Arabia and the war that began in 602.

Primary Source Window

Shapur I's Inscription at the Ka'ba-ye Zartosht (ŠKZ), Naqsh-e Rustam

Creator: Shapur I, king of kings (r. 240/242–270) · Date: after 260, probably in the 260s · Languages: Middle Persian, Parthian, and Greek · Type: Royal inscription (sometimes called the Res Gestae Divi Saporis) carved on the walls of the Ka'ba-ye Zartosht, a tower of Achaemenid date facing the Achaemenid royal tombs at Naqsh-e Rustam.

Context: The inscription was placed on a monument of the earlier Persian dynasty, in a landscape that already carried Ardashir's investiture relief and, nearby, Shapur's own victory reliefs. Reading the site together with its text is part of the evidence.

Paraphrase: Shapur introduces himself with his titles and lineage and claims rule over Iranians and non-Iranians. He lists a very long series of provinces and peoples within his empire, then narrates his campaigns against Roman emperors: Gordian, Philip, and Valerian. He enumerates captured cities and regions, states that he took Valerian prisoner, and describes the deportation of captives. The text also records pious foundations of fires dedicated to himself and his family, and lists nobles and officials of his court.

What Shapur is trying to do: The text asserts that his rule is divinely sanctioned, universal in claim, and victorious over Rome. The long lists of provinces and of dignitaries present the empire as an ordered whole and bind the court's great houses to the king. The pious foundations tie the monarchy to Zoroastrian ritual. Using three languages suggests several audiences: Iranian nobility and priests, the empire's Parthian-speaking elite, and Greek readers and Roman officials.

Corroboration and limits: Roman sources and archaeology confirm parts of the story, including major Roman defeats, the fall of Dura-Europos in the 250s, and Valerian's capture. They differ on details, for instance on how Gordian died and how Valerian was seized. The provincial lists may include areas claimed rather than permanently ruled, and the claims of victory are the king's own. The inscription is damaged in places, and Roman recovery after 260 is absent from it.

  2. Why might the king have chosen this particular site and three languages?

Multiple Perspectives

Living in the Sasanian Empire

These viewpoints are reconstructed from inscriptions, rabbinic and Syriac texts, Armenian and Roman histories, and archaeology. They are analytical syntheses, not quotations from named individuals.

A great noble: Standing, estates, and cavalry command depend on service to the king of kings, and the family's position is also its own inheritance. Rank and precedence are defended against rivals and, when necessary, against the crown itself. Noble support or opposition can make or unmake a king, as the Paikuli inscription and later depositions show.
A Roman captive deported into Iran: Taken from Syria or Anatolia after a siege, the captive is resettled far from home, perhaps in Khuzestan or Fars. Skills in building, metalwork, or farming are put to use on royal cities and estates. Over generations, such communities, often Christian, became part of Sasanian society, and the empire's rulers treated them as both labor and a sign of victory.
A member of a rabbinic circle in Babylonia: Life is governed by Jewish law and a community leadership that also deals with Persian officials. The imperial government is a power to be accommodated, and the principle attributed to Samuel, that the law of the kingdom is binding, captures this accommodation. Taxes, property, and occasional hostile royal measures shape what can safely be done.
An Armenian noble (nakharar): The family holds land and armies in a kingdom between two empires and weighs Roman and Iranian offers against its own interest. After the partition and the end of the Arsacid monarchy in Persarmenia, the family faces a Sasanian marzbān, and the dispute over religion and noble privilege becomes a matter of resistance, as in 450–451, and then negotiation, as in 484.

Chapter Summary

Ardashir I overthrew the Arsacids in 224 and founded a dynasty that presented itself as restoring Iranian kingship while continuing much Arsacid practice. Shapur I's victories over Rome, culminating in Valerian's capture, gave the monarchy lasting prestige.

The empire combined royal ceremony and ideology with governance that depended on provinces, officials, great houses, and local dynasts. Social hierarchy and cavalry power sustained the crown and also constrained it. Mesopotamia and Ctesiphon, not only the plateau, were the economic and strategic heartland, with agricultural revenue, cities, and trade networks reaching the Gulf and beyond.

Zoroastrian institutions enjoyed royal patronage, but a rigid state church from 224 is doubtful. Jews, Christians, Manichaeans and others lived under shifting policy. The Roman-Iranian frontier mixed war and diplomacy, with Armenia as the main buffer; Shapur II, Peroz, Kavadh I, and Khosrow I show how the empire absorbed defeat, succession crisis, and social conflict, and was reformed. By 591, Sasanian Iran looked powerful but carried tensions that Chapters 33 and 34 take forward.

Kavadh I and Khosrow I confronted a crisis of noble power and fiscal strain. The Mazdak movement, deposition and restoration, and the cadastral and military reforms strengthened the crown, though later sources idealized Khosrow, and the chronology is debated.

Roman-Iranian relations combined war, subsidies, ransom, regulated trade, and diplomatic equality, so rivalry was intermittent. Armenia, Iberia, and Albania were active polities whose choices shaped the frontier.

By 591, after Bahram Chobin's revolt and Khosrow II's restoration with Roman help, the empire was wealthy and formidable but politically fragile. This sets up Chapters 33 and 34.

Chapter Reflection

Sasanian Iran changes how late antiquity should be understood. The Mediterranean world was not alone: for four centuries a second great empire, with its own ideology, capital, religion, and army, faced Rome and Byzantium, and the two shaped each other through war, diplomacy, trade, and the movement of captives.

The dynasty's history is also the history of a bargain between kings and aristocrats. Royal ideology, Zoroastrian patronage, and fiscal reform strengthened the crown, but the great houses, regional rulers, and local notables remained indispensable. Mesopotamia's wealth and its religious variety sustained the state and exposed it to attack, and the empire survived defeats, depositions, and eastern pressure, which shows institutional resilience.

The strength of 591 did not remove the structural problems of succession politics, noble factionalism, fiscal pressure, and a long frontier. Chapter 33 turns to Christianity across Rome, Armenia, Mesopotamia, and Iran, and Chapter 34 to Arabia and the confrontation of the early seventh century, where these tensions would be tested.
