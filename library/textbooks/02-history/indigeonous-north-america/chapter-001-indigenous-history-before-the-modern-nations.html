Indigenous History Before the Modern Nations

Indigenous Peoples of North America: A Continental History

Chapter Opening

Before Canada, the United States, Mexico, or the present self-governing arrangements of Greenland within the Kingdom of Denmark existed, the continent they now occupy was already home to hundreds of distinct peoples, nations, and political communities, each with its own history, language, economy, religion, and relationship to the land. Those histories did not begin with European arrival, and they did not end with it either.

Open almost any older general history of North America and the story typically begins in 1492, or perhaps a little earlier with a brief gesture toward "the first Americans" before hurrying on to Columbus, Jamestown, and the founding of colonies that would eventually become nation states. This structure carries a hidden assumption: that Indigenous history matters chiefly as a prologue to someone else's story, a backdrop against which colonization, and later national development, take place. This textbook rejects that structure. It treats Indigenous North America as a continental subject in its own right, with its own chronology, its own turning points, its own internal diversity, and its own continuing present, rather than as a preface to the history of European colonies and the states that succeeded them.

Consider the scale of what this means. At the moment of first sustained European contact, the lands now called North America were home to many hundreds of distinct peoples speaking languages from dozens of unrelated language families, organized into political communities ranging from small kin-based bands to large agricultural towns, regional chiefdoms, and confederacies capable of conducting sophisticated diplomacy across enormous distances. A Haudenosaunee council fire, a Pueblo village government, a Comanche band, a Tlingit clan house, and an Inuit hunting camp were not variations on a single "Indian" theme. They were distinct political and cultural traditions, shaped by distinct histories, environments, and choices, no more reducible to one another than the political traditions of Sweden, Venice, and the Ottoman Empire are reducible to a single "European" model.

This chapter does not begin the chronological narrative of human settlement, migration, or early technology; that story begins in Chapter 2. Instead, this chapter asks a more basic question: how should a history of Indigenous North America actually be written, and what habits of mind does it require of the student before the narrative begins? Answering that question means confronting several problems directly. What do terms such as "tribe," "nation," and "Indian" actually mean, and where do they come from? How do historians know what they know about societies that, for much of their history, left no documents in the sense modern readers expect? What is lost when the story is divided into "prehistoric" and "historic" periods, or into "before contact" and "after contact"? And what does it mean to say that colonization was not a single event in 1492 but a centuries-long, geographically uneven process that unfolded differently in Florida, the Great Lakes, California, the Arctic, and the Southwest?

This chapter also introduces a set of commitments that will recur throughout the textbook's one hundred and fifty chapters: that Indigenous peoples were, and remain, historical actors rather than passive subjects of other people's history; that many Indigenous nations are political communities with continuing governmental and legal standing, not simply ethnic or racial categories; that oral tradition, archaeology, linguistics, and written documentation are all legitimate but differently limited forms of historical evidence; and that Indigenous history did not stop when colonization began. It continued, and continues, through resistance, adaptation, negotiation, cultural renewal, and the ordinary business of living, governing, and remembering.

None of this is intended to minimize the catastrophic consequences that colonization brought to Indigenous peoples across the continent: disease, warfare, dispossession, forced removal, and, in some cases, deliberate destruction of communities and ways of life. Those consequences are central to this textbook and are treated in detail across many later chapters. But a history that begins and ends with catastrophe erases exactly the fuller story this book sets out to tell: peoples who governed themselves, farmed, traded, made war and peace, raised families, told stories, built cities and villages and hunting camps, argued over politics and religion, and adapted to changing circumstances for thousands of years before Europeans arrived, and who have continued to do all of these things in the centuries since.

Understanding how to study this history responsibly requires the same historical toolkit historians bring to any large, diverse, and long-lived set of societies: careful attention to chronology, geography, language, evidence, and the political purposes behind competing national and colonial narratives. It also requires a specific kind of humility, because much of what has been written about Indigenous peoples in the past was written by outsiders, often with little interest in how Indigenous communities understood themselves, and sometimes with a direct interest in justifying dispossession.

This chapter establishes the vocabulary, methodological commitments, and organizing principles that will govern the one hundred and forty nine chapters that follow. It explains why this textbook is organized as a continental history that follows Indigenous developments on their own terms, rather than as a history of European empires and modern nation states into which Indigenous peoples occasionally appear.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why Indigenous North American history is treated as an autonomous continental subject rather than as an introduction to the history of European colonies or modern nation states.
  2. Distinguish among key terms of political and cultural identity, including nation, people, community, band, pueblo, confederacy, tribe, First Nation, Alaska Native, Inuit, Métis, Native American, and American Indian, and explain the contexts in which each is used.
  3. Describe the immense linguistic, political, environmental, and cultural diversity of Indigenous North America, and explain why no single "Indian" culture ever existed.
  4. Identify the major forms of evidence historians use to reconstruct Indigenous history, including archaeology, oral tradition, linguistics, genetics, material culture, and written records.
  5. Evaluate oral tradition as a historical source, explaining both its evidentiary value and the interpretive care it requires.
  6. Analyze the limitations and strengths of archaeological evidence for reconstructing the identities and histories of past peoples.
  7. Explain why the term "prehistoric" describes an absence of written documentation rather than an absence of history, and why this distinction matters.
  8. Critique the use of 1492 as a master dividing line for Indigenous North American history, given the uneven chronology of European contact across the continent.
  9. Describe colonization as a centuries-long, regionally uneven process rather than a single event.
  10. Assess the concept of Indigenous agency and apply it to historical developments discussed later in this textbook.
  11. Explain the concept of Indigenous sovereignty and distinguish political nationhood from racial or ethnic categorization.
  12. Describe the ethical questions surrounding archaeological collection, museum practice, and the repatriation of ancestral remains and cultural items.

Historical Context

Before students encounter the specific content of this textbook, the deep human history of Volume I, the agricultural and urban civilizations of Volume II, the diplomatic and imperial worlds of Volumes III through VI, the conquest and reservation era of Volume VII, the assimilation and reorganization periods of Volumes VIII and IX, and the sovereignty and resurgence movements of Volume X, they need a shared, carefully defined vocabulary and method.

North America stretches from the Arctic coasts of Greenland and northern Canada through the temperate forests, grasslands, deserts, and mountain ranges of Canada, the United States, and Mexico, encompassing an extraordinary range of environments: Arctic tundra and sea ice, subarctic boreal forest, the Northwest Coast's temperate rainforest, the arid Great Basin and Southwest, the tallgrass and shortgrass prairies of the Great Plains, the deciduous forests of the Northeast and Southeast, and the Mediterranean and desert ecologies of California and northern Mexico. No single environment, economy, or political system unified all of these regions before European colonization, or indeed after it. Long before any European power established a permanent presence on the continent, the peoples of these regions had already developed distinct and sophisticated economies suited to their environments: Arctic maritime hunting, Plains bison hunting, Pacific Northwest salmon fishing and cedar woodworking, Southwestern irrigation agriculture, and the intensive maize, bean, and squash agriculture that supported large towns and political centers across much of eastern North America.

Historians studying this continent must resist two opposite errors. The first error treats Indigenous societies as static and unchanging, "timeless" peoples living outside history until Europeans arrived to bring change. This error ignores centuries and millennia of migration, technological innovation, political transformation, religious change, warfare, and economic development that Indigenous societies experienced entirely on their own terms. The second error, a kind of overcorrection, treats colonization as having so completely disrupted Indigenous societies that continuity with the past was severed and Indigenous history effectively ends at the moment of conquest. This textbook adopts neither error. It treats Indigenous societies as dynamic, changing, and historically active before European contact, and it treats Indigenous peoples as continuing historical actors after contact, through resistance, negotiation, adaptation, and cultural renewal that persists into the present.

A further complication concerns political vocabulary. Modern legal and popular usage in the United States and Canada relies on terms such as "tribe," "band," "First Nation," and "nation," each carrying specific legal and historical meanings that developed through particular colonial and national histories rather than through Indigenous peoples' own original terms for themselves. Understanding these terms, their legal weight, and their limitations is essential before any narrative history can be responsibly told. Section 1.2 of this chapter examines this vocabulary directly.

Section 1.1

What Is Indigenous North American History?

Every regional history textbook must, at some point, explain what its subject actually is. For a history of France or Japan, the political referent, while not without its own complications, is at least anchored to a single, currently existing state. A history of Indigenous North America has no such anchor, and deliberately so. It is a history of a continent's original peoples and political communities, encompassing hundreds of distinct nations, dozens of language families, and thousands of years of development, whose histories neither began with, nor are properly organized around, the modern states of Canada, the United States, Mexico, and Greenland.

This opening section establishes the central interpretive commitment of the entire textbook: Indigenous North America is the organizing subject, not a prelude to the history of European colonies or modern nation states. This commitment carries several consequences that recur throughout the following chapters. First, this textbook does not begin its narrative with European arrival, because doing so would make the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries an arbitrary starting point for peoples whose history extends back many thousands of years, and whose political, agricultural, and urban achievements, examined in Volumes I and II, developed entirely independent of European influence. Second, this textbook does not organize its geography according to the modern boundaries of the United States, Canada, and Mexico, because doing so would slice apart peoples, homelands, and trade networks that never respected those boundaries and in many cases long predate them. Third, this textbook does not treat Indigenous history as ending at the moment of conquest, because doing so would erase more than a century of continued political organization, cultural adaptation, and, in the modern era, resurgence that this textbook's later volumes examine directly.

None of these commitments is intended to diminish the historical importance of European colonization, the formation of the United States, Canada, and Mexico, or the profound and often catastrophic transformations these developments brought to Indigenous peoples. Colonization is, and will remain throughout this textbook, one of the central subjects of Indigenous North American history from the sixteenth century onward, and many chapters, particularly across Volumes IV through IX, necessarily give colonial and national policy sustained and serious attention. But treating Indigenous history as merely a backdrop for, or a casualty of, someone else's national story would misrepresent the actual historical record this textbook sets out to explain.

Section 1.2

Peoples, Nations, and the Language of Belonging

Students encountering this textbook for the first time should understand the vocabulary of Indigenous political identity, where it comes from, and why the same population may be described by several different terms depending on legal, historical, or community context.

Indigenous political organization across North America ranged enormously. Some communities were organized as small, mobile bands built around kinship, moving seasonally to exploit different resources. Others were organized as settled agricultural towns, sometimes fortified and governed by councils or hereditary leaders. Still others formed large regional political systems: chiefdoms exercising authority over multiple towns, and confederacies uniting multiple distinct nations under shared diplomatic and military arrangements, such as the Haudenosaunee Confederacy examined later in this chapter and in Chapter 26. No single form of political organization describes all, or even most, Indigenous societies, and this textbook resists any implication that a generic "tribal" model applies uniformly across the continent.

The word "tribe" itself carries a complicated history. It entered common usage through European and later United States and Canadian administrative and legal practice, and it has since acquired specific legal meaning, particularly in United States federal Indian law, where "federally recognized tribe" designates a political status carrying specific rights and government-to-government relationships with the United States. This legal usage is important and cannot simply be discarded, since it governs real political and legal relationships examined in later chapters, including Chapters 93, 114, and 118. At the same time, "tribe" as a general descriptive term can flatten enormous political variation, implying a single, simple form of organization where the historical reality included everything from small bands to complex confederacies and centralized chiefdoms. This textbook uses "tribe" primarily in its specific legal and administrative sense, and prefers more precise terms, nation, people, community, band, pueblo, and confederacy, when describing the historical and political variety of Indigenous societies.

Other terms carry their own histories, and their legal precision varies considerably. "First Nations people" is widely used in Canada for Indigenous peoples who are neither Inuit nor Métis, and it generally includes both Status and Non-Status Indians. "First Nation" is also commonly used for particular communities and their governments, but, importantly, the term itself has no single, general legal definition in Canadian statute; "Indian" and "band" remain the specific terms carrying defined legal meaning under the Indian Act, a distinction examined further in Chapter 85. "Alaska Native" describes Indigenous peoples of Alaska, including Iñupiat, Yupik, Aleut or Unangax̂, and other Alaska Native peoples, and it should be distinguished from two more specific categories: federally recognized Alaska Native tribes and villages, which hold a government-to-government relationship with the United States, and Alaska Native corporations created under the Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act of 1971, which are business corporations owned by Alaska Native shareholders rather than tribal governments, a distinction examined further in Chapter 115. "Native American" and "American Indian" are both used in the United States, sometimes interchangeably and sometimes with different connotations depending on region, generation, and community preference; this textbook uses both terms as appropriate to context and, wherever possible, prefers specific national names.

"Métis" requires similar precision. The term does not simply describe anyone of mixed Indigenous and European ancestry. As Canadian courts and Métis governments themselves have emphasized, "Métis" refers to distinct Indigenous peoples who developed their own communities, customs, collective identities, and ways of life historically, particularly, though not exclusively, in the context of the fur trade economy of the northern Plains and the Great Lakes and Hudson Bay watersheds. Intermarriage between Indigenous and European traders was part of how these distinct communities formed, but ancestry alone does not make a person or a community Métis in the sense this textbook uses the term; a recognized, continuing Métis community, identity, and self-government tradition does, a subject examined further in Chapter 52.

Historical Vocabulary
Nation, Tribe, and Sovereignty

Nation, as used throughout this textbook, describes a distinct people with its own language, territory, government, and history, without implying possession of a fully independent modern state. Many Indigenous nations exercised, and many continue to exercise, forms of self-government, including law-making, diplomacy, and control over membership, that meet reasonable historical and political definitions of nationhood even though they were never organized as nation states in the modern European sense.

Tribe carries both a general descriptive meaning, referring loosely to an Indigenous people or community, and a specific legal meaning in United States federal Indian law, where "federally recognized tribe" designates formal political status and a government-to-government relationship with the United States federal government. Not every historically and culturally distinct Indigenous people holds federal recognition, and recognition status has itself been the subject of extended historical and legal struggle, examined in Chapter 118.

Sovereignty, in the context of Indigenous nations, refers to inherent political authority that predates the colonial and settler states that later claimed jurisdiction over Indigenous territories. The extent to which that authority is recognized, constrained, or actively implemented today varies substantially among the United States, Canada, Mexico, and particular Indigenous nations, a variation this textbook examines directly in Section 1.8 rather than treating as a single uniform legal status. Sovereignty is, in every case, a political and legal concept, not a racial or ethnic one: it describes a nation's right to govern itself, not the ancestry of its individual citizens. This distinction matters because Indigenous identity is frequently, and inaccurately, treated in popular discourse as a matter of racial classification rather than political citizenship in a specific nation, an issue examined further in Section 1.8 and again in Chapter 144.

Indigenous Names and European Names

A related problem concerns naming. Many peoples are commonly known today by exonyms, names given to them by outsiders, often other Indigenous peoples, European colonizers, or later government officials, rather than by their own endonyms, the names they use for themselves. The people commonly called the Navajo, for example, call themselves Diné, meaning "the People." Many of the peoples commonly grouped together under exonyms such as Chippewa or Ojibwe, along with several closely related peoples including the Odawa and Potawatomi, are part of a broader Anishinaabe cultural and linguistic identity, though Anishinaabe is not a simple synonym for Ojibwe alone and specific national names remain important. The Iroquois Confederacy's member nations refer to their political union as the Haudenosaunee, meaning "people of the longhouse." The peoples of the Arctic commonly called Eskimo in older sources generally prefer Inuit, Yupik, or more specific national names, since "Eskimo" is now widely regarded as an exonym with contested and sometimes pejorative origins, though it remains in limited technical and legal use, particularly in Alaska.

This textbook uses Indigenous endonyms wherever they are well established and widely recognized in current scholarship, while also identifying familiar historical exonyms where necessary for clarity, source recognition, and continuity with the documentary record, since many primary sources, treaties, and older secondary scholarship use exonyms exclusively. Recognizing the distinction between endonym and exonym is not a matter of political correctness for its own sake; it is a basic historical skill, since failing to recognize that "Sioux," for instance, is a French rendering of an Ojibwe exonym for the Lakota, Dakota, and Nakota peoples can obscure important distinctions between related but politically distinct nations.

Section 1.3

A Continent of Extraordinary Diversity

No error is more common in popular understanding of Indigenous North America than the assumption of a single, generic "Indian" culture: a composite image, often drawn from Plains equestrian societies of the nineteenth century, applied indiscriminately to peoples as different as Arctic Inuit whale hunters, Pueblo farmers of the arid Southwest, Northwest Coast fishers and woodworkers, and Northeastern Woodlands agriculturalists. This composite image has no basis in historical reality, and this textbook explicitly rejects it.

Linguistically, North America was, and remains, one of the most diverse regions on Earth. Scholars group Indigenous North American languages into dozens of distinct language families, including Algonquian, spoken by peoples from the Atlantic coast to the Great Plains; Iroquoian, spoken by the Haudenosaunee nations and others; Siouan, spoken across much of the Plains and parts of the Southeast; Athabaskan, spoken by peoples of the Subarctic interior as well as the Diné and Apache peoples of the Southwest, whose ancestors migrated south in a movement examined in Chapter 30; Uto-Aztecan, spoken from the Great Basin through the Southwest and into Mesoamerica; Salishan and Wakashan, spoken along the Northwest Coast; and many others, some representing language isolates with no known relatives. These language families are not dialects of a single "Indian language." Many are as different from one another as English is from Mandarin or Arabic, reflecting deep historical separations reaching back thousands of years.

Politically and economically, the diversity is equally striking. The peoples of the Northwest Coast developed complex, socially stratified societies based on abundant salmon fisheries, elaborate woodworking traditions, and ceremonial systems including the potlatch, without practicing agriculture. The peoples of the Southwest developed intensive irrigation agriculture supporting large, permanent Pueblo communities. The peoples of the Eastern Woodlands developed maize, bean, and squash agriculture that supported towns, regional chiefdoms, and, at Cahokia, examined in Chapter 22, a major urban center. The peoples of the Great Plains, prior to the arrival of horses, combined village-based farming along river valleys with seasonal bison hunting, a pattern that changed dramatically after horses spread across the region, examined in Chapter 49. The peoples of the Arctic developed highly specialized maritime and land-based hunting technologies suited to one of the most demanding environments on Earth. No single economic or political model describes this range of adaptation.

Language Families and Cultural Regions

To manage this diversity, scholars use two broad organizing tools: language families, which group peoples by linguistic relationship, and cultural regions, which group peoples loosely by shared environment and broadly similar economic and cultural patterns, including the Arctic, Subarctic, Northwest Coast, Plateau, Great Basin, California, Southwest, Plains, Northeast, and Southeast, along with the northern Mexican borderlands. This textbook uses both frameworks throughout, particularly in Volume III, because they provide genuinely useful ways of organizing an otherwise unmanageable amount of historical material.

However, students must understand that both frameworks are analytical conveniences rather than fixed, natural categories. Cultural regions have fuzzy boundaries, and peoples near the edges of one region often shared significant features with neighboring regions. Language families tell us about historical linguistic relationships, not necessarily about shared culture, political alliance, or common identity; two peoples speaking related Algonquian languages might have been rivals or even enemies, while two neighboring peoples speaking entirely unrelated languages might have maintained close trade and diplomatic relationships for centuries. This textbook uses cultural regions and language families as organizing tools while remaining alert, as this section has emphasized, to the diversity, overlap, and historical change that any such classification necessarily simplifies.

Section 1.4

How Indigenous History Is Known: Evidence and Method

A history of Indigenous North America rests on a distinctive evidentiary base, one that requires methods and habits of interpretation different from those used to study societies documented primarily through a single continuous written tradition. It is important to state this precisely: many Indigenous societies north of Mesoamerica did not maintain surviving precolonial written archives of the kind produced in Mesoamerica, where civilizations such as the Maya developed sophisticated hieroglyphic writing systems and left texts that survive to the present, examined further in Chapter 14. For the regions this textbook treats as its primary geographic focus, historians draw chiefly on archaeology, oral tradition, linguistics, genetics, environmental and climatic evidence, and material culture for the centuries before sustained European colonization, supplemented from the sixteenth century onward by an increasing volume of written documentation, produced initially almost entirely by European observers, missionaries, traders, and officials, and, from the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries onward, increasingly by Indigenous writers themselves.

Oral Tradition as Historical Evidence

Oral tradition deserves particular attention because it has often been dismissed, in older scholarship, as myth or folklore rather than history. This dismissal reflects a narrow assumption that only written documents can carry reliable historical information. In fact, oral traditions among Indigenous North American peoples frequently encode structured, transmitted knowledge about migrations, origins, political relationships, environmental events, and moral and religious understanding, preserved across generations through carefully maintained methods of memorization, ceremony, and formal transmission by designated knowledge keepers. Some oral traditions describe events, such as volcanic eruptions, floods, or long-distance migrations, that independent archaeological or geological evidence has corroborated, suggesting that some oral historical knowledge can preserve information across many centuries.

At the same time, oral tradition requires many of the same critical questions that historians apply to any other source, alongside source-specific cultural protocols governing who may share, interpret, or record particular traditions. Oral narratives are shaped by the purposes of the communities that maintain them: political legitimation, religious teaching, moral instruction, and the reinforcement of identity and belonging. They may compress long periods of time into symbolic events, attribute historical developments to ancestral or supernatural figures, or emphasize meaning over strict chronological sequence. None of this makes oral tradition less valuable as evidence; it makes it a different kind of evidence, requiring different interpretive tools, in the same way that a medieval chronicle written to glorify a royal dynasty requires source criticism rather than literal acceptance. This textbook treats oral tradition neither as literal historical record nor as mere legend, but as a legitimate historical source requiring the same combination of respect and critical interpretation that historians bring to any other form of evidence.

Archaeology and Its Limits

Archaeology provides essential evidence for reconstructing settlement patterns, technology, diet, trade, architecture, and demographic change, particularly for periods and regions with limited written or oral documentation. Radiocarbon dating, stratigraphic analysis, and the study of material remains, from stone tools to ceramics to architectural features, allow historians to reconstruct broad patterns of change over long periods of time.

Archaeology also has significant limits. Material remains rarely indicate language, political organization, religious belief, or specific ethnic or national identity with certainty. Archaeologists studying a particular set of artifacts, sometimes called an "archaeological culture," cannot automatically conclude that the people who made those artifacts spoke a particular language, organized themselves politically in a particular way, or correspond precisely to a later historically or ethnographically documented people. This textbook is careful throughout to distinguish archaeological evidence, which describes material patterns, from claims about identity, language, or political organization, which require additional forms of evidence and, where possible, engagement with the oral traditions and self-understanding of the descendant communities connected to a given archaeological record.

Linguistics, Genetics, and Environmental Evidence

Historical linguistics allows scholars to reconstruct relationships among language families, trace patterns of migration and contact, and estimate, with considerable uncertainty, the relative antiquity of linguistic divergence. Genetic research, particularly the study of ancient DNA, has provided new evidence about population movements and relationships, discussed further in Chapter 2, but this evidence requires careful handling: genetic ancestry does not equate to cultural, linguistic, or national identity, and this textbook does not conflate the two. Environmental and climatic evidence, drawn from pollen records, ice cores, tree rings, and sediment analysis, helps historians reconstruct the changing conditions, droughts, floods, and climatic shifts, that shaped agricultural viability, settlement patterns, and migration across the continent.

Section 1.5

Knowledge, Authority, and Ethics in Studying Indigenous History

The study of Indigenous history raises questions of authority and ethics that this textbook addresses directly rather than treating as peripheral concerns. For much of the history of American and Canadian archaeology and anthropology, non-Indigenous scholars studied Indigenous peoples, their history, and their material culture with little formal consultation, and sometimes active exclusion of, the descendant communities most directly connected to that history. Human remains and sacred and cultural items were, in many cases, removed from burial sites and community control and placed in museum and university collections, often without consent.

In recent decades, this relationship has changed substantially, though not completely. Many Indigenous nations maintain tribal historic preservation offices, cultural resource departments, and formal government-to-government consultation relationships with federal and state agencies regarding archaeological work, land management, and historical interpretation on and near their territories. A growing number of Indigenous scholars now work within, and sometimes reshape, the fields of history, archaeology, and anthropology. Collaborative and community-directed research, in which Indigenous nations participate as partners rather than subjects of study, has become an increasingly important model, though significant institutional and financial inequities remain.

The question of who holds authority to interpret Indigenous history, material culture, and ancestral remains has become a serious and ongoing subject of debate, discussed further in this chapter's Historical Debate feature and in later chapters on repatriation and cultural resource management. The Native American Graves Protection and Repatriation Act, enacted by the United States in 1990, established specific federal statutory and regulatory requirements governing federal agencies and federally funded museums, with major implementing regulations most recently revised in 2023 and 2024, a development examined fully in Chapter 125. Canada has approached repatriation differently and, to date, less uniformly: rather than a single federal statute directly equivalent to NAGPRA, Canadian repatriation has developed through Indigenous-led initiatives, individual museum and institutional policies, specific agreements, provincial legislation, and ongoing federal work toward a broader national framework, examined further in Chapter 125. This textbook previews these developments here because the ethical questions they raise, about consent, ownership, and interpretive authority, bear directly on how the history in the chapters that follow should be read and taught.

Section 1.6

Time, Periodization, and the Myth of "Prehistory"

Historians conventionally use the term "prehistoric" to describe periods before the existence of written documentation. Applied carelessly to Indigenous North America, however, this term risks suggesting something it does not, and should not, mean: that Indigenous societies had no history, or that their history began only when European observers began writing about them. This chapter insists on a precise distinction: "prehistoric," properly used, describes an absence of a particular kind of evidence, contemporary written documents, not an absence of historical events, historical memory, or historical consciousness.

Indigenous societies across North America maintained sophisticated systems of historical knowledge, including oral tradition, pictographic and mnemonic record-keeping systems such as winter counts among Plains peoples, wampum belts recording treaties and events among Haudenosaunee and other Northeastern peoples, and other systems of structured memory and transmission. These systems constitute historical knowledge in a meaningful sense, even though they differ from the written documentary tradition that European historiography developed. This textbook therefore uses "prehistoric" narrowly, as a technical term describing the limits of a specific evidentiary record, and avoids any implication that Indigenous history "begins" only with European written observation.

Historical Vocabulary
Prehistory and Historical Time

Prehistory, in conventional historical usage, refers to the period before the existence of written records documenting a society. It is fundamentally a statement about the limits of a particular kind of evidence, not a judgment about whether a society possessed history, memory, or historical consciousness. Applied to Indigenous North America, the term risks two misleading implications if used carelessly: first, that Indigenous societies lacked historical knowledge of their own past before European contact, which is false, given the oral, pictographic, and mnemonic systems described in this section; and second, that European written observation is what makes a period "historical," which imposes a Eurocentric standard on a continent with its own long-standing systems of historical knowledge.

This textbook uses "prehistoric" sparingly and with this caution attached, and prefers, where possible, more specific chronological or cultural terms, such as "the Archaic period" or "the pre-contact era," while remaining aware that even "pre-contact" requires the qualification developed in the following section: contact occurred at dramatically different times in different regions.

Beyond "Pre-Contact" and "Post-Contact"

A closely related problem concerns the common division of Indigenous history into "pre-contact" and "post-contact" periods, treating 1492, or sometimes a specific regional contact date, as a master dividing line. This framing breaks down under close examination, and the Norse case shows why: Norse settlers established a sustained colonial presence in Greenland beginning around 985 CE that lasted roughly four centuries before disappearing around the fifteenth century, with later interaction with Thule Inuit peoples beginning by about the thirteenth century, while a separate and much briefer Norse presence at L'Anse aux Meadows in present-day Newfoundland, around 1000 CE, lasted only a few years and is described, along with wider Vinland exploration and Indigenous encounters, in the sagas discussed in Chapter 37. These were two different kinds of Norse presence, of very different duration and character, both occurring centuries before Columbus. Spanish contact with Florida and the Southeast began in the early sixteenth century, while sustained European presence in California did not begin until the late eighteenth century, and many interior peoples of the Great Basin, the northern Plains, and the Arctic did not experience direct, sustained contact with Europeans or their descendants until the nineteenth century or, in some Arctic and Subarctic cases, even later.

Complicating the picture further, European-introduced diseases, horses, firearms, and trade goods frequently spread across Indigenous trade and diplomatic networks well ahead of direct European presence. Epidemic diseases could reach interior peoples decades before those peoples ever saw a European in person, examined in Chapter 40. Horses, introduced by the Spanish in the Southwest, spread across the Plains and transformed Plains societies generations before most Plains peoples had direct, sustained contact with Europeans or Euro-Americans, examined in Chapter 49. This means that "contact" was not a single moment for the continent, or even for a single people; it was frequently a staggered process in which the indirect effects of European colonization elsewhere arrived well before Europeans themselves. This textbook accordingly avoids treating any single date as a uniform turning point for the entire continent, and instead traces the uneven, regionally specific chronology of colonization directly.

Section 1.7

Colonization as Process, Not Event

Building directly on the previous section's chronological argument, this section introduces colonization as a conceptual subject the textbook will return to repeatedly rather than narrate in full here. Colonization of North America was not a single event, or even a single kind of event. It involved distinct European and, later, independent settler state actors, Spanish, French, English, Dutch, Russian, and, from the late eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, the independent governments of the United States, Mexico, and Canada, each pursuing different strategies, at different times, in different regions, for different purposes, ranging from resource extraction and mission-based conversion to permanent agricultural settlement and formal territorial annexation.

Section 1.8

Agency, Sovereignty, and Nationhood

One of this textbook's governing principles, introduced here and applied throughout the chapters that follow, is that Indigenous peoples possessed, and exercised, historical agency. They were not simply acted upon by more powerful outside forces. They migrated, negotiated, traded, formed and dissolved alliances, fought wars, adopted new technologies and religious ideas selectively, restructured their own governments in response to changing circumstances, and engaged colonial and later national governments strategically, sometimes successfully and sometimes not.

Recognizing Indigenous agency does not mean minimizing the severe imbalances of power, disease vulnerability, and, eventually, overwhelming demographic and military pressure that shaped the outcomes of colonization. It means insisting that Indigenous peoples remain visible in this history as decision-makers, not merely as a population upon whom history happened. A Haudenosaunee diplomat balancing English and French colonial rivalries, a Comanche band expanding its territory and commercial reach through equestrian power, and a Cherokee political leader adopting a written constitution in the nineteenth century were all exercising forms of political judgment and strategic agency that this textbook treats as central historical subjects in their own right, examined respectively in Chapters 55, 50, and 64.

Closely related to agency is the concept of sovereignty, introduced in Section 1.2 and expanded here. Sovereignty describes a nation's inherent authority to govern itself, its territory, and its citizens, and while the underlying historical claim is broadly shared across the continent, the three major settler states this textbook studies have developed distinct legal frameworks for recognizing it, frameworks this textbook treats separately rather than as a single interchangeable model. In United States federal Indian law, "tribal sovereignty" and government-to-government relations are established federal legal concepts, examined in Chapters 93 and 118. In Canada, the relationship is framed through Aboriginal and treaty rights, the inherent right of self-government, Indigenous legal orders, and evolving Crown-Indigenous relations, examined in Chapter 117. In Mexico, a major 2024 constitutional reform recognized Indigenous peoples and communities as subjects of public law possessing legal personality, with guarantees of self-determination and autonomy, a distinct legal tradition examined further where Mexican Indigenous history intersects this textbook's narrative. These frameworks are not simple equivalents of one another. Citizenship or formal membership in a federally recognized United States tribe or a Canadian First Nation is a defined political status governed by each nation's own enrollment and citizenship criteria, while belonging within a Mexican Indigenous community is generally expressed through community membership, self-identification, and recognition under the community's own normative systems, operating within Mexico's distinct constitutional framework. In each of these three legal traditions, however, Indigenous identity remains a matter of political and community belonging rather than simply a matter of ancestry, blood quantum, or physical appearance, a distinction with continuing legal and political significance examined further in Chapter 144.

Section 1.9

Continuity, Change, and a Connected Continent

A responsible history of Indigenous North America must hold two truths together without allowing either to cancel out the other: Indigenous societies changed dramatically over time, long before and after European contact, and Indigenous societies also maintained deep continuities of language, governance, religious tradition, and identity across those same centuries of change, including the catastrophic disruptions of colonization.

Before any European presence on the continent, Indigenous societies experienced enormous transformation: the spread of agriculture, examined in Chapter 13; the rise and decline of major population centers such as Cahokia, examined in Chapter 22; long-distance migrations, including the southward movement of Athabaskan-speaking peoples that produced the Diné and Apache nations of the Southwest; and shifting patterns of trade, alliance, and conflict across the continent. After European contact, Indigenous societies continued to change, sometimes catastrophically, through demographic collapse, forced removal, and loss of territory, but also through selective adoption of new technologies, religious innovation, political reorganization, and, in the modern era, sustained cultural and political resurgence, examined throughout Volume X.

This continent-wide history was never a set of isolated regional stories. Extensive trade networks moved copper, shell, obsidian, agricultural goods, and finished items across enormous distances centuries before European arrival, as the Hopewell exchange networks examined in Chapter 20 demonstrate. Diplomatic relationships, migrations, intermarriage, and warfare connected peoples across vast distances throughout the period this textbook studies. Recognizing Indigenous North America as an interconnected historical system, rather than a collection of disconnected local stories, is essential to understanding how developments in one region, the spread of horses, the collapse of the buffalo economy, or the policies of a distant colonial or national government, could reshape the lives of peoples who had never had direct contact with the original source of that change.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Indigenous Nationhood from Antiquity to the Present

What Changed

  • The territorial extent of Indigenous nations' controlled homelands contracted dramatically between the sixteenth and early twentieth centuries
  • Political structures adapted repeatedly, from pre-contact chiefdoms and confederacies to nineteenth-century written constitutions and, later, modern tribal governments organized partly around federal frameworks such as the Indian Reorganization Act, examined in Chapter 97
  • Economic life shifted from diverse regional subsistence and trade economies to wage labor, reservation and reserve economies, and, in the modern era, tribal enterprises and gaming, examined in Chapters 121 and 122
  • Population size collapsed catastrophically after sustained contact due to epidemic disease, warfare, and displacement, before beginning a sustained recovery in the twentieth century

What Stayed the Same

  • The underlying claim to nationhood and self-governance persisted through every period of pressure examined in this textbook
  • Language, ceremony, kinship systems, and oral tradition continued to be transmitted, even where colonial policy actively sought their suppression
  • Regional and political diversity among Indigenous nations remained a durable feature rather than dissolving into a single undifferentiated identity
  • Indigenous peoples continued to assert historical and legal claims to land, resources, and political recognition across every period this textbook studies

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both change and continuity prevents two opposite errors: treating Indigenous nations as unchanging relics of the past, or treating colonization as having so thoroughly erased Indigenous political life that its modern continuation is somehow inauthentic.

Section 1.10

Review Checkpoint and How This Book Is Organized

This chapter has introduced the vocabulary, methodological commitments, and organizing principles that will govern the study of Indigenous North America throughout this textbook. Before moving into the specific content of subsequent chapters, students should be able to demonstrate understanding of these foundational concepts.

The core insights of this chapter are: Indigenous North American history is an autonomous continental subject, not an introduction to the history of European colonies or modern nation states. Terms such as nation, tribe, band, confederacy, First Nation, Alaska Native, and Métis carry distinct legal and historical meanings that must be used carefully. No single "Indian" culture, language, or political system ever existed; the continent's linguistic, political, and cultural diversity was, and remains, immense. Historians reconstruct this history using archaeology, oral tradition, linguistics, genetics, and documentary evidence, each with distinct strengths and limitations. "Prehistoric" describes an absence of written documentation, not an absence of history. Colonization was a centuries-long, regionally uneven process rather than a single event tied to 1492. Indigenous peoples exercised historical agency throughout, and many Indigenous nations retain continuing political sovereignty distinct from racial or ethnic categorization.

This textbook is organized chronologically and thematically across ten volumes and one hundred fifty chapters, beginning with the deep human history of the continent in Volume I, proceeding through the agricultural and urban transformations of Volume II, the diverse Indigenous worlds on the eve of colonization in Volume III, the encounters and early colonial era of Volume IV, the age of Indigenous power amid competing European empires in Volume V, the era of expanding settler states in Volume VI, the wars and reservation policies of Volume VII, the assimilation era in Volume VIII, the reorganization and postwar era in Volume IX, and the sovereignty and resurgence movements of the contemporary period in Volume X. This organization follows Indigenous historical developments as its primary chronological spine, using the succession of European empires and settler states as necessary context rather than as the book's organizing structure.

"We are a powerful Confederacy, and by your observing the same methods our wise forefathers have taken you will acquire fresh strength and power; therefore whatever befalls you, never fall out with one another."

Excerpted from the printed transcript of the 1744 Treaty of Lancaster proceedings, as recorded and translated by colonial secretaries and later reprinted in the Pennsylvania treaty publications compiled by Benjamin Franklin. The wording reflects colonial transcription and translation practices of the period rather than a Haudenosaunee-authored text.

Paraphrase of surrounding context: In the same address, Canassatego, speaking for the assembled Six Nations delegation, pressed the Pennsylvania, Maryland, and Virginia commissioners to deal honestly with Haudenosaunee land claims and to coordinate their own conduct toward Haudenosaunee interests, and he explicitly urged the colonies to consider forming a union among themselves comparable to the confederation of the Six Nations.

Analysis: This council exchange is valuable to students of this chapter because it demonstrates sophisticated Indigenous diplomatic practice, confident assertion of Haudenosaunee political interests, and direct, documented engagement with colonial governments as a recognized political counterpart rather than a subject population. It also illustrates the confederacy's own political self-understanding, referenced as a model worth emulating.

Limitations: The surviving record was transcribed and edited by colonial secretaries, not by Canassatego or other Haudenosaunee participants, meaning that word choice, emphasis, and omission reflect colonial recording practices as much as the original speech. Later popular retellings have sometimes exaggerated or oversimplified the episode's connection to later colonial and American political developments, a claim this textbook treats with appropriate caution rather than as settled fact.

Historical Debate

When Does Indigenous North American History Properly Begin?

The question: Should a continental history of Indigenous North America begin with the earliest secure archaeological evidence of human presence, tens of thousands of years before any written record, or should it place its primary emphasis on the more richly documented societies of the last two or three thousand years, when agriculture, urbanism, and denser political organization produced a fuller archaeological and, later, documentary record? Historians differ on how much independent narrative weight to give the earliest, most thinly documented period relative to later, better-evidenced developments.

Interpretation A — The earliest secure evidence should anchor the narrative: Some archaeologists and historians argue that beginning with the earliest confirmed human presence, however thin the evidence, is essential to avoid implying that Indigenous history is only as old as its most visible material record, and that skipping quickly over tens of thousands of years risks treating the deep past as mere prelude to later, more "interesting" civilizations.
Interpretation B — Sustained treatment should concentrate where evidence is richest: Other historians argue that responsible history should devote proportionate space to periods and societies where archaeological, oral, and eventually documentary evidence allows genuinely detailed reconstruction, while acknowledging the deeper past honestly but briefly, since extensive speculation about thinly evidenced periods risks overstating what can actually be known.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the same archaeological and genetic evidence discussed in Chapter 2, but differ in how much interpretive weight and narrative space to give a period for which direct evidence remains comparatively sparse and contested.

Why it matters: This textbook resolves the debate practically: it devotes Chapters 2 through 6 to the peopling of the continent and its earliest societies, treating this period as historically significant and worthy of sustained, evidence-based attention, while acknowledging throughout those chapters where the evidence remains provisional, contested, or subject to revision as new archaeological and genetic research emerges.

Student task: Based on the evidence described above, construct an argument for each interpretation, and then explain which approach you find more persuasive and why. Consider what is gained and what is lost by each choice of emphasis.

Multiple Perspectives

Who Gets to Define a "Tribe"? Perspectives on Naming and Belonging

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns scholars and communities have documented. They are not quotations from any single named individual.

From the perspective of a United States federal Indian law framework: A "tribe" is a political entity that either holds federal recognition, with its accompanying rights and government-to-government relationship with the United States, or does not, a status determined through specific legal and administrative processes examined further in Chapter 118.
From the perspective of a citizen of a specific Indigenous nation: Belonging is defined first by citizenship in one's own nation, governed by that nation's own membership criteria, history, language, and governance, categories that existed long before, and operate independently of, any outside government's system of legal recognition.
From the perspective of a twentieth-century anthropologist working within an older disciplinary tradition: "Tribe" functioned as a general social-scientific category describing a level of political organization, useful for cross-cultural comparison, but this usage, developed largely without Indigenous input, risked flattening real differences in scale, governance, and political complexity among the societies it described.
From the perspective of a Métis or non-status individual navigating Canadian law: Legal categories such as "status Indian," "non-status," and "Métis" have determined access to rights, land, and recognition in ways that do not always correspond to lived community ties, family history, or self-identification, producing ongoing political and legal advocacy examined in Chapter 117.
Cause and Effect

Why "Pre-Columbian" and "Prehistoric" Framing Persists

Long-term causes

The historical dominance of European and Euro-American written sources in constructing the documentary record, combined with earlier academic traditions that equated "history" narrowly with written documentation.

Short-term causes

Textbook, museum, and popular media conventions developed over the nineteenth and twentieth centuries that organized North American history around European exploration and colonization as the natural starting point.

Effect

Indigenous history before European contact, and Indigenous political and cultural continuation after it, are frequently underrepresented, misdated, or treated as marginal to the "main" narrative of North American history.

This textbook's response

Consistent use of precise chronological and evidentiary terminology throughout all one hundred fifty chapters, explicit attention to Indigenous agency and continuity, and an organizational structure that follows Indigenous developments as its primary chronological spine.

Historical complexity: Imprecise terminology is not merely a modern problem of textbook writing; it has roots in centuries of colonial administrative practice, missionary writing, and nationalist historiography that had direct political interests in minimizing Indigenous political continuity and prior claims to land.

Everyday Life and Social History

Why Ordinary Knowledge and Belonging Belong in This Chapter

Definitions of nationhood, sovereignty, and historical evidence are not only matters for scholars, judges, and diplomats; they shape how ordinary people have understood their own identity, community, and place across the many centuries this textbook studies. A child learning a nation's origin story from a grandparent, a young hunter learning environmental knowledge passed down through generations, a council member reciting a wampum belt's recorded history, and a modern tribal citizen navigating both their nation's own membership criteria and federal or provincial recognition systems are all engaging with the same basic historical questions this chapter raises: who belongs, how is knowledge transmitted, and who has the authority to say what the past means.

Throughout this textbook, chapters devoted to social and everyday life examine how ordinary people across the continent experienced kinship, gender roles, childhood and education, work, trade, religious practice, and, in later centuries, the day-to-day realities of reservation and reserve life, wage labor, and urban Indigenous communities. Because much of the surviving written record was produced by colonial officials, missionaries, and, later, government agents rather than by Indigenous people themselves, historians must read these sources critically for indirect evidence about groups who left few records of their own account, while also drawing on oral tradition, archaeology, and, increasingly, Indigenous-authored sources to reconstruct these experiences more fully and fairly.

Chapter Summary

This first chapter has introduced Indigenous North America as this textbook's organizing subject and has carefully distinguished a continental, Indigenous-centered approach from the older convention of treating Indigenous history as a brief prologue to the history of European colonies and modern nation states. Indigenous North America, as this textbook defines and studies it, is a continent's worth of distinct peoples, nations, languages, and political traditions whose history extends back many thousands of years and continues into the present.

The chapter has emphasized that the vocabulary of Indigenous identity, nation, tribe, band, confederacy, First Nation, Alaska Native, Métis, Native American, and American Indian, carries specific legal and historical meanings that must be used with care, and that no single "Indian" culture, language, or political system ever existed across a continent whose diversity, linguistic, political, economic, and environmental, was, and remains, immense.

Students have been introduced to the major forms of evidence historians use to reconstruct this history: archaeology, oral tradition, linguistics, genetics, environmental evidence, and, from the sixteenth century onward, written documentation, each with distinctive strengths and limitations that this textbook treats with appropriate care throughout. Oral tradition in particular has been presented as a legitimate historical source requiring critical interpretation rather than uncritical acceptance or dismissal.

The chapter has explained why "prehistoric" describes an absence of written documentation rather than an absence of history, and why 1492 cannot function as a single dividing line for an entire continent whose peoples experienced European contact at dates separated by as much as four centuries. It has introduced colonization as a variable, contested, region-specific process rather than a single event, previewing the detailed regional treatment found throughout Volumes IV through VII.

Finally, the chapter established Indigenous agency, sovereignty, and nationhood as governing concepts for the entire textbook, distinguishing political nationhood from racial or ethnic categorization, and it addressed the ethical questions surrounding archaeological practice, museum collection, and repatriation directly rather than treating them as peripheral concerns. These definitions, distinctions, and commitments are not abstract housekeeping. They are the interpretive foundation on which the following one hundred forty nine chapters are built, and they will recur, explicitly and implicitly, throughout the textbook's treatment of the continent's first peoples, its ancient civilizations, its diverse worlds on the eve of colonization, its encounters with expanding empires, its wars and removals, its assimilation era, and its modern movements for sovereignty, resurgence, and self-determination.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Nation
A distinct people with its own language, territory, government, and history, used in this textbook without implying possession of a fully independent modern state.
Tribe
A general descriptive term for an Indigenous people or community, and, in United States federal Indian law, a specific legal status designating formal government-to-government relations with the federal government.
Band
A small, often mobile, kin-based political community; also a specific legal category in Canadian Indigenous law.
Confederacy
A political union of multiple distinct nations, such as the Haudenosaunee Confederacy, joined for shared diplomatic, military, or governance purposes while retaining distinct identities.
Sovereignty
The inherent political and legal authority of a nation to govern itself, its territory, and its citizens; a claim that predates colonial states, though the extent of its recognition, constraint, and implementation varies substantially among the United States, Canada, Mexico, and particular Indigenous nations.
First Nation
A widely used contemporary term; "First Nations people" generally includes both Status and Non-Status Indians who are neither Inuit nor Métis, while "First Nation" also names particular communities and governments. The term itself has no single general statutory definition, unlike "Indian" and "band" under the Indian Act.
Alaska Native
A term describing Indigenous peoples of Alaska, including Iñupiat, Yupik, Aleut or Unangax̂, and other Alaska Native peoples, distinct from both federally recognized Alaska Native tribes and villages and Alaska Native corporations formed under federal settlement legislation.
Métis
A distinct Indigenous people or peoples, historically formed particularly through the fur trade economy, with their own communities, customs, and collective identity; not a general term for mixed Indigenous-European ancestry.
Endonym
A name a people uses for itself, such as Diné or Anishinaabe, as opposed to a name applied by outsiders.
Exonym
A name applied to a people by outsiders, such as "Navajo" or "Sioux," which may or may not be used or accepted by the people it describes.
Oral tradition
Structured systems of historical, religious, and cultural knowledge transmitted across generations through speech, narrative, ceremony, and memory, treated as a legitimate historical source requiring critical interpretation.
Archaeology
The study of past human societies through material remains, providing essential but interpretively limited evidence about identity, language, and political organization.
Prehistory
A period before the existence of written documentation of a society; a term describing evidentiary limits rather than an absence of history.
Anachronism
The error of applying a later concept, term, or identity to an earlier period in which it did not yet exist or apply.
Colonization as process
The historical framing of European and settler-state expansion as a variable, contested, regionally uneven set of developments rather than a single event.
Indigenous agency
The historical principle that Indigenous peoples acted as decision-makers, negotiators, and strategic actors rather than merely as subjects of outside forces.
Cultural region
An analytical category grouping peoples by shared environment and broadly similar economic and cultural patterns, such as the Northwest Coast or the Southwest.
Language family
A group of languages descended from a common ancestral language, such as Algonquian, Iroquoian, Siouan, Athabaskan, and Uto-Aztecan.
Repatriation
The return of human remains and cultural items to culturally affiliated Indigenous nations; governed in the United States by the specific federal statutory and regulatory requirements of NAGPRA, and pursued in Canada through Indigenous-led initiatives, institutional policies, agreements, and evolving provincial and national frameworks rather than a single equivalent statute.
Traditional knowledge
Systems of environmental, historical, and cultural understanding developed and transmitted within Indigenous communities over generations.
Continuity and change
The historical thinking skill of identifying what persists and what transforms over a given period.
Historiography
The history of historical writing; the study of how interpretations of Indigenous North American history have changed over time and why.

A pictographic historical record, associated particularly with Plains peoples, recording one notable event per year as a mnemonic aid to oral historical recitation.
Wampum
Beads made from shell, used among Haudenosaunee and other Northeastern peoples to embody, memorialize, authenticate, and aid the transmission of diplomatic agreements and relationships, rather than functioning as a direct written transcript.
Federal recognition
Formal acknowledgment by the United States federal government of a tribal nation's political status and government-to-government relationship.
Blood quantum
A measure of documented Indigenous ancestry used by some nations and governments in determining citizenship or eligibility for enrollment, a contested practice examined further in Chapter 144.

Major People and Concepts

Identification & Significance

Canassatego (active 1740s)
Onondaga leader and Haudenosaunee diplomat whose recorded remarks at the 1744 Treaty of Lancaster are examined in this chapter's Primary Source Window and illustrate sophisticated Indigenous diplomatic practice.
The Haudenosaunee Confederacy
A political union of the Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga, Seneca, and Tuscarora nations, discussed further in Chapter 26.
Sovereignty
The inherent political authority of Indigenous nations to govern themselves, central to this textbook's treatment of Indigenous identity as political rather than racial, discussed further in Chapters 93 and 118.
Oral tradition
A legitimate and historically significant form of evidence requiring critical interpretation, discussed throughout this textbook.
Language families of North America
Groupings including Algonquian, Iroquoian, Siouan, Athabaskan, Uto-Aztecan, Salishan, and many others, reflecting deep historical linguistic relationships, discussed further in Chapter 2 and Volume III.
Cultural regions of North America
The Arctic, Subarctic, Northwest Coast, Plateau, Great Basin, California, Southwest, Plains, Northeast, and Southeast, the principal analytical regions this textbook uses, discussed further throughout Volume III.
NAGPRA
United States federal legislation governing repatriation of Native American human remains and cultural items, previewed here and discussed fully in Chapter 125.
Norse Greenland and Vinland
Norse settlement in Greenland began around 985 CE and lasted roughly four centuries, with later interaction with Thule Inuit peoples beginning by about the thirteenth century; the much briefer, separate Norse presence at L'Anse aux Meadows, in present-day Newfoundland around 1000 CE, is the earliest securely documented Norse site in present-day Canada. Broader Norse exploration and encounters with Indigenous peoples elsewhere in the region are described in the Vinland sagas, discussed further in Chapter 37.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has laid the intellectual groundwork for the entire textbook. The habits of mind it introduces, treating Indigenous North America as a continental subject in its own right, using precise political and legal vocabulary, evaluating oral tradition and archaeology as complementary forms of evidence, rejecting 1492 as a master dividing line, and recognizing Indigenous agency and sovereignty, are not isolated skills for a single assignment. They are ways of thinking that should inform the study of every subsequent chapter in this book.

Indigenous North America's history is one of the most geographically, linguistically, and politically diverse historical subjects any textbook can undertake. It has included tens of thousands of years of human presence before any written record; the rise of agricultural societies, towns, and urban centers entirely independent of outside influence; centuries of diplomacy, trade, warfare, and alliance among hundreds of distinct nations; a staggered, centuries-long process of colonization that unfolded differently in every region; and a modern era of political resurgence, legal advocacy, and cultural renewal that continues into the present. Understanding this diversity is not a challenge to the historical significance of Indigenous North America; it is what makes that significance historically accurate rather than flattened into a simplified or politically convenient story.

Students who understand that Indigenous history did not begin with European arrival and did not end with conquest, that terms such as "tribe" and "nation" carry specific and sometimes contested meanings, and that Indigenous peoples remain political communities with continuing sovereignty rather than a vanished or purely historical population, are well equipped to read the rest of this textbook, from the deep human history of Volume I through the sovereignty and resurgence movements of Volume X, with the same careful, evidence-based attention that professional historians and Indigenous communities themselves bring to this subject.

The story this textbook tells extends from the earliest human presence on the continent, examined beginning in Chapter 2, through the unresolved and ongoing questions of contemporary Indigenous sovereignty examined in the textbook's final chapters. It is a story of many peoples, many languages, and many political traditions, joined by overlapping but never identical histories, not a single unbroken thread and not a story that ends. Understanding that story, with all its complexity, diversity, and continuing relevance, is what the rest of this textbook is for.
