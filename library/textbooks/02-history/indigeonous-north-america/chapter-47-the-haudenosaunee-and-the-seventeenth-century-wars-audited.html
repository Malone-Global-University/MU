The Haudenosaunee and the Seventeenth-Century Wars

Indigenous Peoples of North America: A Continental History

Chapter Opening

In the summer of 1701, within a few weeks of one another, Haudenosaunee diplomats took part in two very different ceremonies. At Albany, on July 19, delegates of the Five Nations put their marks to a deed addressed to the English Crown. At Montreal, on August 4, delegates of the same confederacy joined representatives of the French and of dozens of other Indigenous nations in a general peace. Nearly a century of warfare was ending, not in a victory parade but in a deliberate act of diplomatic balancing.

That double settlement is the best vantage point from which to begin a study of the Haudenosaunee and the wars of the seventeenth century. It shows that the Five Nations were not simply raiders whose attacks overwhelmed their neighbors, nor simply clients of one European power or another. They were political communities that fought, negotiated, adopted captives, signed treaties, and shifted their strategies as conditions changed. The settlements of 1701 were possible because Haudenosaunee leaders had spent decades learning how to preserve their independence between French Canada, English New York, and the many Indigenous nations of the Great Lakes.

The conflicts examined in this chapter are conventionally grouped under the label “Beaver Wars.” The label has a long history in North American scholarship, and it points to something real: the Five Nations, their neighbors, and their European trading partners were deeply entangled in the commerce of furs and European goods. But the label also compresses nearly a hundred years of distinct wars, raids, and negotiations into a single economic motive. It leaves out mourning and the replacement of the dead, the security of villages and cornfields, the politics of individual nations within the confederacy, and the independent strategies of Wendat, Neutral, Erie, Susquehannock, Mahican, Algonquin, Abenaki, Odawa, and other peoples who were never merely targets.

This chapter therefore advances a more complex argument. The wars of the seventeenth century were not simply a struggle to monopolize the beaver trade. They emerged from interacting pressures: population loss, mourning and captive adoption, territorial security, commercial competition, political independence, rivalries among Indigenous nations that predated European arrival, and the expansion of French, Dutch, and English colonies. Haudenosaunee nations responded through warfare and diplomacy, while their neighbors pursued their own strategies of resistance, survival, migration, and alliance. No one of these pressures is a sufficient explanation, and their relative weight varied from decade to decade and from nation to nation.

The chapter covers roughly the years 1609 to 1701, with earlier Haudenosaunee political traditions introduced as background. It treats the Five Nations (the Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga, and Seneca) as distinct nations joined in a shared diplomatic confederacy, not as a centralized military state. The Tuscarora, who are treated as a sixth nation of the confederacy only in the early eighteenth century, do not belong among the Five Nations in this chapter. Throughout, the chapter attends to the human costs of the wars: villages burned, stored food destroyed, captives taken, families dispersed, and communities forced to move.

The evidence for these events is uneven, and students should read it critically. Much of the surviving narrative comes from French Jesuit missionaries, Dutch and English officials and traders, and colonial military officers, each with their own purposes. Archaeology, the study of village sites and material remains, offers a check on written accounts but cannot reconstruct individual campaigns. Haudenosaunee wampum records, oral traditions, and modern community scholarship provide a different vantage point, although they are not a simple substitute for seventeenth-century testimony. Casualty figures in colonial sources are especially unreliable and are presented here as contemporary estimates, not established facts.

This chapter builds on earlier discussions. Chapter 26 presents the foundations of Haudenosaunee political life; Chapter 40 treats epidemic disease; Chapter 45 follows French alliance-making and the Wendat migrations; and Chapter 46 examines the fur trade as a commercial system. Here the focus is on the wars themselves and on what they reveal about Haudenosaunee politics and the political geography of the Northeast. The chapter looks ahead to Chapter 48 (English Atlantic colonies), Chapter 53 (captivity and adoption in comparative perspective), Chapter 55 (Indigenous diplomacy), and Chapter 56 (the Covenant Chain), all of which develop themes introduced here.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why historians dispute the label “Beaver Wars” and summarize the main interpretations of Haudenosaunee warfare in the seventeenth century.
  2. Describe the political organization of the Five Nations and explain how national autonomy coexisted with confederacy-level diplomacy.
  3. Analyze mourning war, captivity, and adoption as social and political institutions rather than as irrational violence.
  4. Trace the principal conflicts from the Champlain engagements of 1609–1615 through the Mohawk–Mahican war and the midcentury attacks on the Wendat, Tionontaté, Neutral, and Erie.
  5. Assess how epidemics, firearms, and commercial change altered the conditions of warfare without serving as universal causes.
  6. Evaluate Haudenosaunee diplomacy of the 1650s–1660s, including the Onondaga peace initiatives, the French mission, and the settlement of 1667.
  7. Explain the French invasions of 1666, 1687, and 1696 and why they damaged Haudenosaunee communities without destroying the confederacy.
  8. Compare the priorities of the Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga, and Seneca and explain how those differences shaped war and peace decisions.
  9. Interpret primary and secondary evidence, including the Jesuit Relations, Dutch and English records, archaeology, and Haudenosaunee knowledge, with attention to perspective and limitation.
  10. Distinguish the Albany Deed of 1701 from the Great Peace of Montreal and explain what each shows about Haudenosaunee diplomatic strategy.
  11. Assess the long-term consequences of the wars for the Five Nations and for displaced and surviving neighboring peoples, avoiding simple judgments of victory or defeat.

Historical Context

By 1600, the Haudenosaunee homelands stretched across the lake-and-valley country of what is now central New York, from the Mohawk Valley in the east through Oneida and Onondaga country to the Cayuga and Seneca homelands of the Finger Lakes and the Genesee region. Villages of bark longhouses, often palisaded, were surrounded by cornfields that supplied the staple of the diet, supplemented by beans, squash, hunting, fishing, and gathering. Households were organized through matrilineal clans, and clan matrons played central roles in naming leaders and in decisions about the community’s welfare. The confederacy that linked the five nations was a council-based diplomatic and ceremonial union, grounded in a tradition of founding and condolence narrated in the Great Law (see Chapter 26).

Haudenosaunee communities were neither isolated nor static. They lived among peoples who spoke related Iroquoian languages (the Wendat, Neutral, Erie, Susquehannock, and Tionontaté) and among Algonquian-speaking neighbors such as the Mahican, the Algonquin, the Innu, and the Abenaki. Relations among these groups included intermarriage, trade, alliance, and feud. Long-distance exchange moved shell, copper, tobacco, maize, furs, and other goods. Rivalries existed long before European ships arrived, and some of them were intense. The sixteenth-century disappearance of St. Lawrence Iroquoian communities from the river valley that Jacques Cartier had visited in the 1530s is a reminder that the regional political map changed even before sustained colonization.

European arrival added new commercial partners and new dangers. French traders and missionaries worked from the St. Lawrence, building alliances with the Innu, Algonquin, and Wendat. Dutch traders, who established posts on the Hudson River in the 1610s and Fort Orange in 1624, supplied cloth, metal goods, and eventually firearms to the Mohawk and others. English colonists settled on the Atlantic coast and later took over the Dutch colony. Epidemic diseases, examined in Chapter 40, struck Indigenous communities repeatedly, with especially severe waves in the 1630s. The fur trade (Chapter 46) did not create Indigenous warfare, but it altered who had access to what goods and at what price, and it raised the strategic stakes of controlling river and lake routes.

The seventeenth-century wars thus unfolded in a setting of interlocking changes: demographic loss, new commercial dependencies, shifting alliances, and the arrival of colonial governments with their own aims. Understanding the wars requires holding all of these in view without collapsing them into a single cause and without treating any actor, whether Haudenosaunee, French, Dutch, English, or neighboring Indigenous nation, as a mere instrument of someone else’s policy.

Section 47.1

47.1 — The Seventeenth-Century Wars as a Historical Problem

The conflicts of this chapter have no single agreed name. Older scholarship, especially work of the early and mid-twentieth century, often called them the Iroquois Wars or the Beaver Wars, and portrayed them as a campaign by the Five Nations to seize control of the fur trade by eliminating or driving away rival suppliers. In that account, the Haudenosaunee, depleted of beaver in their own territory and eager for European goods, attacked the Wendat, Neutral, Erie, and others who stood between them and the furs of the interior. The model has a tidy logic, and it draws on real features of the period: the Dutch and French both wanted furs, Indigenous middlemen profited from the trade, and communities competed for access to European goods.

The difficulty is that the model does not fit all of the evidence. Nearly a century of conflict cannot be explained by a single motive. The Haudenosaunee fought different enemies at different times, sometimes in alliance with one another and sometimes separately. They took thousands of captives and adopted many of them, a practice a purely commercial explanation does not account for. They also negotiated repeatedly, sometimes for peace and sometimes for partnership. Historians now generally agree that commercial competition was one factor among several, but they differ on its weight. Some scholars continue to emphasize economic motives; others give more weight to demographic loss and the social practices of mourning; still others stress territorial security and political independence. These interpretations are compared directly in Section 47.40.

A second problem is that the label can carry assumptions about agency. “Beaver Wars” invites readers to see the Five Nations as an aggressive force and their neighbors as victims of commerce. In fact, the Wendat, Neutral, Erie, Susquehannock, Mahican, Algonquin, and others were political actors with their own diplomacy, alliances, and military capacity. They sometimes initiated attacks, sometimes negotiated, and sometimes chose migration or incorporation as strategies for survival. And the Haudenosaunee themselves were not monolithic: individual nations, villages, and even families disagreed about war and peace.

This chapter uses “seventeenth-century wars” as a neutral descriptive term and uses “Beaver Wars” only when discussing the historiographical label itself. It treats the wars as a series of related but distinct conflicts, each requiring its own explanation, within a long period shaped by demographic crisis, European commercial expansion, and Indigenous political creativity.

Historical Vocabulary
Haudenosaunee, Five Nations, and Mourning War

Definition. Haudenosaunee is the name the confederacy’s peoples use for themselves, commonly translated “people of the longhouse.” The Five Nations refers to the Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga, and Seneca, the member nations during this chapter’s period. Mourning war is the scholarly term for raiding conducted to respond to death: to seek captives who could replace the lost, to honor the dead, and to relieve the grief of bereaved families.

Historical context. Colonial writers called the confederacy “Iroquois,” a name of uncertain origin that was probably an exonym. Source materials from the seventeenth century use “Iroquois,” “Five Nations,” and other terms, and this chapter identifies them where necessary. The Tuscarora joined the confederacy only in the early eighteenth century.

Why it matters. Using accurate names and categories avoids treating the confederacy as a single undifferentiated actor and helps place the wars within a framework of Haudenosaunee social practice.

Potential misunderstanding. “Mourning war” does not mean that every raid was motivated by grief, or that grief was the only motive. The term describes one important dimension of warfare, not a complete explanation.

Section 47.2

47.2 — The Five Nations and the Political Organization of the Confederacy

The Haudenosaunee Confederacy joined five nations, each with its own territory, villages, councils, and leaders. From east to west, the Mohawk (Kanien’kehá:ka) lived along the Mohawk River; the Oneida (Onyota’a:ka) near Oneida Lake; the Onondaga near Onondaga Lake and the uplands to its south; the Cayuga (Gayogohó:no’) around Cayuga Lake; and the Seneca (Onöndowa’ga:’) in the region between Seneca Lake and the Genesee River. In ceremonial and diplomatic language, the Mohawk were the keepers of the eastern door and the Seneca of the western door, while the Onondaga hosted the central council fire. These roles gave each nation responsibilities for the confederacy as a whole without erasing national autonomy.

Decision-making in the confederacy rested on consensus. The Grand Council at Onondaga brought together the confederacy’s civil leaders, often called sachems or chiefs in English sources, who held hereditary titles within particular clans. Each title was conferred and monitored by clan matrons, who also could remove a leader who failed his responsibilities. Councils deliberated by nation and by groupings of nations, and decisions required agreement. The system was designed to preserve internal peace among the member nations and to give the confederacy a common voice in diplomacy, particularly on matters of shared concern.

The same system, however, limited the confederacy’s capacity for coordinated military action. The council could not command a nation to go to war, nor could it prevent a nation from making its own peace. War parties were typically organized by individual leaders or villages, often in response to specific grievances or opportunities. Warriors volunteered; they were not conscripted into a standing army. Consensus could be slow, and one nation could pursue a policy that others opposed. Seventeenth-century European observers sometimes described the Five Nations as more unified than they were, partly because European diplomacy encouraged the idea of a single negotiating counterpart.

Historians also disagree about the date and early development of the confederacy. Haudenosaunee oral tradition recounts its founding through the work of the Peacemaker and Hiawatha, who brought the warring nations into a union and established the Great Law of Peace. Estimated dates for the founding range from as early as the twelfth century to the sixteenth century, depending on the evidence and interpretive approach. For the seventeenth century, what matters is that the confederacy existed, that its institutions shaped diplomacy, and that its capacity for coordinated action depended on persuasion rather than command. The foundational institutions are examined in Chapter 26.

Section 47.3

47.3 — The Geography of Haudenosaunee Power and Neighboring Nations

The Haudenosaunee homeland occupied a strategic position. The Mohawk Valley connected the Hudson River to the Great Lakes watershed, and the string of lakes south of Lake Ontario linked Haudenosaunee villages by water and trail. To the east lay the Hudson Valley and Fort Orange, the principal Dutch (and later English) trading point. To the north, Lake Ontario and the St. Lawrence provided routes toward the French settlements at Quebec and, from 1642, Montreal. To the west and southwest, trails and waterways led toward the Niagara region, Lake Erie, and the Ohio country, and to the south the Susquehanna River provided access to the Chesapeake and the Susquehannock.

This geography meant that Haudenosaunee communities were exposed to both opportunity and risk. The Mohawk were close to Dutch traders and exposed to attacks from the north. The Seneca, in the west, faced the Neutral, the Erie, and later the Susquehannock. The Oneida and Onondaga occupied the center and, depending on the period, faced different threats and opportunities. Because each nation’s position differed, so did its strategic priorities, a theme developed in Section 47.27.

Around the confederacy lived many other peoples. To the northwest, the Wendat (Huron) occupied the lands between Georgian Bay and Lake Simcoe, a densely settled region known to the French as Huronia, which the Wendat called Wendake. Southwest of the Wendat lay the Tionontaté (Petun), and to the south and east of Georgian Bay, the Neutral lived on both sides of the Niagara River and along the northern shore of Lake Erie. The Erie lived along the south shore of Lake Erie. To the south, along the Susquehanna River, the Susquehannock occupied a large territory from which they traded with the Dutch, Swedes, and English. In the Hudson Valley and its tributaries lived the Mahican, and farther east and north lived Algonquian-speaking peoples including the Abenaki. Along the Ottawa River and the north shore of the St. Lawrence were the Algonquin and the Innu, central partners of the French.

Students should be cautious about the map. Seventeenth-century boundaries were not lines but zones of use, shared hunting grounds, and overlapping claims, and the Indigenous peoples named above spoke of themselves and their territories in their own terms. Names, too, are complicated: “Huron,” “Petun,” and “Neutral” are European terms (Wendat, Tionontaté, and the Attawandaron name used by the Wendat for the Neutral are Indigenous ones), and the Erie are known mostly through the testimony of others.

Section 47.4

47.4 — Mourning Wars, Captivity, Adoption, and Social Restoration

Among the Haudenosaunee and many of their neighbors, death was a communal injury that required an answer. A death, especially a violent one, left a gap in a household and clan, and the grief of the bereaved was understood as a force that had to be addressed. One ritual response was condolence: ceremonies of consolation, often accompanied by wampum, that restored the mourners’ ability to think and act. Another was the mourning war, a raid undertaken to take captives who could stand in for the dead. Captives might be adopted into the family of the deceased, taking the name and social position of the lost person, or they might be put to death, depending on decisions made by the mourning household.

Historians have emphasized the role of women in these decisions. Matrons of the bereaved households often determined whether a captive would be adopted or killed, and they could prevent or authorize a raid by supporting or withholding their approval of warriors. Women also controlled much of the agricultural economy and the household labor that supported war parties. This does not mean that women were uniformly pro- or anti-war, or that men acted only at women’s direction; it means that decisions about conflict involved households and clans as well as warriors, and that the formal authority of civil leaders was only one dimension of decision-making. Section 47.24 returns to this point.

Mourning war should not be reduced to irrational violence. It was a social institution that connected death, memory, kinship, and political strength. Captive-taking could increase the size and labor force of a community, and the incorporation of outsiders created new kin ties and sometimes new alliances. At the same time, the system involved real violence: raids killed people, captives were sometimes tortured and killed, and the adoption of prisoners did not erase the trauma of capture. European observers, particularly missionaries, were horrified by the torture they witnessed and sometimes described it in ways that served their own purposes, but their testimony also reflects real events. Students should keep both points in view.

Mourning war also had a political dimension. A nation that lost many people in epidemics or war had greater incentive to pursue captives, and a nation that gained many captives could strengthen itself. This interaction, between population loss and the demand for replacements, is one of the central arguments of the scholars who emphasize the mourning-war explanation. It does not by itself explain why the Haudenosaunee attacked particular enemies at particular times, but it helps explain why warfare could persist even when it was costly. The experience of captives themselves is explored in Section 47.25, and Chapter 53 provides the wider comparative treatment of Indigenous captivity and adoption.

Section 47.5

47.5 — Epidemics, Population Loss, Firearms, and Commercial Change

Three developments altered the conditions of warfare in the early and middle seventeenth century: epidemic disease, the spread of firearms, and the growth of commercial relationships with Europeans. None operated alone, and none was a universal cause. Their effects varied by nation and by decade.

Epidemics struck Indigenous communities of the Northeast repeatedly. Chapter 40 treats the epidemiology in detail; for this chapter it is enough to note that the 1630s were particularly severe. Smallpox appears in European records among the Mohawk and other Haudenosaunee communities in the mid-1630s, and Jesuit missionaries documented severe epidemics among the Wendat in the late 1630s. Population estimates for the period are uncertain. Scholars have proposed substantial declines in some communities, but the figures depend on assumptions about pre-epidemic populations, and the data do not support false precision. What the sources do show is that mortality was high enough to disrupt households, leadership, and ceremonial life, and that it affected the Five Nations and their neighbors alike.

Firearms were a second factor. The Dutch, eager to compete with the French and English, initially restricted gun sales, but the restrictions eroded and private traders supplied firearms to the Mohawk and others by the 1640s. Muskets were not an overwhelming technological advantage in every setting: they were slow to load, unreliable in wet weather, and dependent on supplies of powder, shot, and repair. But in the hands of warriors who had learned to use them in forest ambush and in sieges of palisaded villages, they altered tactics, and access to guns became a strategic concern for every nation. The Wendat and their allies also acquired firearms, so the wars cannot be explained as a contest between armed and unarmed peoples.

Commercial change was the third factor. European goods (metal tools, kettles, cloth, and weapons) became part of daily life, and access to them depended on trading relationships. The fur trade (Chapter 46) gave certain communities, especially those located on key routes, positions as middlemen who could profit by moving furs and goods between European traders and more distant Indigenous suppliers. Historians disagree on how much the Five Nations sought to control such positions and on how much their own beaver supply was depleted. What is clear is that European goods mattered, that trade relationships structured alliances, and that Dutch, French, and English policies influenced who had access to what.

These three developments intersected with the practices examined above. Population loss increased the importance of captives; firearms changed the risks and rewards of raiding; commercial networks altered both the opportunities for alliance and the sources of tension. Yet the sequence was not mechanical. The Wendat, Neutral, and others had also suffered losses and also obtained firearms, and they responded with strategies of their own. The analysis of cause is therefore a matter of combinations and contexts, a point illustrated by the Multiple Perspectives feature below.

Section 47.6

47.6 — Champlain, Indigenous Alliances, and the Battles of 1609–1615

In the summer of 1609, Samuel de Champlain traveled south from the St. Lawrence with a party of Innu, Algonquin, and Wendat men and a small number of Frenchmen, ascending the river now called the Richelieu and entering the lake that now bears his name. On July 29 (by the calendar Champlain used), the party met a group of Haudenosaunee, whom Champlain identified as Iroquois and who were most likely Mohawk or Mohawk-led. The Indigenous allies had asked Champlain to join them in a campaign against longtime enemies. The following morning the two sides faced one another and Champlain, who carried an arquebus, fired at the opposing leaders. In his account, he killed two of them, and the Haudenosaunee fled.

The episode is often presented as a turning point that made the Haudenosaunee permanent enemies of the French. That reading goes beyond the evidence. Champlain was entering a conflict that already existed: the Innu, Algonquin, and Wendat were fighting Haudenosaunee forces for their own reasons, and the French were participating at the invitation of allies whose goodwill was essential to their trade. Champlain joined further actions in 1610, in an attack on a Haudenosaunee barricade near the mouth of the Richelieu, and in 1615, when he accompanied a Wendat-led force against a fortified town south of Lake Ontario, an attack that failed and left Champlain wounded. The town is traditionally identified as Onondaga, though some scholars have proposed Oneida, and the dating and location of the engagement remain subjects of discussion.

For the Haudenosaunee, these encounters demonstrated that French firearms could be decisive in a single engagement and that the French were committed to specific Indigenous alliances. The Mohawk, who were closest to the Dutch trading establishments, could seek European goods elsewhere, and in the following decades they turned to the Dutch. Champlain’s alliance with the Innu, Algonquin, and Wendat did not make later wars inevitable, but it positioned the French as partners of the Haudenosaunee’s northern rivals and created a pattern of mistrust that later diplomacy had to overcome.

The encounters also show the political calculations of Indigenous participants. The Innu, Algonquin, and Wendat sought French weapons and trade and wanted allies against their enemies; Champlain needed access to the interior and the furs it produced. Each side had different aims, and the alliance depended on continuing benefits for both. The Haudenosaunee, for their part, were participants in a regional competition, not simply targets.

Section 47.7

47.7 — The Mohawk, Dutch Trade, and the Hudson Valley

After Henry Hudson’s voyage of 1609, Dutch traders began visiting the river that bears his name. Early posts, such as Fort Nassau near the site of modern Albany, were seasonal and vulnerable to floods, but the Dutch West India Company, chartered in 1621, established Fort Orange on the upper Hudson in 1624. Fort Orange became the center of a trade in which Indigenous suppliers exchanged furs, mainly beaver, for cloth, metal goods, and other merchandise. The Mohawk, whose villages lay to the west, and the Mahican, who lived along the river and to the east, were the principal Indigenous participants.

This trade changed regional bargaining. European goods, especially metal tools and weapons, were valuable, and the nations closest to Fort Orange had an advantage. The Mohawk’s position was strengthened by their access to routes toward the west and by their ability to receive goods without depending on northern middlemen. The Dutch, for their part, were commercial rather than missionary or settler-focused in this period, and their relative lack of political ambition on Indigenous land made them attractive partners. Trade also required diplomacy: exchanges involved gift-giving, wampum, and negotiations about prices and conduct, and Indigenous traders pressed the Dutch to observe their expectations.

The trade also mattered for the production and circulation of wampum, shell beads made largely by coastal Algonquian-speaking peoples of the Long Island and lower Hudson regions. Wampum became increasingly important in diplomacy and trade, and Haudenosaunee demand for it, together with Dutch commerce, connected the Mohawk to peoples far to the south. Chapter 46 treats these commercial institutions in detail; here the point is that the Mohawk’s Dutch trade gave them resources and a stake in regional competition.

Dutch trade did not, however, determine Haudenosaunee policy. The Mohawk used the trade to improve their position, but their strategic decisions reflected rivalries with the Mahican, the French-allied nations, and other neighbors. The Dutch, in turn, were drawn into Indigenous conflicts, sometimes against their own preferences, as the events of the 1620s showed.

Section 47.8

47.8 — The Mohawk–Mahican War of the 1620s

The Mohawk–Mahican war was among the first major conflicts in which European trade at Fort Orange shaped the strategic stakes. The Mahican, whose territory included both banks of the Hudson near the post, had been the principal Indigenous partners of early Dutch traders, and they were positioned to act as intermediaries between the Dutch and the nations to the west. The Mohawk had their own interest in direct access to Dutch goods and in the security of their eastern frontier. Dutch records, and later historical research, place the principal fighting between about 1626 and 1629, although some accounts use broader ranges that reflect earlier tensions and later consequences.

The Dutch became involved in 1626. According to Dutch sources, Daniel van Krieckenbeeck, the commander at Fort Orange, led a small party in support of the Mahican against the Mohawk and was killed in an ambush along with several of his men. The incident, which Dutch records describe as a disaster, led Dutch authorities to adopt a more cautious posture, and the West India Company later reduced its presence at Fort Orange and encouraged neutrality in Indigenous conflicts. The Dutch also attempted to mediate, though they were not in control of the conflict.

The fighting ended with the Mahican losing ground. Mahican communities withdrew eastward across the Hudson, and the Mohawk gained a stronger position in the Fort Orange trade. The Mahican did not disappear; they continued to live in the region, maintained diplomatic relations, and in later decades reappear in the records as participants in the politics of the Hudson Valley. The war illustrates how a conflict among Indigenous neighbors, already shaped by older rivalries, could be transformed by the arrival of a European trading post, which raised the stakes of controlling access to it.

The war should therefore not be reduced to a battle for beaver pelts. The Mohawk and Mahican fought over position, security, and influence in a region that had been changing before the Dutch arrived. Commerce intensified the conflict and offered new resources to the combatants, but it did not create their rivalry. The Dutch, meanwhile, learned that participating in Indigenous wars could be costly.

Section 47.9

47.9 — Disease, Population Pressure, and the Escalation of Conflict in the 1630s

In the 1630s, epidemics struck the Northeast with particular force. A Dutch visitor to Mohawk country in 1634–1635, Harmen Meyndertsz van den Bogaert, left an account of his journey that noted the presence of smallpox in Mohawk villages and the effects of illness on communities; his journal is one of the few contemporary sources for conditions in Haudenosaunee villages in these years. Jesuit missionaries, arriving in Wendat country in the mid-1630s, recorded epidemics there as well, and their testimony shows illness spreading in the late 1630s. These records are reliable evidence that epidemics occurred; they are less reliable as measures of how many died.

Historians estimate that some Indigenous communities lost large portions of their populations, but the numbers are contested because pre-epidemic populations are unknown and because outbreaks varied by place. It is safer to say that the Five Nations and their neighbors experienced serious demographic losses in the 1630s and that these losses affected leadership, ceremonial life, household labor, and military strength. The distinction between documented outbreaks and modern population estimates is important: the former rests on contemporary testimony, while the latter depend on modeling and assumption.

How did demographic pressure affect warfare? One line of argument holds that population loss increased the importance of captive-taking as a means of replacing the dead, which in turn encouraged raiding against neighbors who were also weakened. Another emphasizes that weakened communities might be more vulnerable to attack, creating opportunities for rivals. Neither argument implies that disease produced war automatically. Epidemics also gave communities reasons to avoid war, to relocate, or to seek peace. Disease interacted with commerce, since losses affected trade networks and the ability to harvest furs, and with diplomacy, since epidemics complicated the relationships among nations that exchanged visitors and goods.

The epidemics also strained the confederacy’s internal relationships. Leadership titles had to be refilled, rituals of condolence became more frequent, and communities faced decisions about whether to rebuild, move, or merge. Because the effects differed among nations, they added another source of divergence in the Five Nations’ priorities.

Section 47.10

47.10 — Warfare and Diplomacy on the Eve of the Wendat Crisis

By the late 1630s and early 1640s, the northern theater was marked by intensified raiding and persistent negotiation. The Mohawk, supplied by Dutch trade, increasingly attacked travelers and communities along the St. Lawrence and the Ottawa River. French settlements, including the new establishment at Montreal founded in 1642, were exposed, and the fur canoes that carried Wendat, Algonquin, and Innu goods toward Quebec and Trois-Rivières were vulnerable to ambush. The Wendat, whose commercial role as intermediaries linked the interior to the French, saw their trade disrupted and sometimes lost men and goods.

Captives from these raids appear in French sources. The most widely known is the Jesuit Isaac Jogues, captured in 1642 with Wendat companions and René Goupil, held among the Mohawk, and eventually helped to escape with Dutch assistance. His account describes the treatment of captives and illustrates both the violence and the diplomatic importance of prisoners, who could be used to negotiate. Jogues returned to Mohawk country as an envoy in 1646 and was killed there, a reminder of the instability of the peace attempts of the time.

At the same time, Haudenosaunee and French leaders repeatedly explored negotiation. Such diplomacy might involve the exchange of captives, discussion of trade access, and attempts to separate parties to the conflict. The Mohawk and other nations pursued these overtures partly because war was costly and partly because peace could provide advantages, such as access to French goods or relief from attack. The simultaneous continuation of hostility and diplomacy was characteristic of the period, and it reflects both the multiple actors involved and the practical limits of any single party’s control over warriors and allies.

The Wendat were increasingly the focus of Haudenosaunee attention. Their communities were large, agricultural, and economically important, but they were also weakened by epidemic disease and divided by the arrival of Christian missions, which created tensions between converts and non-converts. They were allied with the French and wished to preserve trade, but their location made them exposed. These conditions set the stage for the major conflicts of the late 1640s, discussed in Division III.

Section 47.11

47.11 — The Peace Negotiations of 1645–1646 and Their Failure

In July 1645, a Mohawk delegation led by a speaker whom French sources call Kiotsaton arrived at Trois-Rivières to propose a settlement. The meeting brought together Haudenosaunee, French, Algonquin, and Innu representatives, and the proceedings followed established diplomatic forms: speeches, exchanges of wampum, and formal gestures meant to bind the parties. The Mohawk delegates addressed the French and their Indigenous allies and offered terms that included the return of captives and a commitment to end hostilities. French officials, including Governor Charles Huault de Montmagny, responded, and the parties agreed to a truce that was intended to extend to the allies of each side.

The agreement did not last. The reasons were structural, not simply a matter of bad faith. The Mohawk could speak for themselves and could attempt to persuade others, but they could not bind the Onondaga, Seneca, or other nations of the confederacy. The French, for their part, represented a loose alliance in which Wendat, Algonquin, and Innu leaders pursued their own priorities. Wendat communities were not fully included in the settlement and remained exposed. In 1646, Jesuit missionary Isaac Jogues returned to Mohawk country as an emissary and was killed there, an act that marked the breakdown of the peace. Interpretations differ on whether the killing reflected the decision of a faction within the Mohawk community, fear of the missionary’s supposed influence during an epidemic, or other motives, and the sources do not allow a confident answer.

The failed peace is nonetheless important. It shows that Haudenosaunee diplomacy was a serious instrument of policy and that Haudenosaunee leaders pursued negotiation even in the midst of war. It also shows the difficulty of settling a multilateral conflict through bilateral agreements. A treaty made with the French at Trois-Rivières could not stop attacks by communities that had not agreed to it, nor could it bind Indigenous allies whose interests differed. Students should therefore treat diplomatic failure as historical evidence about political structures, not simply as a sign that peace was impossible.

Section 47.12

47.12 — The Campaigns against the Wendat, 1648

By 1648 the Wendat were under strain. Epidemics had reduced their population, missions had deepened divisions between Christian and traditional families, and the trade routes that supported their economy were insecure. Haudenosaunee raids had disrupted the canoe convoys that carried furs from Wendake to the St. Lawrence, and the Wendat’s own diplomatic and military efforts had not secured a durable peace. Estimates of the Wendat population in the 1640s are uncertain, but contemporary Jesuit sources describe a large but diminished society, concentrated in a number of fortified villages.

In the summer of 1648, Haudenosaunee warriors, reported as Seneca and Mohawk with possibly others, attacked the Wendat village of Teanaostaiaé, which the Jesuits called Saint-Joseph. The attack came on July 4 while many of the village’s men were away. The defenders who remained were overwhelmed; the Jesuit Antoine Daniel was killed; and the village was destroyed. Jesuit accounts report hundreds killed or captured, but, as with most casualty figures of the period, the numbers are estimates and should not be treated as exact. The attack demonstrated that the Haudenosaunee could reach into the heart of Wendake and that the Wendat’s defensive arrangements were inadequate.

What did the Haudenosaunee seek? The sources do not provide a clear answer, and explanations vary. Some scholars emphasize the disruption of the Wendat trade and the prospect of gaining access to French goods; others stress captive-taking and the weakening of a long-standing rival; still others point to the political advantages of eliminating a threat on a northern frontier. These motives were not mutually exclusive, and different Haudenosaunee communities may have participated for different reasons. The Wendat’s choices also mattered: some leaders sought negotiation, others favored resistance, and the decision to remain in fortified villages left them vulnerable to concentrated attack.

The campaign of 1648 was not yet the end of Wendat political life, but it set up the crisis of the following winter, in which the consequences of the weakened defensive position became clear.

Section 47.13

47.13 — The Crisis of 1649 and the Destruction of Wendat Communities

In the winter of 1648–1649, a large Haudenosaunee force, estimated by the Jesuits at about a thousand men and likely composed of warriors from several nations, entered Wendat territory and spent the season in the region, hunting and positioning itself for an attack. On March 16, 1649, the force struck the village of Taenhatentaron, which the Jesuits called Saint-Ignace, at dawn. The village was taken, and survivors fled or were captured. The attackers then moved on to the nearby village of Saint-Louis, where Wendat defenders resisted fiercely but were eventually overcome. The Jesuits Jean de Brébeuf and Gabriel Lalemant, who had been at Saint-Louis, were captured and killed. Their deaths, described in detail in the Jesuit Relations, later became central to Catholic martyrology.

The destruction of these villages triggered a wider collapse. Wendat leaders and communities, fearing further attacks and lacking adequate food and defense, abandoned and in some cases burned a number of their own villages in the following weeks. Contemporary Jesuit accounts describe a rapid breakdown of the society’s settled order. The mission headquarters, Sainte-Marie among the Hurons, was burned by the French themselves in June 1649, as the Jesuits prepared to leave. Wendat survivors scattered: some fled to Gahoendoe (Christian Island) in Georgian Bay, where famine and illness killed many during the winter of 1649–1650; others took refuge with the Tionontaté or Neutral; some were taken as captives and incorporated into Haudenosaunee communities; and a group traveled to the St. Lawrence and settled near Quebec in 1650. These movements are the subject of Chapter 45’s treatment of Wendat resettlement.

Several cautions apply. First, the sources, mainly the Jesuit Relations, are the writings of missionaries who had lost their mission and who interpreted the events in religious as well as military terms. Second, the idea that all of the Wendat were destroyed or became extinct is incorrect: many survived, and their descendants, among them the Wendat communities of Wendake near Quebec City and the Wyandot/Wyandotte nations in the Midwest and Oklahoma, continue today. Third, the attacks of 1649 should not be treated as a single centrally directed campaign. The Haudenosaunee force consisted of warriors from different nations, and the sources do not describe a unified command. Finally, the crisis occurred amid epidemic disease, famine, colonial pressure, and internal Wendat divisions, factors that scholars emphasize when they place the destruction in a larger context. Some scholars have also considered whether the term genocide applies to this destruction; the question is debated and depends on definitions and on how historians understand the aims of the attackers.

Section 47.14

47.14 — Wendat Resistance, Captivity, and Dispersal

The destruction of Wendat villages was not an uncontested defeat. In the weeks and months after March 1649, Wendat warriors continued to resist, defending refugee communities and counterattacking in some places. Wendat leaders sought allies, negotiated with neighbors, and tried to preserve key institutions. The Wendat population was large and organized in several allied nations, and survivors made different decisions about where to go and whom to join. The pattern of dispersal reflected these political choices as well as military pressure.

A significant number of Wendat were taken captive and incorporated into Haudenosaunee communities. Jesuit observers in the 1650s reported large numbers of Wendat living among the Seneca, Onondaga, and other Five Nations, and some Wendat families became part of Haudenosaunee villages. Incorporation meant new kin relationships and new obligations, and it also meant separation from home communities. The experience varied: some adoptees became fully integrated, while others retained ties to relatives elsewhere or later left. These captives strengthened the Five Nations demographically, a fact that scholars emphasize when they link the wars to demographic recovery, though the precise numbers are uncertain.

Other Wendat moved toward the St. Lawrence, where French Jesuit missions and the French colony offered some security, and still others traveled west to Lake Michigan and the upper Great Lakes, where they joined refugee communities that later became important in the politics of the region (see Chapter 45). The wars thus created refugee populations whose presence shaped alliances and conflicts for decades. Haudenosaunee relations with these communities included hostility but also negotiation, and some groups developed ties that crossed earlier boundaries.

The political consequences were far-reaching. The collapse of Wendake removed a major commercial and diplomatic intermediary between the interior and the French. It also left the French with fewer Indigenous partners on whom to rely and increased the importance of other nations, including the Odawa and other Algonquian-speaking peoples, in the trade networks that tied the Great Lakes to Montreal. For the Five Nations, the events created both opportunities and burdens: new captives and new geopolitical room, but also new enemies and new demands for diplomacy.

Section 47.15

47.15 — The Neutral and Tionontaté Conflicts, 1649–1651

The Tionontaté (Petun) lived southwest of the Wendat and were closely connected to them through trade, kinship, and alliance. In December 1649, a Haudenosaunee force attacked a major Tionontaté village that the Jesuits called Saint-Jean. The Jesuit Charles Garnier was killed in the assault, and the Tionontaté abandoned their settlements in the following months. Some Tionontaté joined Wendat refugees, and over the next decades the combined communities moved westward, eventually playing an important role in the politics of the upper Great Lakes. The Tionontaté leader Kondiaronk, who figures in the diplomacy of 1701, belonged to this lineage of refugee communities.

The Neutral, who lived north of Lake Erie and along the Niagara River, had been a populous confederacy of related villages. They had, in the 1640s, been involved in conflict with Algonquian-speaking peoples to the west and had tried to avoid taking sides in the wars between the Haudenosaunee and the Wendat. In 1650–1651, Haudenosaunee forces attacked Neutral villages; the sources, again largely Jesuit, report the capture or destruction of major settlements and the dispersal of the population. Some Neutral people were taken captive and adopted into Haudenosaunee communities, particularly among the Seneca, and others fled west or joined other refugee groups.

The wars against the Tionontaté and Neutral need to be understood in relation to the preceding events but should not be treated as simply a continuation of the Wendat campaign. Each community had its own relations with the Haudenosaunee, its own diplomatic options, and its own internal politics. The Neutral in particular had a different history from the Wendat, and their location on the Niagara route made them strategically significant for Seneca interests. The narrative must also separate the disintegration of political organizations from the survival of their members. The Neutral confederacy and the Tionontaté villages ceased to function as before, but people survived, and their descendants live in other nations today, including Wyandot communities.

Section 47.16

47.16 — The Erie Wars and the Limits of the Written Record, 1654–1657

The Erie lived along the south shore of Lake Erie, and the French called them the “Nation of the Cat,” a name derived from an Iroquoian term. Our knowledge of them comes largely from outsiders, including Jesuit reports compiled from information gathered from Haudenosaunee and other informants, and from archaeological work on sites in western New York, northwestern Pennsylvania, and Ohio. The Erie did not leave documents of their own, and the written record about them is thin.

The Jesuit Relations of the mid-1650s describe a war between the Erie and the Five Nations, reportedly including Onondaga and Seneca forces. In one account, a large Haudenosaunee expedition, estimated by the Jesuits at well over a thousand men, besieged a fortified Erie town and, after a hard fight, took it, with heavy losses on both sides. The fighting is generally dated to 1654–1657, though the exact sequence of events is not known. The sources do not allow a continuous campaign chronology: they describe particular incidents, rumors, and reports, often at second or third hand, and the location of key sites is uncertain. Historians must therefore be cautious about reconstructing a detailed campaign.

The outcome, however, is clear in general terms. The Erie as a political entity disappeared from the written record by the late 1650s. Some were captured and adopted into Haudenosaunee communities; others dispersed. Some scholars have suggested that Erie survivors migrated south or west and may be connected with groups recorded in Virginia and elsewhere in the 1650s, but those identifications remain uncertain. The Erie case illustrates how a political community can vanish from documents while its members and descendants continue within other communities.

Archaeology, including research by the New York State Museum and other institutions, provides an important complement to the written sources by documenting settlement patterns, village layouts, and material culture. Archaeology cannot, however, tell us who attacked whom in 1654, and it cannot by itself reveal political motives. It shows the value of combining evidence while acknowledging that gaps remain.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Displacement and Political Survival, c. 1640–1700

What changed?

The Wendat confederacy’s settled homeland, the Neutral and Tionontaté villages, and the Erie communities ceased to exist as before. Populations were dispersed, adopted, or relocated, and the geography of alliance in the Great Lakes was reorganized.

What remained continuous?

Languages, kinship structures, clan identities, and political traditions persisted among survivors. Wendat and Tionontaté refugees maintained community identities, formed new alliances, and later became the Wyandot and Wendat communities known today. Adoptees contributed to Haudenosaunee communities while often retaining elements of their origins.

When and how fast?

The crisis was abrupt in the late 1640s but followed years of gradual decline; reconstitution took decades and proceeded unevenly.

Who experienced change most directly?

Refugees, captives, and the families of the dead faced the most direct disruption. Communities that absorbed refugees also changed.

Why the pattern matters

It cautions against equating the disappearance of a political organization from the written record with the disappearance of a people.

Section 47.17

47.17 — The Southern and Eastern Frontiers: Susquehannock, Mahican, and Other Rivals

While attention often focuses on the Great Lakes, Haudenosaunee nations also fought and negotiated on their southern and eastern frontiers. The Susquehannock, a large Iroquoian-speaking people on the lower Susquehanna River, had been in conflict with Haudenosaunee communities since at least the 1630s. They traded with the Dutch, Swedes, and later the English and Marylanders, and by the 1650s and 1660s they had access to firearms and a well-defended fortified town. Rather than being overwhelmed, the Susquehannock resisted effectively, and the fighting was long and costly, with particular pressure on the Seneca and Cayuga, the nations nearest to them. The Susquehannock’s alliance with the colony of Maryland in the 1660s gave them additional support, but by the mid-1670s they were weakened by epidemics, by attacks from colonial militias, and by continued Haudenosaunee pressure, and their remaining communities were displaced and in part incorporated into other groups.

To the east, the Mohawk and other nations dealt with the Mahican and with Algonquian-speaking peoples of New England. The Mahican, displaced from the upper Hudson in the 1620s, remained a political presence in the region, and fighting between them and the Mohawk continued in later decades before a peace in the early 1670s. In 1675–1676, during King Philip’s War in New England, Mohawk warriors attacked Native refugees and combatants moving west into New York, an episode that complicated colonial relationships and that Chapter 48 considers in connection with the English colonies.

These frontiers show that Haudenosaunee strategy was not a single campaign aimed at the beaver grounds of the northwest. Different nations faced different enemies. Seneca and Cayuga leaders were preoccupied by the Susquehannock; the Mohawk by the Mahican, New England Algonquians, and the French; the Onondaga and Oneida by a range of concerns involving the north and the Great Lakes. Imposing a single military objective on the Five Nations would misrepresent a more decentralized pattern of conflict and diplomacy.

Section 47.18

47.18 — The Onondaga Peace Initiatives and French Mission, 1653–1658

In 1653, even as the Haudenosaunee were pressing the Erie and had recently defeated the Wendat, Onondaga leaders initiated negotiations with the French. A delegation visited Quebec, and Onondaga leaders asked for peace and for a French presence in their country. The Jesuit Simon Le Moyne traveled to Onondaga in 1654 and returned with information about the possibility of a settlement. Further missions followed in 1655, and in 1656 a party of French settlers and Jesuits established a small colony, Sainte-Marie de Ganentaa, on Onondaga Lake.

Why did the Onondaga seek peace? Several motives are plausible. Peace with the French could reduce attacks along the St. Lawrence, allow Haudenosaunee warriors to concentrate on other enemies such as the Erie, and bring access to French goods and diplomacy, a way to offset the Mohawk’s advantages in Dutch trade. A French mission could also provide a counterweight in internal Haudenosaunee politics. The Onondaga leader Garacontié, whom French sources describe as friendly to the French, emerges in this period as an advocate of peace. At the same time, some Haudenosaunee, especially among the Mohawk, were skeptical or hostile, and the Oneida and Seneca had their own views. The mission was therefore as much a product of internal debate as of French initiative.

The French undertaking collapsed. By 1658, tensions, rumors of a plot, and the vulnerability of the small French party led the colonists to depart secretly from Onondaga country. Sources differ on the details and on how much danger the French actually faced. The abandonment of the mission did not end diplomacy, but it illustrated the fragility of an arrangement that depended on the goodwill of particular leaders and that could not insulate the French from the hostility of others. For the Onondaga, the episode showed that peace as well as war could be a strategic tool.

Section 47.19

47.19 — The Battle of Long Sault, 1660: Warfare and Colonial Memory

In the spring of 1660, a small French party from Montreal under Adam Dollard des Ormeaux, together with Wendat and Algonquin allies, took up a defensive position at the Long Sault rapids on the Ottawa River. Contemporary sources suggest that the French numbered about seventeen and that their Indigenous allies included some forty Wendat under the leader Annahotaha and a smaller Algonquin contingent. They occupied a hastily improved palisade and were attacked by a Haudenosaunee force, mainly Onondaga and Mohawk, reported in various sources as numbering in the hundreds, with reinforcements arriving during the siege. The fighting lasted several days. Dollard and most of the French were killed, and some of the Wendat and Algonquin allies abandoned the position or were captured.

The immediate significance of the battle was military and political. The Haudenosaunee force, which had suffered losses, withdrew without attacking Montreal. The Wendat and Algonquin defenders, whose participation is often underemphasized, fought for their own reasons: to protect their families and to maintain their alliance with the French. The episode demonstrated the dangers the French faced from Haudenosaunee raiding and the willingness of Haudenosaunee forces to engage when opportunities arose. It also showed the continuing instability of the northern frontier.

Later memory transformed the event. Beginning especially in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, French-Canadian writers presented Dollard as a heroic figure who sacrificed himself to save the colony. The claim that Dollard deliberately saved Montreal or New France from a planned Haudenosaunee attack rests on interpretation rather than strong contemporary evidence, and the claim has been challenged by historians who have returned to the earliest sources. These include correspondence of Marie de l’Incarnation, the Jesuit Relations, and Dollier de Casson’s later history of Montreal, which differ in detail and emphasis. The Dictionary of Canadian Biography and other scholarship have been central to this reassessment.

Historical Debate

What Was the Significance of Long Sault?

The question: Did the battle of 1660 save the French colony from a large Haudenosaunee invasion, or was it a smaller engagement whose importance was enlarged by later memory?

Interpretation A — A decisive defense: Supporters of the heroic account argue that the Haudenosaunee force intended a major attack on the French settlements, that the stubborn resistance of Dollard’s party exhausted the attackers, and that the colony was saved. This interpretation drew heavily on nineteenth- and twentieth-century French-Canadian narratives.
Interpretation B — A limited engagement magnified by memory: Other historians argue that the contemporary evidence does not establish a planned invasion, that the Haudenosaunee party may have had other objectives such as hunting or raiding along the Ottawa, that Dollard’s own aims are unclear, and that the claim of national salvation reflects later political needs rather than seventeenth-century evidence.

Evidence: Early accounts differ on numbers, intentions, and outcomes; some were written years later or at second hand. Haudenosaunee perspectives are largely absent from them.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns interpretation of Haudenosaunee intentions and of Dollard’s purpose, the reliability of later accounts, and the scale of the event.

Why it matters: The debate illustrates how colonial memory can reshape history and how the Indigenous participants on both sides were sometimes marginalized in the narrative.

Section 47.20

47.20 — The Carignan-Salières Regiment and the Mohawk Campaigns, 1665–1666

In 1663 the French Crown took direct control of the colony of Canada, and Louis XIV’s government soon sent substantial military forces. The Carignan-Salières regiment, over a thousand soldiers, arrived in 1665 along with the lieutenant general Alexandre de Prouville, marquis de Tracy. The regiment built a chain of forts along the Richelieu River, the route toward Mohawk country, and prepared a campaign. A winter expedition in early 1666 led by Governor Daniel de Rémy de Courcelles set out for the Mohawk valley but suffered from cold, poor navigation, and illness, and withdrew with little to show for the effort.

In the autumn of 1666, Tracy and Courcelles led a larger force, about thirteen hundred men by French accounts, including regulars, militia, and a number of Indigenous allies, south from the Richelieu and Lake Champlain. The Mohawk, warned of the approach and facing a force armed with firearms and supplies, abandoned their villages. French troops burned the villages and large stores of maize and other food, and the commanders performed ceremonies claiming possession of the land for the French king. The Mohawk did not engage in a decisive battle; they withdrew with their people and later returned to the ruined sites.

The campaign illustrates both the reach and the limits of French power. The French could reach Mohawk country and destroy property, and the loss of food was a serious blow. But they could not hold the territory, did not destroy the Mohawk as a people, and depended on a precarious logistical line. The English conquest of New Netherland in 1664 had also changed the context: the Mohawk now dealt with English New York, which was not a French ally. Mohawk leaders were also dealing with other conflicts at the same time, including fighting with the Mahican. These pressures encouraged a settlement.

Section 47.21

47.21 — The Peace of 1667 and Differing Haudenosaunee Priorities

The settlement of 1666–1667 did not involve a single negotiation by a single Haudenosaunee delegation. The Onondaga, Oneida, and Cayuga leaders, particularly those favorable to peace such as Garacontié, had begun agreements with the French earlier, and the Mohawk and Oneida concluded terms after the campaign of 1666. The Seneca joined the peace in 1667. The nations approached the settlement for different reasons. The Mohawk, who had lost villages and food and faced pressure from several directions, had strong incentives to end French attacks. The western nations, whose homelands had not been invaded, were more interested in preserving opportunities to pursue conflicts with other enemies and in the possibilities offered by French trade and missions.

The peace brought Jesuit missionaries into Haudenosaunee villages again, beginning in 1667. Some Haudenosaunee, particularly among the Mohawk and Oneida, converted, and in the following years Catholic converts migrated to the St. Lawrence, where they founded mission communities such as the one that became Kahnawake near Montreal. These “mission Iroquois” (the term appears in French sources) retained Haudenosaunee identities but developed distinct political relations with the French and, in later wars, sometimes fought against Haudenosaunee communities of the confederacy. Their presence illustrates how religion, politics, and migration could divide families and nations.

Did the peace produce stability? For roughly seventeen years, direct fighting between the French and the Five Nations largely ceased. The conflict did not end, however. Haudenosaunee warfare was redirected toward other enemies, including the Susquehannock, the Illinois, and other nations to the west and south, and new tensions developed over trade and territory. The peace therefore changed diplomatic opportunities and shifted the focus of warfare more than it ended the pattern of conflict.

Section 47.22

47.22 — Continued Haudenosaunee Power and Western Conflicts in the 1670s

In the 1670s, Haudenosaunee influence extended widely, but the nature of that influence was complex. Some Haudenosaunee, particularly Seneca and Cayuga, established villages along the north shore of Lake Ontario, where they could control trade and travel routes and hunt. French officials responded in 1673 by building Fort Frontenac at the eastern end of the lake, a move that was both commercial and strategic and that brought French garrisons and traders into an area where Haudenosaunee interests were strong.

Farther west, Haudenosaunee war parties, mainly Seneca and Cayuga, campaigned against peoples of the Illinois country and the region south of the lakes, including Miami and Illinois communities. These conflicts were partly connected to captive-taking and partly to rivalries over hunting grounds and trade. The idea that the Five Nations cleared a vast Ohio country and held it as an exclusive beaver preserve has been challenged by scholars who point out that evidence of permanent Haudenosaunee occupation is limited and that displaced peoples, among them the Shawnee, often returned or reappeared in the region. Haudenosaunee claims in the Ohio country were mainly claims of conquest and influence, not settlement.

Haudenosaunee communities in this period included large numbers of adopted captives, and their relationships with displaced and neighboring Indigenous peoples were varied. Some refugee groups settled under Haudenosaunee protection, others were hostile, and still others, such as the Mahican and Susquehannock remnants, entered alliance or tributary relationships. Competing territorial and commercial objectives sharpened tensions with the French and their western Indigenous allies. The detailed history of French commerce belongs to Chapter 46; what matters here is that, by the late 1670s, the Five Nations were powerful but also stretched and increasingly entangled in the rivalry between French Canada and English New York.

Section 47.23

47.23 — Haudenosaunee Military Organization, Mobility, and Strategy

Haudenosaunee warfare in the seventeenth century was organized around the war party rather than the standing army. Most operations were raids by small groups of warriors, sometimes a dozen or fewer, who traveled by canoe or on foot, relied on surprise, and sought to strike quickly and withdraw. Leadership was usually based on reputation: a man known for skill and success could attract followers, but followers were volunteers who could withdraw. Larger expeditions, such as those reported against the Wendat in 1648–1649 or the Erie in the 1650s, required coordination among several communities and are described in French sources as involving hundreds or more than a thousand warriors. Such figures are contemporary estimates, and the organization of these forces probably resembled a coalition of war parties more than a single command.

Mobility was a strength. The waterways of the Finger Lakes, Lake Ontario, the St. Lawrence, and the Ottawa allowed canoe-borne parties to travel long distances, and Haudenosaunee warriors could winter in enemy territory, as in 1648–1649, hunting for food while waiting for the opportunity to strike. Intelligence was gathered through scouts, captives, and the networks of kin and trade that linked communities. Raids on travelers, such as the fur canoes of the Ottawa River, exploited the vulnerability of long supply lines.

Defense relied on fortified villages. Haudenosaunee communities, like those of the Wendat and others, often surrounded their longhouses with palisades. Palisades could resist small raids, but they were vulnerable to concentrated attack, particularly when many defenders were absent, as at Teanaostaiaé in 1648. Firearms changed the details of both attack and defense, and Haudenosaunee warriors integrated them with older weapons and tactics rather than abandoning earlier methods. Large-scale siege operations could be successful, but they were costly, and communities often preferred evasion to pitched battle, as the Mohawk did in 1666 and as the Onondaga and Oneida did in 1696.

The Five Nations did not maintain a centralized standing force, and treating them as a militarized state obscures the political and social constraints on war. Participation depended on household support, food supply, spiritual preparation, and the judgment of leaders about whether a particular expedition was worth its risks.

Section 47.24

47.24 — Clan Mothers, Councils, War Leaders, and Decisions about Conflict

Decisions about war involved several overlapping groups. Civil leaders (the sachems or chiefs who held council titles) were responsible for the community’s peace and for diplomacy, and they often advocated restraint. War leaders (often called captains or war chiefs in colonial sources) were men whose reputations rested on martial success and who could organize parties. Clan matrons, women who held authority in the clan and nominated civil leaders, influenced both. They could press for or against particular undertakings, and they supported or withheld the food and supplies on which a war party depended. Mourning households sought replacements for the dead and had a particular stake in raids.

The sources provide only fragments of how these relationships worked in practice. European observers, almost all men, recorded what they saw in councils and diplomatic meetings, and many gave less attention to the domestic discussions in which women’s influence was exercised. Later writers, drawing on Haudenosaunee tradition and on nineteenth-century ethnography, described a constitutional system in which clan mothers held specific powers. Historians must therefore be careful to distinguish what the seventeenth-century evidence documents from generalized claims about an unchanging constitutional order. The evidence supports the conclusion that women played significant political roles, but it does not always permit precise statements about procedure.

What emerges is a system of negotiation rather than command. A war party could form when a respected leader gathered volunteers, but a larger campaign required broader support and could be shaped by debate in councils. Civil leaders could not forbid a raid outright but could refuse to endorse it, and the confederacy as a whole could not prevent individual nations from acting. This decentralization helps explain how one nation could be at war while another negotiated peace.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Haudenosaunee Community in Wartime

Life in a Haudenosaunee village was organized around longhouses, each home to an extended family of related women, their husbands, and children. Women planted, tended, and harvested the maize, beans, and squash that provided the bulk of the food, and they managed stored provisions. Men hunted, fished, traded, built, and conducted diplomacy and war. The cycle of the year governed work: planting in spring, hunting and trading expeditions in summer and fall, and winter spent largely in the village.

War disrupted these rhythms. Men away on raids or long expeditions left women and elders responsible for more of the community’s work, and the threat of attack could require women to work fields under guard or to gather food in smaller groups. Warriors’ return brought both celebration and mourning, as captives arrived and losses were announced. The incorporation of captives changed the composition of households and added labor but also required the community to support newcomers. When villages were burned, as in 1666 and 1687, households lost their homes and stored food, and the coming winter became a matter of survival.

Evidence for daily life comes from archaeology, which reveals house plans, tools, food remains, and village layouts, from Dutch and French observers who visited, and from Haudenosaunee tradition. All have limits. Visitors often described what interested them, and most were men who had limited access to women’s spaces and work.

Section 47.25

47.25 — Captive Experience: Adoption, Incorporation, and Violence

Captivity was a central experience of the seventeenth-century wars. Captives came from many communities (Wendat, Neutral, Erie, Algonquin, Mahican, French, and others), and their fates varied. On capture, prisoners were often forced to travel to Haudenosaunee villages under harsh conditions, and some were killed on the way. On arrival, their futures were decided by communities and, as noted above, particularly by households of the bereaved. Some were adopted, given names, and treated as kin. Others were subjected to ritual torture and death, which European observers described with horror and which Haudenosaunee and other Indigenous communities understood within their own frameworks of mourning, revenge, and the display of courage. Still others remained in uncertain status, working or living on the margins before being incorporated or sent away.

Women and children were more likely than adult men to be adopted, though not exclusively. Adopted children grew up as members of Haudenosaunee communities and might have little memory of their origins. Adult women might be married into families. Men who were adopted could become warriors and, in some cases, leaders. The scale of incorporation was substantial: Jesuit observers in the 1650s and 1660s reported that communities such as the Seneca and Onondaga contained large numbers of adopted Wendat and others, and some described foreign-born residents as a large part of the population. Such statements are impressions, not census data, but they indicate that adoption significantly shaped Haudenosaunee communities.

Incorporation was not painless. Captives experienced violence, separation, and loss, and adoption did not erase coercion. Some resisted, escaped, or retained loyalties to their original communities. Others adapted and built new lives. The documentary record is limited and mostly comes from outsiders, particularly missionaries who focused on conversion and suffering. Captives themselves rarely left testimony, so historians must infer their experiences from fragmentary evidence while resisting the temptation to flatten them into victims or to treat adoption as an unqualified success. Chapter 53 offers a comparative treatment of captivity and adoption across Indigenous North America.

Section 47.26

47.26 — Agriculture, Villages, Food Supply, and the Costs of Warfare

Maize agriculture was the foundation of Haudenosaunee life. A village’s fields, usually located near the settlement, produced the staple that was stored in bark-lined pits and in longhouses and that sustained communities through winter. Villages were moved periodically, often every decade or two, when nearby soil and firewood were depleted, which meant that communities had experience with relocation. War added a new reason to move: the need for defensible sites, sometimes on hilltops or other places that offered protection.

The destruction of fields and stores was one of the most damaging aspects of warfare. French expeditions in 1666, 1687, and 1693–1696 targeted maize and houses, knowing that loss of food would force communities to scatter, depend on neighbors, or seek terms. In 1687, Denonville’s troops reported destroying very large quantities of stored corn in Seneca towns, and in 1696 Frontenac’s army destroyed fields and food among the Onondaga and Oneida. The figures given by commanders are estimates, but the pattern is consistent. The loss of a harvest could mean hunger, and hunger could mean illness and death or the forced dispersal of households.

The effects extended beyond immediate losses. Rebuilding required labor and time, and returning to a ruined village meant replanting and living on reduced supplies. Villages near the frontier might be fortified more heavily or relocated. The need to support adopted captives and refugees added strain. At the same time, the resilience of Haudenosaunee communities is evident: after each major invasion, communities returned, rebuilt, and in many cases resumed their previous activities. The capacity to recover was based on dispersed settlement, kin networks, and shared knowledge of agriculture, and it explains why French invasions, despite their destructive effects, did not end Haudenosaunee power.

Section 47.27

47.27 — Political Differences among Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga, and Seneca

The Five Nations had shared institutions but different interests. The Mohawk, in the east, were closest to Dutch and English traders at Fort Orange and Albany and to French settlements on the Richelieu and Montreal. They were the most exposed to French invasion (as in 1666 and 1693) and had the strongest commercial ties to the Hudson Valley. Their priorities included the security of their villages, the management of trade at Albany, and relations with Mahican and New England Algonquians. Mohawk leaders were frequently the first to feel the impact of French military pressure and often had strong reasons to seek accommodation or to rely on English alliance.

The Oneida, situated between the Mohawk and the Onondaga, faced similar pressures and were also affected by French campaigns, as in 1696. The Onondaga, hosts of the central council fire, played a major role in diplomacy and often provided advocates for negotiation with the French, such as Garacontié. The Cayuga and Seneca, in the west, were most involved in conflicts with the Erie, Neutral, Susquehannock, and the peoples of the Illinois country. The Seneca, the most populous nation by many accounts, were the primary targets of Denonville’s 1687 invasion and had a particular interest in the western trade and in access to the Great Lakes.

These differences produced disagreements. Some nations favored peace with the French when others did not; some prioritized the English alliance while others sought balance. The confederacy’s consensus mechanisms allowed a range of views, and the diversity of interests helps explain both the persistence of the confederacy and its apparent inconsistency in the eyes of European observers. A policy that looked contradictory to a French or English official could reflect the simultaneous action of different nations. Unity was most visible in ceremonial diplomacy and in shared opposition to external threats, but it did not erase national autonomy.

Historical Vocabulary
War Party, Palisade, Condolence, and Adoption

War party. A group of volunteers, led by an experienced warrior, organized for a raid or expedition. War parties varied from a handful to many hundreds, and participation was voluntary. The term should not be confused with a standing army.

Palisade. A defensive wall of upright posts surrounding a village. Palisades protected against raids but could be overcome by large forces and did not shield villages from fire or siege.

Condolence. A set of ritual practices of consolation, central to Haudenosaunee political and social life, used to comfort the bereaved, restore their capacity to act, and, in diplomatic settings, to renew relationships and to install new leaders.

Adoption. The formal incorporation of a person, often a captive, into a family and clan, with a name, kin relationships, and obligations. Adoption did not guarantee equal status or safety, and it varied by circumstance.

Why these terms matter. They help explain how war, grief, diplomacy, and kinship were connected. Potential misunderstanding: English equivalents such as “army” or “adoption” carry modern assumptions that do not fully capture Haudenosaunee practice.

Section 47.28

47.28 — Reading the Evidence: Jesuit Relations, Dutch Records, Archaeology, and Haudenosaunee Knowledge

Few subjects in North American history depend so heavily on sources produced by the opponents or observers of the people being studied. The Jesuit Relations, the annual reports of French Jesuits, offer extended and detailed narratives and are invaluable for events in Wendake and Haudenosaunee villages visited by missionaries. But the Jesuits were committed to conversion, allied with the Wendat and French, and often interpreted what they saw through religious frameworks. Their descriptions of torture, for example, could emphasize cruelty in order to emphasize their own sacrifice, though they also recorded real events. Their numbers for warriors and casualties are generally estimates, often based on hearsay.

Dutch records, including trading accounts, administrative correspondence, and travel narratives, provide information on Fort Orange, trade, and the Mohawk, and include the valuable journal of Van den Bogaert. They typically reflect commercial interests. English records, especially from New York after 1664 and from Albany conferences, document diplomatic exchanges with the Five Nations and the Covenant Chain, with the caveat that English clerks translated and summarized Haudenosaunee speeches. French military and official records document campaigns and negotiations from the colonial perspective.

Archaeology provides independent evidence. Excavations of Haudenosaunee, Wendat, Erie, and other sites document village size, house construction, food remains, trade goods, and sometimes violence or abandonment. They allow historians to test claims about population, settlement shifts, and the adoption of European goods. They cannot reveal individual motives or reconstruct specific battles, and the identification of sites with named historical communities can be uncertain.

Haudenosaunee oral traditions, wampum, and community scholarship preserve understandings of the past that are not found in colonial documents. They are not simple substitutes for contemporary testimony, any more than the Jesuit Relations are: they carry meanings shaped by the communities that preserve them, and their use requires care and respect. Scholars increasingly work with Haudenosaunee knowledge keepers and researchers, and the work of authors such as Kelly Y. Hopkins on Haudenosaunee life and culture reflects that approach.

Terminology also demands attention. Colonial sources used terms such as “savages,” “barbarous,” and “cruel” that conveyed European assumptions. The term “Iroquois” was a European convention, and phrases like “Beaver Wars” are later scholarly constructions. Historians should avoid reproducing loaded language uncritically and should recognize how such terms contributed to misrepresentation of Indigenous military practices as irrational or uniquely savage, when European armies of the same century also burned villages, destroyed crops, and killed civilians.

Section 47.29

47.29 — Western Competition and the Return of Large-Scale War in the 1680s

The settlement of 1667 had redirected Haudenosaunee warfare more than it had ended it, and by the early 1680s the western Great Lakes had become the principal arena of tension. French traders and explorers, among them René-Robert Cavelier de La Salle, were extending posts into the Illinois country and the upper Mississippi watershed, and the French sought to bind western nations such as the Illinois, Miami, and Odawa to the Montreal trade. Seneca and Cayuga war parties, meanwhile, attacked Illinois and Miami communities in the early 1680s, partly in pursuit of captives and partly in pursuit of strategic and commercial advantages. French officials regarded these attacks as threats to their allies and to the trade.

A second source of tension was the English connection. After the English took New Netherland in 1664, the province of New York, and especially its governors in the 1680s, courted the Five Nations as allies and trading partners. Western nations that wished to obtain English goods could reach Albany through Haudenosaunee territory, and French officials feared a diversion of furs from Montreal. Haudenosaunee leaders, for their part, were interested in remaining middlemen and in making use of both European powers. The tensions were not resolved by any single incident: they accumulated through episodes of trade rivalry, captive-taking, attacks on French traders, and mutual suspicion.

By 1684, French officials, convinced that the Five Nations threatened French alliances in the interior, were preparing a military response. The preceding years show why the war that followed cannot be reduced to a single cause or incident. Haudenosaunee actions in the west, the structure of French alliances, English diplomacy, and the commercial interests of all parties intersected, and each side interpreted the others’ behavior through its own priorities.

Section 47.30

47.30 — Governor La Barre and the Failed French Expedition of 1684

Joseph-Antoine Le Febvre de La Barre, governor of New France from 1682, assembled a force in 1684 to confront the Seneca and, by implication, the Five Nations. The expedition, composed of French regulars, militia, and Indigenous allies, set out for Lake Ontario in the summer. It was beset by illness among the troops, shortages of supplies, and the difficulties of transporting a large force by canoe and bateau. At an encampment on the southeastern shore of Lake Ontario, known to the French as La Famine, the force stalled.

Haudenosaunee delegates, led by an Onondaga speaker whom French sources name Otreouti (and whom the writer Lahontan called “Grangula”), met with La Barre in September. The conference produced a settlement in which La Barre, in a weak position, withdrew. The terms were widely seen as unfavorable to the French: they did not secure the Illinois from further attack in a manner satisfactory to the French allies, and the Seneca were not forced to make major concessions. The speech attributed to Otreouti in Lahontan’s account has been quoted often, but the text appears in a literary work published later, and historians treat its wording with caution. What can be said is that Haudenosaunee diplomats met the French from a position of confidence and negotiated terms that preserved their autonomy.

The episode exposed the limits of French military capacity in the interior. Logistics, disease, and distance constrained any large expedition, and French officers in Canada could not command events in the west. La Barre was recalled and replaced by Jacques-René de Brisay de Denonville, who arrived in 1685 determined to take a more forceful course. The outcome of 1684 therefore did not end the conflict; it set the stage for a harder phase.

Section 47.31

47.31 — Denonville’s Seneca Campaign of 1687

Denonville’s policy combined diplomacy, intimidation, and preparation for invasion. In 1687, French officials at Fort Frontenac seized a number of Haudenosaunee men, many from villages on the north shore of Lake Ontario, who had been invited under the pretext of a feast or negotiation. Some were sent to France to serve as galley slaves, an act that provoked long-lasting anger among the Haudenosaunee and later became a grievance in negotiations. A group of survivors was eventually returned to Canada after 1689. The seizure showed that French officials were willing to violate the norms of diplomatic hospitality that Haudenosaunee and other Indigenous peoples expected.

The invasion followed in the summer. A French army of regulars and Canadian militia, supplemented by Indigenous allies from the mission villages near Montreal and the western Great Lakes, crossed Lake Ontario and landed near Irondequoit Bay in July. The force advanced toward the Seneca villages. On July 13, Seneca warriors ambushed the French column near one of the major towns, and the encounter resulted in casualties on both sides. The Seneca then withdrew, and the French burned several villages, including the large town of Gannagaro, and destroyed food stores. French accounts claim to have destroyed enormous quantities of maize, though the numbers are estimates and reflect the commander’s aim of demonstrating success.

From the Seneca perspective, the campaign was a serious blow but not a defeat of the nation. Seneca people dispersed to other villages and to neighboring nations, retained their warriors, and soon began to rebuild. The French built a fort at Niagara, but disease and supply problems doomed the garrison, and the post was abandoned in 1688. The destruction of villages and food intensified hostility and strengthened the position of Haudenosaunee leaders who argued for continued resistance. English officials in New York, particularly Governor Thomas Dongan, encouraged the Five Nations and asserted that they were under the protection of the English Crown, a claim that Haudenosaunee leaders used selectively. The campaign thus produced neither the submission the French sought nor a lasting weakening of the confederacy. Instead, it set the conditions for retaliation.

Section 47.32

47.32 — The Attack on Lachine, 1689, and the Expansion of Imperial War

In the summer of 1688, a diplomatic effort by Haudenosaunee delegates to negotiate with the French was reportedly disrupted by the Wendat-Tionontaté leader Kondiaronk, who, according to a story told in several French sources, ambushed the delegation in order to prevent a peace that would have left his people exposed. The story’s details are debated and some come from later narrative sources, but it illustrates the way that Indigenous allies of the French pursued their own interests and could affect the course of negotiations. By 1689, Haudenosaunee anger over the seizure of captives and the invasion of Seneca country, together with the opportunity created by French weakness, led to a major raid.

Before dawn on August 5, 1689, a large Haudenosaunee force attacked the settlement of Lachine, near Montreal. The raiders burned houses, killed settlers, and took captives. The number of Haudenosaunee participants is variously reported; contemporary estimates are in the range of over a thousand, but such figures are uncertain. The number of French casualties is also disputed. French sources in the aftermath reported dozens killed and many more taken captive, with figures that vary considerably among accounts, and some early reports gave much higher totals. The varying numbers should be read as estimates shaped by fear, rumor, and political purpose. Many captives were taken, and some were later killed or adopted. The raid shocked the colony, contributed to the abandonment of Fort Frontenac, and led to the recall of Denonville and the return of Frontenac to the governorship.

The attack coincided with the outbreak of the Nine Years’ War between England and France, and the conflict in North America became entangled with European war. English and French colonists soon exchanged raids, and the Five Nations were drawn into a wider contest. But Haudenosaunee leaders were not mere instruments of English policy. They acted from their own grievances and goals, and their alliance with the English at Albany was one of convenience and mutual need, as Section 47.36 explains. Retaliation followed quickly, notably in the French and allied raid on Schenectady in 1690, and the cycle of violence that followed involved Indigenous allies on both sides.

Section 47.33

47.33 — War across the Northeast, 1690–1693

The early 1690s saw widespread, small-scale warfare along the frontier between Canada and New York. French and allied parties, including Catholic mission Indians from communities near Montreal such as Kahnawake, struck English and Haudenosaunee settlements. Haudenosaunee and English parties, in turn, raided the St. Lawrence. In 1691, Peter Schuyler, mayor of Albany, led an expedition with Mohawk and other Haudenosaunee participants against La Prairie, across the river from Montreal. These actions inflicted losses on both sides but did not produce decisive results.

The war placed considerable strain on Haudenosaunee communities. Warriors were killed or captured, families were disrupted, and the shortage of men affected hunting and defense. Haudenosaunee leaders also complained that the English did not provide adequate support. In January and February 1693, a French force of several hundred, including Canadians, mission Indians, and Algonquian allies, traveled south and attacked Mohawk villages. They destroyed settlements, took a large number of captives (French accounts speak of about three hundred, a figure that should be taken as an estimate), and withdrew before English and Mohawk forces could intercept them. The treatment of captives was contested: some were later released or escaped, while others remained among the French or mission communities.

The campaign was a severe blow to the Mohawk, who had already suffered losses, and it increased pressure on Haudenosaunee leaders to consider peace. At the same time, the war showed the role of Indigenous allies of the French, including the Catholic Haudenosaunee communities near Montreal, whose presence reveals how the conflict could divide Haudenosaunee kin and communities. Warfare across the northeast was thus both an imperial contest and a conflict among relatives, neighbors, and rivals.

Section 47.34

47.34 — Frontenac’s Campaign of 1696 against Onondaga and Oneida

In the summer of 1696, Louis de Buade, comte de Frontenac, then in his seventies, led the largest French campaign of the war against the Haudenosaunee. The force of regulars, militia, and Indigenous allies traveled from Montreal up the St. Lawrence, across Lake Ontario, and up the Oswego River into Onondaga country. French accounts estimate the force at around two thousand men, though the figure is approximate. Frontenac’s aim was to demonstrate French power, to punish the Onondaga, and to compel the Five Nations to seek terms.

The Onondaga, aware of the approach, burned their main fort and withdrew, leaving the French to find empty and destroyed villages. French troops destroyed fields of maize and other crops and, in a separate operation, struck the Oneida, burning a village and its food. The Haudenosaunee did not offer a pitched battle, and the French, constrained by supplies and the risk of attack, withdrew after a short time. Frontenac’s army had caused considerable damage to the Onondaga and Oneida, but it had not killed or captured large numbers of people, and the confederacy remained intact.

The significance of 1696 lies in its cumulative effect. After the destruction of villages and stores in 1693 and 1696 and years of losses, Haudenosaunee leaders faced growing pressure to negotiate. It would nevertheless be wrong to claim that the campaign decisively defeated the Five Nations: French power in the interior remained limited, English support remained uncertain, and the Five Nations retained their military capacity. The French invasions together demonstrated that neither side could impose its will, an assessment that encouraged diplomacy and that explains the shape of the settlements that followed.

Section 47.35

47.35 — The Human and Political Costs of Prolonged Warfare

By the mid-1690s, decades of warfare had imposed heavy costs on the Five Nations. Colonial observers reported declining numbers of fighting men, and while their figures are unreliable and often reflect their own agendas, the broad impression of strain is consistent across English, French, and Haudenosaunee accounts. Losses came from battle, captivity, illness, and the departure of Haudenosaunee people who moved to French mission communities near Montreal or to other places. Some of those who left were Catholic converts, others were refugees from the destruction of villages, and still others were individuals whose families had been divided by the wars. The precise numbers are unknown, but the movement contributed to the sense of depletion.

Material costs were equally important. The destruction of villages and stores in 1666, 1687, 1693, and 1696 required rebuilding and left communities more dependent on stored resources and on one another. Trade was disrupted: war parties and invasions interfered with hunting and exchange, and the Haudenosaunee’s ability to serve as intermediaries in the fur trade depended on safe passage. Meanwhile, the shortage of warriors affected the confederacy’s ability to sustain operations on multiple fronts.

These pressures varied among the nations. The Mohawk, exposed to repeated French attacks and to the loss of people to the mission communities, were under particular strain. The Onondaga and Oneida faced the invasion of 1696. The Seneca, though they had suffered in 1687, retained a large population and broader western interests. These differences affected how leaders weighed war and peace. The Five Nations were not equally weary, nor equally eager for the same terms.

Section 47.36

47.36 — The Covenant Chain and Haudenosaunee Relations with English New York

The relationship between the Five Nations and the English of New York is commonly described through the metaphor of the Covenant Chain. After the English took New Netherland in 1664, they inherited the Dutch trading relationship with the Mohawk and gradually extended it to the other nations. Over the following decade, Haudenosaunee and English leaders developed a series of agreements, and in 1677 a conference at Albany between Governor Edmund Andros and Haudenosaunee delegates is generally taken as a point of consolidation. The Chain became a way of describing a relationship of alliance and mutual obligation, maintained through regular councils, exchanges of gifts, and renewals of promises. The image of the chain, which could be polished, strengthened, or allowed to rust, was part of the diplomatic vocabulary used by both sides.

For Haudenosaunee leaders, the Chain offered several advantages. It provided access to English trade goods and a counterweight to French pressure. It also gave the Five Nations a way to bring other Indigenous groups, including some displaced New England and Susquehannock communities, into relationships mediated through Albany. At the same time, Haudenosaunee leaders did not regard themselves as subjects of the English Crown. English officials sometimes spoke as if the Five Nations were subordinate, as when Governor Dongan in the 1680s asserted their status as English subjects, but Haudenosaunee speakers used the language of alliance and often reminded the English of their obligations. During the wars of the 1680s and 1690s, Haudenosaunee leaders complained about insufficient English military support, and they used the alliance selectively.

The Chain was therefore an instrument of diplomacy rather than a guarantee of English control. It preserved Haudenosaunee options and complemented, rather than replaced, relations with the French. The institution’s full development is examined in Chapter 56 (The Covenant Chain and Northeastern Diplomacy), and the wider theory and practice of Indigenous diplomacy appears in Chapter 55.

Section 47.37

47.37 — Negotiating Peace with the French and the Western Nations

The movement toward peace was gradual. After the Treaty of Ryswick ended the Nine Years’ War in Europe in 1697, colonial authorities received orders to cease hostilities, but the local war between the French, the Five Nations, and the western nations did not end automatically. Haudenosaunee delegates and French officials exchanged messages, and negotiations focused on several issues: the return of captives held by each side, the security of travel and trade routes, access to hunting territories, and the relationship of the Five Nations to the western nations allied with the French. Frontenac died in 1698, and his successor Louis-Hector de Callière, who became governor in 1699, pursued a general settlement.

The negotiations were not bilateral. The French needed to bring their Indigenous allies, including the Odawa, Anishinaabe peoples of the upper Great Lakes, the Wendat and Tionontaté at Michilimackinac and Detroit-area communities, the Abenaki, the Algonquin, and others, into any agreement, and those allies had their own demands. They wanted the return of their captives held by the Haudenosaunee and assurances about security and trade. Haudenosaunee leaders, for their part, wanted the return of their own captives, including those taken to the St. Lawrence and those who had been sent to French galleys, and they sought assurances for hunting and trade. Diplomats from many nations, including leaders such as Kondiaronk, played central roles, and negotiations moved across multiple councils in 1700 and early 1701.

The process shows that peace was constructed through patient, multilateral diplomacy. It also shows that Haudenosaunee leaders approached it from a position of continuing strength: they had suffered losses but remained a significant force, and they could press their demands. They also pursued English diplomacy at Albany in parallel, which gave them leverage and ensured that no single European power would dominate their choices.

Section 47.38

47.38 — The Albany Deed of July 19, 1701: Land, Hunting Rights, and Diplomacy

On July 19, 1701, at Albany, representatives of the Five Nations and John Nanfan, lieutenant governor of New York, concluded a document commonly known as the Albany Deed or Nanfan Deed. In it, the Haudenosaunee delegates purported to convey to the English Crown a vast territory in the Great Lakes region, which the deed describes as their beaver hunting ground, with the understanding that the king would protect and defend it for their benefit. The deed expressly reserved free hunting for the Five Nations and their descendants, with an expectation of English protection; it did not separately enumerate fishing rights. The territory described is very large, and the boundaries as given in the deed have been interpreted in different ways.

What did the deed accomplish? For the English, it provided a documentary basis for later claims to the interior, and English officials in the eighteenth century invoked it in disputes with France. For the Haudenosaunee, the document had different significance. It was a diplomatic act that placed their claims under the protection of a European power at a moment when they were negotiating peace with another. By describing the lands as a hunting ground to be protected rather than as territory settled by the Five Nations, the deed reflects claims of conquest and use, not exclusive occupation, and it did not necessarily transfer ownership in the sense an English lawyer might assume. The Five Nations later maintained that they had not surrendered their lands.

Neighboring nations were not parties to the deed and did not consent to it. The Anishinaabe, Wendat, Miami, Illinois, and others who lived in or used the territory did not regard themselves as having been included in any transfer. The deed must therefore be treated as evidence of Haudenosaunee and English diplomatic and political claims, not as proof that the territories had been legally surrendered or that other peoples had lost their rights. Its legal significance has been disputed since the eighteenth century.

Section 47.39

47.39 — The Great Peace of Montreal, August 4, 1701

The Great Peace of Montreal was concluded on August 4, 1701, after weeks of councils at Montreal attended by governor Callière, French officials, and delegations from many Indigenous nations. Historians count roughly thirty or more nations as represented and well over a thousand delegates in attendance, though figures differ by source and counting method. The assembly included the Five Nations, the Wendat and Tionontaté, the Odawa and other Anishinaabe peoples, the Abenaki and other eastern Algonquian groups, the Algonquin, and the nations of the western Great Lakes. Kondiaronk, a leading advocate for the settlement, died during the proceedings in early August and was honored with a major funeral, a reminder of the personal and political weight of Indigenous diplomacy.

The treaty provided for an end to hostilities among the signatories, the exchange of prisoners, and arrangements for resolving future disputes, with the French governor playing a role as mediator. Signatories affixed their marks, typically a clan or personal symbol. The Haudenosaunee understood the peace in terms of their own priorities: an end to the war, the return of captives, safe access for trade and hunting, and the preservation of their independence. In the following months, Haudenosaunee leaders articulated a policy of neutrality in conflicts between the French and the English, a policy that, while complex in practice, allowed them to deal with both and gave them influence in the diplomacy of the eighteenth century.

The Great Peace complements the account in Chapter 45, which emphasizes the French–Indigenous alliance system. Seen from the perspective of this chapter, the peace represents an outcome of Haudenosaunee strategy and of the exhaustion and resilience of all participants. It did not erase conflict: tensions over land, trade, and captives persisted, and warfare resumed in later decades. But it did mark the end of the sustained cycle of violence examined here and a transformation in the geopolitical opportunities available to the Five Nations and their neighbors.

Division VIII · Sections 47.40–47.42Historical Consequences, Interpretations, and Memory
Section 47.40

47.40 — Were These Really the Beaver Wars? Historical Interpretations Compared

The question posed at the start of the chapter can now be addressed with the evidence in view. The economic interpretation holds that the Five Nations, having depleted beaver in their own territory and eager to maintain a flow of European goods, attacked neighbors to seize control of the fur trade and its sources. This view draws support from the commercial setting of the wars, from the strategic importance of trade routes, and from the interest of the Dutch, French, and English in furs. Historians who emphasize it point to the targeting of Wendat commerce and to the patterns of attack on convoys.

The mourning-war interpretation stresses demographic loss and cultural practice. In this account, epidemics and warfare created a need to replace the dead, and the capture and adoption of prisoners restored communities and sustained them. The evidence includes the large number of adoptees reported in Haudenosaunee communities and the importance of condolence and captive-taking in Haudenosaunee and neighboring societies. Critics note that it cannot by itself explain the choice of targets or the timing of campaigns.

Territorial and political models emphasize security, the control of strategic routes, and independence. On this view the Haudenosaunee sought to protect their homeland from encirclement by French-allied nations, to maintain influence over neighbors, and to preserve autonomy in an environment of expanding colonial powers. The evidence includes the geography of campaigns and the diplomacy that accompanied them. A fourth emphasis, on European colonial expansion, observes that French, Dutch, and English policies shaped alliances, supplied weapons, and invaded Haudenosaunee territory, so that the wars cannot be understood without them. Scholars such as Daniel Richter, José António Brandão, Jon Parmenter, and Bruce Trigger have developed different combinations of these explanations, and Parmenter in particular has questioned the claim that control of the fur market adequately explains Haudenosaunee expansion.

These interpretations overlap. Commercial opportunity could encourage a raid whose participants were also seeking captives; territorial security could depend on controlling trade routes; disease could heighten both the demand for captives and the vulnerability of neighbors. They differ in how much weight they give each factor, in which events they treat as typical, and in what evidence they privilege. Most historians now treat the wars as multicausal, with different mixtures of motives at different times. The label “Beaver Wars” survives as a convenient shorthand, but it is better understood as one historiographical proposal among several than as a settled description.

Evidence: European narratives, trade records, archaeology, and Haudenosaunee tradition, each with limits discussed in Section 47.28.

Points of disagreement: The debate concerns causation, the relative weight of factors, the interpretation of sources, and the characterization of Haudenosaunee aims.

Why it matters: If the wars were primarily commercial, Haudenosaunee action looks like economic competition; if they were also social and political, they appear as expressions of Haudenosaunee society and strategy. The interpretation also affects how neighboring peoples are understood, as victims, as rivals, or as agents.

Section 47.41

47.41 — Haudenosaunee Sovereignty, Neighboring Nations, and the Legacy of the Wars

The long-term consequences of the wars cannot be summarized as victory or defeat. The Five Nations emerged from the century with their confederacy intact, their homelands held, and their diplomatic position strengthened by the settlements of 1701 and the policy of neutrality. They had absorbed large numbers of captives and refugees and had acquired influence over a wide region. They had also suffered heavy losses, endured invasions that destroyed villages and food stores, and seen some of their people migrate to French missions. Their continued political independence in the eighteenth century, when they negotiated with both the French and the English and played a prominent role in imperial diplomacy, is evidence of resilience, not of passive survival.

For neighboring nations, consequences varied. The Wendat, Tionontaté, Neutral, and Erie lost their homelands and, in most cases, their earlier political organization. Yet their people and traditions persisted: Wendat communities near Quebec (Wendake), Wyandot communities in the Great Lakes and later in Ohio, Michigan, Kansas, and Oklahoma, and descendants of adoptees within Haudenosaunee communities all trace connections to these histories. The Susquehannock and Mahican were displaced and dispersed, though not erased. Other nations, such as the Odawa and other Anishinaabe peoples, gained prominence in the trade and diplomacy of the western Great Lakes. The reorganization of the region created new alliances and new rivalries that shaped the eighteenth century, including the conflicts examined in Chapters 57 and 58.

Haudenosaunee historical perspectives emphasize continuity of political life, the persistence of the Great Law, and the importance of diplomacy and peace-making, as well as the memory of loss. They also complicate triumphalist accounts: communities remember destroyed villages, lost relatives, and the responsibilities of condolence. Non-Haudenosaunee Indigenous nations remember the same events as catastrophes and as the beginning of new histories of migration and renewal. A responsible account must hold these perspectives together without reducing them to a single moral verdict.

The legacy of the wars also includes the ways they have been remembered. Colonial writers cast them as evidence of Indigenous “savagery,” while later nationalist narratives, as in the Long Sault story, used them to serve other purposes. Contemporary scholarship, including Haudenosaunee-centered work, has reexamined the period and given greater attention to Indigenous political aims and to the experiences of the displaced. That reexamination continues.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that the seventeenth-century wars involving the Haudenosaunee were not simply a struggle to monopolize the beaver trade. They emerged from interacting pressures: epidemic and demographic loss, mourning and captive adoption, territorial security, commercial competition, political independence, older Indigenous rivalries, and the expansion of French, Dutch, and English colonies. Different pressures mattered at different times, and the Five Nations responded through both warfare and diplomacy.

The Haudenosaunee Confederacy joined five autonomous nations, the Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga, and Seneca, through consensus-based councils and shared ceremonial institutions. It did not function as a centralized military state. War parties were assembled by volunteers under respected leaders, and decisions about conflict involved civil leaders, war leaders, clan matrons, and mourning households. The Five Nations’ different locations produced different priorities, and the confederacy’s unity coexisted with disagreement.

Early conflicts developed from existing rivalries that European arrivals altered. Champlain’s participation in engagements in 1609–1615 placed the French alongside the Innu, Algonquin, and Wendat. Dutch trade at Fort Orange strengthened the Mohawk and reshaped the Mohawk–Mahican war of the 1620s. Epidemics in the 1630s and expanded access to firearms in the 1640s intensified raiding and strained regional relations, while peace efforts, including the negotiations of 1645, showed that diplomacy continued alongside war.

The midcentury conflicts transformed the Great Lakes. The destruction of Wendat villages in 1648–1649 occurred amid disease, famine, mission-related division, and colonial pressures. Campaigns against the Tionontaté, Neutral, and Erie followed. The collapse of these political communities did not mean the disappearance of their peoples: refugees and captives moved to the St. Lawrence, the upper Great Lakes, and Haudenosaunee villages, and their descendants persist. The sources, particularly the Jesuit Relations, reflect missionary perspectives, and the Erie war in particular illustrates the limits of the written record.

From the 1650s to the 1670s, Haudenosaunee diplomacy included the Onondaga peace initiatives and the French mission, the Long Sault engagement and its later mythologization, the invasion of the Mohawk country in 1666 by the Carignan-Salières regiment and its allies, and the peace of 1667. These events show both French reach and its limits and the different priorities of the Five Nations. Conflicts with the Susquehannock, Mahican, and western peoples continued, and the idea of a single Haudenosaunee strategy does not survive examination.

Warfare had social foundations and social costs. War parties, fortified villages, canoe mobility, and firearms characterized military practice; women and clans shaped decisions; captives experienced violence, adoption, or death; and the destruction of fields and stores could be as damaging as battle. Interpreting these matters requires careful use of French, Dutch, and English sources, archaeology, and Haudenosaunee knowledge, and critical attention to loaded terminology.

The late seventeenth century brought renewed large-scale war. Tensions over the western Great Lakes, French and English rivalry, the expedition of La Barre in 1684, Denonville’s invasion of Seneca country in 1687, the Lachine raid of 1689, the French raids of 1693, and Frontenac’s campaign of 1696 formed a sequence in which Haudenosaunee communities were damaged but not conquered. Casualty figures from these years are estimates, and Indigenous allies fought on all sides.

Pressure for peace mounted through accumulated costs, and Haudenosaunee leaders pursued a two-sided strategy. The Covenant Chain with English New York provided options; the Albany Deed of July 19, 1701, recorded a diplomatic claim, not a clear transfer of neighbors’ lands; and the Great Peace of Montreal of August 4, 1701, brought many Indigenous nations and the French into a general settlement. Neutrality between the empires became a central Haudenosaunee policy.

Interpretations of these events remain debated. Economic, mourning-war, territorial, and colonial explanations each illuminate part of the picture, and most historians now treat the wars as multicausal. The legacy of the wars is not a simple story of victory and defeat: the Five Nations preserved their confederacy and independence, while displaced peoples such as the Wendat survived and rebuilt, and the political geography of Indigenous Northeast North America was reordered.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a single label can hide a century of complexity. By reconstructing the wars as the product of mourning, demographic loss, territorial security, commerce, and imperial expansion, it recovers the Five Nations and their neighbors as political actors who made choices under constraint. The Haudenosaunee fought, negotiated, adopted, and rebuilt; the Wendat, Neutral, Erie, Susquehannock, and Mahican resisted, moved, and survived in altered forms.

The chapter also reveals how historians know what they know. Much of the narrative depends on missionaries, traders, and officers with their own aims, and the Erie case in particular demonstrates how thin the record can be. Archaeology, Haudenosaunee knowledge, and careful reading of colonial sources are all necessary, and none is sufficient alone.

The settlements of 1701 shaped the century that followed. Haudenosaunee neutrality, the Covenant Chain, and the French–Indigenous alliance system created a diplomatic landscape in which Indigenous nations remained powerful, a theme developed in Chapters 55 through 57. The displacement of the Wendat and their neighbors helped shape the Great Lakes world examined in Chapter 58 and later chapters. And the memory of the wars, from colonial stereotypes to modern Indigenous scholarship, continues to influence how this history is told.

Above all, the chapter shows that Indigenous history in the seventeenth century was not a prelude to colonial history. It was a field of political creativity, violence, grief, adaptation, and diplomacy, in which Indigenous nations and European colonies were entangled but not interchangeable, and from which Indigenous peoples emerged transformed but continuing.
