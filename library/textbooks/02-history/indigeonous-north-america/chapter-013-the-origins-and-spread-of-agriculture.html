The Origins and Spread of Agriculture

Indigenous Peoples of North America: A Continental History

Chapter Opening

Long before maize reached the river valleys of the Eastern Woodlands, and long before Europeans imagined the "New World" as a wilderness ripe for cultivation, Indigenous peoples across North America were already tending, selecting, and reshaping plants. Agriculture on this continent was not a single moment of discovery. It was a long accumulation of small, repeated human choices, made independently in more than one place, that gradually and unevenly changed how people ate, worked, stored food, and organized their lives.

Picture a household along a river terrace in what is now the mid-continental United States, more than four thousand years ago. The people who live there in a given season are not "farmers" in any sense a modern reader would immediately recognize. They fish the river in spring, gather hickory nuts and acorns in autumn, hunt deer and small game year-round, and travel to seasonal camps to collect particular plants at the moments they ripen. Yet scattered near their hearths, mixed among the seeds of wild plants they have gathered from the surrounding floodplain, are seeds of a different character: thinner-coated, larger, more uniform than their wild ancestors. These are the marks of a process already well under way, a process this chapter calls domestication, and it did not require maize, irrigation, or a single "Agricultural Revolution" to begin.

This chapter examines how cultivation and, eventually, agriculture entered the lives of Indigenous peoples across a continent whose environments and prior subsistence traditions varied enormously. It resists two temptations common in older, less careful accounts of this history. The first temptation is to treat agriculture as a single dramatic transition, a moment when "farming" replaced "hunting and gathering" the way one chapter of a book replaces another. The second temptation is to treat maize as the origin point of North American agriculture, as though nothing worth calling cultivation occurred until a Mesoamerican crop arrived to civilize the continent's interior. Both temptations distort the archaeological record. Long before maize became agriculturally significant north of Mexico, Indigenous peoples in the Eastern Woodlands were already domesticating their own native plants: sunflower, a chenopod related to modern quinoa, marshelder, and at least one lineage of squash. Domestication in eastern North America, in other words, has its own independent history, unfolding on a timeline separate from, and in some respects earlier than, the northward spread of Mesoamerican crops.

Nor did cultivation, once adopted, mean the abandonment of older ways of getting food. This point deserves emphasis because it runs against a deeply ingrained assumption in popular historical imagination: that once people learn to farm, they stop hunting, fishing, and gathering, and that farming societies are automatically more "advanced," more sedentary, and more populous than societies that do not farm. The archaeological and botanical record from across North America tells a more complicated and, in the end, more interesting story. Communities frequently added cultivated plants to subsistence systems built around deer hunting, fishing, shellfish collection, and the harvesting of nuts and wild seeds, and they often did so for centuries, sometimes for millennia, without cultivated plants ever becoming the dominant source of calories. Some societies eventually built intensive, maize-centered agricultural economies capable of supporting towns and regional political centers, developments examined beginning in Chapter 15 for the Southwest and in Part IV for the mound-building societies of the East. Others incorporated crops only partially, or seasonally, or temporarily, and many communities across the continent, including much of California, much of the Great Basin, the Northwest Coast, the Arctic, and the Subarctic, developed sophisticated, sustainable, and highly productive economies without adopting field agriculture at all, examined more fully in Chapters 8 through 11 and again in Volume III. As this chapter later explains, the Great Basin itself was not uniform in this respect: Fremont communities in portions of the eastern Great Basin practiced small-scale maize farming for centuries, a regional exception that illustrates how variable agricultural adoption could be even within a single cultural region.

Understanding why some communities cultivated intensively, others cultivated lightly, and others did not cultivate at all requires more than a story of technological progress. It requires attention to environment, to the biology of specific plants, to risk and food security, to labor and scheduling, to social and ceremonial life, and to the simple fact that people generally continued doing what already worked well for them unless a new practice offered a clear advantage worth the cost of adopting it. This chapter treats the origins and spread of agriculture as a set of historical decisions made by particular communities in particular circumstances, not as an inevitable stage on a single ladder of human development.

The chapter also takes seriously a methodological problem that will recur throughout this textbook: how do historians and archaeologists actually know that a particular plant was domesticated, and when? Seeds do not come labeled with dates or intentions. Distinguishing a gathered wild plant from a deliberately cultivated and genetically altered crop requires careful scientific analysis of seed size, seed-coat thickness, rind structure, and other physical traits, combined with direct radiocarbon dating of the plant remains themselves rather than of the surrounding soil layer. This chapter introduces these methods directly, because the story of agriculture's origins is inseparable from the story of how that origin has been reconstructed from fragmentary physical evidence.

Finally, this chapter positions agriculture within the larger arc of Volume II. Chapter 12 closed Volume I by describing regional worlds already connected through long-distance exchange and shared ceremonial practices, worlds that were dynamic and historically active without depending on field crops. This chapter, opening Volume II, explains how domesticated plants entered those already sophisticated regional worlds, at different times and to different degrees. Chapter 14 will then turn to Mesoamerica in its own right, examining the civilizations that first domesticated maize and the connections those civilizations maintained with regions farther north. Chapters 15 through 18 will follow agriculture into the arid Southwest, where irrigation and intensive maize farming eventually supported the Mogollon, Hohokam, and Ancestral Pueblo worlds. Chapters 19 through 23 will follow a parallel, though not identical, agricultural story into the river valleys and uplands of the East, where intensifying cultivation contributed to the rise of Adena, Hopewell, and Mississippian societies, including Cahokia. This chapter supplies the botanical and methodological foundation that those later, more detailed regional chapters will build upon.

  4. Analyze the domestication history of squash and explain why it occurred in more than one region and at more than one time.

Historical Context

Chapter 12 closed Volume I by describing the Archaic worlds of North America as increasingly regionalized, interconnected, and historically dynamic before intensive agriculture became economically dominant in many regions north of Mexico. Communities across the Eastern Woodlands, the Great Basin and desert West, California, the Northwest Coast, and the Arctic and Subarctic had already developed sophisticated economies built on hunting, fishing, shellfish collection, and the intensive harvesting, processing, and storage of wild plant foods, from acorns and hickory nuts to camas bulbs and wild rice. Long-distance exchange networks moved copper, marine shell, obsidian, and other materials across enormous distances, and increasingly elaborate ceremonial and mortuary practices point to complex social relationships that did not depend on field crops.

This chapter picks up directly from that foundation. It does not treat the Archaic economies described in Chapter 12 as a "before" that agriculture simply replaced. Instead, it treats cultivation as a set of practices that entered, and in most cases supplemented, already sophisticated subsistence systems. In the Eastern Woodlands specifically, the same communities whose broad-based Archaic economies Chapter 12 described were, by the fourth and third millennia before the present, already engaged in the deliberate cultivation and, in several cases, genetic domestication of native plants, a development this chapter treats as continuous with, rather than a break from, the Archaic pattern of intensive plant management.

Geography matters enormously to this story. North America's environments, from the arid Southwest to the humid river valleys of the Eastern Woodlands to the short growing seasons of the northern Plains and Subarctic, placed sharply different constraints and possibilities before any community considering whether to cultivate a plant. A crop that thrived in the frost-free floodplains of central Mexico could not simply be transplanted without modification into a region with a much shorter growing season; successful northward spread required generations of selection for earlier-maturing, more cold-tolerant varieties. This chapter treats environmental variation not as a background detail but as an active historical factor shaping when, where, and how intensively cultivation took hold.

Students should also understand, before proceeding, that this chapter is chronologically and thematically distinct from Chapter 14, which examines Mesoamerican civilizations in their own right, and from Chapters 15 through 23, which trace the detailed regional histories of the Southwest and the mound-building societies of the East. This chapter establishes the plant-by-plant domestication history and the general historical dynamics of agricultural adoption that make those later, more detailed regional narratives intelligible. It deliberately avoids narrating the Hohokam, Ancestral Pueblo, Adena, Hopewell, or Mississippian stories in advance; those societies appear here only as later destinations for processes this chapter explains in general terms.

Section 13.1

What Do We Mean by Agriculture?

Before tracing the history of domesticated plants in North America, this chapter must define its vocabulary with care, because the words commonly used in everyday speech, "farming," "agriculture," "planting," blur distinctions that matter enormously to historians and archaeologists. Four terms in particular require precise definition: cultivation, domestication, horticulture, and agriculture.

Cultivation refers to human intervention in a plant's growth, such as clearing ground, planting seeds, weeding, watering, or protecting a plant from competitors. Cultivation does not, by itself, imply that a plant has been genetically altered by human selection. A community can cultivate wild plants, tending them in place or transplanting them, without those plants having undergone any biological change from their wild ancestors.

Domestication, by contrast, refers to a genetic and morphological change in a plant population produced by sustained human selection, whether deliberate or unintentional, over many generations. A domesticated plant is measurably different from its wild ancestor in ways that typically make it more useful to humans and, often, less able to survive and reproduce successfully without human assistance. Common domestication traits include larger seeds, thinner seed coats that make germination easier but reduce the plant's ability to survive dormant in the wild, loss of natural seed dispersal mechanisms, and increased uniformity in ripening time. Domestication is a biological process with observable physical consequences; cultivation is a behavioral process that may or may not produce those consequences.

Horticulture generally describes small-scale, garden-style cultivation, often using hand tools, digging sticks, or other non-mechanized methods, frequently combined with other subsistence activities such as hunting, fishing, and gathering. Agriculture, in its more intensive sense, generally describes larger-scale, more labor-intensive cultivation, often involving field preparation, more extensive land clearance, and a greater share of a community's total diet and labor devoted to cultivated plants. The boundary between horticulture and agriculture is not sharp, and this textbook uses both terms as points along a continuum of subsistence intensification rather than as strictly separate categories.

Finally, subsistence intensification describes any process by which a community increases the amount of food, or the reliability of food supply, obtained from a given area of land or a given unit of labor. Cultivation and domestication are two important forms of subsistence intensification, but they are not the only ones; more intensive collection, processing, and storage of wild foods, such as nuts, wild rice, or camas bulbs, can also intensify subsistence without involving domesticated plants at all. Keeping these terms distinct allows this chapter, and the chapters that follow, to describe historical change with precision rather than collapsing every increase in food production into an undifferentiated story of "the rise of agriculture."

Historical Vocabulary
Domestication and Cultivation

Domestication is a biological process: a population of plants (or animals) changes genetically, over generations, in response to sustained human selection, whether that selection is deliberate or an unplanned byproduct of harvesting, storing, and replanting seeds in particular ways. A domesticated plant typically looks and behaves differently from its wild ancestor in ways that benefit human use, such as larger seeds or a thinner seed coat, but that often reduce its ability to reproduce and spread successfully without human help.

Cultivation is a behavioral process: humans intentionally plant, tend, protect, or otherwise manage a plant's growth. Cultivation can occur without domestication, as when people tend wild stands of a plant without altering its genetics. Domestication, in turn, requires cultivation or an equivalent sustained human relationship with the plant, but the reverse is not automatically true.

Potential misunderstanding: Students often assume that any evidence of a plant being used, gathered, or even planted by a human community proves that the plant was domesticated. This is incorrect. Evidence of use is not, by itself, evidence of domestication. Domestication requires evidence of genetic and morphological change, of the kind described in Section 13.3, sustained across multiple generations of the plant.

Section 13.2

Agriculture Before "Agricultural Societies"

Chapter 12 described the Archaic societies of North America as increasingly regionalized, technologically inventive, and connected through long-distance exchange, all without relying on field crops. This section extends that portrait directly, because the plants examined later in this chapter were first cultivated and domesticated by communities whose overall way of life would still be recognized, in nearly every other respect, as a continuation of the broad-spectrum Archaic economies Chapter 12 described.

This point deserves emphasis because textbooks have sometimes implied a clean chronological boundary: an "Archaic period" of hunting, fishing, and gathering, followed by a "Woodland period" or "Formative period" defined by the arrival of agriculture. The archaeological evidence does not support so sharp a division. Communities in the Eastern Woodlands were managing and, in several documented cases, genetically altering native plants by the fourth and third millennia before the present, while continuing to rely primarily on deer hunting, fishing, and the collection of nuts, particularly hickory and acorn, for the great majority of their caloric intake. Early cultivated plants supplemented, rather than replaced, an already successful and diverse subsistence base.

This section therefore treats early plant cultivation as a continuation and refinement of Archaic subsistence intensification rather than as a new and separate historical era. Communities that had already developed sophisticated methods of processing, storing, and scheduling access to wild plant foods, methods examined in Chapter 7, extended those same skills to a small number of native plants whose seed characteristics made them especially responsive to human selection. The people involved in this process were not becoming a different kind of society; they were doing what humans across many long-lived societies have often done, refining existing practices incrementally in response to opportunity and need.

Recognizing this continuity matters for how students should read the rest of this chapter, and the rest of this textbook. Agriculture, wherever and whenever it appears in Indigenous North American history, entered communities that already possessed deep ecological knowledge, established economies, and functioning social and ceremonial systems. It did not create these things from nothing, and it did not, in most cases, immediately or completely displace them.

Section 13.3

How Archaeologists Identify Domestication

Because domestication is a biological rather than a purely behavioral process, archaeologists and archaeobotanists rely on specific, measurable physical evidence to distinguish a domesticated plant from a wild one gathered or even lightly managed by human communities. This section explains the principal forms of evidence and their limitations, because the chronology and interpretation of North American agriculture depend directly on these methods.

Seed and Fruit Morphology

The most direct evidence of domestication comes from changes in the physical structure of seeds, fruits, or other plant parts recovered from archaeological sites. In chenopod, for example, domesticated seeds show a thinner, smoother seed coat than wild seeds, a change that makes germination easier once planted but reduces the seed's ability to survive dormant in the soil for long periods, a trait wild chenopod needs to survive unpredictable conditions but a domesticated, human-tended population does not. In squash and other cucurbits, archaeologists examine changes in rind thickness, seed size, and peduncle, or stem, structure. In sunflower and marshelder, seed size provides the clearest signal, since domesticated seeds are substantially larger than their wild counterparts. These changes did not happen instantly; they represent the cumulative effect of many generations of human harvesting and replanting, whether or not early cultivators intended to reshape the plant's biology.

Phytoliths, Starch Grains, and Microscopic Evidence

Not all plant evidence survives as visible seeds. Phytoliths, microscopic silica structures that form within many plants and survive long after the rest of the plant has decayed, and starch grains, which can adhere to stone tools, cooking vessels, and even dental calculus on human teeth, provide additional lines of evidence for plant use and, in some cases, for changes associated with domestication. These microscopic forms of evidence have become increasingly important in recent decades because they allow archaeologists to detect plant use even at sites where larger seeds did not survive.

Direct Radiocarbon Dating of Plant Remains

A methodological development of major importance to this chapter's chronology has been the increasing use of direct accelerator mass spectrometry, or AMS, radiocarbon dating applied to individual plant specimens themselves, such as a single charred seed, rather than to surrounding soil, charcoal, or other material assumed to be the same age as the plant remains. Earlier excavations sometimes dated a layer of soil and assumed that seeds found within that layer were the same age, an assumption that can be seriously mistaken if seeds moved through the soil after deposition, whether through animal burrowing, root disturbance, or other natural processes. Direct dating of individual seeds has, in several well-documented cases, revised earlier domestication chronologies, sometimes making a domestication event appear later, and in some cases earlier, than previously believed. This textbook presents chronological ranges rather than single fixed dates for many domestication events precisely because ongoing direct dating continues to refine this record.

Limitations of the Evidence

Even with these methods, significant uncertainty remains. Morphological change is gradual, and archaeologists must decide where, along a continuum from clearly wild to clearly domesticated, a given specimen falls; this is sometimes described as a "gray zone" in which a population is undergoing selection but has not yet developed the full suite of traits associated with a fully domesticated plant. Preservation is uneven: seeds survive better in dry caves and rock shelters, such as those found in parts of the Southwest and the Ozark Plateau, than in the wetter environments common across much of the Eastern Woodlands, which can bias the record toward regions with better preservation conditions rather than necessarily reflecting where domestication first occurred. Finally, evidence of a domesticated plant at a site tells historians about that plant's biology; it does not, by itself, indicate what proportion of the community's diet the plant represented, how the community organized labor around it, or how the community understood its significance. Those questions require additional evidence, discussed later in this chapter and in the regional chapters that follow.

Historical Method

How Can a Seed Show Domestication?

Consider two chenopod seed assemblages recovered from Eastern Woodlands rock shelters. Assemblage A consists of seeds with thick, rough, dark seed coats of variable size. Assemblage B consists of seeds with thin, smooth, lighter-colored seed coats that are notably larger and more uniform in size than Assemblage A.

Archaeobotanists interpret Assemblage A as consistent with wild chenopod, whose thick seed coat allows the seed to remain dormant in the soil for years until conditions favor germination, a survival strategy suited to unpredictable wild environments. They interpret Assemblage B as consistent with a domesticated population, since a thinner seed coat allows faster, more reliable germination when planted deliberately by a human cultivator, but leaves the seed poorly equipped to survive on its own in the wild without human assistance. The larger, more uniform size of Assemblage B further suggests generations of human selection favoring more productive, easier-to-process seeds.

Method Analysis Questions:

  1. Why does a thinner seed coat suggest human cultivation rather than natural evolution alone?
  2. Why is seed uniformity, rather than seed presence alone, useful evidence of domestication?
  3. What can an archaeobotanist conclude, and not conclude, from finding Assemblage B at a site: does it prove the community was primarily agricultural, primarily sedentary, or neither?
  4. Why might direct radiocarbon dating of the seeds themselves, rather than of the surrounding soil, change the interpretation of when Assemblage B first appeared?
Section 13.4

The Eastern Agricultural Complex

One of the most historically significant, and for many years underappreciated, developments in North American history is the independent domestication of a group of native plants in the Eastern Woodlands, a group archaeologists and botanists call the Eastern Agricultural Complex. This complex is generally understood to include sunflower, marshelder, chenopod, erect knotweed, and, as Section 13.5 explains, at least one lineage of squash, along with maygrass and little barley, two additional native seed plants that show clear evidence of cultivation and management even where the evidence for full genetic domestication is less certain than for the plants named first. None of these plants derives from Mesoamerican domestication. Their independent development demonstrates that Indigenous peoples of eastern North America were not merely recipients of agricultural knowledge developed elsewhere; they were themselves originators of a domestication tradition built on plants native to their own region. This chapter concentrates on sunflower, marshelder, chenopod, and squash because they are the most thoroughly documented, but students should understand that the full complex was broader than these four plants alone.

Sunflower

Sunflower, Helianthus annuus, is among the clearest and best-documented cases of North American plant domestication. Wild sunflower produces relatively small seeds on a plant with multiple branching heads. Domesticated sunflower, by contrast, produces markedly larger seeds, often concentrated on a single large flower head, changes that reflect generations of human selection for a more easily harvested, more calorie-dense plant. Archaeobotanical evidence indicates that sunflower domestication was under way in the Eastern Woodlands by roughly 4,800 years ago, making it one of the earliest well-documented plant domestication events north of Mexico.

Marshelder (Sumpweed)

Marshelder, or sumpweed, Iva annua, was domesticated for its oily, protein-rich seeds by roughly 4,400 years ago, somewhat later than sunflower but still within the earliest horizon of Eastern Agricultural Complex development. Domesticated marshelder seeds are substantially larger than wild seeds, again reflecting sustained human selection. Marshelder is no longer widely cultivated today, and it receives less popular attention than sunflower or maize, but its historical significance is considerable: it demonstrates that Eastern Woodlands communities were managing more than one native plant species simultaneously, developing a genuinely diversified early agricultural repertoire rather than relying on a single crop.

Chenopod and Erect Knotweed

Chenopod, Chenopodium berlandieri, a wild relative of the South American crop quinoa, shows clear evidence of seed-coat thinning and other domestication traits in Eastern Woodlands archaeological assemblages by roughly 3,800 years ago, somewhat later than the earliest sunflower and marshelder evidence but still within the broader multi-century-to-millennium horizon of Eastern Agricultural Complex development. Chenopod seeds are small but highly nutritious, and their systematic domestication further illustrates the pattern of Eastern Woodlands communities investing sustained attention in more than one native seed crop. Erect knotweed also became a domesticated member of the Eastern Agricultural Complex, while maygrass and little barley were cultivated; the precise chronology and strength of domestication evidence differ among these less intensively documented crops, and this textbook does not attach a single shared date to all of them.

Why the Eastern Agricultural Complex Matters

The significance of this complex extends well beyond the specific plants involved. It demonstrates, with direct physical evidence, that agricultural innovation in North America did not require external stimulus from Mesoamerica. Communities in the Eastern Woodlands possessed the ecological knowledge, the sustained residential and seasonal patterns, and the selective practices necessary to transform wild plants into domesticated crops entirely on their own historical terms. This finding, established through decades of increasingly precise archaeobotanical research, corrected an older and less accurate assumption, common in earlier twentieth-century scholarship, that agriculture in the regions of North America lying north of Mexico was essentially a delayed and diminished echo of Mesoamerican achievement. The Eastern Agricultural Complex shows instead that North America as a whole, understood to include Mexico, contains at least two, and considering squash's complex history discussed next, arguably more than two, independent centers of plant domestication, with the Eastern Woodlands representing an independent center located entirely within the portion of the continent lying north of Mexico.

At the same time, students should not overstate the economic weight the Eastern Agricultural Complex carried in its early centuries. Even after sunflower, marshelder, and chenopod show clear domestication traits, communities across the Eastern Woodlands continued to rely primarily on hunting, fishing, and the collection of wild nuts for the majority of their diet for a very long time afterward, a pattern examined further in Section 13.10.

Section 13.5

Squash and the Complexity of Early Domestication

Squash deserves separate treatment because its domestication history is more complicated, and more historically instructive, than a single origin point would suggest. Squash and pumpkins belong to the genus Cucurbita, which includes several distinct species domesticated independently in different parts of the Americas at different times. This matters for North American history because it means that "squash" is not a single crop with a single point of origin that then spread outward in one direction; it is a category containing multiple, historically distinct domestication events.

Archaeobotanical evidence indicates that at least one lineage of Cucurbita pepo, the species that includes many familiar squash and pumpkin varieties, was domesticated within eastern North America, independent of the separate domestication of related Cucurbita species and varieties in Mesoamerica and other regions of the Americas. Early squash and gourd remains recovered from Eastern Woodlands sites show rind thickness, seed size, and other traits consistent with human management and eventual domestication at a time contemporaneous with, or in some interpretations even preceding, the clearest evidence for sunflower and marshelder domestication described in Section 13.4.

This complexity carries an important lesson for how students should think about domestication generally. It is tempting to imagine domestication as a single dramatic breakthrough, achieved once by one community and then diffused outward like a technology. The squash evidence resists this narrative. Instead, it suggests that human communities in different regions of the Americas, working with related but distinct wild plant populations, arrived at broadly similar outcomes, the deliberate selection and eventual genetic transformation of a useful plant, through separate historical processes shaped by local ecology and local need. Squash domestication should therefore be understood as a family of related but historically distinct events rather than a single origin story.

Practically, early squash and gourds served multiple purposes beyond food. The hard rinds of mature gourds provided containers, and some evidence suggests that early cultivation of gourds and squash-family plants may have been motivated as much by their usefulness as containers, and by their edible seeds, as by the flesh of the fruit itself, which in wild and early domesticated forms was often bitter and less palatable than the varieties familiar today. This observation is a useful caution against assuming that early cultivators had modern culinary preferences in mind; their motivations, examined further in Section 13.9, were shaped by the plant's full range of uses.

Section 13.6

Maize: Origins in Mesoamerica

No crop looms larger in the popular imagination of North American Indigenous agriculture than maize, and no crop has been more persistently, and inaccurately, treated as though its arrival single-handedly created North American farming. This section explains maize's actual origin, while intentionally limiting its scope: the fuller history of the Mesoamerican civilizations that developed alongside and after maize domestication belongs to Chapter 14, and this section addresses only what students need to understand maize as a crop before tracing its spread northward in Section 13.7.

Maize, Zea mays, descends from teosinte, and specifically from lowland Zea mays ssp. parviglumis, a wild grass native to the Balsas River region of southwestern Mexico. Genetic and archaeobotanical research indicates that the domestication process began roughly nine thousand years ago in this region, though, as with the domestications discussed above, dating such a gradual process to a precise year or even a precise century is not possible, and ongoing genetic and archaeological research continues to refine the timeline. Teosinte, in its wild form, looks strikingly different from modern maize: it produces multiple small branching stalks with tiny, hard-cased kernels arranged in a way that would be difficult for humans to harvest or process efficiently. Achieving the recognizable single-stalked plant with a large, exposed cob of soft kernels that characterizes maize today required an extraordinary, sustained, multi-generational program of human selection, targeting genetic changes that affected cob size, kernel exposure, plant architecture, and other traits.

This process of transformation illustrates, in an especially dramatic form, the general point made in Section 13.1: domestication is not a single event but a cumulative genetic transformation extending across many human generations. Maize's transformation from teosinte was more extensive, and its wild ancestor less immediately recognizable as a food plant, than the transformations undergone by the Eastern Agricultural Complex plants described above, which is one reason maize domestication has attracted especially intense scientific interest.

It is important for students to hold two facts together without letting either one distort the other: maize domestication was a Mesoamerican achievement, developed by Indigenous peoples living well to the south of this textbook's primary Volume II geographic focus; and maize's Mesoamerican origin does not diminish, and should not be confused with, the entirely separate history of Eastern Agricultural Complex domestication described in Sections 13.4 and 13.5. Both developments deserve full historical recognition on their own terms.

Section 13.7

Maize Moves Northward

Maize's journey from the Balsas River region of southwestern Mexico into regions of North America far to the north was gradual, uneven, and shaped at every step by the biological challenge of adapting a crop developed for one set of environmental conditions to very different growing seasons, soils, and climates. This section traces that spread in general terms, leaving the detailed regional consequences to Chapters 15 through 23.

Maize reached the Southwest considerably earlier than it reached most of the Eastern Woodlands, consistent with the more direct overland and exchange routes connecting northern Mexico to the arid Southwest. Directly dated maize remains from the Tucson Basin and other Southwestern sites establish secure evidence for maize in the region by roughly 4,100 years ago, an early date made possible by dry cave and rock shelter conditions that preserve organic material unusually well and by direct AMS dating of the maize remains themselves. Even at this early date, maize appears to have been present in small quantities and to have supplemented, rather than replaced, existing plant-gathering and hunting practices for a considerable period before agriculture intensified in the ways examined beginning in Chapter 15.

Maize's spread into the Eastern Woodlands followed a separate, later, and less straightforward trajectory. The evidence here requires particular care, because different lines of evidence point to somewhat different chronologies. Microbotanical evidence, such as maize phytoliths recovered from soil and residue samples, suggests that maize was present in some eastern regions by the late first millennium BCE or the early first millennium CE, considerably earlier than securely dated macrobotanical remains, meaning actual charred kernels or cob fragments, become common in the same regions. Directly dated macrobotanical maize is well established across much of the interior Eastern Woodlands by the middle of the first millennium CE, while in some areas, including parts of southern New England, secure macrobotanical evidence for maize does not appear until considerably later even though microbotanical evidence suggests an earlier presence. Throughout this long period, wherever it was present, maize remained a minor dietary component, coexisting with the already well-established Eastern Agricultural Complex crops described in Section 13.4 rather than displacing them. Only considerably later, generally after roughly 800 to 1000 CE and continuing into subsequent centuries, does maize become a dominant agricultural staple across much of the Eastern Woodlands and Southeast, a shift closely connected to the population growth and political developments associated with Mississippian societies examined in Chapters 21 and 22.

This gap between maize's initial arrival and its later dietary dominance is one of the most historically important points in this chapter. It demonstrates that the spread of a crop across space and the increase of that crop's importance within a local economy are two separate historical processes, governed by different factors. A community might grow small amounts of maize for many generations, using it as one plant food among many, long before local conditions, whether social, agronomic, or a combination of the two, favored a shift toward maize as a primary staple. Historians and archaeologists must therefore be careful to distinguish the date maize first appears at a site, whether that evidence is microbotanical or macrobotanical, from the date maize became agriculturally dominant there, since conflating the two produces a misleadingly compressed and premature account of agricultural transformation.

Adapting maize to more northern and higher-elevation environments also required biological change in the plant itself. Varieties suited to the long, warm growing season of southwestern Mexico were not automatically suited to the shorter, cooler growing seasons of much of North America. Over generations, cultivators in different regions selected maize varieties that matured more quickly and tolerated cooler conditions, a process of ongoing agricultural adaptation that continued for centuries after maize's initial arrival in any given region. This same process of regional adaptation also allowed maize to reach the margins of its climatic range: as later sections of this chapter and Chapter 31 discuss, Fremont farmers in portions of the eastern Great Basin grew maize under short-season, marginal conditions from roughly the eighth through the thirteenth centuries CE, demonstrating how far selection for cold tolerance and rapid maturation ultimately carried the crop.

Section 13.8

Beans, Squash, and Changing Crop Systems

Modern popular understanding of Indigenous North American agriculture is often organized around the "Three Sisters," the interplanting of maize, beans, and squash in a complementary system in which maize provides a structure for climbing bean vines, beans fix nitrogen in the soil benefiting the other plants, and squash's broad leaves shade the ground, suppressing weeds and retaining soil moisture. This system, well documented in numerous Indigenous communities in more recent centuries, represents a genuine and sophisticated agricultural achievement. It should not, however, be projected backward as though it existed as a complete, integrated system from the earliest moments of cultivation.

As Sections 13.4 through 13.7 have shown, maize, beans, and squash have separate domestication histories and separate, staggered chronologies of arrival and adoption in any given North American region. Squash, or at least one lineage of it, was cultivated in eastern North America before maize became agriculturally significant there. Beans, domesticated in Mesoamerica and, in the case of some bean species, in South America as well, generally appear in the archaeological record of regions north of Mexico considerably later than maize. Direct AMS dating of common bean remains across the northern Eastern Woodlands places the crop's secure appearance mainly in the thirteenth century CE, with intercropped maize-beans-squash field systems not securely documented in much of the Northeast before roughly 1300 CE, later than the earlier phases of intensified maize agriculture in the same regions. This means that for a substantial period, communities that grew maize did so without beans as part of the same field system, relying instead on other crops, including the Eastern Agricultural Complex plants, or on other means of maintaining soil fertility and dietary balance.

The gradual assembly of the maize-beans-squash combination into a single interplanted system therefore represents an agricultural innovation with its own history, developed and refined over generations by farmers who observed how these particular plants grew well together, rather than a package that arrived, fully formed, from a single point of origin. By the time European observers began documenting Indigenous agricultural practices in detail, beginning in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries and examined in Volume IV, Three Sisters interplanting was well established as a sophisticated, ecologically effective system in many, though by no means all, agricultural communities across eastern North America and parts of the Southwest. This textbook presents that system as the product of a long developmental history rather than as a static, timeless practice.

Section 13.9

Why Adopt Cultivation?

Having established what was domesticated, when, and where, this chapter now turns to a harder historical question: why did communities begin cultivating plants at all, given that many of them already possessed successful, well-adapted subsistence economies built on hunting, fishing, and gathering? Historians and archaeologists have proposed several explanations, and this section presents them as a set of factors that likely operated in combination, and with different relative importance in different regions, rather than as competing theories with a single correct answer.

Population Pressure

One long-standing explanation holds that growing populations placed pressure on existing wild food resources, making cultivation an increasingly attractive way to increase food supply from a given area of land. This explanation retains some evidentiary support in certain regions and periods, but it cannot be treated as a universal cause, since some of the earliest and clearest evidence for plant domestication in the Eastern Woodlands comes from a period without strong independent evidence of dramatic regional population pressure, and some regions with substantial populations, such as parts of California, never adopted intensive agriculture at all.

Risk Management and Storage

Cultivated plants, particularly those that produce storable seeds, can provide a more predictable and bufferable food source than wild resources whose availability may fluctuate unpredictably from year to year due to weather, animal population cycles, or other factors outside human control. For communities already skilled in storage, a skill Chapter 7 describes as well developed among Archaic societies of the Eastern Woodlands, adding cultivated seed crops to an existing storage-oriented economy could reduce the risk of seasonal or annual food shortages without requiring wholesale economic transformation.

Scheduling and Labor Organization

Cultivating a small number of plants near a residential base could allow a community to schedule labor more efficiently across the year, filling gaps between the availability of major wild resources such as fish runs, nut harvests, or seasonal game concentrations. Rather than replacing other subsistence activities, early cultivation may often have functioned as a way of using labor that could be scheduled around other seasonal subsistence demands more effectively, filling otherwise slower periods of the annual round.

Social and Ceremonial Motivations

Some archaeologists have proposed that certain early cultivated plants, particularly those useful for ceremonial feasting, gift exchange, or the reinforcement of social relationships, may have been valued initially for social rather than purely nutritional reasons. A plant that ripens reliably and can be produced in a controlled quantity may be especially useful for a community that wants to guarantee food or goods for a specific ceremonial occasion, independent of that plant's overall caloric contribution to the diet.

Environmental Change and Existing Knowledge

Changing environmental conditions, including the long process of post-glacial climate stabilization described in Chapter 6, could alter the relative availability of different wild resources in ways that made certain plants more attractive candidates for cultivation. At the same time, communities did not cultivate plants at random; they built on generations of accumulated ecological knowledge about which plants responded well to disturbance, transplanting, or selective harvesting, knowledge inherited directly from the intensive plant-management practices Chapter 7 attributes to Archaic societies.

No single explanation among these fully accounts for agricultural origins across the whole of North America, and this chapter deliberately avoids reducing the process to one dominant cause. Current scholarship has not converged on a single mechanism even for the Eastern Agricultural Complex specifically, with researchers continuing to weigh foraging efficiency, demographic change, risk management, and niche construction differently. One plausible pattern in several early contexts is that cultivation began as a modest supplement to an already successful, broad-spectrum subsistence system, but the causes and relative costs of adopting cultivation varied substantially by plant, by place, and by period, and this textbook presents that variability as a genuine, unresolved feature of the evidence rather than a problem to be explained away.

Historical Debate

Why Did People Begin Cultivating Plants?

The question: What combination of factors best explains why communities across North America began cultivating native and, later, introduced plants, given that many already possessed successful subsistence economies that did not require cultivation?

Interpretation A — Demographic and ecological pressure: Some researchers emphasize population growth and resulting pressure on wild resources as a central driver, arguing that cultivation offered a way to extract more food from a limited area as population density increased in certain regions.
Interpretation B — Risk management and storage economics: Other researchers emphasize the value of cultivated, storable seed crops as a buffer against the unpredictability of wild resource availability, arguing that communities already oriented toward storage-based economies were especially well positioned to add cultivated plants to that strategy.
Interpretation C — Social competition, feasting, and ceremony: Still other researchers emphasize the social and ceremonial value of certain early cultivated plants, arguing that the ability to reliably produce a surplus for feasting, gift-giving, or ceremonial use may have motivated early cultivation independent of, or alongside, nutritional considerations.
Interpretation D — Niche construction and accumulated ecological knowledge: A more recent body of scholarship emphasizes that humans had already been reshaping their local environments, through burning, transplanting, and selective harvesting, for millennia before formal domestication, suggesting that domestication emerged gradually and almost imperceptibly out of existing land-management practices rather than as a deliberate new invention.

Evidence: All four interpretations draw on the same archaeobotanical and settlement evidence described in Sections 13.3 through 13.5, but they weigh demographic, economic, social, and ecological factors differently, and the relative importance of each factor may well have varied by region and by specific plant.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement here concerns relative weighting and mechanism rather than a simple factual dispute; most researchers today accept that more than one of these factors operated, but they differ on which factors were primary in particular cases.

Why it matters: The explanation historians favor shapes how they interpret the pace and character of agricultural adoption elsewhere in the continent, examined in the regional chapters that follow. A population-pressure explanation predicts agriculture appearing where population density was highest; a risk-management explanation predicts agriculture appearing where wild food supply was least predictable; a social-ceremonial explanation predicts agriculture appearing in connection with communities already engaged in the kind of long-distance exchange and ceremonial practice described in Chapter 12.

Student task: Using the evidence presented in Sections 13.4 through 13.9, evaluate which interpretation, or combination of interpretations, best explains the Eastern Agricultural Complex specifically, and explain what additional evidence would help resolve remaining uncertainty.

Section 13.10

Farming Without Abandoning Foraging

This section addresses directly what may be the single most important corrective this chapter offers: the adoption of cultivated plants, even domesticated ones, did not require, and in the great majority of documented North American cases did not produce, the abandonment of hunting, fishing, and gathering. "Farmer" and "hunter-gatherer" should not be treated as mutually exclusive categories describing entirely separate kinds of people; across most of the period this chapter examines, they describe overlapping and often simultaneous activities carried out by the same communities, households, and, frequently, the same individuals.

The archaeological evidence supporting this point is substantial. At Eastern Woodlands sites where sunflower, marshelder, and chenopod show clear domestication traits, the surrounding faunal and botanical assemblages continue to show heavy reliance on deer, fish, and wild nuts for a large share of overall caloric intake, often for many centuries after the earliest evidence of plant domestication. In the Southwest, communities that grew maize in small quantities continued to rely substantially on hunting and on the collection of wild plants such as agave, mesquite pods, and various seeds, a pattern that persisted, in varying degrees, well into the periods examined in Chapters 15 through 18. Even after agriculture intensified significantly in particular regions, as it eventually did in much of the Eastern Woodlands and Southeast by the second half of the first millennium CE, hunting and fishing typically remained economically and culturally important rather than disappearing.

This pattern makes sense once cultivation is understood, as Section 13.9 described it, as a supplement to an existing subsistence system rather than a wholesale replacement for it. A community that could reliably obtain fish in spring, deer year-round, and nuts in autumn had little reason to abandon those proven resources simply because it had also begun cultivating sunflower or maize. Cultivated plants offered advantages, greater storability, more predictable scheduling, and, in some interpretations, social or ceremonial value, without requiring communities to give up subsistence strategies that continued to work well.

Recognizing this pattern also affects how students should interpret settlement evidence. Increased sedentism, meaning longer-term or more permanent settlement in one location, sometimes accompanied agricultural intensification, but sedentism and agriculture are not identical, and neither automatically causes the other. Some highly productive wild-resource economies, such as those built around salmon fishing on the Northwest Coast or acorn processing in parts of California, examined in Chapters 9 and 10, supported substantial sedentism without any field agriculture at all. Conversely, some agricultural communities retained significant seasonal mobility for hunting or other purposes even after cultivation became economically important. This chapter therefore treats sedentism, population growth, and agricultural intensity as related but analytically separate historical variables, each requiring its own evidence rather than being assumed from the presence of any one of the others.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Cultivation Did Not End Hunting and Gathering

What Changed

  • Communities in the Eastern Woodlands began deliberately selecting and, over generations, genetically transforming sunflower, marshelder, chenopod, and a lineage of squash
  • Maize, domesticated in Mesoamerica, gradually spread northward and, in some regions and periods, eventually became a dominant dietary staple
  • In some regions, cultivation eventually supported greater sedentism, denser populations, and, in the case of societies examined later in this volume, more elaborate political and ceremonial structures
  • Field preparation, planting, tending, and harvesting became part of the annual labor calendar for many communities, alongside existing seasonal rounds of hunting, fishing, and gathering

What Stayed the Same

  • Hunting of deer and other game remained economically and culturally significant across nearly every region examined in this chapter, for centuries after the earliest cultivation evidence
  • Fishing, shellfish collection, and the harvesting of wild nuts and seeds continued as major, often dominant, sources of food in agricultural and non-agricultural communities alike
  • Many regions of North America, including California, most of the Great Basin, the Northwest Coast, the Plateau, and the Arctic and Subarctic, never adopted field agriculture at all and continued to build sophisticated economies on intensive wild-resource management, although Fremont communities in portions of the eastern Great Basin practiced small-scale maize farming
  • Ecological knowledge developed during the Archaic period, described in Chapter 7, continued to underpin both cultivation and continued foraging

Why the pattern matters: Treating agriculture as a total replacement for hunting and gathering misrepresents the actual historical record and obscures the flexible, additive way most North American communities actually incorporated new food sources into existing, successful ways of life.

Section 13.11

Regional Agricultural Pathways

Having examined specific plants and general explanatory factors, this section draws together a comparative overview of how agricultural adoption varied by region, without narrating in advance the detailed regional histories that Chapters 14 through 23 and Chapter 29 will provide. The purpose here is to establish that timing, crop combinations, and social consequences differed substantially from one region to another, so that students approach those later chapters already understanding agriculture as a regionally variable process rather than a single continental story.

The Eastern Woodlands

The Eastern Woodlands hosted the earliest well-documented plant domestication events among the regions of North America lying north of Mexico, through the Eastern Agricultural Complex, followed much later by the gradual arrival and eventual dominance of maize, and later still by the incorporation of beans into an increasingly integrated field system. This region illustrates a pattern of layered agricultural development, in which older native crops persisted alongside, rather than being immediately displaced by, newer arrivals, examined further beginning in Chapter 19.

The Southwest

The Southwest received maize relatively early, given its geographic proximity to Mesoamerica and northern Mexico, but the transformation of Southwestern communities into densely settled, agriculturally intensive village societies took many centuries and depended heavily on region-specific developments such as irrigation engineering, examined in Chapter 17 for the Hohokam, and distinctive architectural and social adaptations examined in Chapters 16 and 18 for the Mogollon and Ancestral Pueblo traditions.

The Southeast

The Southeast shares much of its early agricultural history with the broader Eastern Woodlands but developed its own distinctive combination of native and introduced crops, and, in later centuries, contributed to and drew from the Mississippian agricultural economy examined in Chapters 21 through 23.

The Plains

The Plains developed a regionally distinctive pattern in which village-based maize, bean, and squash agriculture along river valleys coexisted with, and was combined with, seasonal bison hunting, a combination examined in Chapter 29 as it existed before the equestrian transformation of the eighteenth century.

Regions Largely Without Field Agriculture

Finally, substantial regions of North America, including California, examined in Chapter 9; the Northwest Coast, examined in Chapter 10; most of the Great Basin and Plateau, examined in Chapter 8 and Chapter 31; and the Arctic and Subarctic, examined in Chapter 11, developed sophisticated, often densely populated economies without adopting field agriculture at all. These regions are not exceptions requiring special explanation for a supposed failure to progress; they represent successful, sustained subsistence strategies, built on salmon, acorns, marine mammals, camas, wild rice, and other resources, that did not require field crops to support substantial populations and complex societies. The Great Basin illustrates why "largely" rather than "entirely" is the more accurate qualifier: Fremont communities in portions of the eastern Great Basin practiced small-scale maize farming from roughly the eighth through the thirteenth centuries CE, at the marginal, short-season edge of maize's climatic range, while many neighboring Great Basin communities continued economies built entirely on hunting and the intensive collection of wild seeds and other plant foods. This regional variation within the Great Basin itself, examined further in Chapter 31, reinforces the chapter's broader argument that agricultural adoption was a localized historical choice rather than a fixed regional destiny.

Taken together, these regional pathways demonstrate that agriculture's history in North America cannot be reduced to a single timeline or a single causal story. Each region's experience reflected its own environment, its own inherited subsistence traditions, and its own historical circumstances, a pattern this textbook will continue to trace in increasing regional detail across the chapters that follow.

Section 13.12

Agriculture and the Reorganization of Regional Worlds

This concluding section asks what cultivation could change within a community's life, while insisting, as the chapter has throughout, that none of these changes occurred automatically, uniformly, or everywhere. Where agriculture did intensify significantly, it could affect settlement duration, encouraging longer or more permanent occupation of favorable agricultural locations; food storage, as surplus cultivated grain provided a more concentrated and storable food source than many wild resources; labor calendars, as planting, tending, and harvesting created new demands on household and community labor at specific times of year; land tenure, as investment in cleared and improved fields could create new relationships between specific households or lineages and specific pieces of land; population density, as more reliable and storable food supplies could, though did not automatically, support larger and more concentrated populations; and ceremonial life, as agricultural cycles of planting and harvest frequently became occasions for ritual and communal observance, connections examined further in later chapters covering specific agricultural societies.

None of these potential consequences followed automatically or universally from the adoption of cultivated plants. As this chapter has emphasized throughout, many communities cultivated plants for centuries without these changes occurring at all, or occurring only partially. The relationship between agriculture and social transformation is a historical question to be investigated region by region and period by period, not a formula to be assumed. Chapters 15 through 18 will examine how agriculture, combined with irrigation and other regional adaptations, contributed to increasingly settled village life in the Southwest. Chapters 19 through 23 will examine how intensifying agriculture in the East contributed, over many centuries and in combination with many other factors, to the rise of increasingly large and politically complex mound-building societies, culminating in the urban center of Cahokia examined in Chapter 22. In each case, agriculture will appear as one important factor among several, entering already dynamic regional worlds rather than creating those worlds from nothing.

This chapter closes, then, not with a single triumphant narrative of agricultural progress, but with an invitation to read the chapters that follow attentively for the specific, regionally variable ways that cultivated plants reshaped, or in many cases only modestly supplemented, the lives of the diverse peoples who grew them, tended them, stored them, and, in the fullness of time, built entire agricultural traditions and food systems that remain significant to their descendants today.

Primary Source Window

The Riverton Site, Illinois, c. 3800 BP

Type of evidence: Charred plant remains and faunal remains recovered through flotation and excavation from a Late Archaic habitation site in the lower Wabash River valley, Illinois · Method: Water flotation of excavated soil to recover carbonized plant remains, morphological analysis to identify domesticated versus wild specimens, and comparative analysis of faunal remains and nondomesticated plant foods recovered from the same contexts

Context: The Riverton site, dated to roughly 3,800 years before the present, is one of the most thoroughly documented Late Archaic sites used in the scholarly literature on the Eastern Agricultural Complex. Its excavated plant assemblage contains identifiable remains of at least three domesticated annual seed plants alongside abundant nutshell, other nondomesticated plant remains, and the bones of terrestrial and riparian animals, making it a genuine, published example of the kind of mixed subsistence economy this chapter describes, rather than a hypothetical composite.

The Riverton assemblage includes domesticated seed remains identified among the plant assemblage, alongside a much larger quantity of hickory nutshell and other nut remains, additional nondomesticated plant taxa, and faunal remains from deer, fish, and other terrestrial and riverine animals, reflecting a broad-based subsistence economy of which early domesticated seed crops formed only one part.

Analysis: Riverton provides strong evidence for what Section 13.10 argues in general terms: domesticated seed crops formed part of a broad-spectrum subsistence system that also included substantial use of nuts, other wild plants, and hunted and fished animals. The presence of identifiable domesticated seeds at the site provides direct evidence that genetic transformation, not merely occasional use, had already occurred in at least some plant populations by this date; the surrounding assemblage of nutshell and faunal remains supports the interpretation that domestication had not displaced the community's broader, long-established subsistence base.

Limitations: Published counts of plant and faunal remains from Riverton, like those from any archaeobotanical site, reflect a combination of original dietary importance, differential preservation, and recovery method, and cannot be converted directly and precisely into a percentage of ancient diet. Riverton is a single, well-studied site rather than a representative sample of every Late Archaic community in the Eastern Woodlands, and its specific findings should be generalized to the wider region with appropriate caution, corroborated by comparison with other sites in the archaeobotanical literature.

Multiple Perspectives

Living Through the Adoption of Crops

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns established through archaeological and comparative ethnographic research. They are not quotations from any named individual.

From the perspective of a household already skilled in nut and seed processing: A new cultivated seed crop looks less like a revolutionary technology and more like an additional plant to fit into an already demanding annual schedule of gathering, processing, and storing food, worth adopting only if it clearly earns its place alongside proven resources.
From the perspective of a community relying heavily on a highly productive wild resource, such as salmon or acorns: Field cultivation may offer little practical advantage when an existing wild resource already provides abundant, storable food with well-established processing and storage methods, helping explain why some regions never adopted agriculture at all.
From the perspective of a community managing risk in an environment with unpredictable wild-food availability: A storable, humanly controlled seed crop offers a valuable buffer against years when wild resources fail, making the labor of cultivation worthwhile even if the crop supplies only a modest share of total food.
From the perspective of an archaeobotanist working today: Distinguishing early cultivation from full domestication, and distinguishing a crop's first appearance from its later economic importance, requires painstaking laboratory analysis of tiny, often fragmentary seed remains, work that continues to revise scholarly understanding of when and why agriculture began.
Cause and Effect

The Consequences of Cultivation

Long-term causes

Generations of accumulated ecological knowledge from intensive Archaic-period plant management, described in Chapter 7, provided the skills and attentiveness necessary to notice and select useful plant variation over time.

Short-term causes

Specific local factors, such as the desirability of a storable seed crop, a need for reliable ceremonial surplus, or the observed usefulness of a particular wild plant, could tip a community toward sustained cultivation of that plant in a given period.

Effect

Where cultivation intensified over time, communities could experience greater sedentism, denser populations, new patterns of land association, and new labor calendars, though none of these outcomes occurred automatically or everywhere.

This textbook's response

Later chapters trace these potential consequences region by region and period by period, treating agriculture as one variable among several rather than as a single sufficient explanation for settlement, population, or political change.

Historical complexity: The same crop could produce very different social consequences in different regions: maize supported small, dispersed farmsteads in some contexts and dense, politically complex towns in others, a variation explored fully in the regional chapters that follow.

Everyday Life and Social History

Fields, Households, and the Seasonal Round

Behind the botanical evidence discussed throughout this chapter lie ordinary households organizing their labor across the year. Planting, weeding, and harvesting cultivated plants had to be scheduled alongside fishing seasons, nut-gathering periods, hunting expeditions, and the many other tasks that sustained a household: preparing hides, making tools and containers, cooking and processing food for storage, and caring for children and elders. The archaeological evidence for early cultivation, small seeds recovered through painstaking flotation of soil samples, represents the physical trace of decisions made by individual households about how to allocate their limited time and labor most effectively across an increasingly varied set of food sources.

Gender and age likely shaped who performed particular agricultural tasks, though the archaeological evidence for this division of labor in the earliest cultivation period is limited, and this textbook is careful not to project patterns documented much later, through direct European observation beginning in Volume IV, backward onto communities separated from those later accounts by many centuries. What can be said with confidence is that early cultivation was woven into household economies already organized around a demanding, skill-intensive seasonal round, and that adding cultivated plants to that round represented an incremental adjustment to existing labor patterns rather than the creation of an entirely new way of life.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced the origins and spread of agriculture across North America as a set of independent, regionally variable historical processes rather than a single continental transition. It began by distinguishing cultivation, the deliberate tending of a plant, from domestication, the genetic and morphological transformation of a plant population produced by sustained human selection, a distinction essential to understanding the evidence presented throughout the chapter.

The chapter has shown that agriculture did not begin with maize. Communities in the Eastern Woodlands independently domesticated sunflower, marshelder, chenopod, erect knotweed, and at least one lineage of squash, forming what archaeologists call the Eastern Agricultural Complex, a domestication tradition built entirely on native North American plants. Maize itself was domesticated in Mexico several millennia before the Eastern Agricultural Complex began, but the Eastern Agricultural Complex developed on a timeline substantially earlier than maize became agriculturally important in the Eastern Woodlands, the region with which North American Indigenous farming is most commonly associated in popular imagination.

Maize itself was examined as a Mesoamerican achievement, domesticated from wild teosinte over thousands of years before gradually spreading northward into the Southwest and, later, the Eastern Woodlands. The chapter emphasized that maize's arrival at a site and its later dominance as a dietary staple represent two distinct historical developments, often separated by many centuries, a distinction essential for accurately dating agricultural transformation in any given region.

The chapter also examined why communities began cultivating plants at all, presenting population pressure, risk management and storage, labor scheduling, social and ceremonial motivation, and accumulated ecological knowledge as complementary rather than competing explanations, whose relative importance likely varied by region and by specific plant.

A central argument of this chapter has been that cultivation, even where it led to genuine domestication, did not require or typically produce the abandonment of hunting, fishing, and gathering. Communities across North America incorporated cultivated plants into already successful, broad-based subsistence economies, often for centuries, without cultivated plants becoming dietarily dominant, and many regions, including California, most of the Great Basin, the Northwest Coast, the Plateau, and the Arctic and Subarctic, never adopted field agriculture at all while continuing to build sophisticated, populous societies on intensive wild-resource management, though Fremont farmers in portions of the eastern Great Basin were a notable regional exception.

The chapter surveyed regional agricultural pathways in the Eastern Woodlands, the Southwest, the Southeast, and the Plains, establishing that timing, crop combinations, and social consequences varied considerably from one region to another, without narrating in advance the detailed regional histories that later chapters will provide.

Finally, the chapter examined the potential, but never automatic or universal, consequences of agricultural intensification: changes in settlement duration, storage, labor organization, land tenure, population density, and ceremonial life. These consequences will be traced in specific regional detail beginning in Chapter 14, which turns to Mesoamerica in its own right, and continuing through Chapters 15 through 23, which follow agriculture into the Southwest and the mound-building societies of the East.

These foundations, an understanding of what domestication is, how it is identified, why it occurred, and how selectively and unevenly it spread, equip students to read the following chapters as an account of divergent historical choices and adaptations rather than as a single, inevitable march from foraging to farming.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Cultivation
Human intervention in a plant's growth, such as planting, weeding, or protecting it, without necessarily implying genetic change in the plant.
Domestication
A genetic and morphological change in a plant population produced by sustained human selection over many generations, typically making the plant more useful to humans and less able to survive without human assistance.
Horticulture
Small-scale, garden-style cultivation, often combined with other subsistence activities such as hunting, fishing, and gathering.
Agriculture
More intensive, larger-scale cultivation, often involving field preparation and a greater share of a community's diet and labor.
Subsistence intensification
Any process by which a community increases the amount or reliability of food obtained from a given area of land or unit of labor, including but not limited to cultivation.
Seed morphology
The physical structure and appearance of a seed, used by archaeobotanists to distinguish wild from domesticated plant populations.
Rind thickness
A physical trait of squash and gourds used to help distinguish wild from domesticated Cucurbita specimens.
Phytolith
A microscopic silica structure formed within many plants that survives long after the rest of the plant decays, used as archaeological evidence of plant presence and use.
Starch grain
A microscopic plant residue that can adhere to tools, vessels, or teeth, used as evidence of plant processing and consumption.
Direct AMS radiocarbon dating
Accelerator mass spectrometry radiocarbon dating applied directly to a plant specimen itself rather than to surrounding soil or charcoal, improving chronological accuracy.
Eastern Agricultural Complex
A group of native North American plants, including sunflower, marshelder, chenopod, and a distinct squash lineage, independently domesticated in the Eastern Woodlands.
Sunflower (Helianthus annuus)
A native North American plant domesticated in the Eastern Woodlands for its edible, oil-rich seeds.
Marshelder / sumpweed (Iva annua)
A native North American plant domesticated for its oily, protein-rich seeds; part of the Eastern Agricultural Complex, no longer widely cultivated.
Chenopod (Chenopodium berlandieri)
A North American relative of quinoa domesticated in the Eastern Woodlands for its edible seeds.
Cucurbita / squash
A genus including several species domesticated independently in different regions of the Americas, including at least one North American lineage.
Teosinte
The wild grass ancestor of maize, native to Mesoamerica.
Maize (Zea mays)
A domesticated grain developed from teosinte in Mesoamerica, which spread gradually and unevenly into North America.
Three Sisters
An interplanting system combining maize, beans, and squash, developed gradually over centuries rather than arriving as a single, ready-made package.
Archaeobotany
The scientific study of plant remains recovered from archaeological sites.
Flotation
An archaeological recovery method using water to separate lightweight carbonized plant remains from excavated soil.
Risk management (subsistence)
Strategies, including cultivation and storage, that reduce a community's vulnerability to unpredictable fluctuations in food supply.
Niche construction
The concept that humans actively reshape their environments over time, gradually creating conditions favorable to domestication through practices such as burning, transplanting, and selective harvesting.
Broad-spectrum foraging
A subsistence strategy drawing on a wide range of plant and animal resources rather than specializing narrowly.
Mixed subsistence economy
An economy combining cultivated plants with hunting, fishing, and gathering, characteristic of most North American communities examined in this chapter.
Sedentism
Long-term or permanent settlement in one location, related to but not automatically caused by agricultural adoption.
Storage economy
A subsistence system organized around the storage of food, whether wild or cultivated, to buffer seasonal or annual shortages.
Regional pathway
A region-specific pattern of agricultural timing, crop combination, and social consequence, distinct from patterns in other regions.
Domestication syndrome
The characteristic suite of morphological changes, such as larger seeds and thinner seed coats, that distinguish domesticated plants from their wild ancestors.
Fremont (agricultural context)
Communities of the eastern Great Basin who practiced small-scale maize farming at the margins of the crop's climatic range from roughly the eighth through the thirteenth centuries CE, illustrating regional variation within the Great Basin, examined further in Chapter 31.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has replaced a familiar but misleading story, agriculture as a single revolutionary transition triggered by the arrival of maize, with a more accurate and, in the end, more historically interesting account: independent domestication in more than one North American region, a gradual and uneven northward spread of Mesoamerican crops, and centuries of coexistence between cultivated plants and the older hunting, fishing, and gathering practices that had sustained Indigenous communities for thousands of years before any plant was ever domesticated.

This account matters because it restores historical agency to communities across the Eastern Woodlands who transformed sunflower, marshelder, chenopod, and squash into domesticated crops entirely through their own sustained observation and selection, without needing an external stimulus to innovate. It also matters because it prevents students from assuming that agriculture automatically produces sedentism, population growth, or social complexity, an assumption that would badly mislead readers approaching the regional chapters that follow, in which agriculture appears as one factor among several, weighted differently in the Southwest, the Eastern Woodlands, the Southeast, and the Plains, and largely, though not entirely, absent from the successful, sophisticated societies of California, the Northwest Coast, the Great Basin and Plateau, and the Arctic and Subarctic.

The methodological lessons of this chapter, that seeds can be dated directly, that morphology can reveal genetic change invisible to the naked eye, and that a plant's first appearance at a site is a different historical fact from its later dietary importance, will recur throughout this textbook's treatment of ancient North America. Students who understand these distinctions are prepared to read Chapter 14's account of Mesoamerican civilization, and the detailed regional agricultural histories of Chapters 15 through 23, with the same careful attention to evidence and to regional variation that this chapter has modeled.

Above all, this chapter asks students to resist the temptation to see agriculture as a single ladder that every society either climbed or failed to climb. Cultivation entered a continent already full of successful, historically dynamic societies, and it did so on many different regional timelines, with many different combinations of crops, and with many different, never automatic, consequences. Understanding agriculture this way, as a set of divergent historical choices rather than a universal destiny, is essential preparation for the volume that follows.
