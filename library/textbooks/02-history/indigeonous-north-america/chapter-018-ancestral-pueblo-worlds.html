Ancestral Pueblo Worlds

Indigenous Peoples of North America: A Continental History

Chapter Opening

In a shallow, sun-blasted canyon in the San Juan Basin of what is now northwestern New Mexico, communities of farmers spent more than two centuries constructing a set of enormous, multi-story stone buildings unlike anything else built in North America north of Mesoamerica before them. They were not the first people to farm the Colorado Plateau, and their descendants did not disappear. They moved, and they are still here.

Chaco Canyon today is a quiet, arid place, far from any modern town, reached by miles of unpaved road. Standing inside the walls of Pueblo Bonito, a visitor can still see room after room, some three and four stories tall, arranged around a great open plaza, built and rebuilt across roughly two hundred years by people using stone tools, without draft animals, wheeled vehicles, or metal implements. The scale of the construction has drawn outside attention for nearly two centuries, and it continues to raise some of the same questions that occupied the first Euro-American visitors who saw it: who built this, why here, and what happened to the people who did?

This chapter answers those questions carefully, and it begins by rejecting the framework in which they are often asked. Chaco Canyon, Mesa Verde, and the hundreds of smaller villages, hamlets, and farmsteads scattered across the Four Corners region were not built by a vanished or mysterious people. They were built by the ancestors of the contemporary Pueblo nations of New Mexico and Arizona, including Acoma, Zuni, the Hopi villages, and the pueblos along the Rio Grande. The word once used most often in popular writing and older archaeology for these ancestral communities, "Anasazi," is a Navajo-language term that outside scholars borrowed in the nineteenth century; it is not the name these communities used for themselves, and it is not the term most Pueblo people use for their own ancestors. This textbook uses "Ancestral Pueblo," a term generally preferred in current scholarship and by many Pueblo communities themselves, and explains the reasons for that choice directly in Section 18.1.

The Ancestral Pueblo story spans nearly three thousand years of change: from small, increasingly settled groups who began experimenting with maize cultivation as early as the middle centuries of the second millennium BCE, through village-dwelling farmers who built the first true pueblos of contiguous, above-ground rooms in the eighth and ninth centuries CE, to the extraordinary monumental building and long-distance connections centered on Chaco Canyon between roughly 850 and 1150 CE, and finally to the well-known cliff dwellings of Mesa Verde and the sweeping population movements of the thirteenth century that emptied much of the Four Corners region and redistributed its people into the communities their descendants occupy today.

This is also a story about evidence and its limits. No Ancestral Pueblo community left a written archive in the sense familiar to historians of literate societies. What survives is an unusually rich archaeological record: room blocks, kivas, roads, pottery, textiles, trash middens, tree-ring dates of remarkable precision, and, increasingly, the oral traditions, cultural knowledge, and active participation of the descendant Pueblo, Hopi, Zuni, and other Indigenous communities whose own histories connect directly to this landscape. Archaeologists can describe with real confidence how rooms were built, when trees were cut for roof beams, and what foods people ate. They can say much less with confidence about political titles, religious doctrine, or the exact reasons any single family left any single village. This chapter is careful throughout to distinguish what the evidence can establish from what remains inferred, debated, or unknowable in the terms archaeology alone can answer.

Finally, this is a story that must not end at Chaco Canyon's decline or at the depopulation of Mesa Verde in the late thirteenth century, however dramatic those developments were. The people who left the Four Corners region did not vanish. Over the course of several generations, and through more than one migration stream moving in more than one direction, they resettled in the Rio Grande Valley, on the Hopi mesas, and in the Zuni and Acoma country, where their descendants live today as citizens of sovereign Pueblo nations with continuing governments, languages, ceremonial traditions, and historical memory of this landscape. Framing this chapter's ending as disappearance would misstate the historical record and would repeat exactly the error this textbook's opening chapter warned against: treating Indigenous history as ending at the moment it becomes difficult to trace in outside sources.

This chapter builds directly on Chapter 15's account of the origins of Southwestern village agriculture and stands alongside Chapter 16's treatment of Mogollon communities and Chapter 17's treatment of Hohokam irrigation societies in the Sonoran Desert. Together these three chapters describe three related but historically distinct Southwestern trajectories, unified by shared reliance on maize, beans, and squash, extensive exchange, and long, layered histories of movement, but shaped by different environments, architectural traditions, and social patterns. Where Hohokam communities engineered canals to move desert river water across the Sonoran Desert, Ancestral Pueblo communities on the higher, drier, and colder Colorado Plateau depended on rainfall, runoff, and careful water management on a different scale, and built in stone and adobe rather than earthen canals and pit-house villages alone.

This chapter also devotes deliberate attention to worlds beyond Chaco Canyon itself. Chaco was the most architecturally spectacular Ancestral Pueblo center, but it was never the whole of Ancestral Pueblo life, and this chapter treats high-desert farming, household and kiva organization, and the related but distinct Kayenta, Mesa Verde, and Rio Grande regional traditions as subjects deserving sustained treatment in their own right, not merely as backdrop to Chaco.

Historical Context

Chapter 15 traced the broader origins and spread of Southwestern village agriculture, describing how maize, beans, and squash moved north from Mesoamerica over the course of many centuries and gradually became central to the diets and settlement patterns of communities across the Southwest. Chapter 16 examined the Mogollon peoples of the mountainous zone spanning present-day southern New Mexico, southeastern Arizona, and adjoining parts of northern Mexico, and Chapter 17 examined the Hohokam communities of the Sonoran Desert, whose extensive canal systems transformed the Gila and Salt River valleys into productive agricultural landscapes. This chapter turns north and east, to the higher, cooler, and generally drier Colorado Plateau, a region of deep canyons, broad mesas, and river systems including the San Juan, the Chaco Wash, and the tributaries of the Colorado River, where a related but architecturally and historically distinct set of farming communities developed over the following span of roughly three thousand years.

The Colorado Plateau presented farmers with real challenges: a short growing season at higher elevations, unpredictable and often scant rainfall, and, in many areas, no permanent rivers comparable to the Gila or Salt. Communities responded with a range of strategies, including dry farming that depended on capturing and directing seasonal runoff, terracing and check dams on hillsides, careful selection of planting locations near canyon mouths and washes where water concentrated after storms, and, in some periods and places, more substantial engineered water-control features. These strategies developed and changed considerably over time, and this chapter traces that development alongside changes in settlement, architecture, and regional organization.

The chronological framework conventionally used for this region divides its history into a sequence of developmental periods, most commonly labeled Basketmaker II, Basketmaker III, and Pueblo I through Pueblo IV, based on changes in architecture, material culture, and subsistence documented archaeologically. Students should treat these period labels, like the cultural-region and language-family frameworks discussed in Chapter 1, as analytical tools developed by archaeologists rather than categories the ancestral communities themselves would have recognized. The boundaries between periods are gradual and vary somewhat by location; this chapter uses approximate date ranges throughout and flags areas of ongoing chronological refinement.

Section 18.1

Naming a Past: From "Anasazi" to Ancestral Pueblo

Before this chapter can describe the farming communities of the Colorado Plateau, it must address the vocabulary used to name them, because that vocabulary has itself been a source of historical confusion and, more recently, active reconsideration. For much of the twentieth century, English-language archaeology referred to these communities as the "Anasazi," a term archaeologist Richard Wetherill and others popularized after adopting it from Navajo usage in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. The word is often translated loosely as "ancient enemies" or "ancient ones," though its precise meaning and connotation in Navajo is debated among linguists and Navajo speakers themselves. Whatever its exact original meaning, the term was not the name any Ancestral Pueblo community used, or uses, for itself, and many contemporary Pueblo people have objected to a Navajo-origin term, applied by outside archaeologists, being used to describe their own ancestors.

Since the late twentieth century, most professional archaeologists, the National Park Service, and many Pueblo communities have shifted toward "Ancestral Pueblo" or "Ancestral Puebloan" as a more accurate and less contested term, since it directly names the historical relationship this chapter emphasizes throughout: the people who built Chaco Canyon, Mesa Verde, and hundreds of related sites were ancestral to the contemporary Pueblo nations. This textbook follows that convention. Some scholarly and tribal writing continues to use "Anasazi" in specific technical or historical contexts, particularly when quoting or discussing older scholarship, and students encountering the term in outside reading should recognize both its history and its limitations rather than assuming it is simply incorrect or simply acceptable.

Naming carries a second complication addressed in this chapter: no single, unified "Ancestral Pueblo nation" ever existed. As with "Adena," "Hohokam," and other archaeological terms examined elsewhere in this textbook, "Ancestral Pueblo" describes a broad tradition of related architectural, agricultural, and material-culture patterns spread across a large region and many centuries, not one political community, language, or self-identified people. Communities in Chaco Canyon, the Mesa Verde region, the Kayenta area of northeastern Arizona, and elsewhere shared important features, including maize agriculture, pueblo-style architecture, and particular styles of black-on-white pottery, while also maintaining distinct local traditions and, in all likelihood, separate political identities. The contemporary Pueblo nations themselves are linguistically diverse, speaking languages from at least four distinct language families, including Keresan, Tanoan, Zuni, an isolate, and Hopi, a Uto-Aztecan language. This linguistic diversity is important evidence against assuming a single, homogeneous ancestral population, but students should be cautious about drawing firmer conclusions from it than the evidence supports: modern linguistic diversity cannot, by itself, establish the precise language or languages spoken by the communities of Chaco, Mesa Verde, or Kayenta, since population movement, language shift, and the incorporation of different communities into shared settlements during and after the thirteenth-century migrations complicate any simple projection of later linguistic boundaries backward in time.

Historical Vocabulary
Kiva, Great House, and Great Kiva

Kiva refers to a specialized, typically circular, often partly or wholly subterranean room found throughout Ancestral Pueblo architecture, generally understood, on the basis of continuity with practices documented among contemporary Pueblo communities, to have served ceremonial and social functions in addition to any residential use in earlier periods. The word itself comes from Hopi usage and has been adopted broadly by archaeologists; its use for structures many centuries older than direct ethnographic documentation involves an inference of cultural continuity that this textbook treats as reasonable but not certain in every architectural detail.

Great house describes the large, multi-story, room-block structures built with distinctive coursed masonry at Chaco Canyon and, in smaller and more variable forms, at outlying sites across the San Juan Basin. Great houses are distinguished from ordinary residential room blocks by their scale, formal architectural planning, substantial masonry construction, and, frequently, a lower density of everyday household refuse than would be expected if every room were used purely for full-time residence, a pattern examined further in Sections 18.6 and 18.7's discussion of function and interpretation.

Great kiva refers to an unusually large, formally constructed kiva, sometimes exceeding fifty feet in diameter, generally interpreted as a space for gatherings larger than a single household or lineage, and frequently associated with great houses, though also found at some non-great-house sites. This textbook treats great kivas as important evidence for community-scale or even multi-community ceremonial gathering, while cautioning against overly specific claims about the exact rituals performed within them, which archaeology alone cannot establish.

Section 18.2

The Colorado Plateau World

The Colorado Plateau is a high, semi-arid tableland spanning parts of present-day Utah, Colorado, Arizona, and New Mexico, cut by deep canyons, including the drainages of the San Juan, Chaco Wash, Little Colorado, and Colorado Rivers themselves, and broken by isolated mesas, buttes, and mountain ranges that rise well above the surrounding tableland. Elevations across the region most relevant to Ancestral Pueblo settlement generally range from about 5,000 to 8,000 feet, producing a shorter growing season and greater risk of frost than the lower-elevation deserts inhabited by Hohokam communities to the south, discussed in Chapter 17.

Rainfall across the plateau is limited and highly variable from year to year, arriving mainly as winter snow, which contributes to spring runoff, and as intense, localized summer monsoon storms, which can produce sudden flash flooding in canyon washes. Farmers on the plateau therefore could not rely on the kind of large, permanent rivers that supported Hohokam canal irrigation. Instead, communities developed strategies for capturing and directing the water that was available, discussed in fuller detail in Section 18.5, including planting near canyon mouths and side-drainage outlets, building check dams and terraces, and, at Chaco Canyon itself, constructing more substantial engineered water-control features that redirected runoff from the canyon rims toward fields on the canyon floor.

This environment placed real limits on the scale of population any single locality could support in an average year, and it made communities vulnerable to multi-year droughts, which tree-ring records show occurred repeatedly across this history. At the same time, the plateau's landscape offered resources beyond farmland alone: piñon pine nuts, a nutritionally significant wild food in good years; deer, rabbits, and other game; and high-quality building stone, particularly sandstone, that shaped the region's distinctive masonry architecture. Turquoise deposits in parts of the region also became an important resource for craft production and long-distance exchange, discussed further in Section 18.7.

Section 18.3

Basketmaker Beginnings

The earliest phase of this history, conventionally labeled Basketmaker II and dated approximately from 1500 BCE to 500 CE, though these boundaries vary regionally and remain subjects of ongoing refinement, describes communities that combined increasingly intensive maize cultivation with continued heavy reliance on hunting and the gathering of wild plants. Maize itself reached the Colorado Plateau well before the Common Era, consistent with the broader Southwestern agricultural chronology traced in Chapter 15, though it took many centuries more for farming to become the dominant basis of subsistence in most communities. These communities are named for their sophisticated basketry, which survives unusually well in the dry caves and rock shelters of the region, and for the near absence of pottery, which appears more consistently only in the succeeding period. People during this phase often lived in caves, rock shelters, and shallow pit structures rather than substantial above-ground architecture, and many groups likely remained at least seasonally mobile, moving between farming locations and areas richer in wild resources.

The following phase, Basketmaker III, dated approximately 500 to 750 CE, saw significant changes several centuries after farming's initial adoption: the adoption of the bow and arrow, which gradually replaced the atlatl and dart as the primary hunting technology across the region; the addition of beans to the existing maize and squash complex, completing the "Three Sisters" combination examined in Chapter 13; and the construction of more substantial semi-subterranean pithouses, often clustered into small villages of a handful to several dozen structures. Plain and later painted pottery also appears widely during this period, marking an important shift in material culture and, plausibly, in patterns of food storage, cooking, and exchange.

Archaeologists infer from this evidence a gradual shift toward greater sedentism, deeper investment in particular locations, and increasing reliance on cultivated foods relative to wild resources, though the pace and completeness of this shift varied by region and, very likely, by household. This textbook cautions against describing Basketmaker communities as uniformly or suddenly transformed into full-time farmers; considerable variation and continued use of wild foods persisted well into later periods, and archaeological evidence for this transition should be read as describing general trends across many centuries rather than a single uniform event experienced identically everywhere.

Section 18.4

Pueblo I: Villages and New Forms

The Pueblo I period, dated approximately 750 to 900 CE, marks the architectural shift that gives this entire tradition its name: the construction of contiguous, above-ground rooms built of jacal, a wattle-and-daub technique using a framework of poles and brush covered with mud plaster, and, increasingly, of masonry. These room blocks were typically arranged in a curved or linear row, often facing south or east, with an associated open area and, frequently, one or more pit structures retained nearby, structures that appear to have taken on an increasingly specialized, likely ceremonial function even as everyday habitation moved into the new above-ground rooms. This transitional pit structure is generally regarded by archaeologists as an important precursor to the kiva of later periods.

Population during Pueblo I appears to have grown and, in places, aggregated into larger villages than had been typical during Basketmaker III, with some sites containing scores of households. This aggregation was not permanent or universal; archaeological evidence from several well-studied Pueblo I villages, including sites in the Dolores River valley of southwestern Colorado, indicates cycles of village growth followed by dispersal, suggesting that large village life during this period may have been experimental, difficult to sustain, or responsive to specific short-term conditions rather than an immediate, one-way trend toward permanent large settlements.

By the end of Pueblo I, the basic architectural and social elements that would characterize the following centuries were established: above-ground masonry and jacal room blocks for daily life and storage, semi-subterranean kivas for ceremonial and communal use, small-scale dry farming supplemented by wild resources, and a settlement pattern combining dispersed farmsteads with occasional larger village aggregations. Section 18.6 examines how, beginning in the following period, some communities in the San Juan Basin began building on a dramatically larger scale.

Section 18.5

High-Desert Farming and Household and Kiva Organization

Before turning to Chaco Canyon's monumental architecture, this chapter pauses to examine, in more sustained detail, how ordinary Ancestral Pueblo households farmed a difficult landscape and organized their daily and ceremonial lives, since these patterns supported every period discussed in this chapter and did not stop developing once monumental construction began elsewhere.

Farming strategies on the plateau depended heavily on local topography. Households and communities favored locations near the mouths of side canyons and washes, where runoff from higher ground concentrated after storms and could be directed onto fields through simple diversion features. In some areas, low stone or earthen check dams slowed runoff across hillside terraces, reducing erosion and allowing moisture to soak into planting areas rather than running off entirely. At Chaco Canyon itself, archaeologists have documented more substantial engineered features, including diversion dams along the canyon rim and canals directing water toward fields on the canyon floor, evidence that some communities invested considerable labor in water management well beyond simple dry farming. Even with these strategies, yields remained sensitive to annual rainfall, and the tree-ring record examined throughout this chapter shows that multi-year droughts posed a recurring and serious risk.

Households themselves were typically organized around an extended family or lineage occupying a cluster of rooms within a larger room block, sharing storage space, work areas, and, in many cases, an associated kiva. Archaeological evidence, combined cautiously with patterns documented among historically observed Pueblo communities, suggests that descent and household organization in at least some communities followed matrilineal lines, in which kinship and certain rights pass through the mother's line, a pattern well documented among several contemporary Pueblo nations, though this textbook treats the deep-time applicability of this pattern as a reasonable inference rather than a certainty archaeology alone can establish for every period and place.

The kiva functioned as more than a purely religious space in the narrow sense. Alongside ceremonial use, kivas plausibly served as settings for practical community decision-making, teaching, craft production during colder months, and the maintenance of social relationships extending beyond any single household. Great kivas, examined further in Section 18.6, extended this function to a larger, likely multi-household or multi-community scale. This textbook resists two temptations common in earlier popular writing about the Ancestral Pueblo past: reducing daily life to mere backdrop for monumental architecture, and assuming without evidence that household and kinship patterns documented among later, historically observed Pueblo communities applied without change across many centuries of earlier history. Continuity is plausible and, in some respects, well supported; it should not be assumed automatically in every detail.

Section 18.6

Chaco Canyon and the Great Houses

Beginning in the ninth century and accelerating through the tenth, eleventh, and early twelfth centuries, communities in and around Chaco Canyon began constructing a series of great houses on a scale unmatched elsewhere in the Ancestral Pueblo world. Pueblo Bonito, the largest, eventually contained several hundred interconnected rooms arranged in a D-shaped plan around a central plaza, rising three and four stories in places, with walls built in a distinctive coursed masonry technique using a rubble core faced with carefully shaped sandstone veneer stones. Other major great houses within the canyon, including Chetro Ketl, Pueblo del Arroyo, and several others, display similar formal planning, large scale, and multiple great kivas.

Precise dendrochronological dating, made possible by the exceptional preservation of wooden roof beams in the dry canyon environment, allows archaeologists to date individual construction episodes at Chaco with a level of chronological precision rare anywhere in the pre-contact Americas, often to within a specific year or narrow range of years. This evidence shows that great house construction at Chaco was not a single building project but an extended, episodic process, with major construction phases occurring across roughly two centuries, interspersed with periods of more modest building activity and, eventually, a marked decline in new large-scale construction during the mid-twelfth century.

Great houses are distinguished from ordinary residential room blocks not primarily by wall thickness understood as structurally excessive, since the substantial rubble-core, veneer-faced masonry walls of Chacoan great houses in fact provided the load-bearing strength needed to support large, multi-story buildings, but by a broader set of recurring features: unusually formal and consistent architectural planning; a lower density of everyday domestic refuse, such as hearths, trash, and worn grinding stones, relative to the number of rooms, suggesting that many rooms may not have been used for full-time, everyday household life in the way ordinary residential rooms elsewhere clearly were; and the presence of unusual, high-value materials, including turquoise, worked shell, copper bells likely obtained through exchange networks extending into Mesoamerica, and the remains of scarlet macaws, tropical birds native to regions far to the south, which must have arrived at Chaco through long-distance trade or, in some cases, the transport of live birds across enormous distances.

These patterns have led most archaeologists to conclude that great houses served purposes beyond ordinary residence, potentially including storage, periodic large-scale gathering, ceremonial activity, the housing of ritual specialists or a relatively small resident population associated with those functions, and the reception and redistribution of goods moving through regional exchange networks. This textbook treats these possibilities as complementary rather than mutually exclusive, and returns to the broader question of Chaco's political and social organization in this chapter's Historical Debate feature.

Historical Method

How Do Archaeologists Recognize Migration?

Because this chapter builds toward a major population movement examined in Section 18.11, students should understand, in advance, how archaeologists identify migration in a society that left no written record of its own relocation. No single form of evidence proves migration by itself; archaeologists instead look for several forms of evidence pointing in the same direction.

Ceramic and architectural style: The sudden appearance of a pottery style, masonry technique, or architectural form in a new region, closely matching styles previously associated with a different, now-depopulated region, suggests that people carrying that knowledge and those traditions relocated, rather than that local communities simply invented the same forms independently.

Settlement abandonment and new settlement founding: Evidence of planned, orderly abandonment, such as sealed rooms and removed portable goods, distinguished from catastrophic destruction, combined with the founding or rapid growth of new settlements elsewhere during the same narrow window of time, supports a migration interpretation rather than a simple population collapse.

Demographic and settlement-pattern data: A sharp regional population decline recorded through survey and excavation data, occurring at the same time as a sharp population increase in another region, is stronger evidence of migration than either trend considered alone.

Skeletal and isotopic evidence: Bioarchaeological analysis, including strontium and oxygen isotope studies of teeth and bone, can indicate whether an individual grew up in the region where they were buried or arrived from elsewhere, offering direct, if limited and ethically sensitive, evidence of individual movement.

Oral tradition and receiving-community evidence: Migration narratives maintained by descendant communities, describing movement from named ancestral places, provide an additional and historically significant line of evidence, one that should be considered alongside, rather than subordinated to, the material evidence above.

No single line of evidence is sufficient on its own; archaeologists build a migration argument by showing that several independent kinds of evidence converge on the same conclusion, a method students should apply directly when evaluating Section 18.11's discussion of the thirteenth-century migrations.

Material Evidence Window

Room 33, Pueblo Bonito: Burials, Kinship, and Interpretation

The find: Room 33, a small room within Pueblo Bonito, was excavated in 1896 by the Hyde Exploring Expedition and found to contain an unusually rich burial assemblage, including two individuals interred beneath a wooden floor with tens of thousands of turquoise beads and pendants, along with additional burials and grave goods in the same room, making it one of the most materially significant burial contexts documented anywhere in the Ancestral Pueblo world.

Later analysis: Genetic analysis published in the twenty-first century, examining ancient DNA from individuals buried in Room 33, found evidence of a shared maternal line connecting several of the individuals across multiple generations, suggesting the room was used for the burial of related individuals over an extended period.

Interpretation and its limits: Some researchers have proposed that this genetic evidence supports the existence of an inherited, elite matrilineal descent group at Chaco, comparable in some respects to ruling lineages documented in other early complex societies. Other researchers caution that biological kinship among a small number of individuals buried together does not, by itself, establish that this lineage held exclusive political authority, hereditary rule, or anything resembling a formal "dynasty" in the sense that term is used for better-documented societies elsewhere in the world. A genetic relationship is a demonstrated fact; the political and social meaning of that relationship remains an interpretation requiring additional, still-debated evidence.

Research ethics: The excavation, retention, and genetic analysis of ancestral remains from Room 33 also raise the ethical questions introduced in Chapter 1's discussion of authority and NAGPRA, examined fully in Chapter 125. The 1896 excavation long predated modern standards for consultation with descendant communities. More recently, Indigenous scholars and allies criticized the twenty-first-century ancient-DNA study itself for proceeding without meaningful tribal consultation. Current ethical guidance increasingly emphasizes early consultation, sustained community partnership, respect for tribal research authority, and, where required by tribal or institutional protocols, community authorization before destructive analysis of ancestral remains, standards that were not consistently met by either the original excavation or, according to its critics, the later genetic study.

Questions for students:

  1. What does the Room 33 genetic evidence demonstrate with confidence, and what does it not demonstrate on its own?
  2. Why is it important to distinguish a documented biological relationship from a claim about political authority or hereditary rule?
  3. How does the 1896 excavation of Room 33 compare to current standards for research involving ancestral remains?
  4. Why might contemporary Pueblo nations have varied perspectives on genetic research involving their ancestors?
Section 18.7

Roads, Astronomy, and Regional Connections

Chaco Canyon's influence, or at least its architectural and cultural connections, extended well beyond the canyon itself. Archaeologists have identified more than a hundred "outlier" great houses across a wide area of the San Juan Basin and beyond, sharing key Chacoan architectural features, including formal masonry and, in many cases, great kivas. Connecting a significant number, though not all, of these outlier communities to Chaco Canyon and to one another is a system of engineered roads: wide, straight, carefully constructed roadways, in places bordered by low earthen berms or cut directly into bedrock, running for substantial distances across terrain that a more purely practical route would likely have avoided. The National Park Service and researchers who have studied this system note that the roads are unevenly preserved, that not every mapped segment can be confirmed to connect directly to a specific outlier, and that their intended function, whether primarily practical transportation, ceremonial pilgrimage, or symbolic connection, remains a genuinely open and actively debated question rather than a settled conclusion.

Astronomical knowledge embedded in Chacoan construction is better documented at the level of individual buildings and architectural features than for the road network as a whole. Several great houses and other structures include specific window, doorway, and wall alignments that correspond to solstice, equinox, or other significant solar and, in some cases, lunar events. Some Chaco rock art has been interpreted as possibly representing celestial events, including one well-known pictograph sometimes associated with the supernova of 1054, although such interpretations remain debated and alternative cultural or astronomical explanations have also been proposed. This textbook presents building- and site-level astronomical alignments as well-supported evidence of sophisticated observational knowledge, while treating broader claims that the road system as a whole was organized primarily around astronomical alignment, and specific claims about individual rock art panels, as more speculative extensions of that evidence, supported by some researchers but not established with the same confidence as the alignments documented in specific buildings.

This regional network extended still farther through exchange. Turquoise, mined and worked at several locations across the wider Southwest, moved into and through Chaco Canyon in large quantities, and Chacoan turquoise ornaments and unfinished materials have been found at sites hundreds of miles away. Marine shell, obtained ultimately from the Gulf of California and Pacific coast, likely moved north through exchange networks that included Hohokam communities, discussed in Chapter 17, and other Southwestern intermediaries, though the precise chain of exchange behind any specific Chacoan shell assemblage generally cannot be reconstructed with certainty. Copper bells and scarlet macaws obtained through connections reaching into Mesoamerica, and cacao residue identified in some Chacoan pottery vessels, further indicate that Chaco Canyon was embedded within exchange systems extending far beyond the immediate San Juan Basin. This textbook cautions, as it does throughout, against assuming that the presence of exotic materials by itself proves direct long-distance travel by any specific individual; these goods moved through chains of exchange relationships whose full extent and mechanism remain the subject of ongoing research.

Section 18.8

Regional Pueblo Worlds Beyond Chaco

Chaco Canyon has drawn disproportionate scholarly and popular attention because of its scale and its unusually precise dating, but it represented one distinctive center within a much wider and more varied Ancestral Pueblo world, and this chapter treats that wider world as a subject in its own right rather than as an afterthought to Chaco's story.

The Kayenta region of present-day northeastern Arizona, extending into adjoining parts of Utah, developed a related but architecturally and materially distinct tradition throughout the same broad centuries covered by this chapter. Kayenta communities generally built smaller, more dispersed settlements than the great houses of Chaco, often incorporating masonry towers, distinctive black-on-white and, later, polychrome pottery styles, and, in some areas, substantial cliff and canyon-alcove dwellings of their own, including sites within present-day Navajo National Monument, predating and running alongside the more famous Mesa Verde cliff dwellings examined in Section 18.9. Kayenta communities participated in regional exchange networks and shared broad architectural and agricultural patterns with Chacoan and Mesa Verde communities, while maintaining their own settlement rhythms, ceramic traditions, and, very likely, distinct community and political identities.

Other communities across the wider San Juan Basin and Rio Grande drainage maintained their own local traditions throughout this period, some connected to Chaco through the outlier and road network discussed in Section 18.7, others largely independent of it. Archaeologists increasingly emphasize that Ancestral Pueblo history should be understood as a mosaic of related but locally distinct communities responding to shared environmental pressures and participating in overlapping but not identical exchange and ceremonial networks, rather than as a single, Chaco-centered narrative into which every other community is fitted as a satellite. This framing matters directly for Section 18.11's discussion of the thirteenth-century migrations, since population movement out of the Four Corners region drew on, and fed into, this whole regional mosaic rather than a single point of origin or a single destination.

Section 18.9

Mesa Verde and the Cliff Dwellings

As construction activity at Chaco Canyon slowed markedly during the twelfth century, a development examined further in Section 18.11, population and architectural innovation shifted increasingly toward other parts of the Ancestral Pueblo world, particularly the Mesa Verde region of southwestern Colorado. Communities there had a long history of their own occupation predating and overlapping with the Chacoan era, including substantial mesa-top villages, and the region's most famous architectural achievement, the cliff dwellings, developed over a longer span than popular accounts sometimes suggest: construction of the major cliffside sites began as early as the 1190s and continued through the 1270s, with occupation of the largest structures, including Cliff Palace, generally dated to roughly 1190 through 1280 CE, in the decades immediately preceding the region's broader depopulation.

Cliff Palace, the largest of these structures, contains roughly one hundred fifty rooms and more than twenty kivas built into a large, naturally sheltered sandstone alcove. Other well-preserved cliff dwellings, including Balcony House and Long House, display similar characteristics: masonry rooms and kivas built within natural alcoves along canyon walls, generally accessed by hand- and toeholds carved into the rock or by ladders, with associated farmland located on the mesa tops above. Archaeologists have proposed several possible explanations for this shift toward defensible, alcove-based construction late in the region's occupation, including concerns about warfare or raiding, protection from weather, proximity to specific water sources such as seeps within the alcoves themselves, and symbolic or ceremonial considerations, and most researchers now regard some combination of these factors, rather than any single explanation, as most consistent with the evidence.

Notably, even though cliffside construction began as early as the 1190s, occupation of the major Mesa Verde cliff dwellings ended within a relatively narrow window, with the entire region depopulated by roughly 1300 CE, a development examined in detail in Section 18.11. The cliff dwellings therefore represent a development spanning several decades of the late twelfth and thirteenth centuries, not a long-term stable settlement pattern of many generations, and their occupation concluded during a period of significant documented environmental and social stress.

Section 18.10

Craft Production, Exchange, and Daily Labor

Behind the monumental architecture of Chaco Canyon and the dramatic cliff dwellings of Mesa Verde lay the ordinary rhythms of farming households, whose labor sustained both daily life and the periodic large-scale construction and gathering this chapter has described. Households processed maize using flat and troughed grinding stones called manos and metates, an activity that occupied a significant portion of daily labor, primarily performed by women according to patterns documented among historically observed Pueblo communities and inferred, with appropriate caution, for earlier periods. Cotton, cultivated in some warmer parts of the region and obtained through exchange elsewhere, was spun and woven into textiles, some of which survive in the exceptional dry-cave preservation conditions of the region. Turkeys were domesticated and kept for feathers, used in textile and blanket production, and, in some times and places, for food, though the extent of turkey consumption relative to their use for feathers varied across the region and over time.

Pottery, particularly the black-on-white painted wares that are among the most useful chronological and stylistic markers archaeologists use to date and distinguish Ancestral Pueblo sites, was produced by hand-coiling clay, without the potter's wheel, and fired in open or simple pit kilns. Distinct regional and chronological pottery styles, including differences between Chacoan, Mesa Verde, and Kayenta traditions discussed in Section 18.8, allow archaeologists to trace exchange, stylistic influence, and, to some degree, population movement across the region.

Turquoise working deserves particular attention as a specialized craft. Raw turquoise, mined at several Southwestern sources, was cut, drilled, shaped, and polished into beads, pendants, and inlay pieces, some produced in substantial quantities at workshop areas identified within Chaco Canyon itself. Finished turquoise items moved outward through exchange networks reaching well beyond the immediate region, evidence, alongside the macaws, copper bells, and marine shell discussed in Section 18.7, of the Ancestral Pueblo world's active participation in exchange systems connecting the Southwest to the Sonoran Desert, the Pacific coast, and Mesoamerica.

Everyday Life and Social History

Households, Kin, and Community

Daily labor divided along lines that likely combined age, gender, and household role: farming, construction, hunting, craft production, food processing, and childrearing all required sustained, often collaborative effort. As Section 18.5 has already emphasized, kivas provided settings for ceremonial life but also, very likely, for practical community decision-making, teaching, and the maintenance of social relationships beyond the individual household.

This textbook resists two temptations common in earlier popular writing about the Ancestral Pueblo past: reducing daily life to backdrop for monumental architecture, and assuming without evidence that gender roles, kinship structures, or family organization documented among later, historically observed Pueblo communities applied without change across many centuries of earlier history. Continuity is plausible and, in some cases, well supported; it should not be assumed automatically.

Section 18.11

The Thirteenth-Century Migrations

By the late thirteenth century, the Four Corners region, including Mesa Verde, Chaco Canyon's immediate surroundings, and much of the Kayenta area, had been substantially depopulated, its communities relocating southward and eastward into areas that remain home to Pueblo communities today. This depopulation was not sudden or unexplained in the sense sometimes implied by older popular accounts describing a "mysterious disappearance"; rather, it reflects a documented and, in its environmental dimension, unusually precisely dated process of population movement, driven by multiple interacting causes and unfolding over several generations through more than one migration stream rather than as a single coordinated relocation. Importantly, this movement did not begin only after the final drought of the 1270s through 1290s. Evidence from the Rio Grande Valley, including the Pajarito Plateau, indicates that migrants from the Four Corners region were already arriving and settling there by around 1150 CE, with this movement continuing and intensifying through the thirteenth century rather than occurring as a single late exodus. Chaco's decline, Mesa Verde's rise and subsequent depopulation, and Rio Grande population growth should therefore be understood as overlapping, concurrent processes rather than as a strict one-after-another sequence.

Tree-ring records provide exceptionally detailed evidence of a severe, sustained drought across the Colorado Plateau lasting from approximately 1276 to 1299 CE, sometimes called the "Great Drought," which coincides closely with the final depopulation of Mesa Verde and neighboring areas. An earlier, though somewhat less severe, dry period during the 1140s and 1150s CE similarly coincides with the marked slowdown in Chacoan great house construction discussed in Section 18.6. These correlations provide strong evidence that climatic stress was a major factor in both developments, though most archaeologists caution against treating drought as a sufficient explanation on its own.

Additional factors proposed by researchers include long-term environmental degradation from centuries of farming and wood harvesting, which may have reduced soil fertility and depleted nearby timber resources needed for construction and fuel, and evidence of increased violence and conflict in some areas during the thirteenth century, including fortified sites and skeletal evidence of trauma at some locations, though the scale and causes of this violence remain debated. Social and religious change also very likely played a role, though its place in this causal picture requires care: rather than treating new ceremonial systems as a cause that pushed people to leave the Four Corners region, most current archaeological evidence places the clearer, more archaeologically visible spread of practices associated with the Katsina, or Kachina, ceremonial complex in the period during and after aggregation into new, larger communities in the Rio Grande Valley and among the Hopi and Zuni, in the late thirteenth and especially the fourteenth centuries. In this reading, such ceremonial developments functioned less as a cause of the original migration and more as an important mechanism through which immigrant and resident populations, often speaking different languages and carrying different local traditions, integrated into new, larger communities after relocating, a subject examined further in Section 18.12.

Cause and Effect

Why the Four Corners Emptied

Long-term causes

Centuries of intensive farming, wood harvesting, and population growth on a landscape with limited water and soil resources, gradually reducing the region's resilience to environmental stress.

Short-term causes

The severe, well-documented drought of 1276–1299 CE, along with evidence of increased local conflict and competition over remaining productive land in some areas during the same period.

Effect

Near-total depopulation of Mesa Verde, the immediate Chaco area, and much of the Kayenta region by approximately 1300 CE, unfolding over several generations and through multiple, regionally varied migration streams.

Post-migration development

Substantial population growth in the Rio Grande Valley, on the Hopi mesas, and in the Zuni and Acoma areas, accompanied by the increasingly visible spread of Katsina-associated ceremonial practices that helped integrate newly aggregated, often diverse communities.

Historical complexity: No single cause fully explains this depopulation, and archaeologists continue to debate the relative weight of environmental, social, and religious factors. This textbook presents drought as a well-documented and significant factor while resisting environmental determinism: the same drought conditions did not produce identical outcomes in every community, and human decisions, social relationships, and religious change all shaped how communities responded to environmental stress and how they rebuilt community life afterward.

Whatever the precise combination of causes, the outcome is reasonably well documented: over the course of several generations, Ancestral Pueblo communities from the Four Corners region relocated into areas already inhabited by related farming communities, including the Rio Grande Valley, the Hopi mesas of northeastern Arizona, and the Zuni and Acoma areas of west-central New Mexico, contributing to significant population growth and, in some cases, new community formation in those regions during the late thirteenth and fourteenth centuries.

Section 18.12

Continuity: From Ancestral Pueblo to Modern Pueblo Nations

This chapter closes, as it must, by insisting on the continuity between the Ancestral Pueblo past and the present, while stating that continuity claim with appropriate precision. The Pueblo nations of New Mexico, including Acoma, Zuni, Laguna, and the many pueblos along the Rio Grande, along with the Hopi villages of northeastern Arizona, are not successors to a vanished people; their citizens trace ancestry to the communities this chapter has described, and many Pueblo communities maintain oral traditions describing migration from ancestral homelands, including areas identified with Chaco Canyon and Mesa Verde, that align in important respects with the archaeological evidence presented here, while also carrying meanings, teachings, and significance that archaeology alone cannot capture or verify.

This general ancestral relationship is well established and should not be doubted. At the same time, this textbook is careful not to overstate its precision: establishing that Ancestral Pueblo communities broadly are ancestral to contemporary Pueblo, Hopi, and Zuni nations is not the same as establishing a confirmed, one-to-one link between every specific ancient regional population, such as the particular communities of Chaco Canyon or a particular Kayenta village, and one specific present-day nation. Given the multiple, overlapping migration streams described in Section 18.11 and the mosaic of regional traditions described in Section 18.8, the historical relationship between specific ancestral communities and specific modern nations is, in many cases, an active subject of collaborative research among archaeologists and tribal historic preservation offices rather than a settled one-to-one correspondence for every site.

This continuity carries direct consequences for how Chaco Canyon, Mesa Verde, and related sites are studied, managed, and interpreted today. Contemporary Pueblo nations, along with the Navajo Nation and other Indigenous communities with historical and cultural connections to the region, participate in formal consultation with the National Park Service and other federal agencies regarding archaeological research, site management, and interpretation, a relationship examined more fully in Chapter 1's discussion of authority and ethics in Section 1.5 and in Chapter 125's treatment of repatriation. Many Pueblo communities regard Chaco Canyon and Mesa Verde as living, sacred landscapes connected to ongoing ceremonial traditions rather than as abandoned archaeological ruins alone, a perspective this textbook treats as historically significant in its own right rather than as an alternative to, or in competition with, archaeological evidence.

Recognizing this continuity does not mean assuming that every specific practice, belief, or social structure remained unchanged across seven or more centuries, any more than continuity between ancient and modern societies elsewhere in the world implies an absence of change. It means recognizing that the Ancestral Pueblo story does not end in depopulation or mystery. It continues directly into the history, governance, language, and ceremonial life of the contemporary Pueblo nations, whose own historical experience under Spanish, Mexican, and United States rule is examined in later chapters, including Chapters 42 through 44 on Spanish colonization and the Pueblo Revolt of 1680, and Chapter 30's broader survey of the Southwest and the Pueblo world on the eve of colonization.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From Ancestral Pueblo to Modern Pueblo

What Changed

  • The geographic center of population shifted dramatically from the Four Corners region to the Rio Grande Valley, the Hopi mesas, and the Zuni and Acoma areas after roughly 1300 CE
  • Monumental Chacoan-style great house construction ceased and was not replicated at the same scale in the communities that followed
  • Settlement patterns shifted toward the multi-story, plaza-centered pueblo villages more familiar from later historical documentation
  • Ceremonial life incorporated the more widely and visibly shared Katsina complex, associated in current archaeological interpretation with the post-migration integration of aggregated communities

What Stayed the Same

  • Maize, beans, and squash remained the agricultural foundation of Pueblo communities into the historic period and beyond
  • The kiva remained central to community ceremonial life among most Pueblo communities
  • Coiled pottery production, dry-farming adaptations, and turquoise working continued as important craft and economic traditions
  • Oral tradition, kinship organization, and connection to specific ancestral landscapes persisted as a living part of community identity

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both dramatic change and deep continuity, while being precise about the limits of what can be established between specific ancient communities and specific modern nations, prevents the twin errors this textbook has warned against since Chapter 1: treating Ancestral Pueblo history as a closed, vanished past, or assuming that continuity requires an absence of adaptation, change, and legitimate historical uncertainty.

Primary Source Window

Lieutenant James H. Simpson's 1849 Description of Pueblo Bonito

Creator: Lieutenant James H. Simpson, United States Army Corps of Topographical Engineers · Date: 1849 · Type: Official military expedition report, later published by the United States government, describing ruins encountered during a campaign into Navajo country

Context: In 1849, Simpson accompanied a United States military expedition through northwestern New Mexico and, guided in part by local Diné (Navajo) and Mexican guides who were already familiar with the canyon, documented and measured several of the great houses at Chaco Canyon, including Pueblo Bonito. His report, illustrated with drawings by expedition artist Richard Kern, became one of the earliest detailed outside written accounts of the site to reach a wide reading public. The full report survives and remains available in published form; this textbook paraphrases it here for concision and to keep the surrounding analysis focused, not because its wording cannot be verified.

Paraphrase of the report's description: Simpson described walls of finely fitted, thin sandstone masonry rising several stories, an unusual number and density of rooms compared to any pueblo he had previously observed, and construction he judged to reflect a level of skill and formal planning exceeding what he expected to find among the ruins of the region.

Analysis: Simpson's account is significant as one of the earliest sustained outside written descriptions of Chaco Canyon, and it helped draw later archaeological attention to the site. It also illustrates how nineteenth-century military and government expeditions, often traveling for other purposes, became important vehicles for outside documentation of Indigenous sites and, at times, for the beginning of their removal from Indigenous management and interpretation.

Limitations: Simpson's report reflects the perspective, assumptions, and purposes of a nineteenth-century military officer, writing during a period of active United States military campaigns against the Navajo, and it did not draw on, or generally acknowledge, existing Pueblo and Navajo knowledge of the site's history and significance beyond his guides' practical assistance. It provides valuable descriptive and measurement data but should not be read as a neutral or complete account of the site's meaning.

Historical Debate

What Was Chaco Canyon?

The question: Was Chaco Canyon primarily a political capital exercising some form of centralized authority over outlying communities, a religious and ceremonial pilgrimage center without formal political control, a hub for the redistribution of goods and food across a resource-variable region, or some combination of these functions that does not map neatly onto any single familiar category?

Interpretation A — Chaco as a centralized political center: Some archaeologists argue that the scale of construction, the road network, evidence of a possible small resident elite population, and the concentration of exotic and high-value goods at great houses point toward a regionally integrated political system with some degree of centralized authority, even if it does not resemble a state in the European or Mesoamerican sense.
Interpretation B — Chaco as a ceremonial and pilgrimage center: Other archaeologists argue that the low density of everyday domestic refuse in great houses, the emphasis on great kivas and formal ceremonial architecture, and the road system's likely symbolic and, in specific buildings, astronomical significance suggest that Chaco functioned primarily as a periodic gathering and ceremonial destination, drawing dispersed communities together for ritual and social purposes without necessarily exercising ongoing political rule over them.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the same body of architectural, artifactual, and settlement-pattern evidence, but differ in how much weight to give indicators of centralized control versus indicators of periodic, voluntary ceremonial gathering.

Points of disagreement: Scholars disagree chiefly over interpretation of ambiguous evidence, including the small number of burials found within great houses that display unusually rich grave goods, the meaning of low domestic refuse density, and how directive, as opposed to symbolic, the road network's function actually was.

Why it matters: How historians characterize Chaco shapes broader comparative questions about the diversity of complex societies in the ancient Americas and cautions against assuming that large-scale monumental construction always indicates the same kind of political organization found in centralized states elsewhere in the world.

Student task: Using the evidence presented in Sections 18.6 and 18.7, construct an argument for the interpretation you find more persuasive, or explain why elements of both interpretations may be necessary to account for the full body of evidence.

Living With and Interpreting Chaco

From the perspective of an archaeologist working within current professional standards: Chaco Canyon offers an unusually well-preserved and precisely dated record for testing hypotheses about labor organization, exchange, and regional integration, but responsible interpretation requires ongoing consultation with descendant communities and caution against imposing familiar political categories on evidence that may not fit them.
From the perspective of a citizen of a contemporary Pueblo nation with ancestral ties to the region: Chaco Canyon is not simply an archaeological site to be excavated and explained; it is an ancestral and, for many communities, still-active spiritual landscape, and decisions about research, tourism, and interpretation should reflect the authority and knowledge of the descendant nations connected to it.
From the perspective of a National Park Service manager balancing preservation, tourism, and consultation: Chaco Culture National Historical Park must protect fragile archaeological resources from erosion, looting, and the pressures of visitation while maintaining formal consultation relationships with associated tribes and presenting historically responsible interpretation to the public.
From the perspective of a nineteenth-century outside observer such as Simpson: The ruins represented an impressive but puzzling achievement, evaluated largely by comparison to architectural traditions the observer already knew, with little inclination to consult or credit existing Indigenous knowledge of the site beyond practical guidance.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced the history of the Ancestral Pueblo world across nearly three thousand years, from small Basketmaker foraging and early farming communities of the mid-second millennium BCE through the extraordinary monumental construction of Chaco Canyon, the cliff dwellings of Mesa Verde, and the thirteenth-century migrations that redistributed the population of the Four Corners region into the Pueblo nations that continue to exist today. Throughout, this chapter has insisted on a term, "Ancestral Pueblo," that reflects the historical relationship between these communities and their descendants, rather than the outside, Navajo-derived term "Anasazi" that dominated earlier scholarship, while also cautioning against overstating the precision of that ancestral relationship at the level of specific ancient populations and specific modern nations.

The chapter began by establishing the environmental conditions of the Colorado Plateau and traced the developmental sequence from Basketmaker II farming, which began well before the Common Era, through Basketmaker III pithouse villages several centuries later, to Pueblo I's shift toward above-ground room-block architecture. It then paused to examine, in dedicated detail, how households farmed a difficult high-desert environment and organized daily and ceremonial life around kivas and kin-based household clusters, treating these subjects as significant in their own right rather than as backdrop to monumental construction.

The chapter devoted substantial attention to Chaco Canyon, examining the scale, construction sequence, and possible functions of its great houses, correcting the common misconception that great house walls were structurally excessive rather than appropriately engineered for large multi-story buildings, and to the roads and architectural astronomical alignments that connected Chaco to a wider region, while carefully distinguishing well-documented building-level astronomical evidence from more speculative claims about the road network as a whole. It also devoted a dedicated section to Ancestral Pueblo regional worlds beyond Chaco, particularly the Kayenta tradition, emphasizing that Chaco represented one distinctive center within a wider mosaic of related communities.

The chapter then turned to Mesa Verde and the well-known cliff dwellings, dating their construction across several decades from the 1190s through the 1270s rather than compressing them into the region's final years, and it examined craft production, exchange, and daily labor as evidence of both self-sufficiency and long-distance connection. The chapter's account of the thirteenth-century depopulation of the Four Corners region emphasized multiple, interacting causes unfolding over several generations and multiple migration streams, and it placed the archaeologically visible spread of Katsina-associated ceremonial practices primarily in the period of post-migration community integration rather than treating it as a direct cause of the initial departure.

The chapter concluded by tracing migration into the Rio Grande Valley, the Hopi mesas, and the Zuni and Acoma areas, and by insisting on continuity between Ancestral Pueblo communities and the contemporary, sovereign Pueblo nations, while distinguishing that well-established general continuity from more uncertain claims about specific ancient-to-modern population correspondences. These themes connect this chapter to the surrounding chapters of Part III and anticipate the fuller treatment of the Southwest and the Pueblo world in Chapter 30 and of Spanish colonization of Pueblo communities in Chapters 42 through 44.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Ancestral Pueblo
The preferred term for the farming communities of the Colorado Plateau ancestral to the contemporary Pueblo, Hopi, and Zuni nations.
Basketmaker
The earliest developmental periods of this tradition, named for sophisticated basketry and characterized by increasing maize cultivation and, later, pithouse villages.
Pithouse
A semi-subterranean dwelling common during the Basketmaker III period and a likely architectural precursor to the kiva.
Pueblo (architecture)
Contiguous, above-ground room-block construction, first appearing during the Pueblo I period, that gives this tradition its name.
Kiva
A typically circular, often partly subterranean room used for ceremonial and social purposes throughout Ancestral Pueblo architecture.
Great kiva
An unusually large, formally constructed kiva associated with community- or multi-community-scale gathering.
Great house
A large, multi-story, formally planned masonry structure with substantial rubble-core, veneer-faced walls, exemplified by Chaco Canyon's Pueblo Bonito.
Jacal
A wattle-and-daub construction technique using a pole-and-brush framework covered with mud plaster, common in earlier Pueblo I architecture.
Coursed masonry
A formal stone-building technique using shaped sandstone veneer over a rubble core, distinctive of Chacoan great house construction and structurally suited to multi-story buildings.
Dendrochronology
Tree-ring dating, which allows unusually precise dating of construction events at Ancestral Pueblo sites through analysis of preserved wooden beams.
Dry farming
Agriculture dependent on rainfall and captured runoff rather than irrigation from a permanent river or canal system.
Check dam
A small constructed barrier used to slow runoff and retain soil moisture on hillsides and in drainages.
Chacoan road
An engineered, unusually straight roadway associated with Chaco Canyon and a number of, though not all, outlying great houses.
Outlier community
A settlement outside Chaco Canyon sharing Chacoan architectural features, in some cases connected to the canyon by road.
Cliff dwelling
A masonry structure built within a natural rock alcove, exemplified by Mesa Verde's Cliff Palace, generally dated across the 1190s through 1270s.
Trash midden
An accumulated deposit of household refuse used by archaeologists to reconstruct diet, activity, and chronology at a site.
Black-on-white pottery
A widespread Ancestral Pueblo ceramic tradition used by archaeologists as an important chronological and regional marker.
Turquoise
A blue-green mineral mined and worked at several Southwestern locations, extensively traded through and associated with Chaco Canyon.
Macaw
A tropical bird native to regions far south of the Southwest, whose remains at Chacoan sites indicate long-distance exchange connections reaching toward Mesoamerica.
Katsina/Kachina complex
A ceremonial tradition documented among historic Pueblo communities, whose clearer archaeological visibility dates mainly to the period of post-migration community aggregation in the late thirteenth and fourteenth centuries.
Repatriation
The return of ancestral remains and cultural items to culturally affiliated Indigenous nations, governed in part by NAGPRA, introduced in Chapter 1 and examined fully in Chapter 125.
Oral tradition
Structured historical and cultural knowledge transmitted across generations, treated in this textbook as a legitimate historical source requiring critical interpretation.
Colorado Plateau
The high, semi-arid tableland spanning parts of Utah, Colorado, Arizona, and New Mexico that forms the geographic setting for this chapter.
Aggregation
The process by which dispersed or migrating populations combined into larger, denser communities, notably during and after the thirteenth-century migrations.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has shown that some of the most visually striking achievements in North American history north of Mesoamerica, the great houses of Chaco Canyon and the cliff dwellings of Mesa Verde, emerged from farming communities adapting, over nearly three thousand years, to one of the more challenging agricultural environments on the continent. Their achievement was not inevitable, and it did not happen all at once. It developed through gradual shifts in subsistence, architecture, and social organization, punctuated by periods of remarkable construction and by periods of stress, reorganization, and movement.

The chapter has also modeled a form of historical caution this textbook applies throughout: recognizing what monumental architecture, precise scientific dating, and long-distance exchange evidence can establish, while resisting the temptation to fill remaining uncertainty with confident but unsupported claims, whether about political titles and religious doctrine or about the precise structural meaning of a masonry wall. Genuine scholarly disagreement about Chaco Canyon's political and social organization is not a weakness in the historical record; it is an honest reflection of what a rich but non-documentary evidentiary record can and cannot tell historians.

Most importantly, this chapter has insisted that the Ancestral Pueblo story does not end with the depopulation of the Four Corners region, while also insisting that continuity be stated with historical precision rather than as a sweeping, unqualified claim. The story continues directly into the history, governance, ceremonial life, and continuing sovereignty of the Pueblo, Hopi, and Zuni nations, whose own historical experience under Spanish, Mexican, and United States rule occupies substantial attention later in this textbook. Understanding Chaco Canyon and Mesa Verde as ancestral homelands of living nations, rather than as ruins of a vanished people, and understanding the wider Ancestral Pueblo world as a mosaic of related regional traditions rather than a single Chaco-centered story, is essential to reading the rest of this textbook's treatment of the Southwest with the historical accuracy this subject requires.

The story of Ancestral Pueblo farming, monumental construction, environmental adaptation, and migration also connects forward to Part IV's treatment of the mound-building traditions of eastern North America, examined beginning in Chapter 19, and backward to Chapters 15 through 17's account of the origins of Southwestern agriculture and the Mogollon and Hohokam traditions. Together, these chapters demonstrate the profound regional diversity of pre-contact North American societies while identifying the shared threads, agricultural innovation, monumental construction, long-distance exchange, and enduring continuity between past and present, that run throughout this textbook's account of Indigenous North America.
