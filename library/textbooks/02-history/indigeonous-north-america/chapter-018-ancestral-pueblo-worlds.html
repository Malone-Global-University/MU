Ancestral Pueblo Worlds

Indigenous Peoples of North America: A Continental History

Chapter Opening

A visitor who walks the plaza floor of Pueblo Bonito, in Chaco Canyon, or climbs the wooden ladder into Cliff Palace, at Mesa Verde, is standing inside one of the most extensively studied archaeological records anywhere in North America. That visitor is not standing inside a mystery of a vanished people. Descendants of the communities who built these places live today in the Pueblo nations of the Rio Grande Valley, at Hopi, at Zuni, and at Acoma, among others, who trace ancestral and cultural connections to the Ancestral Pueblo world this chapter examines.

Few chapters in North American archaeology have attracted more public fascination, and more public misunderstanding, than the story of the Ancestral Pueblo peoples of the Colorado Plateau. Popular writing has often treated Chaco Canyon's great houses and Mesa Verde's cliff dwellings as ruins of a people who somehow disappeared, leaving behind spectacular architecture and no living descendants to explain it. This framing is inaccurate, and this chapter rejects it directly. The communities that built the structures examined in this chapter did not vanish. Beginning in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, their descendants reorganized, migrated, and established new communities to the south and east, communities whose direct descendants are the contemporary Pueblo peoples of Arizona and New Mexico, introduced more fully in Chapter 30.

This chapter picks up a story that Chapter 15 began: the slow transition of Southwestern communities from mobile foraging, supplemented by maize cultivation, toward increasingly settled village life built around dependable, though never fully secure, dry-farming agriculture. Chapters 16 and 17 traced two other major Southwestern trajectories, the Mogollon highland communities of the mountainous borderlands and the Hohokam irrigation society of the Sonoran Desert. This chapter turns to a third major regional trajectory, one centered on the Colorado Plateau, the Four Corners region where the modern boundaries of Arizona, New Mexico, Utah, and Colorado meet, and the drainages that feed the San Juan and Little Colorado rivers. Archaeologists conventionally call the communities of this region the Ancestral Pueblo peoples, a term this chapter defines carefully in its first section.

Between roughly the sixth century and the end of the thirteenth century of the Common Era, these communities moved through a long sequence of change: from scattered pithouse hamlets to substantial farming villages; from villages to the extraordinary multi-story great houses of Chaco Canyon and their far-flung network of associated communities; from Chaco's regional prominence to the large aggregated pueblos and dramatic cliff dwellings of the Mesa Verde region; and, by the end of the thirteenth century, to a sweeping reorganization of settlement across the northern Southwest, in which entire regions were left by their farming populations while new and enduring communities grew farther south. None of this sequence was simple, uniform, or predetermined. Different communities in different parts of the Colorado Plateau followed different timelines, adopted different architectural solutions, and responded differently to environmental and social pressures.

This chapter treats agriculture, architecture, community organization, regional exchange, and migration as interconnected subjects rather than as separate topics to be checked off in sequence. Ancestral Pueblo farmers built water-control features and storage systems that reveal sophisticated environmental knowledge. Ancestral Pueblo builders constructed roomblocks, kivas, and great houses that reveal changing forms of household and community organization. Chaco Canyon's great houses and their associated roads reveal a regional system whose exact political and religious character remains a genuine subject of scholarly debate, examined directly in this chapter's Historical Debate feature. And the migrations that reorganized the Pueblo world at the end of the thirteenth century reveal a story of adaptation and continuity rather than collapse.

Studying this history responsibly requires resisting two temptations that have shaped much popular writing about the Ancestral Pueblo world. The first temptation treats archaeological remains as evidence of a lost, exotic civilization disconnected from any living people, a framing that erases the ancestral relationship living Pueblo nations maintain with these places today. The second temptation treats the sequence from pithouse to pueblo to cliff dwelling to migration as a simple evolutionary ladder, each stage more advanced than the last, culminating naturally in cliff dwellings before an unexplained ending. Neither temptation matches the archaeological or historical record. This chapter instead presents a history of continuous adaptation: farmers responding to a demanding and variable environment, communities experimenting with different forms of settlement and architecture, a widespread regional system centered on Chaco Canyon rising and receding in influence, and, in the end, a great migration that redistributed population without erasing identity, memory, or continuity.

By the chapter's close, students should understand Ancestral Pueblo history not as the story of a mysterious ancient civilization that disappeared, but as one chapter, however remarkable, in a still-continuing history whose living continuation is examined fully in Chapter 30.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Define "Ancestral Pueblo" as a broad archaeological and historical category encompassing diverse communities, and explain why "Anasazi" is generally avoided in current scholarship and by Pueblo communities themselves.
  2. Describe the geography of the Colorado Plateau and Four Corners region and explain how elevation, precipitation, and water sources shaped settlement and farming decisions.
  3. Trace the transition from Basketmaker-period pithouse communities to substantial above-ground pueblo villages without treating this transition as a simple, uniform evolutionary sequence.
  4. Explain the range of dry-farming and water-control strategies Ancestral Pueblo farmers used to manage an unpredictable high-desert environment.
  5. Analyze the function and limitations of kivas and great kivas as evidence for community and ceremonial organization.
  6. Describe Chaco Canyon's great houses, their architecture, and their material connections to distant regions.
  7. Evaluate competing scholarly interpretations of the Chacoan regional system, including political, ceremonial, and economic models.
  8. Identify major Ancestral Pueblo regional traditions beyond Chaco, including Mesa Verde, Kayenta, and Cibola-area communities.
  9. Explain the aggregation of population into large villages and cliff dwellings in the thirteenth century and its social and environmental context.
  10. Assess multiple contributing causes for the late-thirteenth-century depopulation of the northern San Juan region, avoiding single-cause explanations.
  11. Distinguish migration and reorganization from disappearance or extinction, and connect Ancestral Pueblo migrations to the formation of later Pueblo communities.
  12. Explain the continuing ancestral relationship between contemporary Pueblo nations and Ancestral Pueblo archaeological sites.

Historical Context

Chapter 15 traced the arrival and gradual adoption of maize agriculture across the Southwest, a process that unfolded unevenly over many centuries and combined with, rather than immediately replaced, older patterns of hunting and gathering. Chapters 16 and 17 then followed two of the three major regional trajectories that developed from this shared agricultural foundation: the Mogollon communities of the mountainous uplands along the modern Arizona–New Mexico border, and the Hohokam irrigation society of the Sonoran Desert basin around the Gila and Salt rivers. This chapter turns to the third major trajectory, centered on the Colorado Plateau and the Four Corners region, where communities that archaeologists group under the label Ancestral Pueblo developed distinctive farming strategies, architectural traditions, and forms of community and regional organization.

The Colorado Plateau presented Ancestral Pueblo farmers with a demanding environment. Elevations across the region commonly range from about 5,000 to 8,000 feet, producing short growing seasons and a real risk of early or late frosts that could destroy a maize crop. Average annual precipitation across much of the plateau is modest and highly variable from year to year, arriving partly as winter snowmelt and partly as summer monsoon rainfall that can be violently localized, drenching one drainage while leaving a neighboring one dry. Farmers could not simply plant and expect a harvest; they had to actively manage water, soil, and timing, adapting strategies to local topography, elevation, and soil conditions that varied considerably even within short distances.

Despite these challenges, Ancestral Pueblo communities developed increasingly substantial villages, then dispersed and re-aggregated repeatedly over several centuries, and eventually built the elaborate great houses of Chaco Canyon and the cliff dwellings of Mesa Verde before undertaking one of the best-documented population movements in North American archaeology at the close of the thirteenth century. Understanding why communities settled, built, farmed, aggregated, and eventually moved as they did requires attention to farming strategy, architecture, community and regional organization, exchange, and environmental change together, since none of these subjects can be fully understood in isolation from the others.

Section 18.1

What Does "Ancestral Pueblo" Mean?

"Ancestral Pueblo" is an archaeological and historical category, not the name of a single ancient nation, state, language, or political system. It describes a broad tradition of farming communities that developed across the Colorado Plateau and Four Corners region over roughly a thousand years, sharing overlapping, though never identical, patterns of architecture, farming strategy, pottery, and community organization. Within this broad tradition, archaeologists distinguish numerous regional variants, including communities in the Chaco, Mesa Verde, Kayenta, and Cibola areas, each with its own local history, pace of change, and distinctive material traits, examined further in Section 18.9. Treating "Ancestral Pueblo" as though it named a single unified people would misrepresent this diversity in the same way that treating "Mogollon" or "Hohokam" as single unified peoples would misrepresent those traditions, discussed in Chapters 16 and 17.

Older popular and scholarly writing frequently used the term "Anasazi" for these communities, a word borrowed from a Navajo term sometimes translated as "ancient enemies" or "ancient ones," depending on interpretation. Because the term originates outside the communities whose ancestors it describes, and because many contemporary Pueblo people find it inappropriate for referring to their own ancestors, this textbook follows current National Park Service and scholarly practice in generally using "Ancestral Pueblo" or "Ancestral Puebloan" instead. This is not merely a matter of vocabulary preference. It reflects the same methodological principle introduced in Chapter 1 and applied throughout this textbook: naming choices carry historical and political weight, and responsible historical writing should use terms that accurately reflect how the communities most directly connected to a history describe themselves and their ancestors, while still explaining older terminology so that students can recognize it in earlier scholarship, museum labels, and historic site names.

It is equally important not to overcorrect by imagining a single, continuous "Ancestral Pueblo civilization" marching in lockstep from pithouses to pueblos to Chaco to Mesa Verde to migration. The communities this chapter studies were numerous, regionally distinct, and capable of independent decision-making. Some regions built great houses similar to those at Chaco; others did not. Some communities aggregated into large villages in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries; others remained more dispersed. Some areas were abandoned by farming populations by the late thirteenth century; others, including areas along the Rio Grande and at Hopi and Zuni, saw population grow. This chapter treats "Ancestral Pueblo" as an umbrella term useful for organizing a genuinely diverse regional history, not as a label for a single people whose story can be told as a simple, unbroken narrative line.

Historical Vocabulary
Ancestral Pueblo and "Anasazi"

Definition: "Ancestral Pueblo" is the term this textbook uses for the archaeologically defined farming communities of the Colorado Plateau and Four Corners region, from roughly the first centuries of the Common Era through the late thirteenth century, whose descendants include the historically and currently documented Pueblo nations of the Rio Grande Valley, Hopi, Zuni, and Acoma.

Historical context: The term "Anasazi," used widely in twentieth-century archaeology and popular writing, derives from a Navajo (Diné) term and was adopted by outside researchers rather than by the communities whose ancestors it names. Many contemporary Pueblo people consider it an inappropriate label for their own ancestors, and most federal land management agencies, museums, and archaeologists working in the region have shifted toward "Ancestral Pueblo" or "Ancestral Puebloan" in recent decades.

Why it matters: Terminology choices affect whether living Indigenous communities are treated as connected to, or disconnected from, the archaeological record of their own ancestors. Using "Ancestral Pueblo" reinforces the historically accurate point that this chapter's subject has living descendants and a continuing history, rather than being a closed chapter belonging only to the distant past.

Potential misunderstanding: Neither "Ancestral Pueblo" nor "Anasazi" refers to a single tribe, nation, language, or political system. Both are broad analytical categories covering many distinct communities across a large region and a long span of time.

Section 18.2

Landscapes of the Pueblo World

The Colorado Plateau is a vast upland region of canyons, mesas, and river drainages centered on the Four Corners area, where the modern states of Arizona, New Mexico, Utah, and Colorado meet. Elevations across the plateau typically range from roughly 4,500 feet in lower canyon bottoms to more than 8,000 feet on the highest mesas and mountain flanks, producing significant local variation in growing season length, frost risk, and available moisture within relatively short distances. A farming community on a canyon floor near a reliable spring faced different possibilities and risks than a community on an exposed, higher-elevation mesa top only a few miles away.

Precipitation across the region arrives through two distinct patterns: winter snow, which accumulates in higher elevations and feeds streams and springs through gradual snowmelt, and summer monsoon rainfall, which can be intense but highly localized, sometimes drenching one small drainage while leaving a neighboring valley dry. This unpredictability, more than simple aridity by itself, shaped Ancestral Pueblo farming strategy, discussed fully in Section 18.4. Farmers needed methods for capturing, directing, and conserving whatever water became available, since a strategy calibrated only for a normal season could fail catastrophically in a dry one.

Major sub-regions within this broader landscape include the San Juan Basin and the drainage of the San Juan River, home to Chaco Canyon and numerous associated communities; the Mesa Verde region of southwestern Colorado and adjacent Utah, cut by deep canyons and capped by pinyon-and-juniper-covered mesas; the Kayenta area of northeastern Arizona; the drainage of the Little Colorado River and the Hopi Mesas; and the Cibola area associated with later Zuni-region communities. Farther east, the Rio Grande Valley and its tributaries offered a different set of possibilities, including more reliable river-adjacent farming in some stretches, and would become increasingly important as a population center in the centuries this chapter's later sections examine.

Section 18.3

From Early Farmers to Pueblo Communities

Chapter 15 examined the long, uneven process by which maize agriculture spread into the Southwest and became integrated with existing patterns of hunting and gathering. This section briefly reviews how those early farming communities on the Colorado Plateau developed the settlement and institutional patterns that archaeologists identify as Ancestral Pueblo, without repeating the fuller agricultural-origins narrative Chapter 15 already provided.

By the period archaeologists label Basketmaker II, roughly the centuries around the turn of the Common Era, communities across parts of the Colorado Plateau combined maize and squash cultivation with continued hunting and wild-plant gathering, living in relatively small, sometimes seasonally occupied settlements. Storage of surplus food, often in specially constructed cists or pits, became increasingly important, reflecting the growing role of cultivated crops in community subsistence even as mobility and foraging remained significant.

During the Basketmaker III period, roughly the sixth through eighth centuries, several important changes converged. Communities built more substantial, deeper pithouses, semi-subterranean structures with wooden and earthen superstructures that provided effective insulation against seasonal temperature extremes. Pottery became a widespread and characteristic technology during this period, transforming cooking and storage practices across the region. The bow and arrow, a technology whose adoption is documented archaeologically at various points across the Southwest, provided a more effective hunting technology than the earlier atlatl and dart. Domesticated turkeys became a regular part of some communities' economies during this period, kept particularly for their feathers and for other non-food uses; their importance as a significant meat source increased substantially only in later Pueblo periods, particularly after roughly 1050 CE. Taken together, these changes reflect communities becoming more settled, more dependent on stored agricultural surplus, and more invested in particular locations, even though residential mobility, especially in response to poor harvests, never disappeared entirely.

These Basketmaker III villages, sometimes consisting of a dozen or more pithouses along with associated storage structures, represent the immediate foundation from which the architectural and community developments examined in the following sections grew. They were not yet "pueblos" in the sense of the substantial above-ground masonry villages that would develop over subsequent centuries, but they established patterns of settled village life, agricultural dependence, and community organization that later Pueblo period communities would build upon and transform.

Section 18.4

Farming the High Desert

Agriculture on the Colorado Plateau required active, continuous management rather than simple planting and waiting. Farmers faced a fundamental problem: average precipitation across much of the region was often marginal for maize cultivation, and, more importantly, that average concealed enormous year-to-year and place-to-place variability. A farming strategy suited to a wet year could fail in a dry one, and a field that produced well in one location might fail entirely a short distance away due to differences in soil, slope, or exposure. Ancestral Pueblo farmers responded to this uncertainty with a diverse toolkit of techniques rather than a single method, and different communities combined these techniques differently depending on local conditions.

Dry farming, planting crops to rely primarily on direct rainfall without constructed irrigation, was widespread, but it was rarely passive. Farmers selected planting locations carefully, often choosing spots where runoff naturally concentrated, such as the mouths of small drainages or the base of slopes, so that fields received more moisture than rainfall alone would provide. Check dams, low rock or earthen structures built across small drainages, slowed and spread runoff, reduced erosion, and allowed water and sediment to accumulate behind them, creating small pockets of arable, moisture-retentive soil. Terracing similarly slowed runoff on sloped terrain and helped retain both soil and moisture.

In some areas, farmers built more elaborate water-control features, including grid gardens and so-called waffle gardens, small square plots bordered by low earthen ridges that held irrigation or rain water in place around individual plants, a technique especially useful for gardens near settlements rather than for extensive field agriculture. On the Pajarito Plateau around present-day Bandelier, farmers took advantage of pumice and pebble mulch: porous volcanic pumice and small gravel spread over planting surfaces absorbed snowmelt and rainfall and then released that moisture gradually, helping retain soil moisture, moderate soil temperature, and reduce evaporation. Cinder-mulch farming, a related but distinct technique using volcanic cinders, is documented in other volcanic-influenced areas of the Southwest. Communities near reliable springs or streams sometimes built simple diversion or check systems to direct available surface water toward fields, though large-scale canal irrigation of the kind examined in Chapter 17's discussion of Hohokam communities was not typical of the Ancestral Pueblo world, whose canyon and mesa topography generally did not support comparable canal networks.

Storage was as important as cultivation. Because harvests varied so unpredictably from year to year, households and communities built storage rooms, cists, and, in later periods, dedicated storage suites within roomblocks and great houses, allowing surplus from good years to buffer against poor ones. Turkeys, beans, and a range of wild plant foods, including pinyon nuts, supplemented maize and squash, providing some measure of dietary flexibility when any single resource fell short. This combination of careful site selection, water management, storage, and dietary diversification reflects not a single farming technique but an integrated strategy for managing risk in an environment where a single poor season could threaten a community's food supply, and where several poor seasons in succession, as later sections examine, could threaten a community's ability to remain in a given place at all.

Section 18.5

From Pithouses to Pueblos

The architectural history of the Ancestral Pueblo world is often summarized as a straightforward progression from pithouses to above-ground masonry pueblos to multi-story great houses to cliff dwellings. This summary captures a real long-term trend but risks flattening a more complicated reality in which different architectural forms coexisted, overlapped chronologically, and served different purposes simultaneously rather than simply replacing one another in strict sequence.

During the period archaeologists label Pueblo I, roughly the eighth and ninth centuries, communities across parts of the Colorado Plateau began constructing above-ground rooms built of adobe, jacal (a wattle-and-daub-style construction), or simple masonry, typically used for storage and, increasingly, for living space, arranged in linear or curving roomblocks. At the same time, pithouses did not disappear; instead, they increasingly took on more specialized, often communal or ceremonial functions, a shift that anticipates the later development of the kiva, examined in Section 18.6. This period therefore represents not a clean replacement of one architectural form by another, but a gradual redistribution of function between above-ground and below-ground structures.

By the Pueblo II period, roughly the tenth through early twelfth centuries, masonry construction became more sophisticated and widespread, and roomblocks grew larger and more elaborate in many areas, including the great houses of Chaco Canyon examined in Section 18.7. Multi-story construction appeared in some of the largest sites, allowing greater room counts within a compact building footprint. Yet many Ancestral Pueblo communities throughout this period continued to live in relatively small, single-story habitations; the large, multi-story great houses represent a distinctive and, in most regions, comparatively rare architectural investment rather than the typical Ancestral Pueblo dwelling of the period.

The Pueblo III period, roughly the mid-twelfth through late thirteenth centuries, saw the growth of large aggregated villages in several regions, including the cliff dwellings of the Mesa Verde region, examined in Section 18.10. Mesa Verde's own architectural sequence illustrates the danger of assuming a single evolutionary ladder: the well-known Step House site preserves evidence of a Basketmaker-period pithouse settlement in the same alcove later reoccupied for above-ground pueblo construction centuries afterward, with a substantial gap in between, demonstrating that specific locations could be used, abandoned, and reused in ways that do not correspond to a tidy, uninterrupted sequence.

Students should therefore understand the pithouse-to-pueblo-to-cliff-dwelling sequence as a genuine long-term trend in the aggregate record across the whole region, while recognizing that at any given time and place, multiple architectural forms could coexist, that not every community followed the same timeline, and that the most famous examples of each architectural stage, particularly Chaco's great houses and Mesa Verde's cliff dwellings, represent especially prominent episodes within a much longer and more varied architectural history rather than universal norms.

Section 18.6

Kivas, Households, and Community Organization

Ancestral Pueblo settlements were built around households, typically organized into roomblocks containing living spaces, storage rooms, and work areas, arranged around open plazas that served as shared outdoor space for daily activity. Above this household level, communities built structures intended for gatherings larger than a single household: kivas, and, at a still larger scale, great kivas.

A kiva is typically a round, though in some earlier periods rectangular, subterranean or semi-subterranean room, generally featuring a central hearth, a ventilation shaft, a sipapu (a small hole in the floor that, in later historic Pueblo practice, carries specific ceremonial and cosmological significance), and, in some cases, roof entry by ladder. Kivas appear to have served multiple, possibly overlapping functions across different times and places, including domestic activity, craft production, and communal or ceremonial gathering. Many kivas are directly associated with specific roomblocks or household clusters, suggesting a scale of use tied to particular extended families or small community segments.

Great kivas are considerably larger, sometimes exceeding forty feet in diameter, and appear to have served gatherings drawing together multiple households or, in some cases, multiple communities, for purposes that likely included ceremony, decision-making, and the reinforcement of shared identity across a dispersed population. Chaco Canyon's great houses, examined in the next section, typically incorporate one or more great kivas of substantial scale, and great kivas also appear at numerous outlying communities connected to the Chacoan regional system.

Historians and archaeologists must exercise particular caution when interpreting the ceremonial meaning of ancient kivas. Historic and contemporary Pueblo communities maintain kivas with specific, often closely held ceremonial and religious significance, and features such as the sipapu carry meanings documented through ethnographic study of living Pueblo religious practice. It is reasonable, and supported by architectural continuity, to see ancient kivas as ancestral to this living tradition. It is not responsible, however, to assume that a specific ancient kiva's precise ceremonial content, associated beliefs, or ritual practice can be reconstructed in detail simply by analogy with practices documented centuries later among particular contemporary communities, since religious practice, like architecture, changed over time and varied among different Pueblo communities. This chapter therefore describes kivas as spaces whose general communal and likely ceremonial functions are well supported by the archaeological and architectural record, while avoiding specific claims about ancient belief that only living Pueblo communities themselves, drawing on their own oral tradition and religious knowledge, are positioned to make.

Historical Vocabulary
Kiva and Great Kiva

Definition: A kiva is a round or rectangular subterranean or semi-subterranean room used by Ancestral Pueblo communities for domestic, craft, and communal or ceremonial purposes; a great kiva is a substantially larger version of the same general form, apparently used for gatherings serving multiple households or communities.

Historical context: Kivas developed in part from earlier pithouse architecture, and their use continues in modified form among many contemporary Pueblo communities, where kivas retain specific religious and ceremonial significance documented through community practice and, where communities choose to share it, ethnographic study.

Why it matters: Kivas provide some of the clearest architectural evidence for continuity between ancient Ancestral Pueblo communities and living Pueblo nations, supporting this chapter's broader argument against narratives of disappearance.

Potential misunderstanding: The presence of a kiva does not by itself tell historians the specific ceremonies, beliefs, or social groups associated with a particular ancient structure. General communal and ceremonial function is well supported; specific ancient ritual content generally is not, and should not be asserted with false certainty.

Section 18.7

Chaco Canyon and the Great-House World

Chaco Canyon, in the San Juan Basin of what is now northwestern New Mexico, contains one of the most extraordinary concentrations of monumental architecture in North America north of Mesoamerica. Between roughly the ninth and twelfth centuries, communities in and around the canyon built a series of great houses, multi-story masonry buildings containing dozens to hundreds of rooms, along with associated great kivas, plazas, and, in some cases, earthen berms and other constructed features.

Pueblo Bonito, the largest and most extensively studied of these great houses, eventually grew to include several hundred rooms across multiple stories, built up over more than two centuries through successive episodes of construction and renovation. Its masonry, featuring carefully coursed and often finely finished stonework, represents a level of architectural investment and planning well beyond that of ordinary contemporary habitations. Other major great houses within the canyon, including Chetro Ketl, Pueblo del Arroyo, and Una Vida, share broadly similar architectural characteristics while varying in size, layout, and construction history.

Excavations at Chaco's great houses have recovered substantial evidence of long-distance exchange and connection, including turquoise, much of it apparently sourced from mines outside the immediate region; scarlet macaws, tropical birds native to regions far to the south in Mesoamerica, evidently obtained through extended exchange networks; marine shell originating from the distant Pacific and Gulf coasts; and cacao residues identified in some ceramic vessels, again pointing toward connections with Mesoamerican societies examined in Chapter 14. These finds demonstrate that Chaco Canyon, despite its location in a demanding and, on its own, agriculturally limited setting, was deeply connected to exchange networks extending across much of the Southwest and, indirectly, into Mesoamerica.

Questions about Chaco's population and daily life remain genuinely debated among archaeologists. Some scholars have argued that the great houses housed relatively small resident populations, serving primarily as ceremonial, administrative, or storage centers used seasonally or periodically by a wider dispersed population, while others have argued for more substantial permanent residency, at least during particular periods. The relative scarcity of certain kinds of ordinary domestic debris, such as extensive trash middens proportional to the great houses' room counts, has been cited as evidence for lower permanent occupancy than the buildings' scale might otherwise suggest, though interpretations continue to be refined as new research is conducted. Rather than resolving this question with false certainty, this chapter presents Chaco Canyon's great-house world as a subject of active, ongoing archaeological investigation, examined further in this chapter's Historical Debate feature.

Section 18.8

Roads, Exchange, and the Chacoan Regional System

Chaco Canyon did not stand alone. Archaeologists have documented well over a hundred outlying great houses and associated communities across the San Juan Basin and beyond, sharing key architectural features with the canyon's own great houses, including formal masonry construction, great kivas, and, in some cases, connection to the road network described below. This wider network, often called the Chacoan regional system, extended Chaco's architectural and, apparently, social or ceremonial influence across a substantial portion of the northern Southwest during the system's period of greatest extent, roughly the eleventh and early twelfth centuries.

Among the most striking features of this system are the so-called Chacoan roads, unusually wide, carefully engineered linear features, in places cut directly into bedrock or bordered by constructed berms, radiating outward from Chaco Canyon toward outlying communities. Some road segments run for many miles in remarkably straight lines, sometimes traversing terrain that a purely practical transportation route would likely have avoided. This straightness, combined with the roads' considerable width relative to the modest volume of foot traffic the canyon's population would have generated, has led many archaeologists to conclude that at least some road segments served symbolic, ceremonial, or processional purposes in addition to, or instead of, ordinary transportation, though the relative weight of practical and symbolic functions likely varied by segment and remains a subject of interpretation rather than settled fact.

The precise nature of Chaco's authority over this wider network is similarly debated. Some interpretations emphasize a centralized political system, in which Chaco Canyon functioned as something like a capital exercising administrative or even coercive authority over outlying communities. Other interpretations emphasize a more decentralized ceremonial or pilgrimage system, in which outlying communities voluntarily maintained connections to Chaco for religious, social, or exchange purposes without necessarily submitting to centralized political control. Still other interpretations propose mixed or evolving models, in which Chaco's role changed over time or varied by region, exercising different kinds and degrees of influence over different outlying communities. This chapter does not resolve this debate, because the archaeological evidence itself does not currently allow a single confident resolution; rather, it presents the debate directly as an example of how historians and archaeologists must sometimes hold multiple plausible interpretations in view simultaneously while continuing to weigh new evidence.

Section 18.9

Regional Pueblo Worlds Beyond Chaco

Chaco Canyon's prominence, however extraordinary, should not obscure the fact that Ancestral Pueblo history unfolded simultaneously across multiple distinct regions, each following its own trajectory even during the period of greatest Chacoan influence. The Mesa Verde region of southwestern Colorado developed its own architectural and community traditions throughout this period, participating in some respects in wider regional exchange while maintaining distinctive local characteristics that would become especially prominent in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, examined in Section 18.10.

The Kayenta region of northeastern Arizona developed communities with their own distinctive pottery styles, architectural preferences, and settlement patterns, generally showing less direct architectural connection to the Chacoan great-house tradition than communities closer to the San Juan Basin. The Cibola area, associated with the drainage systems near the modern Zuni region, likewise developed along a partially independent trajectory, with connections to Chacoan-related communities alongside distinctive local traditions that would eventually contribute to the historically documented Zuni Pueblo world examined in Chapter 30.

The Chuska Mountains, west of Chaco Canyon, held particular importance within the broader regional system as a source of high-quality timber, used extensively in great-house construction throughout the San Juan Basin, and of certain stone materials used in tool production. Communities in the Chuska area participated actively in the exchange networks connecting the wider region, and, notably, some archaeological evidence suggests that population and architectural energy shifted toward this area as Chaco Canyon's own prominence began to recede in the mid-twelfth century.

Aztec Ruins, along the Animas River north of Chaco Canyon, represents a particularly instructive case of regional continuity and change. Built beginning in the early twelfth century, during the period when Chaco's influence was already beginning to shift, Aztec's great house incorporated Chacoan-style architecture and a substantial great kiva while also reflecting later modifications and continued use that extended past Chaco Canyon's own period of greatest prominence, illustrating how architectural traditions associated with Chaco persisted and adapted in other locations even as the canyon itself became less central. Communities along the Little Colorado River and its tributaries similarly developed their own local histories, contributing eventually to the population and cultural world of the Hopi Mesas, examined further in Section 18.12 and in Chapter 30.

Taken together, these regional histories demonstrate that Chacoan influence, however extensive, did not erase substantial regional diversity across the Ancestral Pueblo world. They therefore caution against assuming uniform centralized control extending evenly across every outlying community; as Section 18.8 has already emphasized, how much political authority Chaco actually exercised over particular outlying communities, as opposed to looser ceremonial, economic, or symbolic ties, remains genuinely debated. Multiple communities, in multiple regions, retained the capacity to make their own decisions about architecture, farming, exchange, and community organization, sometimes drawing on Chacoan models and sometimes developing independent traditions, a diversity that would prove significant when the major population movements of the late thirteenth century, examined in Section 18.11, redistributed people across this varied regional landscape.

Section 18.10

Aggregation, Mesa Verde, and the Thirteenth Century

As Chaco Canyon's regional prominence receded during the middle decades of the twelfth century, population and architectural energy in several areas of the northern Southwest, including the Mesa Verde region, moved toward a different pattern: the aggregation of population into larger, more densely built villages. This process unfolded gradually over more than a century before culminating, during the final decades of the thirteenth century, in the construction of the dramatic cliff dwellings for which the Mesa Verde region is most widely known today.

Throughout much of the twelfth and early thirteenth centuries, most Mesa Verde-area communities lived in mesa-top villages, substantial masonry roomblocks and associated kivas built on the relatively level, arable mesa surfaces above the region's characteristic canyons. These villages grew larger over time in many locations, reflecting increasing population aggregation, a pattern also documented in several other Ancestral Pueblo regions during this general period. Aggregation of this kind can result from multiple overlapping factors, including population growth, the social and economic benefits of larger cooperative farming and labor groups, defensive considerations, and the consolidation of communities from a broader dispersed area into fewer, larger settlements.

Beginning in the late twelfth century and continuing through much of the thirteenth, during roughly the final seventy-five to one hundred years of the Mesa Verde region's occupation, many communities relocated from mesa-top villages into the alcoves and rock shelters along the region's canyon walls, constructing the cliff dwellings, including Cliff Palace, Long House, and numerous smaller sites, that have become the region's most famous architectural legacy. These cliff dwellings offered certain practical advantages, including some protection from weather, proximity to canyon-bottom water sources and arable land in some locations, and, plausibly, defensive benefits, though the relative importance of environmental, social, and defensive motivations for this specific architectural shift continues to be studied and debated. Some sites elsewhere on the Colorado Plateau from this general period show more direct evidence of interpersonal violence and conflict, suggesting that social tension, and in some cases outright violence, was a genuine feature of at least some communities' experience during the thirteenth century, though the scale and prevalence of such conflict varied by region and should not be generalized uncritically across the entire Ancestral Pueblo world.

Students should resist treating the Mesa Verde cliff dwellings as the "final" or "highest" stage of Ancestral Pueblo architectural achievement in any evolutionary sense. They represent a genuinely late and, within the Mesa Verde region's own long occupation, a comparatively brief architectural phase, concentrated within roughly the final century of that region's use by Ancestral Pueblo farming communities, and they were followed not by further architectural development in place but by the large-scale population movement examined in the next section.

Section 18.11

Migration and Reorganization

By the close of the thirteenth century, the northern San Juan region, including the Mesa Verde area, had been left by its farming population, in one of the best-documented episodes of population movement in North American archaeology. Tree-ring dating, which allows unusually precise chronological control in the arid Southwest, places a pronounced and prolonged dry period, sometimes called the "Great Drought," across roughly the final quarter of the thirteenth century, coinciding closely with the archaeological evidence for regional depopulation.

This chronological coincidence has led many historians and archaeologists to treat drought as a central factor behind the migration, and the evidence for a genuinely severe, sustained dry period during this window is strong. At the same time, responsible historical analysis should resist reducing a complex, region-wide population movement to a single environmental cause. Droughts of comparable or even greater severity occurred at other points in the region's history without producing comparable depopulation, suggesting that the late thirteenth-century movement resulted from drought interacting with other pressures that had been building for some time. These likely included long-term soil depletion and reduced agricultural productivity in areas farmed intensively for generations; resource competition among an aggregated population that had grown larger even as farming productivity faced increasing strain; social and political tension, in some cases escalating into violence, within and possibly among communities under stress; and, quite plausibly, religious, ideological, or leadership factors that are difficult to reconstruct from material evidence alone but that likely shaped how and why particular communities or groups chose to leave.

It is equally important to recognize that individual households and communities likely made their own decisions to leave for their own particular combinations of reasons, rather than experiencing a single uniform "push" that affected everyone identically at the same moment. Migration decisions of this kind are rarely made by an entire population simultaneously and for identical reasons; they typically unfold as a series of choices, made by different households and communities at different times, that cumulatively produce a large-scale demographic shift. This chapter therefore presents the late thirteenth-century Ancestral Pueblo migrations as a genuinely multi-causal historical event, one in which drought provided crucial environmental pressure without acting as a sole or simple explanation.

Cause and Effect

The Thirteenth-Century Migrations

Long-term causes

Generations of intensive farming placing cumulative stress on soils and water resources in the northern San Juan region; population growth and aggregation increasing demand on a finite agricultural base.

Short-term causes

The prolonged dry period of the late thirteenth century, documented through tree-ring evidence; evidence of social tension and, in some areas, violence during this same window.

Event

The gradual but ultimately near-total depopulation of the Mesa Verde region and much of the northern San Juan Basin by farming communities over the final decades of the thirteenth century.

Effects

Substantial population growth in the Rio Grande Valley, at Hopi, and in the Zuni and Acoma areas; the reorganization, rather than the ending, of Ancestral Pueblo community life, examined in Section 18.12.

Historical complexity: No single cause fully explains this migration. Attributing it to drought alone ignores comparable earlier droughts that did not produce comparable depopulation; attributing it purely to social conflict ignores the strong independent evidence for severe environmental stress. Historians must hold multiple contributing factors together rather than selecting one as sufficient by itself.

Section 18.12

Pueblo Continuity and New Centers

The population that left the Mesa Verde region and much of the northern San Juan Basin during the final decades of the thirteenth century did not disappear. Archaeological evidence documents growing populations during this same period, and in the decades immediately following, in several areas farther south and east: the Rio Grande Valley of present-day New Mexico, the Hopi Mesas of northeastern Arizona, and the Zuni and Acoma areas of west-central New Mexico. Population growth in these receiving regions, along with pottery styles, architectural features, and other material traits showing connections to northern San Juan traditions, supports a direct historical relationship between the communities that left the Mesa Verde region and the communities that grew in these new centers.

This relationship is affirmed not only by archaeological evidence but by the oral traditions of contemporary Pueblo nations themselves, whose own historical and religious understanding connects their communities to migrations from earlier ancestral homelands, including areas within the region this chapter has examined. The National Park Service and many contemporary Pueblo communities explicitly describe the peoples of Mesa Verde, Chaco Canyon, and related sites as direct ancestors of the present-day Pueblo nations of Hopi, Zuni, Acoma, and the Rio Grande Valley, a broad ancestral relationship this textbook treats as well-supported and community-affirmed rather than a speculative hypothesis, even though the precise routes connecting particular ancient communities to particular present-day nations remain, in some cases, a subject of continuing archaeological and community study.

The new communities that grew in the Rio Grande Valley, at Hopi, and at Zuni and Acoma during the fourteenth century and afterward were not simple continuations of Mesa Verde or Chacoan village life transplanted intact to new locations. They developed their own distinctive architectural, agricultural, and social adaptations suited to their new environments, while carrying forward language, ceremonial knowledge, agricultural expertise, and community organization developed over the many centuries examined in this chapter. This combination of adaptation and continuity is entirely consistent with the pattern this textbook has emphasized since Chapter 1: Indigenous societies changed continuously while maintaining deep continuities of identity, knowledge, and community across even dramatic transformations.

This chapter therefore closes not with an ending but with a redirection. The story of Ancestral Pueblo farming, architecture, regional organization, and migration flows directly into the story of the historically documented Pueblo nations, alongside the Diné, Apache, and O'odham peoples of the wider Southwest, examined fully in Chapter 30. Readers should carry forward from this chapter the understanding that the builders of Chaco Canyon's great houses and Mesa Verde's cliff dwellings have living descendants today, that their departure from the northern San Juan region was a reorganization rather than a disappearance, and that the history of the Pueblo world continues well beyond the chronological close of this particular chapter.

Historical Method

How Do Archaeologists Recognize Migration?

Identifying a historical population movement in the archaeological record, rather than simply local decline or continuity in place, requires converging evidence from several independent sources, since no single kind of evidence is conclusive by itself.

Settlement evidence: A sharp, region-wide decline in the number and size of occupied sites, without corresponding evidence of a comparable population remaining through other means such as burials, points toward departure rather than simple population decline through mortality alone.

Material culture evidence: The appearance of pottery styles, architectural features, or other traits associated with one region in the archaeological record of another region, particularly when these appear as an addition to, rather than a gradual local development within, existing local traditions, can indicate the arrival of migrants carrying their own traditions.

Population and growth evidence: A documented increase in population, settlement size, or the number of new sites in a candidate receiving region, occurring at a time closely following depopulation in a candidate sending region, provides supporting circumstantial evidence, especially when timed closely and geographically plausible.

Environmental and chronological evidence: Tree-ring dating and other environmental proxies can establish whether depopulation coincided with an environmental stress event severe enough to plausibly contribute to a decision to relocate.

Oral tradition: Where living descendant communities maintain oral historical traditions describing migration from earlier ancestral homelands, this evidence, evaluated with the same interpretive care described in Chapter 1, can corroborate and enrich the archaeological and environmental record, and, in the Ancestral Pueblo case, does so significantly.

Why convergence matters: No single line of evidence proves migration by itself. Settlement decline alone could reflect population loss through disease or reduced fertility rather than movement; new pottery styles alone could reflect trade or local imitation rather than migration. Historians and archaeologists gain confidence in a migration interpretation specifically because multiple independent kinds of evidence point toward the same conclusion at the same time.

Material Evidence Window

Room 33, Pueblo Bonito

Site: Pueblo Bonito, Chaco Canyon, New Mexico · Context: A room excavated in 1896, during the Hyde Exploring Expedition's 1896–1899 excavations, within one of the oldest sections of the great house · Type: Architectural and burial context with associated material goods

Context: Room 33, located within an older, formative section of Pueblo Bonito, was found during the Hyde Exploring Expedition's excavations in the 1890s to contain human burials accompanied by an unusually large quantity of finely crafted material goods, including many thousands of turquoise beads and pendants, shell ornaments, and other worked materials, deposited over what appears to have been an extended period of use.

Analysis: The concentration of exceptionally fine, labor-intensive craft goods, along with materials sourced from considerable distances, has led researchers to consider the room's occupants and the individuals buried there as holding particular social, ceremonial, or leadership significance within Chacoan society, though the exact nature of that significance, whether primarily political, religious, hereditary, or some combination, remains debated. More recent ancient-DNA analysis of remains from this context found that nine successfully sampled individuals shared an identical mitochondrial lineage, while nuclear DNA recovered from six of those individuals identified close lineal, mother-line relationships among them across an extended span of time, a biological finding that researchers have generally accepted. The further interpretation that this pattern reflects a long-lived hereditary elite or political dynasty is a further inference built on top of that biological evidence, combined with archaeological and burial context, rather than something the genetic data demonstrates by itself. This research has also generated direct engagement and disagreement from some Pueblo communities and Indigenous scholars regarding the ethics of studying ancestral remains in this way.

Limitations: Material wealth in a burial context does not, by itself, establish a specific political system, such as hereditary kingship, nor does it reveal precise details about religious belief, ritual practice, or the specific social relationships among the individuals involved. Any interpretation must also weigh contemporary ethical concerns, addressed in Chapter 1's discussion of NAGPRA and repatriation, regarding the excavation, scientific study, and display of human remains and associated funerary objects without full engagement with, and consent of, culturally affiliated communities.

Perspective: This context has been studied primarily by non-Indigenous archaeologists and geneticists using methods and interpretive frameworks developed outside Pueblo communities. Contemporary Pueblo nations, who maintain their own understanding of ancestral sites and materials, have voiced perspectives on this research, including concerns about the ethics of genetic study of ancestral remains, that this chapter treats as directly relevant to how such evidence should be interpreted and used.

Historical Debate

What Was Chaco?

The question: Was Chaco Canyon primarily a political capital exercising centralized authority over a wide region, a ceremonial or pilgrimage center drawing periodic gatherings without centralized political control, an economic and exchange hub organizing the movement of goods across the region, or some combination of these functions that may have changed over time?

Interpretation A — Chaco as a centralized political system: Some archaeologists point to the scale of construction labor required for the great houses, the extent of the associated road network, and evidence of social differentiation, such as the material wealth documented in contexts like Room 33, as indicating a hierarchical political system capable of organizing labor and resources across a wide region.
Interpretation B — Chaco as a ceremonial or pilgrimage center: Other archaeologists emphasize the relatively modest evidence for permanent, dense residential occupation within the great houses relative to their size, the ceremonial associations of great kivas and certain road segments, and comparisons with other societies in which periodic large gatherings at a symbolically important center did not require centralized political rule over a surrounding population.
Interpretation C — A mixed or evolving system: Still other researchers argue that Chaco's role combined political, ceremonial, and economic elements in proportions that shifted over the system's roughly two centuries of prominence, with different outlying communities relating to the canyon in different ways and for different purposes.

Evidence: All three interpretations draw on the same basic body of evidence: great-house architecture and labor investment, the road network, great kivas, long-distance exchange goods, and burial contexts such as Room 33. They differ in how much political, as opposed to ceremonial or economic, weight they assign to this evidence.

Why it matters: How historians characterize Chaco affects broader questions about the scale and forms of political complexity that developed in North America north of Mesoamerica before sustained European contact, and about how much weight to give material wealth and architectural scale as evidence for centralized political authority versus other forms of social organization.

Student task: Using the evidence described in Sections 18.7 and 18.8 and the Material Evidence Window on Room 33, evaluate which interpretation you find most persuasive, and identify what additional evidence might help resolve the debate further.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Architecture Without a Single Evolutionary Ladder

What Changed

  • Domestic architecture shifted over time from pithouses to above-ground masonry rooms in many, though not all, communities
  • Settlement scale grew substantially in some regions, from small hamlets to the multi-story great houses of Chaco and the large aggregated villages of Mesa Verde
  • Population distribution across the Colorado Plateau shifted dramatically by the late thirteenth century, moving away from the northern San Juan region toward the Rio Grande Valley, Hopi, and Zuni areas
  • The relative regional prominence of different areas shifted over time, from Chaco Canyon's centrality in the eleventh and early twelfth centuries to Mesa Verde's prominence in the twelfth and thirteenth

What Stayed the Same

  • Maize, beans, and squash remained the agricultural foundation of community life throughout the period this chapter examines
  • Kivas and communal gathering spaces persisted in some form across every architectural period, connecting ancient practice to living Pueblo tradition
  • Communities continued to manage agricultural risk through storage, water control, and dietary diversification throughout every phase of settlement change
  • Identity, knowledge, and community organization carried forward through migration rather than being erased by it

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both change and continuity prevents treating Ancestral Pueblo architectural history as a simple ladder of progress ending at cliff dwellings, and instead reveals a long, adaptive history whose final chapters, examined in Chapter 30, are still being written by living Pueblo nations today.

Everyday Life and Social History

Farming, Household Labor, and Community Life

Behind the monumental architecture of Chaco Canyon and the dramatic cliff dwellings of Mesa Verde lay the ordinary, essential labor of Ancestral Pueblo households: planting and tending scattered fields across varied terrain, hauling water, grinding maize on stone metates, weaving cotton and yucca fiber into cloth and cordage, firing pottery, hunting small game and gathering wild plant foods to supplement cultivated crops, and raising children who would inherit both practical farming knowledge and the community's ceremonial and social traditions.

Households were likely organized around extended kin groups, sharing labor for tasks such as planting, harvesting, and construction that exceeded what a single small family could accomplish alone. Roomblocks and their associated storage rooms suggest that households maintained both privacy for daily domestic life and connection to a wider community through shared plazas, kivas, and, at the largest sites, great kivas used for gatherings beyond the household level. Skeletal evidence from various sites indicates that agricultural labor, including grinding maize, left physical traces on the bodies of those who performed it repeatedly over a lifetime, a reminder that the achievements examined in this chapter rested on sustained, demanding physical work performed by ordinary people whose individual names and experiences are, for the most part, not recoverable from the archaeological record, even though the patterns of their labor are.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced the long history of the communities archaeologists group under the label Ancestral Pueblo, farming societies of the Colorado Plateau and Four Corners region whose settlement, architecture, and community organization changed continuously across roughly a thousand years. The chapter began by defining "Ancestral Pueblo" carefully as a broad archaeological category encompassing diverse regional communities, and by explaining why "Anasazi," an outside term with contested connotations, is generally avoided in favor of terminology that recognizes these communities' direct ancestral connection to living Pueblo nations.

The demanding, variable environment of the Colorado Plateau shaped every aspect of this history. Farmers developed a diverse toolkit of dry-farming techniques, including check dams, terracing, water-control gardens, and careful site selection, to manage an unpredictable combination of winter snowmelt and localized summer rainfall, while storage and dietary diversification provided buffers against the inevitable poor harvest years.

Architecturally, this chapter traced a genuine long-term trend from pithouse villages toward above-ground masonry construction, while insisting that this trend was neither uniform nor a simple evolutionary ladder. Pithouses persisted alongside above-ground rooms for generations, taking on increasingly specialized communal functions that anticipated the later development of the kiva, a structure this chapter examined both for its clear communal and ceremonial significance and for the interpretive caution historians must exercise when attributing specific ancient beliefs to it.

Chaco Canyon received sustained attention as the most architecturally spectacular and extensively studied phase of Ancestral Pueblo history, its great houses, exchange connections extending as far as Mesoamerica, and associated road network representing a genuinely extraordinary regional achievement. At the same time, the chapter treated Chaco's precise political and ceremonial character as an open scholarly question rather than a settled fact, presenting competing interpretations fairly through this chapter's Historical Debate feature.

The chapter emphasized that Ancestral Pueblo history was never centered on Chaco alone. Mesa Verde, Kayenta, Cibola, the Chuska Mountains, and communities along the Little Colorado River each followed partially independent regional trajectories, a diversity essential to understanding how population redistributed itself during the major migrations of the late thirteenth century.

The chapter examined the growth of large aggregated villages and, in the Mesa Verde region, the construction of cliff dwellings during a comparatively brief final phase of that region's occupation, followed by the depopulation of the northern San Juan region by the century's end. This depopulation, this chapter insisted, resulted from multiple interacting causes, including severe and prolonged drought, long-term agricultural strain, and social tension, rather than any single explanatory factor.

Finally, the chapter traced the redirection of population toward the Rio Grande Valley, the Hopi Mesas, and the Zuni and Acoma areas, emphasizing that this migration represented reorganization and continuity rather than disappearance. Archaeological evidence, corroborated by the oral traditions of contemporary Pueblo nations, establishes a direct historical connection between the builders of Chaco Canyon and Mesa Verde and the living Pueblo peoples whose fuller history is examined in Chapter 30.

Read alongside Chapters 16 and 17, this chapter completes a three-part portrait of Southwestern agricultural societies before sustained European contact: Mogollon communities adapting to highland environments, Hohokam communities engineering an irrigated desert economy, and Ancestral Pueblo communities developing dry-farming strategies, architectural traditions, and a far-reaching regional system on the Colorado Plateau. Each trajectory demonstrates that infrastructure, monumental architecture, and regional exchange networks do not automatically reveal a single, simple form of political organization, and that population movement and settlement abandonment do not equal cultural disappearance.

These lessons carry forward directly into the remainder of this textbook's treatment of the Southwest, and they anticipate the central argument of Chapter 30: that the historically documented Pueblo nations, alongside the Diné, Apache, and O'odham peoples, are the continuing inheritors of a long, adaptive, and still-unfolding regional history rather than the successors to a vanished ancient civilization.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Ancestral Pueblo
The archaeological and historical term for diverse farming communities of the Colorado Plateau and Four Corners region, ancestral to contemporary Pueblo nations.
Anasazi
An older term derived from Navajo, generally avoided in current scholarship because it originates outside the communities whose ancestors it names.
Colorado Plateau
The upland region of canyons, mesas, and drainages centered on the Four Corners area, the primary setting of Ancestral Pueblo history.
Basketmaker period
The earlier phase of Ancestral Pueblo history, marked by pithouse villages, developing agriculture, and the gradual adoption of pottery and the bow and arrow.
Pueblo period
The later phase of Ancestral Pueblo history, marked by increasing above-ground masonry construction, larger villages, and, in some areas, great houses and cliff dwellings.
Pithouse
A semi-subterranean dwelling with a wooden and earthen superstructure, the characteristic domestic structure of the Basketmaker period.
Roomblock
A connected arrangement of above-ground rooms, typically including living and storage space, organized around plazas.
Kiva
A round or rectangular subterranean or semi-subterranean room used for domestic, communal, and ceremonial purposes.
Great kiva
A large-scale kiva apparently used for gatherings serving multiple households or communities.
Sipapu
A small hole in a kiva floor carrying cosmological significance in later historic Pueblo religious practice.
Great house
A large, multi-story masonry building, exemplified by Chaco Canyon's Pueblo Bonito, representing substantial architectural investment.
Chacoan regional system
The network of great houses, roads, and associated communities connected to Chaco Canyon between roughly the ninth and twelfth centuries.
Chacoan roads
Unusually wide, engineered linear features radiating from Chaco Canyon, whose full function remains debated between practical and ceremonial interpretations.
Dry farming
Cultivation relying primarily on direct rainfall, supported by active techniques for capturing and conserving available moisture.
Check dam
A low rock or earthen structure built across a small drainage to slow runoff and build up arable, moisture-retentive soil.
Waffle garden
A small planting plot bordered by low ridges that hold water around individual plants.
Aggregation
The process by which dispersed households or small communities consolidate into larger, denser settlements.
Cliff dwelling
A structure built within a natural rock alcove or shelter, exemplified by Mesa Verde's Cliff Palace and Long House, representing a late and comparatively brief architectural phase.
Tree-ring dating (dendrochronology)
A dating method using patterns in tree growth rings, providing unusually precise chronology in the arid Southwest.
The "Great Drought"
A severe, prolonged dry period documented through tree-ring evidence across roughly the final quarter of the thirteenth century.
Migration (archaeological)
Population movement identified through converging settlement, material culture, chronological, and, where available, oral historical evidence.
Regional system
A network of communities connected through shared architecture, exchange, and, potentially, political or ceremonial ties, exemplified by the Chacoan system.
Cultural continuity
The persistence of identity, knowledge, and practice across periods of significant change, central to this chapter's treatment of Ancestral Pueblo migration.
Turquoise exchange
The long-distance movement of turquoise into Chaco Canyon, evidence of the canyon's connection to wider exchange networks.
Archaeological culture
A pattern of shared material traits identified by archaeologists, which does not automatically correspond to a single people, language, or political system.

Major People and Places

Identification & Significance

Chaco Canyon
Center of an extensive regional system of great houses and associated communities across the San Juan Basin, examined in Sections 18.7–18.8.
Pueblo Bonito
The largest and most extensively studied Chacoan great house, discussed in Section 18.7 and this chapter's Material Evidence Window.
Chetro Ketl, Pueblo del Arroyo, Una Vida
Additional major great houses within Chaco Canyon, sharing broad architectural characteristics with Pueblo Bonito while varying in scale and history.
Mesa Verde region
Home to mesa-top villages and, in the region's final occupation phase, cliff dwellings including Cliff Palace and Long House, examined in Section 18.10.
Aztec Ruins
A great-house site along the Animas River reflecting Chacoan architectural influence extending past Chaco Canyon's own period of greatest prominence, examined in Section 18.9.
Chuska Mountains
A source of timber and stone materials for Chacoan construction, and an area of growing importance as Chaco's prominence receded, examined in Section 18.9.
Kayenta and Cibola regions
Distinct regional Ancestral Pueblo traditions with their own architectural and material characteristics, examined in Section 18.9.
Rio Grande Valley, Hopi Mesas, Zuni and Acoma areas
Receiving regions of late-thirteenth-century migration, foundational to the historically documented Pueblo nations examined in Chapter 30.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has told a history that resists two of the most persistent misunderstandings surrounding the Ancestral Pueblo world: the idea of a single unified ancient people, and the idea of a mysterious disappearance. Neither survives close examination of the evidence. The communities this chapter has studied were numerous, regionally diverse, and historically dynamic, developing distinctive farming strategies, architectural traditions, and forms of community and regional organization across many centuries, and their departure from the northern San Juan region at the close of the thirteenth century was a redirection of ongoing history rather than its ending.

The chapter's treatment of Chaco Canyon illustrates a broader lesson this textbook has emphasized since Chapter 1: monumental architecture and extensive regional networks do not automatically reveal a single, simple political system, and responsible historians must sometimes present genuine scholarly uncertainty rather than manufacturing false confidence. The chapter's treatment of the thirteenth-century migrations illustrates an equally important lesson: complex historical events, including population movements under environmental and social stress, virtually never result from a single cause, and historians must weigh multiple interacting factors together.

Most importantly, this chapter has insisted throughout that Ancestral Pueblo history connects directly to a living present. The Pueblo nations of the Rio Grande Valley, the Hopi Mesas, and the Zuni and Acoma communities are not merely inheritors of a distant, closed chapter of prehistory; they are the continuing communities whose ancestors built Chaco Canyon's great houses and Mesa Verde's cliff dwellings, adapted to a demanding environment across many centuries, and, when conditions required it, moved to new homelands while carrying forward the knowledge, language, and ceremonial traditions this chapter has traced.

The story begun in this chapter continues directly into Chapter 30, where the Pueblo nations, alongside the Diné, Apache, and O'odham peoples of the wider Southwest, appear as historically documented political communities on the eve of sustained European contact. Readers who understand the depth, complexity, and continuity of the history examined in this chapter are well prepared to recognize those later Pueblo nations not as the successors to a vanished civilization, but as living descendant peoples whose histories continue the Ancestral Pueblo past through migration, adaptation, and community transformation, a long history this chapter has only begun to tell.
