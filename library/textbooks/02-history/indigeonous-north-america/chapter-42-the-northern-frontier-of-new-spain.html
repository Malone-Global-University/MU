The Northern Frontier of New Spain

Indigenous Peoples of North America: A Continental History

Chapter Opening

In 1555, in Spain, a Caxcan leader named Francisco Tenamaztle placed before the Crown a petition arguing that the war he had fought fifteen years earlier in western Mexico was a lawful defense of his people and that the enslavement and dispossession they suffered were crimes. Few documents so directly record an Indigenous interpretation of the colonial north, and few show so clearly how much about that frontier was contested from its first decades.

Tenamaztle had been one of the principal figures in the Mixtón War of 1540 to 1542, when the Caxcan and neighboring peoples of Nueva Galicia rose against Spanish occupation, fortified mountain strongholds, and for a time endangered the entire Spanish position in western Mexico. By the time his petition was prepared, he had surrendered, been taken from his homeland, and found himself arguing his case in the courts and councils of the empire that had invaded it. His document survives because Spanish officials preserved it, and it was shaped by Spanish legal language and by the clerical advocates who helped him. It nevertheless gives historians something unusual: a claim, made in the language of imperial law, that Indigenous communities of the north possessed rights that the conquerors had violated.

This chapter follows the Spanish expansion into the vast region north and northwest of central Mexico, from the first invasions of western Mexico in the late 1520s to the end of the seventeenth century. That region included the semi-arid Mexican plateau, the Sierra Madre ranges, the river valleys of Sinaloa and Sonora, the deserts of Chihuahua and Coahuila, and, by 1598, the upper Río Grande. It was home to farming communities along rivers, dispersed settlements in mountain valleys, hunting and gathering peoples whose seasonal movements covered great distances, and societies whose economies combined several of these ways of life. Spanish observers, working from a few shared and often derogatory categories, described them with a vocabulary that concealed enormous differences. Part of the task of this chapter is to recover those differences.

The chapter's central argument is that northern New Spain was not simply an extension of the Spanish conquest of central Mexico. In the Valley of Mexico, Spaniards seized control of existing states with their tribute systems, cities, and concentrated populations. In the north, there was usually no single political structure to capture. Spanish authority had to be built community by community and valley by valley, through war, enslavement, negotiation, missionary work, and the placement of garrisons and settlements. The discovery of silver at Zacatecas in 1546 gave this expansion its economic engine, creating demand for land, water, food, livestock, transport, labor, and military security that spread outward from the mining districts and brought Spaniards into repeated conflict with Indigenous communities.

The outcome was a patchwork rather than a uniform conquest. Some peoples were incorporated into mining labor forces, mission towns, or Spanish-supervised pueblos. Others fought long wars, such as the Chichimeca War of the later sixteenth century or the Tepehuán uprising of 1616. Still others negotiated: Yaqui and Mayo communities, for example, shaped the terms of the missions that eventually appeared on their rivers. Indigenous allies from central Mexico, including Tlaxcalans, Otomí, and Mexica, were not only Spain's auxiliaries but settlers who bargained for privileges of their own. Even where Spanish institutions took root, Indigenous communities reshaped them, and large areas remained beyond Spanish control at the end of the seventeenth century.

The chapter draws on a varied and uneven evidentiary base. Spanish chronicles, military reports, mission histories, legal records, mining and tax documents, and the letters of viceroys and missionaries supply most of the written record. They were produced by people with stakes in the outcomes they described, and they often reduce Indigenous actions to rebellion, apostasy, or treachery. Archaeology, linguistics, ethnohistory, and the documents occasionally produced by Indigenous people themselves, along with the continuing historical knowledge of descendant communities such as the Yoeme (Yaqui), Rarámuri, Tohono O'odham, and Caxcan descendants, allow historians to read these sources critically and to reconstruct Indigenous motives and strategies more fully.

Chapter 42 occupies a specific place in this textbook. Chapters 38 through 40 treated the Caribbean invasion, the conquest of central Mexico, and the demographic catastrophe, and Chapter 41 examined Spanish expeditions and missions in Florida and the Southeast. This chapter turns to the northern margins of the Spanish dominion in Mexico. It introduces the Southwest as a field of early Spanish expeditions and ends with the Spanish foothold in New Mexico established in 1598, but the sustained Spanish and Pueblo history of New Mexico belongs to Chapter 43, and the Pueblo Revolt of 1680 to Chapter 44. The goal here is to explain the wider colonial system from which those histories emerged.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Describe the diversity of Indigenous societies, economies, and environments across northern Mexico and the Southwest before and during Spanish expansion.
  2. Explain why the northern frontier cannot be understood as a simple extension of the conquest of central Mexico.
  3. Trace the development of the northern silver economy from the 1546 discovery at Zacatecas and analyze how mining transformed demands for land, water, labor, transport, and security.
  4. Distinguish among the peoples called "Chichimecas" by Spaniards and explain why the term obscures more than it reveals.
  5. Analyze the Mixtón War, the Chichimeca War, and the Tepehuán uprising as products of Indigenous strategy and Spanish policy rather than as isolated "rebellions."
  6. Assess the roles of Indigenous allies and settlers from central Mexico, especially the Tlaxcalan migration of 1591, in the expansion of colonial settlement.
  7. Reconstruct the Coronado expedition and the Tiguex War from Spanish and Indigenous perspectives, noting the limits of the surviving evidence.
  8. Compare the institutions of the northern frontier, including missions, presidios, mining towns, and ranches, and explain how each depended on Indigenous communities.
  9. Differentiate forms of Indigenous labor, including enslavement, repartimiento, wage work, and community service, and explain why they must not be treated as one institution.
  10. Compare the western, northeastern, and northwestern frontiers, including Nueva Galicia, Nueva Vizcaya, Coahuila, Nuevo León, Sinaloa, Sonora, and Pimería Alta.
  11. Evaluate the roles of negotiation, diplomacy, and continuing Indigenous autonomy in shaping the limits of Spanish authority.

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  2. How did the demands of silver mining reshape Indigenous lands, labor, and political relationships far beyond the mines themselves?
  3. What does the label "Chichimeca" reveal about Spanish perceptions of Indigenous peoples, and what does it conceal?

  9. To what extent was northern New Spain a "conquered" territory around 1700?

Historical Context

By the time Spanish forces began moving north and west from the Valley of Mexico, the invasion of the Aztec (Mexica) dominion, examined in Chapter 39, had been completed in its principal military phase, and the Spanish Crown was building a colonial government. The Viceroyalty of New Spain was established in 1535 with Antonio de Mendoza as its first viceroy, and a high court, the audiencia, sat in Mexico City. Spanish settlers received grants of Indigenous labor and tribute known as encomiendas, and religious orders, principally Franciscans, Dominicans, and Augustinians, were extending missionary work among Indigenous communities of the center. The Society of Jesus, or Jesuits, arrived in New Spain in 1572 and would become the dominant mission order across much of the northwest.

Spanish expansion northward drew on this central Mexican experience but quickly departed from it. In Mesoamerica, Spaniards confronted dense populations organized in altepetl and other states that collected tribute and maintained recognized local rulers; colonial authority could be layered over these structures. North of the Mesoamerican heartland, the situation was different. Some communities, such as the Caxcan of the Juchipila valley or the farming peoples of the Sinaloa rivers, lived in substantial settlements with local leaders. Others were organized as bands or rancherías, groups of related families who moved seasonally or farmed dispersed fields. Even where leaders existed, their authority often rested on persuasion and kinship rather than on tribute and hereditary office, and a Spanish agreement with one leader did not bind neighbors. Spaniards often found no single capital to seize.

Environment mattered, though it did not determine outcomes. The high plateau of central and northern Mexico is semi-arid, cut by mountain ranges, and cross-grained by a few rivers. The Sierra Madre Occidental rises west of the plateau and drops toward the Pacific through deep canyons and river valleys. Farming was possible along rivers and in favorable valleys, while large areas depended on hunting and gathering. This ecological variation helps to explain why Indigenous economies, political organization, and relationships with outsiders differed so widely. It also shaped Spanish interests: silver lay in the mountains and along the plateau's edges, and the ranching and farming that supplied the mines needed land and water that Indigenous communities already used.

Historians must also resist two opposite oversimplifications. One portrays the northern peoples as "wild" or "uncivilized" opponents of an expanding civilization, adopting the colonizers' own vocabulary. The other treats them as uniformly heroic resisters, overlooking the many Indigenous communities that allied with Spaniards, negotiated terms, or sought missions for their own reasons. Indigenous people in the north made strategic choices under severe pressure: they fought, bargained, migrated, entered Spanish towns, joined or opposed Spanish campaigns, and adopted selected elements of Christianity and European technology. The chapter treats these as historical decisions rather than as stages in a foregone conclusion.

Finally, the chapter relies on a vocabulary that Spaniards created and that modern scholars sometimes continue to use. "Chichimeca," "Tepehuán," "Tarahumara," and "Pima" are Spanish renderings, sometimes of Indigenous names and sometimes of terms applied by neighbors. Where possible, the chapter also uses endonyms, such as Rarámuri for the Tarahumara, Yoeme for the Yaqui, Yoreme for the Mayo, and O'odham for the Pima and Papago (Tohono O'odham) peoples. As in Chapter 1, the distinction between names peoples use for themselves and names given by outsiders is a basic historical skill.

Division I — Indigenous Northern Mexico and the First Spanish Expansion · Section 42.1

The Northern Frontier Was Not an Empty Land

Spanish writers of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries often described the lands north of the Valley of Mexico as wilderness, thinly peopled by "barbarians" who lived without towns, laws, or religion. The image served a purpose. It made it easier to describe Spanish expansion as the settlement of open country, to justify the seizure of land and the capture of people, and to present the missionaries' work as the introduction of order. It also reflected real unfamiliarity: Spaniards encountered peoples whose settlement patterns, political structures, and economies differed from the Mesoamerican states they had already learned to recognize.

The historical reality was a dense mosaic of homelands. In the west, the Caxcan, Tecuexe, Coca, and other peoples lived in the valleys and highlands of what became Nueva Galicia, practicing agriculture and organized into communities with local leaders. Along the rivers flowing from the Sierra Madre to the Gulf of California, Cahitan-speaking peoples, including the ancestors of the Yoeme (Yaqui) and Yoreme (Mayo), farmed floodplains with maize, beans, and squash, and maintained extensive kinship networks. In the Sierra Madre and its foothills lived peoples later known as Acaxee, Xixime, Tepehuán, Tarahumara (Rarámuri), and Concho. Farther north and west, the Ópata and O'odham lived in the river valleys and deserts of Sonora and southern Arizona. On the central plateau and in the northeast, a variety of mobile and semi-mobile peoples relied on hunting, gathering, and sometimes cultivation, and moved across large territories according to seasonal resources.

Treating this region as "empty" also ignores its political relationships. Indigenous peoples of the north fought, traded, intermarried, and formed alliances with each other long before Spaniards arrived. They knew the routes, resources, and rivalries of their regions, and many already had contact with Mesoamerican societies through exchange networks and migration. When Spaniards arrived, Indigenous leaders interpreted them within this existing political landscape, sometimes as potential allies against neighbors, sometimes as enemies, and sometimes as sources of new goods and technologies. The historical record shows Indigenous communities making such calculations repeatedly.

Another reason the "empty land" image fails is demographic. Historians cannot give reliable population totals for the sixteenth-century north, because evidence is scarce, settlement was dispersed, and epidemic disease, examined in Chapter 40, altered populations before or alongside Spanish documentation. What can be said is that many northern valleys were densely enough settled to support substantial farming populations, and that Spanish accounts of captured, enslaved, and tributary people imply large communities. Estimates vary considerably, and the chapter avoids false precision.

Frontier as a Historical Concept

The word "frontier" itself requires care. In older scholarship, a frontier was often imagined as a line separating a civilized zone from a wild one. For northern New Spain, historians now generally see frontiers as zones of interaction in which Spanish and Indigenous actors negotiated, fought, traded, and transformed each other. This chapter uses "frontier" in that sense, remaining aware that the term was a Spanish and later national construct. For the peoples who lived there, these regions were homelands, not margins.

Section 42.2

Deserts, Mountains, Rivers, and Indigenous Adaptations

Northern Mexico encompasses several contrasting environmental zones, and Indigenous economies varied accordingly. The Mexican plateau, or Altiplano, forms a broad upland between the Sierra Madre Occidental on the west and the Sierra Madre Oriental on the east. It is generally semi-arid, with seasonal rainfall that falls unevenly, and its vegetation includes grasslands, scrub, mesquite, agave, and prickly pear (nopal). Peoples of the plateau, among them those the Spaniards called Zacateco and Guachichil, used these plants extensively: agave and nopal fruits were important foods, and mesquite pods and various seeds supplemented hunting. Their seasonal movements allowed them to harvest resources as they ripened across different elevations and districts.

The Sierra Madre Occidental is a rugged mountain system cut by deep canyons. Its slopes and valleys supported farming communities in favorable locations, often with dispersed households rather than compact towns, and pine forests, game, and wild plants supplemented crops. Rivers descending toward the Pacific, such as the Fuerte, Mayo, and Yaqui, create fertile floodplains where Cahitan-speaking peoples practiced irrigation or floodwater farming. The Sonoran Desert to the northwest and the Chihuahuan Desert to the northeast are much drier. In those regions farming depended on rivers like the Sonora and the Conchos or on seasonal floodwaters, while desert peoples relied heavily on wild plants and hunting. The O'odham, for example, combined floodwater farming and wild plant gathering in the Sonoran Desert, adjusting their settlements to the rhythm of seasonal rains.

These adaptations meant that Spanish expectations about "civilized" settlement did not match local conditions. Spaniards typically judged a society by whether it lived in compact towns, cultivated fields, and followed a recognizable hierarchy. Many northern peoples lived in rancherías, clusters of households near fields that were separated from similar clusters by some distance. This pattern was well suited to uneven rainfall and variable water sources, but it made Spanish administration difficult, since a mission could not simply attach itself to a town. Missionaries and officials therefore pursued policies of reducción or congregación, the concentration of dispersed people in a central village. These policies disrupted settlement patterns, altered access to land, and often brought together groups that had no tradition of living together.

Environment should not be treated as destiny. The same landscapes supported many different political and economic arrangements, and Indigenous peoples modified their environments through burning, planting, water management, and resource use. Nor were ecological boundaries fixed: droughts and wetter periods could shift the viability of farming or the abundance of wild foods. Historians therefore treat environmental variation as a set of constraints and opportunities that Indigenous communities and Spaniards both had to navigate. As the chapter will show, Spanish expansion itself changed ecologies, since livestock, mining, deforestation for fuel and timber, and irrigation altered grazing lands and water supplies on which Indigenous communities depended.

Section 42.3

Indigenous Networks between Mesoamerica and the Southwest

Long before Spanish expeditions, northern Mexico was linked to Mesoamerica and the Southwest by exchange, migration, and shared technologies. Archaeologists have documented this connectivity across many centuries, as earlier volumes have explained, particularly in Chapter 14, which introduced Mesoamerican civilizations and their northern connections, and in Chapters 15 through 18, which treated the agricultural societies of the Southwest. This section summarizes only what students need to understand how these networks shaped the world Spaniards entered.

In what are now Zacatecas and Durango, farming communities of the Chalchihuites tradition built ceremonial and residential centers over several centuries, mined turquoise and other minerals, and participated in exchange with societies farther south. The monumental site of La Quemada in Zacatecas, occupied during an earlier period, shows that substantial, fortified or defended centers had existed on the northern edge of Mesoamerica. Scholars debate the political and cultural character of these societies and the reasons for the later decline or abandonment of some centers, and the evidence does not support the older idea of a simple "northern frontier" of Mesoamerica that advanced and retreated with climate alone. Interpretations emphasize a combination of environmental change, shifting trade, and local political developments.

Farther north, the site known as Paquimé, or Casas Grandes, in northwestern Chihuahua, flourished from roughly 1200 to the mid-fifteenth century, with dates and chronology still debated. It was a large settlement with distinctive adobe architecture, ball courts, water-management features, and evidence of extensive craft production and exchange. Archaeologists have found evidence there of scarlet macaws and copper bells that were obtained through long-distance networks reaching toward Mesoamerica, along with shell from the Pacific and Gulf of California, and turquoise. Paquimé's relationship to the Pueblo societies of the Southwest, to the Mogollon world discussed in Chapter 16, and to the societies that followed it remains a subject of research. The site illustrates that complex political and economic centers existed in the northern region several generations before the Spanish arrived.

These networks did not vanish. Maize varieties, ceramic styles, ritual ideas, and trade goods moved among communities in the centuries before Spanish contact, and the routes connecting the Pacific coast, the Sierra Madre valleys, and the Río Grande persisted as Indigenous travelers' paths. Spanish expeditions often depended on these paths and on Indigenous guides, and information circulated along them. Reports of towns and wealth in the north, which fed Spanish expeditions in the 1530s, drew partly on Indigenous accounts of real settlements and trade, a point revisited in Section 42.12.

The Mesoamerican frontier also had a political edge. The Tarascan (Purépecha) state in Michoacán, which Chapter 39 introduced in relation to the conquest, had maintained a border facing the peoples of the plateau, and the Mexica had used the word "Chichimeca" for peoples of the northern lands. The term carried complicated meanings in Nahuatl, including both disparaging references to unsettled northerners and respectful references to remote ancestors. Spaniards inherited the pejorative usage from their Nahua allies and informants and applied it widely, a point that Section 42.8 will develop.

Section 42.4

Nuño de Guzmán and the Conquest of Nueva Galicia

The first major Spanish advance into western Mexico was led by Nuño Beltrán de Guzmán, a Spanish official who had served as governor of Pánuco before becoming president of the first audiencia of New Spain in 1528. Guzmán was a rival of Hernando Cortés and an ambitious administrator, and in 1529 he set out with a force of Spaniards and a large number of Indigenous auxiliaries from central Mexico to conquer territory to the west and north. Contemporary and later accounts differ about the size of his army, and the sources on his campaign were produced largely by participants and by opponents who later testified against him. The numbers of Indigenous auxiliaries are therefore uncertain, though they apparently outnumbered the Spaniards considerably.

Guzmán's route passed through Michoacán, where he arrested Tangaxoan II, the ruler of the Tarascan state, who had earlier accepted Spanish authority. Tangaxoan II was tortured and executed in 1530, an act that Spanish officials later cited among the charges against Guzmán. The expedition then moved into the lands of the Tecuexe, Cazcan, and other peoples, and onward through Nayarit and Sinaloa to Culiacán, founding Spanish settlements, including Compostela and San Miguel de Culiacán, and claiming a province that Guzmán named Nueva Galicia. Spaniards seized people as slaves, branded them, and distributed or sold them. Guzmán had previously been involved in the shipment of enslaved Indigenous people from Pánuco, so enslavement was not an unintended side effect of his campaign but a feature of how he and his men sought profit.

Indigenous peoples responded in multiple ways. Some communities fled to mountains or other refuges. Others resisted, and others made accommodations with the invaders, accepting the demands of Spanish officers in an attempt to limit the damage. Central Mexican allies, who had their own reasons for joining the expedition, included Mexica and Tlaxcalan soldiers, porters, and servants. They were not passive tools: they sought booty, status, and a place in the new colonial order, and they sometimes protected or exploited the peoples they encountered. Their presence meant that the conquest of the west was also an episode in the continuing political history of central Mexican Indigenous peoples under Spanish rule, a theme developed in Section 42.10.

Spanish authorities in Mexico City eventually removed Guzmán from power. Complaints about his abuses reached the Crown, and he was recalled and later imprisoned in Spain, where he lived the rest of his life. But removing the conqueror did not undo his work. Spanish settlers remained, encomiendas had been granted, and Spanish settlements had been placed in hostile territory with few safeguards. The audiencia of Nueva Galicia was established in 1548 and later centered at Guadalajara, which had been founded in 1531 and moved several times before reaching its permanent site in 1542. The political consequences of Guzmán's invasion therefore included both a new Spanish jurisdiction and a legacy of grievance among Indigenous communities whose people had been enslaved, whose lands had been taken, and whose leaders had been humiliated or killed.

The conquest of Nueva Galicia left unsettled how far Spanish authority could extend, and at what cost. The numerous small Spanish settlements and the encomenderos who held tribute from nearby Indigenous communities depended on Indigenous labor and food. In the absence of strong colonial institutions, this dependence took violent forms. By the end of the 1530s, tensions in the region had reached a point at which a coordinated Indigenous rising became possible.

Section 42.5

The Mixtón War and Indigenous Resistance, 1540–1542

The Mixtón War, which takes its name from one of the mountain strongholds where fighting occurred, began in 1540 among the Caxcan and neighboring peoples of Nueva Galicia and lasted into 1542. It was the largest Indigenous rising against Spanish rule in western Mexico in the sixteenth century and one of the most serious challenges to the early Viceroyalty. The causes were rooted in the experience of the preceding decade: enslavement, forced labor, tribute demands, the destruction of local authority, and the removal of many of the region's Spanish and Indigenous military resources toward expeditions elsewhere. In 1540, Francisco Vázquez de Coronado, who was governor of Nueva Galicia at the time, left with his expedition for the north, which further reduced Spanish strength in the province.

Caxcan resistance combined military and religious elements. Contemporary accounts describe prophetic leaders and calls to abandon Christianity, and Indigenous communities withdrew to fortified positions on hilltops known as peñoles, such as those at Nochistlán and Mixtón. Francisco Tenamaztle emerged as a principal leader. The rising spread across communities that had not previously acted together, a sign that grievances were widespread and that Indigenous leaders could coordinate over some distance. Spanish accounts, which provide most of the narrative, emphasize the danger the revolt posed to Spanish settlements and describe attacks on Spaniards and on Indigenous allies.

A Spanish force marching to relieve the besieged settlers was defeated in 1541, and Pedro de Alvarado, the veteran conquistador who had joined the campaign, died after being crushed during the retreat at Nochistlán. The setback convinced Viceroy Mendoza that the revolt was a danger to the whole colony. In late 1541 he took the field himself with Spanish troops and a large force of Indigenous allies, including Mexica and Tlaxcalan warriors and others. Reports of the campaign describe the siege of several peñoles, with final assaults that overcame the defenders by storm or starvation. Numbers of dead, captured, and enslaved are reported inconsistently in the sources and cannot be stated reliably, though Spanish accounts agree that many captives were enslaved or executed and that the campaign was devastating for the Caxcan.

The Mixtón War illustrates several themes of northern history. Indigenous peoples could form large coalitions and use fortified terrain to resist cavalry and firearms for months. Spanish success depended heavily on Indigenous allies, whose presence reflects the complex alliances of the post-conquest world rather than a simple "Spanish versus Indian" divide. And the aftermath demonstrated the willingness of Spanish authorities to use enslavement as punishment, even as debates over Indigenous slavery were intensifying in Spain and the Crown was issuing the New Laws of 1542, which sought to restrict the enslavement of Indigenous people and the power of encomenderos. The laws' effects on the northern frontier were limited and uneven, and they made exceptions for peoples deemed rebellious, a loophole that frontier governors would use repeatedly.

Tenamaztle after the War

Tenamaztle himself evaded capture and continued resistance for several years, apparently from mountain refuges, before surrendering around 1551. He was then taken to Mexico City and sent to Spain. There he was supported by the Dominican friar Bartolomé de las Casas, a leading critic of Spanish treatment of Indigenous people, and in 1555 a petition was prepared in his name. His later fate is not securely documented. The document that he presented, examined below, makes his case before the very institutions that had authorized the conquest of his homeland.

Primary Source Window

Francisco Tenamaztle's Petition to the Crown, 1555

Creator: Francisco Tenamaztle, Caxcan leader, with the legal and clerical assistance of Bartolomé de las Casas and others · Date: 1555 · Type: Legal petition (memorial) to Spanish royal authorities, written in Spanish and surviving in Spanish colonial archives

Context: After surrendering in the early 1550s, Tenamaztle was taken to Spain, where he sought redress from the Crown and the Council of the Indies. His petition was composed in Spain in 1555 in the legal idiom of Spanish jurisprudence, with significant help from Las Casas, who had spent decades arguing that Spanish conquests and enslavement of Indigenous people were unjust. The surviving text has been published in modern editions and discussed by scholars of colonial Mexico and of the history of human rights, including Miguel León-Portilla.

Paraphrase of the document's main arguments (not a quotation): The petition, as modern scholars summarize it, argues that Tenamaztle and his people had never been subjects of the Spanish Crown when Spaniards invaded, that the enslavement, killing, and dispossession of Caxcan communities were unlawful, and that his own resistance was a legitimate defense of his people and homeland rather than rebellion. It asks that he be freed, that his people be released from servitude, and that responsibility for the abuses be recognized. The reasoning draws on arguments about natural law and just war that were being debated in Spain.

Analysis: The petition is valuable because it is one of the few sources in which an Indigenous leader of the northern wars confronts Spanish authorities in their own legal terms. It records, from the perspective of an Indigenous leader, grievances against enslavement and conquest that Spanish military sources describe as the cause of unrest only indirectly. It also shows that Indigenous leaders could understand and exploit the legal debates of the empire.

Limitations: The petition was prepared with substantial help from Spanish clerics and lawyers, and its language and legal arguments reflect Spanish and especially Las Casas's thinking. Historians cannot easily separate which words and arguments belong to Tenamaztle and which to his advocates. The document also represents the viewpoint of one leader and does not speak for all Caxcan or other communities, many of whom made different choices. It was produced for a particular legal purpose and must be read accordingly.

Perspective: The source presents an Indigenous leader's claim as mediated by a Spanish reformer's campaign. It does not represent Spanish encomenderos, colonial officials, or Indigenous allies who fought on the Spanish side in the war.

  4. What can historians learn from it about Indigenous understandings of conquest and resistance?

The Discovery of Silver and the Expansion to Zacatecas

In 1546, Spaniards led by Juan de Tolosa, in partnership with settlers including Cristóbal de Oñate, Diego de Ibarra, and Baltasar Temiño de Bañuelos, found rich silver deposits in the hills of Zacatecas. Traditional accounts say that local Zacateco people showed Tolosa the ore or that he learned of it through them. The details of the discovery are uncertain, as the earliest narratives are later and often romanticized, but the economic result is clear. Within a few years Zacatecas became a major mining district, and its success attracted Spanish settlers, merchants, miners, Indigenous workers from central Mexico, and enslaved Africans. Further discoveries followed in the 1550s at places such as Guanajuato, Fresnillo, and Sombrerete, creating a northern arc of mining districts.

A mining camp, known as a real de minas, was not simply a town built around a mine. It was an economic system that required timber for construction and fuel, water for processing ore, mules and oxen for transport, food for workers and animals, and capital for tools and, increasingly, for mercury. After the mid-1550s the amalgamation or "patio" process, developed in New Spain by Bartolomé de Medina, spread through the mining districts. It used mercury to extract silver from ore and made it possible to work lower-grade deposits economically, though it made miners dependent on mercury supplies from Almadén in Spain and, later, from Huancavelica in Peru, administered by the Crown. Mining output therefore depended on imperial supply chains as well as local labor.

Silver changed Spanish priorities. Earlier expeditions had sought treasure, slaves, and Indigenous tribute; now the north offered a steady and substantial source of revenue to the Crown through the royal fifth (quinto) and other levies, and to private investors. Spanish colonial officials began to regard securing the roads, supply routes, and agricultural hinterlands of the mining districts as an imperial priority. The principal route, the road linking Mexico City to Zacatecas, passed through country occupied by peoples who had not been conquered and who attacked wagon trains, as Section 42.9 will describe. The security of this road became a driving concern of colonial policy and a constant source of expense.

Silver also reshaped the geography of New Spain's economy. Mining districts needed food, so settlers established farms and cattle ranches in the surrounding country, mainly on land that had belonged to or been used by Indigenous peoples. Merchants brought cloth, iron, mercury, and other goods north and carried silver south. Towns grew along the road, and the political power of northern mining magnates, such as the Oñate and Ibarra families, rose. These were not merely entrepreneurs: they were also political and military leaders, financing expeditions and holding offices. As one example, Cristóbal de Oñate's son Juan would later lead the colonizing expedition to New Mexico.

For Indigenous peoples of the plateau, the new mining districts meant intrusion into territories they had used for generations. The Zacatecos, Guachichiles, and others saw ranches and mines established on lands that supplied their foods and water, and wagon trains carrying food, cloth, and livestock passed through their country. The conflict that followed was not an irrational response to civilization, as some Spanish authors portrayed it, but a reaction to the occupation of land and the disruption of established ways of life.

Section 42.7

Mining Camps, Labor, Ranching, and Indigenous Lands

The northern mining districts needed workers, and the sources of labor differed from the Valley of Mexico. The local Indigenous population was relatively sparse and often hostile, and the central Mexican system of drafted village labor was less easily applied. Mine owners relied on several groups: free wage laborers, often Indigenous migrants from central Mexico and Michoacán who came for wages; enslaved Africans and, in some areas, enslaved Indigenous people; and, in places, drafted workers assigned through the repartimiento system. In the early decades of Zacatecas, wage labor appears to have been the dominant form for Indigenous workers, with high wages and the chance to retain a portion of ore in some cases drawing workers north. The reality was uneven and varied by district and period, and the working conditions, including mine collapse, toxic mercury exposure, and exhausting labor, were severe.

Historical Vocabulary
Repartimiento

Definition: In colonial Spanish America, repartimiento ("distribution" or "allotment") referred to systems in which officials assigned rotating drafts of Indigenous workers to Spanish employers or public works for fixed periods, usually with legally required wages. The term was also used for other allotments, including forced purchases of goods, so context matters.

Historical context: The repartimiento of labor developed in the sixteenth century as the Crown tried to replace encomienda tribute labor with a regulated system. In central Mexico it was often called the cuatequil. In the northern mining districts, it was used unevenly and often alongside or in place of free wage labor, and its importance increased in some mining regions in the seventeenth century, including parts of Nueva Vizcaya.

Why it matters: Repartimiento was not the same as slavery, nor the same as free wage labor. It was a coerced but legally regulated draft in which Indigenous communities and leaders had some ability to negotiate or contest the terms.

Potential misunderstanding: Students should not use "repartimiento" as a synonym for encomienda, enslavement, or any forced labor in general. Section 42.19 examines the distinctions.

Ranching and farming expanded together with mining. Spaniards established cattle, sheep, and horse ranches, known as estancias, and larger agricultural and pastoral properties known as haciendas, across the plateau. The introduction of European livestock transformed the landscape: cattle and sheep grazed grasslands that Indigenous peoples depended on for wild plants and game, and trampled or consumed cultivated fields. Livestock was also a resource that Indigenous people took, either by raiding for food or, increasingly, for use and exchange. Spanish writers described Indigenous attacks on herds as theft or banditry. From the perspective of communities whose land and food supplies were being displaced, such raids were also a response to occupation and a way of obtaining new resources. Chapter 49 will examine how horses spread across the continent and became a source of power for Indigenous peoples on the Plains.

Water and land were contested resources. The mines and the ore-processing works required large amounts of water. Farmers diverted streams. Ranchers sought pasture. Indigenous communities, which might have held rights through long use, found their access reduced, and Spanish legal mechanisms, such as land grants (mercedes) issued by viceroys and governors, favored Spanish applicants. The consequences varied: some Indigenous communities were pushed from fertile land, some became laborers on haciendas, and some used legal petitions to defend what they could. Evidence for these struggles comes largely from grants, litigation, and complaints, which tell us what Spaniards claimed and what Indigenous communities protested, but rarely give a full picture of lived experience.

Transport created its own labor system. Mules, muleteers, wagoners, and road-side inns supplied the mines, and Indigenous and mixed-ancestry workers staffed them. The northern road system, particularly the Camino Real de Tierra Adentro, would eventually link Mexico City with northern towns and, by 1598, with New Mexico. The same roads that carried silver and supplies became arteries of conflict when Indigenous fighters targeted convoys and ranches, which is why road security shaped Spanish strategy throughout the Chichimeca War.

Section 42.8

Who Were the Peoples Spaniards Called "Chichimecas"?

The Spanish word "Chichimeca" came from Nahuatl, and Spaniards used it to designate the many peoples of the northern plateau and its margins who did not live in the Mesoamerican manner. In Spanish usage it often meant hostile, uncivilized, or nomadic people, and it appeared in legal and military language justifying war. Modern scholars use "Chichimeca" with caution: it is a convenient shorthand for a set of related questions about the northern frontier, but it was not the name of a single nation, language, or polity.

Historical Vocabulary
Chichimeca

Definition: A Nahuatl-derived term applied by central Mexicans and then by Spaniards to peoples of the northern plateau and, loosely, to many northern peoples outside the Mesoamerican world. In Spanish colonial usage it often functioned as a broad label for groups considered unsettled and hostile.

Historical context: In Nahua tradition, "Chichimeca" could refer to both disparaged outsiders and revered ancestors, and some central Mexican peoples claimed Chichimec descent. Spanish officials adopted the negative usage during the sixteenth-century wars, using it in military reports and legal arguments for just war.

Why it matters: The term was part of the justification for war and enslavement and hid the specific names, languages, and political arrangements of the peoples concerned.

Potential misunderstanding: "Chichimeca" does not identify one people, and the Spanish description of them as purely nomadic hunters is oversimplified. Some groups practiced forms of cultivation, and many had well-defined territories. Historians prefer specific names, such as Guachichil, Zacateco, Guamare, and Pame, wherever the sources allow, while recognizing that those names were also often Spanish or neighboring-group renderings.

The principal groups usually identified in the sources are the Zacatecos, who lived around the district that became Zacatecas; the Guachichiles, who occupied a broad area across the northern and eastern plateau, including territory that later included San Luis Potosí; the Guamares, who lived farther south toward the Bajío; and the Pames, who inhabited the Sierra Gorda and adjacent areas and who included both more settled and more mobile communities. Sources also mention Cazcanes, Tecuexes, Otomí communities on the southern edge, and others. Each had its own language or dialect, territory, leaders, and alliances, and Spanish writers sometimes distinguished among them by dress, weapons, and customs, though their descriptions were shaped by hostility and by limited acquaintance.

Political organization on the plateau generally lacked the centralized, hereditary authority familiar to Spaniards from central Mexico. Leadership was typically based on kinship, reputation, and success in war, and bands could act independently or coordinate when interests aligned. This fluidity made Spanish diplomacy difficult but also made Indigenous resistance resilient: killing or capturing one leader did not end resistance, because authority was distributed. At the same time, it meant that agreements with one band could not be assumed to apply to others, a fact that later peace negotiators would need to recognize.

The economy of these peoples combined hunting, gathering, and in some cases cultivation. Their seasonal movements took advantage of plant harvests such as prickly pear and mesquite, and the large spaces of the plateau supported this pattern. The Spanish term "nomadic" captures part of this, but it can mislead if taken to imply aimlessness; movement followed seasonal and territorial patterns, and groups recognized territorial boundaries. Spanish ranching and mining directly threatened these patterns, which is one reason conflict arose along the mining roads.

Section 42.9

The Chichimeca War, c. 1550–1590

The Chichimeca War is the name conventionally given to the prolonged conflict between Spaniards and the peoples of the northern plateau from roughly 1550 to 1590. Historians treat the dates as approximate. Fighting was not continuous or uniform, and it consisted of many local episodes rather than a single campaign, but the period was marked by repeated attacks on roads, ranches, mining settlements, and Indigenous towns allied with Spaniards. The war began in the years after the Zacatecas discovery, when wagon trains and settlements along the road to the north became targets, and it intensified in the 1560s and 1570s.

Indigenous fighters used mobility, knowledge of the terrain, and the bow and arrow to great effect. They ambushed travelers and convoys, struck ranches and small settlements, took livestock, and then withdrew into rough country where Spanish cavalry and Indigenous allies could not easily follow. Spanish accounts, such as the later history of the war by the encomendero Gonzalo de las Casas, describe the fear and costs of the conflict, and they often emphasize cruelty by Indigenous fighters. The Spanish accounts also reveal that Spaniards used forts, escorts, and punitive expeditions, and that Spanish captains sometimes took captives and sold or distributed them as slaves.

Spanish policy in this period was often called "war by fire and blood" (guerra a fuego y a sangre). Under this policy, Spanish authorities and commentators treated the Chichimecas as rebels or enemies against whom just war could be waged, which in practice allowed captives to be enslaved. Viceroy Martín Enríquez, who governed from 1568 to 1580, strengthened the defense of the roads by establishing presidios, garrisoned forts, and escorts along the principal route, a policy that would shape frontier defense for centuries. The costs were considerable for the royal treasury, and the results were limited, since small garrisons could not control a vast open country.

Spaniards were not unanimous. Some clergy and officials argued that the war was unjust or counterproductive. The Augustinian friar Guillermo de Santa María, who had experience of the frontier, wrote against the harshness of the war and advocated peace based on gifts and fair treatment. Others, including many settlers and miners, favored continued force. The debate echoed the broader controversies of the Spanish empire over conquest, slavery, and Indigenous rights, and it was shaped by practical concerns about cost and effectiveness. By the 1580s, the evidence that force alone would not secure the roads was accumulating.

The war's human and economic costs are difficult to quantify. Spanish sources provide fragmentary reports of deaths, destroyed ranches, and captured goods, but they do not give reliable totals, and Indigenous casualties and displacements were rarely recorded. What can be said is that the conflict disrupted trade and settlement, increased the costs of defending the mining districts, and caused substantial suffering among Indigenous communities, including those enslaved or displaced. It also demonstrated that Spanish power on the plateau was limited by geography, logistics, and Indigenous resolve, which prepared the shift in strategy described in Sections 42.10 and 42.11.

Section 42.10

Indigenous Allies and the Northern Settlement Strategy

From the beginning, Spanish expansion in the north depended on Indigenous allies from central Mexico. Otomí, Tlaxcalan, Mexica, Purépecha, and other people served as soldiers, porters, guides, interpreters, farmers, and settlers. Their roles were varied and their motives differed. For many, service offered status, exemptions, and opportunities in the colonial system. For some, it reflected earlier rivalries with northern peoples or the habits of alliance formed during the conquest of central Mexico. Indigenous allies were not simply recruited; they negotiated, and their communities often sought benefits in return.

The Otomí are a prominent example. Otomí communities in the Bajío region, on the southern edge of the plateau, had lived in contact and conflict with Chichimec peoples. Otomí leaders such as Conín, who took the Christian name Fernando de Tapia, played a role in the early history of Querétaro, which tradition dates to 1531, although the earliest accounts are later and include legendary elements, so the details should be treated as tradition rather than established fact. Otomí soldiers and settlers continued to serve on the frontier in following decades.

The most significant organized program of Indigenous settlement was the migration of Tlaxcalan families to the north in 1591. Tlaxcala had been Spain's most important Indigenous ally in the conquest of central Mexico and had received privileges as a result. As Spanish officials, notably Viceroy Luis de Velasco II, sought ways to consolidate peace and stabilize the frontier, they negotiated with Tlaxcalan leaders to send hundreds of families to settle among the Chichimec peoples. Roughly four hundred families are usually mentioned, and they founded or joined communities at places including San Esteban de la Nueva Tlaxcala near Saltillo, and at Colotlán, Mexquitic, and Venado. The idea was that Tlaxcalan farmers and officials would provide an example of settled life, help to stabilize frontier towns, and serve as military reserves.

The agreements are important because they show Indigenous bargaining power. The Tlaxcalan settlers received privileges that reportedly included exemption from tribute and certain taxes, recognition of noble status for the settlers, land, the right to organize their own town governments, and protection from being placed in encomienda. These terms reflect Tlaxcalan leaders' assertion of their status and past service. At the same time, the migration served the objectives of colonial administrators, who intended the Tlaxcalan colonies as tools of pacification and Hispanicization. The two sets of aims overlapped but were not identical, and historians distinguish between what Tlaxcalan leaders and families sought, such as security, status, and autonomy, and what officials hoped to achieve.

The Indigenous settlers' relationship with the northern peoples was also complex. Tlaxcalan and other settlers lived among, near, or alongside Chichimec peoples, and relations included conflict, cooperation, intermarriage, and exchange. Over time, many northern Indigenous communities and settler communities became intertwined in ways that Spanish categories of "Indian," "Spaniard," and "Chichimeca" do not capture. The long-term consequences included the persistence of Nahua-speaking communities in the north and a layering of Indigenous histories in the region.

Section 42.11

Miguel Caldera, Negotiated Peace, and the Limits of Conquest

By the 1580s, Spanish officials had concluded that military force alone was not securing the frontier. The strategy that emerged, often summarized as "peace by purchase" (paz por compra), combined limited military pressure with negotiation, gifts of food, clothing, tools, and livestock, the establishment of missions and settlements, and the use of intermediaries who could speak with Indigenous leaders. It was costly, but the Crown and viceroys judged it cheaper than continued war. The approach was associated particularly with Viceroy Luis de Velasco II, who took office in 1590, and with frontier captains who had experience on the ground.

Miguel Caldera, born in Zacatecas around the late 1540s to a Spanish father and an Indigenous mother, is the best known of these captains. Sources identify his mother as Guachichil, and his bilingual and bicultural background helped him serve as an intermediary. In the late 1580s and early 1590s, Caldera negotiated with Guachichil and other leaders, offering provisions and settlement arrangements and reportedly traveling with small escorts rather than large armies. His role was not solitary: Franciscan and Jesuit missionaries, Indigenous interpreters, and Tlaxcalan settlers all contributed, and so did Indigenous leaders who accepted or rejected the terms. The discovery of silver at Cerro de San Pedro in 1592 led to the founding of San Luis Potosí, another sign that Spanish settlement was expanding even as peace arrangements were being built.

The peace settlements did not end Indigenous autonomy. Many Chichimec communities accepted provisions and missions on terms they judged advantageous, and others remained outside Spanish control or continued to raid. Some were incorporated into mission towns or labor systems, and some groups were gradually absorbed into the broader population of the north, often through migration, intermarriage, and the adoption of new livelihoods. The historical record is uneven, and the Spanish sources emphasize pacification as a success. Indigenous perspectives are mostly inferred from later developments, from petitions and legal records, and from archaeology.

Historical Debate

Did Negotiated Peace End the Chichimeca War, and Whose Victory Was It?

The question: Historians agree that large-scale fighting on the northern plateau declined in the 1590s. They differ on how to explain the change and on what it meant for Indigenous autonomy and Spanish sovereignty.

Interpretation A — Peace by purchase as effective Spanish statecraft: Some historians emphasize the Spanish shift from "fire and blood" to negotiation, gifts, missions, and settlement as a successful strategy, arguing that it ended a costly war through a better understanding of frontier conditions and the use of experienced intermediaries such as Caldera.
Interpretation B — Indigenous strategy, exhaustion, and adaptation: Other scholars emphasize Indigenous agency. On this view, communities accepted peace when it offered food, security, and recognition, after suffering from war, enslavement, and shifts in food supplies caused by livestock and settlement. Peace reflected their calculation of costs and benefits, and many retained significant control over their lives and movements.
Interpretation C — Peace as a continuation of colonization by other means: A third approach argues that peace arrangements facilitated the consolidation of Spanish settlement, missionary influence, and labor extraction, and that they eroded Indigenous communities through dependence, disease, and acculturation, even where overt violence declined. Enslavement and coerced labor did not vanish.

Evidence: The debate draws on viceregal correspondence, military reports, accounts by participants such as Gonzalo de las Casas, mission records, settlement agreements, and archaeological evidence. Each source type has limits: Spanish documents emphasize their own policies, Indigenous perspectives survive mostly indirectly, and archaeological evidence is difficult to link to specific named groups.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns interpretation and emphasis more than the basic chronology. Scholars differ on how much weight to give Spanish policy compared with Indigenous decisions, on how to evaluate peace in light of continued coercion, and on the scale of disease, demographic loss, and cultural change.

Why it matters: The answer shapes how historians describe the north: as a conquered region, a negotiated frontier, or an area in which Indigenous communities survived by adapting. These are not mutually exclusive, but they imply different narratives of agency and power.

Division III — The Spanish Expeditions into the Southwest · Section 42.12

Reports of Northern Wealth and the Search for Cíbola

While Spanish settlers were establishing mining and ranching districts in the west and on the plateau, a separate current of Spanish interest turned to the lands farther north, the region later called the Southwest. This interest was driven by reports, rumors, and hopes, and understanding how those reports arose requires distinguishing what Indigenous informants said from what Spaniards believed they heard.

In 1536, four survivors of the failed Narváez expedition to Florida reached Spanish-held territory in western Mexico after years of travel across the continent: Álvar Núñez Cabeza de Vaca, Alonso del Castillo Maldonado, Andrés Dorantes de Carranza, and Estebanico, an enslaved African man from Morocco. Chapter 41 examines the Narváez expedition, and its route does not need to be reconstructed here. What matters for this chapter is that the survivors encountered Spanish slave raiders in the region of Culiacán, a meeting that exposed the contrast between their experience of Indigenous hospitality and the violence of Spanish operations, and that they brought to Mexico City reports of what they had heard about peoples and settlements to the north. Their accounts were vague on specifics and mixed observation with hearsay, but they raised Spanish curiosity.

Viceroy Mendoza responded by sending the Franciscan friar Marcos de Niza in 1539, accompanied by Estebanico and Indigenous guides, to investigate. Estebanico traveled ahead of the friar and was killed, according to later accounts, at or near Háwikuh, one of the towns of the Zuni (A:shiwi) people in what is now western New Mexico. Fray Marcos claimed that he viewed a large city from a distance, and his report described Cíbola as wealthy and populous. Modern historians treat his account with considerable skepticism, noting its internal difficulties and the likelihood that he exaggerated what he saw, or saw little, and reported hearsay as fact.

"Cíbola" appears to derive from a Zuni-related name for the Zuni towns, which are real, substantial pueblos with multistory architecture. The Spanish error lay not in the existence of towns but in expectations of gold and silver. Spaniards had just conquered the Aztec and Inca states and assumed that more such treasures lay beyond the frontier. Indigenous informants, who may have described towns, trade goods, turquoise, and populations in terms meaningful to them, were heard through translators and through the filter of Spanish hopes. The idea of rich northern cities was a product of interpretation as much as of information.

The expectation also served political and personal interests. Viceroy Mendoza and ambitious captains competed for the right to conquer new lands, and the news from the north justified investment. The result was a large and expensive expedition in 1540, discussed in the next section, whose failure to find the expected treasure demonstrated the gap between rumor and reality and reshaped Spanish views of the region for decades.

Section 42.13

Coronado and the Expedition of 1540

Francisco Vázquez de Coronado, then governor of Nueva Galicia, led the expedition that departed from Compostela in early 1540. It included several hundred Spanish soldiers and a substantial number of Indigenous auxiliaries, servants, and others, along with livestock, including horses, cattle, and sheep. Estimates vary, and the sources suggest that Indigenous participants, many from central Mexico and Nueva Galicia, may have numbered well over a thousand, though precise counts are uncertain. These Indigenous participants worked as warriors, porters, herders, and interpreters, and their stories are known mostly through brief mentions in Spanish documents. The expedition's logistics depended on them.

The force reached the Zuni towns in July 1540 and attacked Háwikuh. The Zuni defenders resisted, and the Spaniards, who were hungry and short of supplies, took the town. The conquerors found that the pueblo did not hold the treasure Fray Marcos had described, a disappointment that reportedly led to anger against him. Coronado then sent detachments to explore neighboring regions, including the Hopi towns, where one party under García López de Cárdenas, guided by Hopi people, reached the rim of the Grand Canyon, and the Río Grande region, where Hernando de Alvarado visited Pecos and other pueblos. A Plains man called "the Turk" by Spaniards, who was then a captive at Pecos, told stories of a rich land called Quivira, and Spaniards followed these stories.

The Spanish relationship with Pueblo communities was shaped by the expedition's demands. The army needed food, clothing, and shelter, and it needed them in quantity during winter. Pueblo communities, which stored limited surpluses and had their own seasonal requirements, were pressed to provide supplies. Indigenous leaders responded in different ways: some provided food and guides, some tried to avoid the Spaniards, and some resisted. The expedition's reliance on requisition and force, and the failure to find the wealth Spaniards sought, produced violent confrontation at Tiguex, discussed in the next section.

The expedition illustrates the limits of Spanish knowledge and capacity in the north. Spaniards were operating at great distances from their bases, with limited ability to resupply, and they depended on the goodwill or compulsion of Indigenous communities. Their intelligence came through interpreters, often captives or auxiliaries, and was filtered by language and mutual suspicion. Spanish accounts, written during or after the expedition, record Indigenous peoples chiefly in relation to Spanish aims, so Indigenous motives, internal debates, and strategies must be inferred. Archaeologists have supplemented the documentary record by identifying sites associated with the expedition and with the affected Pueblo communities, and descendant communities preserve their own histories of the encounter.

Section 42.14

The Tiguex War and Pueblo Resistance

Tiguex refers to the Tiwa-speaking pueblos along the Río Grande in what is now central New Mexico, where Coronado established winter quarters in late 1540. The expedition's arrival coincided with the cold season and heavy demands on the pueblos for supplies. According to the account of the expedition member Pedro de Castañeda, Spaniards demanded large quantities of cloth and food and took clothing from residents in a manner that left them exposed to the cold, and they requisitioned housing, forcing residents out. Castañeda also reports that a Spanish soldier assaulted a Tiwa woman, an incident that heightened tensions. Pueblo people responded by taking some of the Spaniards' horses and resisting the occupation, and Coronado's forces moved against the pueblos.

The ensuing conflict, usually called the Tiguex War, lasted through the winter of 1540–1541 and into the spring. Spanish forces besieged and attacked several pueblos. According to Castañeda, a long siege at one of them, which he calls Moho, lasted for weeks before the Spaniards prevailed, and Spaniards killed and executed many people, including captives who had surrendered. The accounts differ on details and numbers, and the surviving descriptions come mostly from Spaniards who sought to justify their actions or to explain the failure of the expedition. Even so, the sources are clear that the Spaniards used severe violence, that Pueblo communities fought hard and defended their towns, and that many people fled or were killed.

The Pueblo response reflected political thinking, not simply panic. Communities coordinated, withdrew to defensible positions, and used diplomacy and resistance to protect themselves. The pueblos were separate political units, so responses varied, but the sources suggest that news of the violence spread and that communities elsewhere along the Río Grande became wary of the Spaniards. Resistance continued after the immediate fighting through avoidance, withholding of supplies, and the burden of hostility that the expedition faced. The Tiguex War therefore left a legacy in Pueblo memory and in the Spanish understanding that Pueblo towns could not be easily subdued.

The Tiguex War deserves consideration as a distinct episode of Pueblo history and not only as a chapter in the life of Coronado. It was the first major sustained conflict between Pueblo communities and Europeans, and it occurred decades before Spanish colonization in New Mexico. The memory of Spanish violence may have shaped how Pueblo communities received later expeditions and settlers, though the evidence for this chain of influence is indirect. Chapter 43 will examine the more sustained Spanish-Pueblo relationship that began after 1598, and Chapter 44 the revolt of 1680, which should be understood against this longer background of encounter and resistance.

Its criticism of Spanish behavior is notable and suggests that some participants regarded the conduct at Tiguex as both unjust and strategically damaging.

His account of Indigenous actions and motives is filtered through Spanish perspectives and may be inaccurate. Numbers of dead and details of specific events cannot be confirmed independently, and other expedition documents, including letters and testimonies, sometimes differ.

Perspective: The perspective is that of a Spanish soldier reflecting on a failed expedition. Pueblo perspectives are not represented directly; they survive mainly in oral tradition, archaeology, and the evidence of continuity in Pueblo communities.

Section 42.15

Quivira, the Great Plains, and the Failure of the Expedition

In the spring of 1541, Coronado left Tiguex with the main body of the expedition to seek Quivira, guided by the Turk and other informants. The route led east across the high plains. Spanish accounts describe a vast, nearly featureless grassland inhabited by bison and by peoples they called Querechos, who lived in portable hide tents and moved with the bison herds. Spaniards observed the use of dogs to carry loads and the way in which these peoples used every part of the animal. Scholars debate the identity of the Querechos and the later-mentioned Teyas, with the Querechos often linked to ancestral Apachean peoples and the Teyas to other Plains groups. The question matters for the longer histories treated in Chapter 49, on the transformation of the Plains by horses, and Chapter 51, on Apache worlds.

Spanish doubts about the Turk grew as the journey continued, and eventually it became clear that the expedition had been led far from the rich lands that had been promised. Coronado sent most of the force back toward the Río Grande and went on with a small party of Spaniards and allies to Quivira, in what is now central Kansas. There he found villages of farming and hunting peoples, generally associated by scholars with ancestors of the Wichita and related Caddoan-speaking peoples. The houses were grass-thatched, and the people raised maize, beans, and squash and hunted bison. They had no gold or silver, only copper and other goods that were valuable in local terms. The Turk was executed, apparently on suspicion that he had deliberately misled the Spaniards, though the sources do not allow certainty about his motives. Whether he lied, exaggerated, or was misunderstood remains uncertain.

Coronado returned to the Río Grande and then, after a serious injury and the exhaustion of supplies and morale, led the expedition back to New Spain in 1542. The Franciscan friar Juan de Padilla stayed in the region and was reportedly killed by Indigenous people. The Spanish government investigated Coronado's conduct, and the expedition was judged a failure in terms of its goals. It had, however, produced geographical knowledge, ethnographic descriptions, and a legacy of violence and suspicion.

For Indigenous peoples, the expedition's impact varied. In the Pueblo communities, it brought requisitioning, destruction, and loss of life, and it left a memory that shaped later relations. In the Plains, the encounter was brief but showed the Spaniards' interest in the region and introduced Plains peoples to Europeans, horses, and firearms in a limited way. Indigenous trade and information networks carried news of the expedition, and the goods and animals left behind may have had some influence, though historians caution against attributing the later spread of horses to this expedition. The spread of horses is a more complex process, tied to later Spanish settlements, as Chapter 49 explains.

The failure of the expedition also affected Spanish policy. For decades after 1542, the Crown and viceroys showed little interest in colonizing the Southwest. The wealth that mattered was in silver, and it was found in the mining districts of the plateau, not in the northern pueblos. Spanish colonization of New Mexico would resume only in 1598, in a different context shaped by mining capital, missionary ideals, and imperial rivalry. The Coronado episode thus stands both as a distinct encounter and as the start of a long Spanish and Pueblo history that the following chapters will trace.

Division IV — Constructing the Northern Colonial Frontier · Section 42.16

Nueva Vizcaya and the Expansion of Northern Settlement

While the Chichimeca War was still unfolding on the plateau, Spanish expansion pushed north and northwest into the region that became Nueva Vizcaya. Francisco de Ibarra, a nephew of the Zacatecas mining magnate Diego de Ibarra, led exploring and conquering expeditions from the mid-1550s into the valleys and mountains beyond Zacatecas. In 1562, he was named governor of the new province of Nueva Vizcaya, and in 1563 he founded Durango, then called Villa de Guadiana, as its principal Spanish town. The province, which was named for Ibarra's family's Basque homeland, covered a vast territory that included parts of present-day Durango, Chihuahua, and neighboring regions, though its boundaries were loosely defined and changed over time.

Mining drove the settlement of Nueva Vizcaya. Camps such as San Martín, Avino, and later Santa Bárbara, which was founded in the late sixteenth century and became an important base for further expansion, brought Spanish miners, merchants, and soldiers. The discovery of rich silver deposits at Parral in 1631 produced a major mining center that attracted settlers and laborers from across northern New Spain and became a focus of Rarámuri, Concho, and other Indigenous labor demands. Ranches and agricultural estates followed the mines, and mission frontiers advanced alongside them, particularly Jesuit missions among the Tepehuán, Tarahumara, and others after the late sixteenth century.

Colonial institutions were built gradually. A governor, local magistrates, and a modest bureaucracy handled administration, while the Crown and the audiencia at Guadalajara and later at Mexico City exercised distant oversight. The costs of defense and administration were high, and the settler population remained small relative to the Indigenous population. Spanish authority was strongest in and around mining towns and weakest in the mountains and desert margins. Settlers, officials, and missionaries often disagreed about how to deal with Indigenous communities, particularly over labor, and these disagreements shaped the course of colonial development.

Section 42.17

Coahuila, Nuevo Reino de León, and the Northeastern Frontier

The northeast of New Spain differed from the west and northwest in both environment and colonial development. The northeastern frontier encompassed varied territories in present-day Coahuila, Nuevo León, and Tamaulipas: the arid basins and mountain valleys of Coahuila, much of them within the Sierra Madre Oriental physiographic region, and the eastern slopes, river valleys, and Gulf coastal lowlands extending through Nuevo León and Tamaulipas. The peoples of these territories encountered sharply different environments and colonial pressures. Its Indigenous population consisted of numerous small, mobile bands of hunter-gatherers, whose names appear in the Spanish sources in great variety. Modern writers sometimes group them as "Coahuiltecans," but the label is a later scholarly convenience that covers a wide variety of languages and peoples and should not be read as a unified identity. Spanish sources recorded dozens of group names, often with uncertain relationships, and they described the peoples as lacking permanent settlements, although many had well-defined territories and seasonal movements.

Spanish settlement in the northeast was slower and less successful than in the west. Saltillo was founded in 1577 by Alberto del Canto as a Spanish town on the route between the plateau and the northeast. Luis de Carvajal y de la Cueva received a Crown grant in the 1570s for a colony called Nuevo Reino de León, and his settlement efforts in the 1580s were marked by conflict with Indigenous peoples and with other Spaniards, and he was eventually arrested by the Inquisition. The city of Monterrey, founded in 1596 by Diego de Montemayor, became the lasting Spanish center of Nuevo León. In Coahuila, earlier settlement attempts included Nuestra Señora de Guadalupe in 1674; Alonso de León made the definitive foundation of Santiago de la Monclova in 1689. These efforts reflected continuing Spanish interest in the region, including concern about the Gulf coast and rival European powers.

A distinctive feature of the northeast was the practice of slave raiding. Settlers in Nuevo León and neighboring areas seized Indigenous people from the small bands of the region and put them to work in mines, ranches, and households, sometimes selling them elsewhere. Spanish officials issued prohibitions, but enforcement was weak and settlers often defended the practice as necessary because of the shortage of labor and the supposed hostility of the peoples. The consequences for the Indigenous communities included deaths, family separation, the disruption of seasonal economies, and displacement. Some groups fled, some were absorbed into Spanish settlements, and some fought back.

Missions also developed in the northeast, and Franciscans established missions at various points in Coahuila and Nuevo León, often in connection with Tlaxcalan settlers and presidios. These missions faced difficulties: the small, mobile bands did not easily settle at mission sites, food supplies were uncertain, and Spanish demands and violence undermined trust. The northeast thus shows another form of northern colonial history, with fewer large-scale mining districts, many small bands, persistent slaving, and a frontier that remained thinly settled and contested well into the eighteenth century, when Apache and Comanche expansion, examined in Chapters 50 and 51, would transform the region again.

Section 42.18

Presidios, Missions, Mining Towns, and Ranches

Northern colonial expansion rested on four main kinds of institution, each with a different function and each dependent on Indigenous communities. Understanding how they fit together, and how they competed, is essential to understanding why Spanish authority took the forms it did.

Mining towns (reales de minas) concentrated Spanish settlers, merchants, enslaved Africans, free Indigenous workers, and officials around ore deposits. They generated silver revenue and anchored Spanish settlement. They also created intense demand for labor, food, wood, and water, and many of their problems, including labor shortages, disorder, and conflicts with neighboring Indigenous peoples, were exported to the countryside. Mining towns were typically the best-defended Spanish places, but they depended on supplies and protection from beyond their borders.

Ranches and farms (estancias and haciendas) supplied food, animals, hides, and tallow to the mines and towns. They occupied large areas of land, often through grants, and they employed Indigenous and mixed-ancestry workers as herders, farmhands, and laborers. Their expansion displaced Indigenous communities from land and water, and the livestock they raised changed the ecology of the region. They also became important sites of social change, where Indigenous workers from many origins lived and worked alongside each other and alongside Spaniards, mestizos, and Africans.

Historical Vocabulary
Presidio

Definition: A garrisoned fort or military post of the Spanish Crown, staffed by soldiers who received pay and, in many cases, grants or other supports. Presidios were often small, with a modest number of soldiers, and could include a chapel, living quarters, and storage.

Historical context: On the northern frontier, presidios appeared first along the roads linking the mining districts during the Chichimeca War in the 1570s and later were established in Sinaloa, Nueva Vizcaya, and other regions. Their soldiers escorted travelers, protected missions and settlements, and carried out punitive campaigns, often together with Indigenous auxiliaries.

Why it matters: Presidios were the military element of the colonial system. Their small size and limited reach explain why Spanish control was uneven, and their dependence on Indigenous allies and supplies shows how the frontier worked in practice.

Potential misunderstanding: A presidio was not necessarily a large fortress, and it did not guarantee security over the surrounding country. Many soldiers were stationed for years in isolated posts with limited resources.

Presidios provided military force. The small garrisons, together with militia and Indigenous allies, escorted convoys, guarded mission settlements, pursued raiders, and imposed punishments. Their effectiveness depended on the loyalty of auxiliaries, supply lines, and the willingness of nearby communities to cooperate or at least tolerate them. Presidio captains, such as Diego Martínez de Hurdaide in Sinaloa, could wield considerable personal influence and negotiating power, since they were often the principal representatives of Spanish authority in their districts.

Missions, run by Franciscans, Jesuits, and other orders, aimed to convert Indigenous people and to concentrate them in settled communities under missionary supervision. Missions received subsidies from the Crown, grew crops and raised livestock, and in some cases supplied surplus to Spanish settlements. They offered Indigenous communities some advantages, including protection from slave raiding and labor drafts in some circumstances, and they also imposed religious instruction, labor obligations, and changes to settlement and family life. Missionaries often clashed with settlers and miners over access to Indigenous labor, and these conflicts were a recurring feature of frontier politics.

The four institutions cooperated but also competed. Miners and ranchers wanted labor and land that missionaries sought to reserve for mission communities. Presidio captains wanted to maintain alliances with Indigenous leaders even when settlers demanded harsher action. Officials in Mexico City sought to balance these interests while minimizing expense. Indigenous communities, for their part, learned to exploit divisions: a community might seek missionary protection against a rancher, or ally with a presidio captain against a rival group. The institutional landscape was therefore not a unified system of control but an arena of negotiation.

Section 42.19

Indigenous Labor, Enslavement, and Colonial Economic Change

Indigenous labor underpinned the northern economy, but the institutions that organized it differed in law, practice, and consequences. Treating them as a single system of "forced labor" obscures the choices available to Indigenous communities and the different ways in which they could resist or negotiate.

Enslavement was the most severe form. In the early conquest of Nueva Galicia and in the Chichimeca War, Spaniards enslaved captives, claiming just war against rebels, and in the northeast slave raiding persisted into the seventeenth century. Crown legislation repeatedly sought to restrict or prohibit Indigenous enslavement while allowing exceptions for captives taken in just war, and enforcement on the frontier was weak. Enslaved Indigenous people were bought, sold, and held for life or for terms, and they were frequently taken far from their homes. Enslaved Africans were also present in the mining districts, and Chapter 53 examines systems of Indigenous slavery and captivity more broadly.

The encomienda, a grant of Indigenous tribute and labor to a Spanish settler, existed in the north but was less central than in central Mexico, because the sparse and mobile population offered fewer reliable tribute payers. Where it was granted, it could be a source of abuse. The repartimiento, discussed in Section 42.7, provided rotating drafts of workers on a regulated basis, and it grew in some regions, such as Nueva Vizcaya, in the seventeenth century as miners sought stable labor. Wage labor, by workers called naboríos or by other names, was important in the early mining districts, and in many places workers could move among employers. By the seventeenth century, some haciendas and mines used debt to attach workers, though historians debate how widespread and consequential debt dependence was, and it varied by region and period.

Mission communities generated their own labor systems. Missionaries organized communal fields and workshops, and they could draft Indigenous labor for building, farming, and herding, and for supporting the mission. Some missions supplied workers to nearby mines and ranches, and arrangements for labor were negotiated among missionaries, settlers, and Indigenous leaders. Indigenous communities sometimes preferred missions to the labor demands of private employers, and at other times resisted both.

These systems changed over time and differed in practice from the law. The consequences for Indigenous people included deaths from overwork, disease, and violence; separation from families; loss of land and subsistence resources; and also, for some, new skills, wages, and opportunities. Many Indigenous workers used legal petitions, flight, or negotiation to improve their conditions, and some Indigenous leaders became intermediaries in labor systems, gaining status and advantage. The economic transformation of the north therefore did not simply exploit passive victims; it created a complex and contested world in which Indigenous people pursued their own interests within severe constraints.

Section 42.20

Indigenous Diplomacy, Communities, and Colonial Authority

Indigenous communities of the north were not only objects of colonial policy; they were political actors who negotiated with Spanish authorities, used Spanish legal institutions, and exercised influence through their own leaders. The surviving documents record petitions to viceroys and audiencias, agreements with presidio captains, requests for missionaries, and complaints against abuses. They also record raids, flight, and revolt. These were not separate worlds but different tools within a spectrum of political action.

Leadership took many forms. In farming communities along the rivers, leaders might include headmen, elders, and religious specialists whose authority rested on kinship and reputation. In mission communities, Spaniards often appointed or recognized Indigenous officials, such as governors, mayors, and fiscales (church assistants), who served as intermediaries. These officials could use their positions to advance community interests or personal ones. Some became powerful figures, controlling access to labor and goods, and some led resistance when the demands of colonial authorities became unbearable. Spaniards frequently mistook the limited authority of Indigenous leaders for absolute power, which could lead to misunderstandings when a leader's agreement did not bind others.

Indigenous militias and auxiliaries were another channel of influence. Indigenous soldiers participated in Spanish campaigns, and leaders negotiated rewards for service, such as exemptions, goods, and recognition. Auxiliary forces gave Indigenous communities a stake in colonial military affairs, and they could shift the balance of local conflicts. At the same time, service in Spanish campaigns could generate resentment among those who were targets of the campaigns and could turn Indigenous groups against each other.

Religion also provided a space for negotiation and adaptation. Indigenous communities adopted aspects of Christianity, including ritual, festivals, and organizational forms such as confraternities, and combined them with existing beliefs and practices. Chapter 54 examines Native Christianity more broadly. In the north, mission communities often retained significant elements of their own religious life, sometimes openly and sometimes covertly, and religious specialists and prophets could lead resistance, as in the Tepehuán uprising discussed in Section 42.25.

Section 42.21

The Río Grande and the Opening of Spanish New Mexico

After the Coronado expedition, Spanish interest in the upper Río Grande lay largely dormant for decades. It revived in the 1580s as the mining frontier extended northward into Nueva Vizcaya and as individuals sought new fields for conquest and conversion. In 1581, a small party led by the Franciscan Agustín Rodríguez and the soldier Francisco Sánchez Chamuscado reached the Pueblo country, and in 1582 and 1583 Antonio de Espejo led another expedition. Their reports described the Pueblo towns and encouraged further plans. In 1590 and 1591, Gaspar Castaño de Sosa led an unauthorized colonizing expedition that was arrested by royal officials, a sign of the political contest over who could claim the region.

The effort that succeeded was led by Juan de Oñate, the son of the Zacatecas mining magnate Cristóbal de Oñate. Juan de Oñate negotiated a contract with the Crown in the mid-1590s and financed much of the venture with his family's wealth. In early 1598, he led several hundred settlers, soldiers, and missionaries, with wagons and livestock, north across the Chihuahuan Desert. In late April 1598, near the Río Grande at a point close to present-day El Paso, the expedition performed a formal ceremony claiming possession of the land for the Spanish Crown. The expedition then moved up the river and established headquarters in the Pueblo country, at a location near the confluence of the Río Grande and the Chama, in the summer of 1598.

The venture drew on resources and methods of northern New Spain: mining capital, ranching, mission orders, and the experience of frontier soldiers and Indigenous auxiliaries. It followed the Camino Real de Tierra Adentro, which tied New Mexico to the mining districts and the rest of the colony and which would remain its lifeline. The Spaniards who came north in 1598 brought the expectations formed in two generations of northern expansion, including the habit of demanding supplies and labor from Indigenous communities and of using force against resistance.

The history of Spanish New Mexico after 1598 belongs to the next chapters. Chapter 43 will examine the establishment of Spanish rule, missionization, labor systems, and Pueblo responses, including the conflict at Acoma in 1599. Chapter 44 will examine the Pueblo Revolt of 1680. What this chapter emphasizes is that New Mexico was both distinct, as a region with a dense farming population and a long Pueblo political history, and connected, as the northernmost extension of a colonial system that had already been developing for decades in northern Mexico. The connections ran through silver, roads, mission personnel, soldiers, and the habits and conflicts of the frontier.

Division V — The Northwestern Mission Frontier and Indigenous Resistance · Section 42.22

Indigenous Sinaloa and Sonora and the Arrival of the Jesuits

The Pacific slope of northwestern Mexico, stretching from the Sinaloa River northward through Sonora, was home to some of the densest and most productive farming communities of the north. Along the rivers Sinaloa, Fuerte, Mayo, and Yaqui, Cahitan-speaking peoples cultivated maize, beans, squash, and cotton on floodplains, supplemented by hunting, fishing, and gathering. Spanish sources name many groups, among them the Sinaloa, Ahome, Zuaque, Tehueco, Mayo, and Yaqui, and the lines between them were fluid, since they spoke closely related languages, intermarried, and formed shifting alliances and rivalries. Settlements were often strung along river valleys in rancherías rather than compact towns, and leadership rested on respected men with followings of kin and allies.

North and east of the Cahitan peoples lived others with their own histories. The Ópata and related Eudeve and Jova communities occupied valleys of central and northern Sonora. The O'odham lived farther north and west in what became Pimería Alta. In the Sierra Madre to the east lived the Acaxee, Xixime, Tepehuán, and Rarámuri. The region was divided by language, ecology, and political relationships, and its peoples did not share a common identity. Spanish observers, however, often described them with the same sweeping terms, and missionaries and officials planned on the assumption that similar methods would work everywhere.

Spaniards entered the area first as slave raiders and conquerors. Guzmán's expedition established San Miguel de Culiacán in 1531, and in the following decades Spaniards raided the region, taking captives. Francisco de Ibarra's expeditions in the 1560s explored the coast. Sustained Spanish presence began in 1591, when the Jesuits Gonzalo de Tapia and Martín Pérez started work among the Indigenous communities of Sinaloa, supported by a small military garrison, the presidio of San Felipe y Santiago de Sinaloa, established in the 1590s. Tapia was killed in 1594, and the Jesuits continued. Jesuit historians, notably Andrés Pérez de Ribas, later wrote detailed accounts of the mission frontier, which are the principal sources for the period.

The Jesuit missions developed differently from the Franciscan system that would emerge in New Mexico. In New Mexico, as Chapter 43 will show, Spanish settlers received encomiendas and lived near large existing Pueblo towns, and the Franciscans worked within a colonial society with a significant Spanish population. In the northwest, Jesuits typically operated with few Spanish settlers nearby, a small subsidy from the Crown, and the support of a limited garrison. They worked to concentrate dispersed rancherías into mission pueblos, to develop mission agriculture, and to control the flow of labor to Spanish employers, a policy that brought them into conflict with miners and ranchers. The Jesuits also relied on Indigenous officials and on negotiated relationships with leaders who had to be persuaded to accept missionaries. The result was a mission network whose character depended heavily on Indigenous choices.

Yaqui and Mayo Diplomacy and the Mission Communities

The Yoeme (Yaqui) and Yoreme (Mayo) communities on the lower Yaqui and Mayo rivers were among the largest and most productive of the northwest. Their experience of Spanish expansion shows that the eventual mission arrangements resulted from negotiation as much as from conquest. Spanish and Jesuit sources describe the Yaqui as formidable warriors who resisted Spanish forces from the Sinaloa presidio. According to those sources, expeditions under Captain Diego Martínez de Hurdaide, who commanded the Sinaloa presidio for a quarter-century from about 1600, met strong Yaqui resistance in the first decade of the seventeenth century, and the Yaqui, defending their territory with effective tactics, repelled the Spaniards. Around 1610, after these confrontations, Yaqui leaders sought peace.

The Yaqui peace deserves careful explanation. Jesuit and military accounts present it as a victory of Spanish diplomacy, but the circumstances suggest that Yaqui leaders had reasons of their own. They had defeated or deterred Spanish attacks, and they wished to end the conflict on favorable terms and to establish a relationship that would protect them from raids and enslavement. They also faced rivalries with other peoples and perhaps saw Spaniards as potential allies or sources of goods. The Mayo likewise entered into a peace with the Spaniards around the same time and received missionaries, with Jesuit work among the Mayo beginning around 1614. The Yaqui invited, or accepted, missionaries a few years later, and the Jesuits Andrés Pérez de Ribas and Tomás Basilio began work among them in 1617.

The Jesuits organized the Yaqui into eight mission towns on the river, typically listed as Bácum, Cócorit, Tórim, Vícam, Pótam, Ráhum, Huírivis, and Belem. These towns did not simply erase earlier Yaqui settlement: they reflected an agreement between Yaqui leaders and missionaries, and the Yaqui retained a strong sense of territorial and political unity, expressed in their later insistence on control of their eight pueblos and lands. Yaqui communities incorporated Catholic ritual, saints, and festival organization in ways that merged with and reshaped existing ceremonial life, and they developed leadership structures that combined mission offices with traditional authority. Yaqui and Mayo men also served as soldiers in Spanish campaigns, and they traveled to mines and Spanish towns for work, bringing wages and goods back to their communities.

The arrangement was not stable or free of conflict. Missionaries and settlers competed for labor and land, and the demands of the colonial economy, along with recurrent epidemics reported by Jesuit writers from the 1590s onward, placed pressure on communities. Yaqui and Mayo leaders protested abuses and, in later periods, rose against colonial authorities when they felt agreements were violated. Those later conflicts are beyond the scope of this chapter, but they illustrate that the settlement of the 1610s was a negotiated order that Indigenous communities defended and renegotiated. It was not a final conquest.

Andrés Pérez de Ribas on the Jesuit Missions of Sinaloa and the Yaqui

Creator: Andrés Pérez de Ribas, Jesuit missionary and later provincial · Date: Published in Madrid in 1645 · Type: Published mission history in Spanish, titled in short Historia de los triumphos de nuestra santa fee, with modern Spanish and English-language editions and translations of selected portions

Context: Pérez de Ribas worked in Sinaloa and among the Yaqui, and later wrote a long history celebrating the Jesuit missions of northwestern New Spain. It was published decades after many of the events it describes and was intended to demonstrate the success and sacrifice of the Society of Jesus.

Paraphrase of relevant content (not a quotation): The work describes Spanish military expeditions against the Yaqui, the Yaqui leaders' decision to seek peace, and the subsequent request for or acceptance of missionaries, and it portrays the founding of the Yaqui mission pueblos as a triumph of faith and patient diplomacy. It also includes descriptions of Indigenous customs, leaders, wars, and illnesses.

Analysis: The book is among the fullest accounts of the mission frontier and records names, events, and arrangements for which little other evidence survives. It indicates that Yaqui leaders played an active role in negotiating peace and in deciding whether to accept missionaries, and it records Indigenous leaders' arguments and strategies as the missionary understood them.

Limitations: Pérez de Ribas wrote to praise the Jesuit order and to persuade readers of its achievements. He interprets Indigenous actions in religious terms, minimizes Spanish violence and Jesuit conflicts with settlers, and presents conversion as the central motive of Indigenous leaders. The account cannot be used by itself to establish what Yaqui leaders thought or intended, and it must be compared with military reports, administrative records, and later Yaqui history.

Perspective: The source presents the viewpoint of a Jesuit missionary writing for a European audience. Yaqui perspectives appear only indirectly, and the perspectives of Spanish settlers who competed with the Jesuits are mostly represented as obstacles.

Section 42.24

Ópata and Other Indigenous Communities of Sonora

The Ópata, whom Spaniards also divided into Eudeve, Teguima, and related groups, lived in the river valleys of central and northern Sonora, including the Sonora, Moctezuma, and Bavispe rivers. They farmed irrigated and floodplain fields, maintained villages that were more compact than the rancherías of some neighbors, and participated in trade networks reaching toward the coast and the north. Spanish sources describe Ópata leaders and warriors and note alliances and rivalries with neighboring peoples. Their territory lay along important corridors connecting the Pacific slope with Pimería Alta and the northern interior.

Jesuit missions reached the Ópata later than they reached the Cahitan peoples, with significant expansion in the middle decades of the seventeenth century, and the mission network in Sonora grew over the following decades. The Ópata generally accepted missions and, in many cases, maintained relatively stable relationships with missionaries and Spanish authorities, though this should not be read as passive submission. Ópata leaders used mission offices, trade, and alliances to protect their communities and to shape their position in the colonial order. In later periods, Ópata soldiers served as auxiliaries against Apache and other raiders, a development tied to the eighteenth-century conflicts examined in Chapter 51.

Other communities in Sonora followed different paths. The Pima Bajo and Jova groups, the Seri on the coast, and communities of the northern valleys each had distinct experiences. Some resisted missions, some accepted them selectively, and some retreated or were displaced. The Seri, a coastal people of the Sonoran Desert shore with a distinctive language and economy based on fishing, hunting, and gathering, resisted Spanish incorporation for a long period, maintaining autonomy in their territory. Spanish sources frequently described such groups as hostile, but their position reflected the limits of Spanish power and their own political choices.

The variety of Sonoran experience illustrates the chapter's larger argument. There was no single route into colonial institutions. Ópata communities entered mission and settlement networks with relatively little sustained armed conflict, while Seri and some other groups remained largely outside Spanish control. Mining also developed unevenly, creating pockets of Spanish settlement and labor demand. The result was a mosaic of communities with different degrees of Spanish influence, a pattern that would persist into the eighteenth century and beyond.

Section 42.25

The Acaxee, Xixime, and Tepehuán Uprisings

The Sierra Madre Occidental, between the plateau mining districts and the Pacific slope, was home to peoples whose communities were often small, dispersed, and difficult for Spanish authorities to reach. The Acaxee and Xixime lived in mountain valleys and canyons west of Durango and in the Topia region, while the Tepehuán occupied lands in the highlands and valleys of southern Nueva Vizcaya. As mining districts expanded into the Sierra, particularly after the late sixteenth century, Spaniards sought labor and tried to concentrate dispersed communities near mines and missions, and this pressure generated conflict.

The Acaxee rose in 1601 in the mountains of the Sierra de San Andrés and Topia region, in response to Spanish labor demands, mission settlement policies, and the work of Jesuit missionaries such as Hernando de Santarén. The Spanish response included military campaigns and punishment of leaders, and the uprising was ultimately suppressed. The Xixime were involved in conflicts in the following decade. Spanish accounts of the Xixime often include allegations of ritual cannibalism, which historians treat with caution because such claims frequently served to justify conquest and the characterization of Indigenous peoples as barbarous, even though some practices of ritual violence may have existed. The Xixime and Acaxee communities were greatly reduced by war, disease, and displacement, and many survivors were relocated or absorbed into other populations.

The Tepehuán uprising, which began in November 1616, was among the most serious Indigenous rebellions in the history of northern New Spain. It began in the Tepehuán highlands with attacks on Spanish settlers, mining and ranching sites, and Jesuit missionaries, including the killing of Jesuits at Santiago Papasquiaro, and it spread across a wide area, with Tepehuán fighters threatening Durango itself. Spanish sources describe the event as a sudden and coordinated rising, and the evidence suggests that the Tepehuán leadership planned the action in advance and drew on a broad range of grievances, among them labor demands, forced concentration, the effects of disease, the intrusion of Spanish ranches, and hostility to missionary demands. Among figures named in Spanish and Jesuit sources were Quautlatas, associated with the religious mobilization preceding the uprising, and the military leader usually rendered Gogojito, Gogoxito, or Cogoxito. Their precise roles and names depend on partisan colonial testimony, and the uprising involved multiple local leaders rather than a single command.

The revolt had a religious dimension. Spanish accounts describe leaders who drew on spiritual authority, rejected baptism and Christian rites, and called for the removal of Spaniards and the restoration of Indigenous ways. Historians approach these descriptions critically: they reflect Spanish and Jesuit interpretations, and the original Tepehuán religious ideas can be recovered only partially. It is reasonable to say that the uprising had both political and religious aspects, and that it combined resistance to labor and land demands with defense of Indigenous religious and social life. The uprising also drew in other peoples and included Indigenous participants who fought on the Spanish side.

The Spanish response, led by Governor Gaspar de Alvear and others, involved raising troops, mobilizing allies, and conducting campaigns over several years. The main coordinated uprising was broken by about 1618, although resistance and the pursuit of surviving leaders, including Gogojito, continued toward 1620. Military defeat, executions, enslavement of captives, and negotiated accommodation marked the subsequent years. The consequences were severe for the Tepehuán: population declined through war and displacement, communities were broken up, and Spanish settlement and mission activity continued in the region. The uprising nevertheless demonstrated how vulnerable Spanish settlement was to coordinated Indigenous resistance, and it shaped frontier defense and missionary strategy in the following decades.

Section 42.26

Rarámuri Communities, Mining Expansion, and Continuing Revolt

The Rarámuri, known to Spaniards as Tarahumara, lived in the Sierra Madre of what is now Chihuahua, in dispersed settlements adapted to the rugged terrain of canyons and high valleys. They farmed maize and other crops, kept small livestock after European introduction, hunted, and gathered, and their political life was organized around local leaders and kin networks. Their neighbors included the Concho to the east, whose lands on the Conchos River were closer to Spanish settlements, and the Tepehuán to the south. Rarámuri communities are known today for the tradition of long-distance running, a cultural feature with deep roots, though historians of the colonial period concentrate on their political and economic histories.

Jesuit missionaries began working among the Rarámuri in the early seventeenth century, with initial contacts around 1607, but the pressure on Rarámuri communities increased sharply after the discovery of silver at Parral in 1631. Parral drew Spanish settlers, merchants, and laborers to the region, and miners and ranchers demanded Indigenous workers. Many Rarámuri and Concho communities were subjected to drafts and to pressures to move closer to Spanish towns and missions. The demands disrupted agricultural cycles and family life, and exposure to Spanish settlement increased the effects of disease and violence.

Resistance took many forms. Some communities withdrew into the canyons and high country, where Spanish control was weak. Others negotiated with missionaries and officials or entered the colonial economy as laborers while seeking to preserve land and autonomy. In the 1640s, Concho and other communities, along with nomadic peoples of the surrounding deserts, took part in conflicts with the Spanish, and in 1648 a major Rarámuri uprising occurred. Further uprisings followed in 1650 and 1652 and again in 1690 and 1696–1698, although the dates, participants, and geographic extent differ across documentary accounts. These were multiple conflicts involving distinct communities, not one uninterrupted rebellion. Spanish responses combined military campaigns, executions, relocation, and attempts at renewed mission work.

The repeated Rarámuri revolts show that Spanish control in the Sierra Madre was never secure. They also show how the pressures of mining and missions could interact. Mining created labor and land demands, missions encouraged concentration and religious change, and the combination produced grievances that Indigenous communities addressed through armed resistance as well as negotiation. At the same time, the Rarámuri were not a single political unit, and communities made different decisions. Some groups remained relatively free of Spanish control for generations, retreating into mountainous refuges, while others became part of mission communities and mining labor forces.

Cause and Effect

Mining, Missions, and Indigenous Resistance in the Sierra Madre

Long-term causes

Spanish occupation of Nueva Galicia and the plateau; the growth of silver mining and ranching; earlier legacies of enslavement and conflict; and Indigenous societies with dispersed settlement and strong local autonomy.

Short-term causes

New silver discoveries, such as Parral in 1631; labor drafts and concentration of dispersed communities; missionary efforts to change religious and social practice; epidemic disease; and the arrival of Spanish ranches and settlers.

Immediate trigger or context

Specific incidents, such as labor demands, punishments by missionaries or officials, or attacks on leaders, often triggered revolts, and the details varied by community and year.

Event or transformation

The Acaxee, Xixime, Tepehuán, Concho, and Rarámuri uprisings, as well as repeated smaller conflicts, between 1601 and the 1690s.

Immediate effects

Spanish deaths and destruction of missions and ranches; severe Indigenous casualties, enslavement, executions, and displacement; and new military measures, including additional presidios and campaigns.

Long-term consequences

Reorganized mission and labor systems; demographic decline; a persistent pattern of Indigenous refuge and resistance in the mountains; and continued negotiation between communities and colonial authorities.

Historical complexity: No single factor explains the revolts. Mining, missions, disease, local leadership, religion, and politics interacted, and each uprising had its own circumstances. Indigenous communities also differed in their responses, and some fought with the Spanish rather than against them.

Section 42.27

Pimería Alta, O'odham Peoples, and the Late Seventeenth-Century Frontier

Pimería Alta, or "Upper Pima Land," is the Spanish name for the region of northern Sonora and southern Arizona inhabited by the O'odham peoples. It included the Sobaipuri O'odham, who farmed along the San Pedro and Santa Cruz rivers; the Tohono O'odham, who lived in the arid desert and moved seasonally between field and wild-plant locations; the Akimel O'odham on the Gila and Salt rivers; and others, with local differences in language, subsistence, and political connections. These communities had long-standing exchange networks connecting them to Sonoran peoples, to the Gulf of California, and to the Pueblo and Hohokam-descended worlds treated earlier in this textbook.

In 1687, the Jesuit missionary Eusebio Francisco Kino, who was born in the Trentino region of northern Italy and had come to New Spain in 1681, established a mission at Cosari, which became Nuestra Señora de los Dolores, and began traveling widely among O'odham communities. Kino made repeated journeys, introduced cattle, wheat, and other European crops and animals, established or promoted missions and visitas, and, in the 1690s and early 1700s, explored routes to the Colorado River and the Gulf of California. He recorded his work in writings such as the memoir often called Favores celestiales, which are the principal source for the period. Kino's missions attracted some O'odham communities that were interested in new goods, livestock, and protection against enemies, and the missionary used gifts, diplomacy, and a willingness to travel to people's homes.

The 1695 uprising shows the limits of that diplomacy. In the spring of that year, O'odham communities, notably around Tubutama and in the region of Caborca, rose against Spanish and mission authority. They killed the Jesuit Francisco Javier Saeta and several others and attacked mission property. Spanish military forces responded, and the conflict was followed by negotiations in which Kino and others sought to restore peace. Historians cite a range of causes: resentment of labor demands, tensions between O'odham communities and Spanish settlers and soldiers, disputes arising from missionary punishments and demands, and the broader pressures of the colonial economy. Because the principal accounts come from missionaries and officials, the Indigenous leaders' own motives are not fully known.

Kino's own narratives portray mission growth as a series of conversions and miracles, and they treat conflict as the work of a few troublemakers or as a result of Spanish misconduct. Historians therefore treat his accounts as a mixture of valuable observation and promotional writing. O'odham interests are visible in other evidence: the choices communities made about missions, their continued control of much of their territory, their trading and farming, and their diplomatic maneuvering between Spaniards and Indigenous neighbors. In this far northwestern region, as elsewhere, Indigenous communities sought what advantages they could from Spanish institutions while maintaining their own political and economic lives, and the Spanish presence remained limited in scale at the end of the century.

Division VI — Indigenous Continuity and the Limits of Northern New Spain · Section 42.28

Indigenous Families, Communities, Beliefs, and Cultural Continuity

Beneath the narrative of expeditions, wars, and missions lay the daily lives of families and communities, whose experience is harder to recover but essential to understanding the north. The documentary record gives only glimpses: mission registers of baptisms and marriages, censuses, legal cases, inventories, and the occasional observation by a missionary or traveler. Archaeology and oral tradition add further evidence. Together they show communities adapting to new conditions while maintaining their own social and cultural frameworks.

Agriculture remained the foundation of life for farming peoples. Yaqui, Mayo, Ópata, O'odham, and many Sierra Madre communities continued to grow maize, beans, and squash, and they adopted European crops, notably wheat, and animals such as cattle, sheep, goats, and horses. Livestock offered both opportunity and risk: it provided meat, wool, transport, and trade goods, but it also competed for land and water. Missions encouraged communal fields, and Indigenous families balanced obligations to mission labor with their own farming. Among hunting and gathering peoples of the plateau and northeast, seasonal cycles persisted but were constrained by Spanish ranching and settlement, and some groups turned to raiding livestock or to labor on Spanish properties.

Kinship and marriage structured communities. Households were generally built around extended families, and marriage alliances connected communities. Missionaries tried to impose Christian marriage norms, including monogamy, and to reshape household arrangements, and mission registers record how Indigenous people used baptism and marriage within the colonial system. The effects of epidemic disease, enslavement, and labor drafts on families were severe, and widowhood, orphaning, and separation were common. Indigenous women, who performed essential agricultural and domestic work, also became intermediaries in households that included Spaniards, mestizos, and Africans, and the growth of mixed-ancestry populations transformed the social landscape of the north.

Religion was a field of both change and continuity. Indigenous people adopted Catholic practices selectively, incorporating saints, processions, and festival cycles into community life, and sometimes using mission offices and confraternities as vehicles for local authority. Many also maintained beliefs and ceremonies connected to land, agriculture, healing, and ancestors, sometimes openly and sometimes out of sight of missionaries. Missionaries often denounced these practices as idolatry or sorcery. The resulting religious life was neither a simple conversion nor a simple survival of the past, but a layered and changing synthesis that varied among communities and over time. Chapter 54 explores these themes across the continent.

Language and migration also mark the period. Many Indigenous languages persisted, and in some regions Nahuatl and Spanish spread as languages of administration and trade. Migration, voluntary and forced, moved people across the frontier: Tlaxcalan colonists, Indigenous mine workers, refugees from war, and enslaved captives all contributed to a mixing of communities. Some peoples declined or were absorbed so completely that Spanish records cease to name them, a process that scholars treat cautiously, since disappearance from the records does not always mean disappearance of descendants. Others, including the Yoeme, Rarámuri, O'odham, Seri, and Ópata communities, and descendants of Caxcan and Tlaxcalan communities, remain in the region today. Chapter 141 will return to these communities in the twenty-first century.

Everyday Life and Social History

Work, Food, and Community on the Northern Frontier

For an Indigenous family on the Pacific slope, the agricultural year followed the rivers: planting after floods or rains, tending fields, harvesting maize and beans, and supplementing the diet with fish, game, and wild plants. A mission pueblo added new rhythms, with days of communal labor, attendance at religious instruction, and the festivals of the Catholic calendar. A man might also leave for a season to work in a mine or on a ranch, earning wages in cloth or goods, and return to take part in community ceremonies.

In the Sierra Madre, communities lived in scattered households near small fields and moved between summer and winter dwellings. Rarámuri families responded to labor demands and Spanish settlement by shifting residences, sometimes into more remote canyons. On the plateau, Guachichil and other peoples who relied on wild foods saw their territories changed by cattle and mining camps, and some turned to raiding or to employment in Spanish settlements. In Spanish mining towns, Indigenous, African, mestizo, and Spanish workers lived in close contact in neighborhoods, taverns, and workplaces, forming the mixed urban society that characterized the north.

Because most surviving documents were written by Spaniards, historians must read them critically for indirect evidence about those who left few records of their own. Mission registers, wills, court cases, and tax lists reveal names, relationships, and conflicts, and archaeological work on mission and village sites, along with the oral histories of descendant communities, contributes crucial evidence for daily life.

Section 42.29

Northern New Spain around 1700: An Unfinished Colonial Project

By 1700, Spain had been expanding into northern Mexico and the Southwest for roughly two centuries, but the result was not a uniformly conquered territory. Instead, the north was a patchwork of different zones. Mining districts such as Zacatecas, Guanajuato, and Parral formed centers of Spanish and mixed-ancestry settlement. Spanish towns and haciendas dotted the plateau and the road to the north. Mission communities, run by Jesuits in the northwest and Franciscans in parts of the north and northeast, formed zones in which Indigenous communities lived under missionary supervision and Indigenous officials. Presidios and military posts protected roads and settlements, though their reach was limited. Beyond these, large areas, including mountain canyons, deserts, and the northern edges of the Spanish dominion, remained effectively under Indigenous control or influence.

Comparing the regions shows both patterns and variation. The plateau, once the scene of the Chichimeca War, was by 1700 a region of established Spanish towns, haciendas, and Indigenous and mixed communities, including Tlaxcalan settlements, and violent conflict had receded. Nueva Vizcaya combined mining towns, missions, and large areas of Rarámuri and other Indigenous settlement and repeated revolt. The northwest had mission networks of varying depth, with strong mission communities on the Yaqui and Mayo rivers, relatively stable Ópata missions, and the newer, fragile Pimería Alta frontier. The northeast remained thinly settled, and New Mexico, reconquered after the revolt discussed in Chapter 44, formed a separate and distinctive colonial province.

Why was the project unfinished? The reasons include geography, resources, and demography, but also Indigenous choices and capacities. Spanish forces were small and expensive, and their reach was limited by distance and terrain. The Crown's priorities lay in silver revenue rather than territorial completeness, and the mission orders and settlers pursued competing aims. Indigenous communities used refuge, negotiation, alliance, and resistance to protect their autonomy. At the same time, the Spanish presence was not trivial: it changed economies, ecologies, populations, religions, and landscapes, and it created new social groups. The north in 1700 was neither a land untouched by colonization nor a fully conquered colony.

New developments were also under way. Livestock and horses were spreading, Apache and other mobile peoples were reshaping relations on the northern and northeastern margins, and imperial rivalry with France and other powers was beginning to influence Spanish strategy. These developments belong to later chapters, notably Chapters 49 through 51, but the groundwork lay in the structures established in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Indigenous Communities across the Northern Frontier

What Changed

  • Many communities were concentrated in mission towns or Spanish-supervised settlements, altering settlement patterns and local governance
  • Labor systems, including wage labor, repartimiento, enslavement, and mission labor, drew Indigenous workers into the colonial economy
  • Epidemics, wars, enslavement, and displacement reduced populations and, in some cases, ended the separate existence of named groups
  • Livestock, wheat, metal tools, and Catholic practices became part of Indigenous life, and new mixed-ancestry communities emerged

What Stayed the Same

  • Many communities kept control of significant lands and agricultural systems, and kinship and local leadership remained central
  • Indigenous languages and ceremonial life persisted, often blended with Catholic forms
  • Large areas of the Sierra Madre, deserts, and northern margins remained under Indigenous control or influence
  • Indigenous communities continued to use diplomacy, petition, flight, and resistance to defend their interests

Timing and pace: Change was uneven and often abrupt in places of direct Spanish pressure, such as mining districts and war zones, and slower in remote areas. Mission residents and mine workers experienced change most directly, while communities in refuges experienced greater continuity, though not without indirect effects from disease, trade, and displaced neighbors.

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both change and continuity prevents two errors: treating the north as an empty land transformed by Spanish civilization, and treating Indigenous communities as unchanged by two centuries of colonial pressure.

Section 42.30

Chapter Review and Historical Thinking Checkpoint

This section consolidates the chapter's arguments and provides the review apparatus. Before moving on, students should be able to explain why northern New Spain was a distinct colonial formation, how silver mining, missions, presidios, and Indigenous diplomacy and resistance interacted, and why Spanish authority in 1700 remained incomplete and negotiated.

The core insights of this chapter are: Northern Mexico and the Southwest were inhabited by diverse Indigenous societies with their own economies, politics, and networks. Spanish expansion proceeded through violence, enslavement, negotiation, missions, and alliance with Indigenous auxiliaries, rather than through the capture of a single state. Silver mining, beginning with Zacatecas in 1546, drove demands for land, water, food, labor, and security. Indigenous peoples shaped outcomes through war, diplomacy, petition, and adaptation, as the Mixtón War, the Chichimeca War, the Tiguex War, the Yaqui and Mayo peace, and the Tepehuán uprising all demonstrate. The northern frontier took different forms in the plateau, Nueva Vizcaya, the northeast, the northwest, and New Mexico, and by 1700 it remained a mosaic.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that northern New Spain was not an extension of the conquest of central Mexico but a distinct colonial formation built through many local confrontations and negotiations. The region north and northwest of the Mesoamerican heartland was home to a mosaic of Indigenous societies, from irrigating farmers along the Pacific-slope rivers to mobile hunter-gatherers of the plateau and northeast. They had long-standing exchange and political relationships, including with Mesoamerica and the Southwest, and they were not the "empty land" that Spanish writers sometimes described.

Spanish expansion began with Nuño de Guzmán's invasion of western Mexico in 1529, which established Nueva Galicia through violence and enslavement. The resulting grievances contributed to the Mixtón War of 1540 to 1542, a large Caxcan-led rising that was suppressed only after the viceroy took the field with large numbers of Indigenous allies. Francisco Tenamaztle's later petition to the Crown, prepared in 1555 with Bartolomé de las Casas's help, preserves an Indigenous argument for the legitimacy of resistance and against enslavement, though it must be read as a mediated source.

The discovery of silver at Zacatecas in 1546 gave expansion its economic engine. Mining districts demanded labor, food, livestock, timber, water, and secure roads, and ranches and farms occupied lands used by Indigenous communities. These pressures produced the Chichimeca War, conventionally dated from about 1550 to 1590, in which peoples whom Spaniards called Chichimecas, including Zacatecos, Guachichiles, Guamares, and Pames, resisted through mobility and ambush. The label itself obscured their differences and served to justify war and enslavement.

Spanish policy shifted from "war by fire and blood" toward "peace by purchase," combining provisions, missions, settlement, and intermediaries such as Miguel Caldera. Indigenous allies, especially Otomí and Tlaxcalan settlers, were central to this strategy, and the negotiated Tlaxcalan migration of 1591 shows how allies bargained for privileges even as officials used them for colonial ends. Historians debate whether the resulting peace reflected effective Spanish statecraft, Indigenous strategy and exhaustion, or continued colonization by other means, and the evidence supports elements of each.

In the Southwest, expeditions inspired by reports from Cabeza de Vaca's survivors and Fray Marcos de Niza culminated in Coronado's expedition of 1540 to 1542. The expedition failed to find the expected wealth, and its requisitioning and violence provoked the Tiguex War along the Río Grande, in which Pueblo communities resisted a Spanish occupation. Castañeda's retrospective account is the principal source and reveals both Spanish brutality and the limits of Spanish intelligence. The Southwest did not return to Spanish attention until Juan de Oñate's colonizing expedition of 1598, whose later history belongs to Chapters 43 and 44.

Spanish colonial infrastructure developed gradually through Nueva Vizcaya, Coahuila, and Nuevo León, and through a system of mining towns, ranches, missions, and presidios that cooperated and competed. Labor systems, including enslavement, encomienda, repartimiento, wage labor, and mission labor, differed in law and practice, and Indigenous communities used petition, negotiation, and resistance to influence them. Indigenous diplomacy and local leadership shaped colonial outcomes throughout.

The northwestern mission frontier shows the importance of negotiation. Jesuit missions in Sinaloa and Sonora developed from 1591, and the Yaqui and Mayo, after resisting Spanish expeditions, negotiated peace around 1610 and accepted missionaries on terms they helped to set. Other regions saw revolt: the Acaxee, Xixime, and especially the Tepehuán rose against labor demands, concentration, and missionary pressure, and Rarámuri and Concho communities resisted repeatedly after the opening of Parral in 1631. In Pimería Alta, Eusebio Kino's missions among the O'odham from 1687 were shaken by the uprising of 1695.

Throughout, Indigenous families and communities adapted while preserving social and cultural continuities. They farmed, adopted livestock, worked in mines and ranches, incorporated Christian practices into their own religious lives, intermarried with other groups, and migrated or took refuge when necessary. Epidemic disease, war, and enslavement caused severe loss, and some peoples disappeared from the record, but many communities persisted.

By about 1700, northern New Spain was an unfinished colonial project. Mining districts, Spanish towns, mission communities, and presidios coexisted with large regions of substantial Indigenous autonomy. Spanish power was real and transformative but uneven and negotiated. The chapter's central lesson is that the history of the north must be understood as the joint product of Spanish expansion and Indigenous action, an understanding that prepares students for the Pueblo histories of Chapters 43 and 44 and the later Plains and borderlands histories of Chapters 49 through 51.

"War by fire and blood," the Spanish policy of waging just war against northern peoples, permitting the enslavement of captives.
Paz por compra
"Peace by purchase," the strategy of buying peace through provisions, gifts, missions, and settlement arrangements.
Encomienda
A grant of Indigenous tribute and labor to a Spanish holder, less central in the north than in central Mexico.
Repartimiento
A regulated draft of rotating Indigenous workers assigned to Spanish employers or public projects, distinct from both slavery and free wage labor.
Naborío
A free or semi-free Indigenous wage laborer who worked for Spanish employers without being part of an encomienda or repartimiento draft.
Just war
A legal and theological doctrine used by Spaniards to justify war and the enslavement of captives taken from peoples deemed rebellious.
Presidio
A garrisoned Spanish fort that protected roads, missions, and settlements, typically with a small complement of soldiers.
Misión
A religious settlement administered by a missionary order that aimed to convert and concentrate Indigenous people, supported by Crown subsidies and Indigenous labor.
Reducción / congregación
The concentration of dispersed Indigenous people in compact settlements for administration and instruction.
Ranchería
A settlement of dispersed households near fields, typical of many northern farming peoples and a challenge for Spanish administration.
Visita and cabecera
A mission head town (cabecera) and the outlying communities (visitas) served by traveling missionaries.
Estancia / hacienda
Spanish ranching and agricultural properties that supplied mines and towns and occupied Indigenous lands.
Indigenous auxiliaries
Indigenous soldiers, porters, and settlers, such as Tlaxcalans, Otomí, and Mexica, who served alongside Spaniards.
Camino Real de Tierra Adentro
The road connecting Mexico City, the plateau mining districts, and eventually New Mexico, a route of trade, travel, and conflict.
Tiguex War
The 1540–1541 conflict between Coronado's expedition and Tiwa Pueblo communities along the Río Grande.
Pimería Alta
The Spanish name for the region of northern Sonora and southern Arizona inhabited by O'odham peoples.
Endonym
A name a people uses for itself, such as Yoeme, Rarámuri, or O'odham, as opposed to names given by outsiders.

Chapter Reflection

Chapter 42 matters because it shows that the Spanish empire in North America was built differently in different places, and that those differences depended on Indigenous societies as much as on Spanish plans. The conquest of the Mexica dominion was a decisive event, but it did not provide a template for the north. There, Spanish authority had to be negotiated or imposed one valley at a time, and its limits were set by Indigenous choices, geography, and the economics of silver.

The chapter also reveals larger processes that recur throughout this textbook: the role of extractive economies in driving colonial expansion, the use of Indigenous allies and auxiliaries, the interplay of missions and military power, the instability of labor systems built on coercion, and the capacity of Indigenous communities to resist, bargain, and adapt. The Mixtón War, the Chichimeca War, the Tepehuán uprising, and the Yaqui peace each illustrate these processes in different forms.

Later developments grew from the structures described here. The Spanish colonization of New Mexico after 1598 and the Pueblo Revolt of 1680, examined in Chapters 43 and 44, cannot be understood apart from the mining frontier, the mission system, and the habits of expansion that developed in northern Mexico. The spread of horses and the rise of Comanche and Apache power in Chapters 49 through 51 reshaped the same frontier in the eighteenth century, and the borderlands histories of Chapters 68 and 141 trace Indigenous communities through later changes in sovereignty.

Finally, the chapter points to continuities that survived beyond its period. The Yoeme, Rarámuri, O'odham, Ópata, Seri, and many other peoples remain in the region, and their histories of negotiation and resistance are part of the living memory of northern Mexico and the Southwest. Understanding how they met Spanish expansion helps students see the north as a place of continuing Indigenous presence, not an empty frontier that was simply settled.
