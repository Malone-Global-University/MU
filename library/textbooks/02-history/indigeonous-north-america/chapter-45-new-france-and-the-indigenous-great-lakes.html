New France and the Indigenous Great Lakes

Indigenous Peoples of North America: A Continental History

Chapter Opening

New France did not make the political world of the Great Lakes. It entered that world, depended on it, and, for most of the seventeenth century, could survive in it only on terms that Indigenous nations were willing to accept.

In the first days of August 1701, the town of Montreal was crowded with people who had traveled, in many cases, for weeks by canoe. Delegates from dozens of Indigenous nations, drawn from the St. Lawrence Valley, the Great Lakes, and the country beyond, had assembled to meet the French governor, Louis-Hector de Callière, and representatives of the Haudenosaunee. Among the most influential voices in the gathering was Kondiaronk, a Tionontaté and Wendat leader from Michilimackinac whom French sources called Le Rat. During the negotiations he fell ill, and he died in Montreal on 2 August. The surviving French records describe a funeral conducted with honors that reflected his standing in the alliance. A few days later, on 4 August, hundreds of delegates put their marks to what is now called the Great Peace of Montreal. The treaty did not belong to a single empire. It was an Indigenous diplomatic settlement in which the French were one party among many.

That scene, which this chapter reaches in Section 45.30, is a useful corrective to a familiar narrative. Older histories of Canada often describe the seventeenth century as a story of French explorers pushing westward across an unknown continent, planting crosses, building forts, and claiming territory for a king. The explorers and the claims are real. But the movements of canoes, the survival of settlements, the supply of food, the translation of languages, the security of travel, and the safety of missionaries and traders depended, in nearly every case, on decisions made by Indigenous people. French officials knew this. They spent much of their energy not commanding allies but persuading, honoring, feeding, and placating them.

This chapter examines New France from the other side of the relationship: as seen from the Indigenous nations of the St. Lawrence Valley and the Great Lakes. Its central argument is that French expansion into the Great Lakes was possible because Indigenous peoples controlled the political alliances, transportation corridors, commercial networks, and diplomatic relationships upon which the French depended. The chapter therefore asks not only what the French did, but why Innu, Algonquin, Wendat, Odawa, Ojibwe, Potawatomi, Miami, Illinois, and other nations chose to engage with them, on what terms, and with what results.

The chapter follows a chronological arc of roughly 1603 to 1701. Division I describes the Indigenous Great Lakes before sustained French colonization. Division II traces the formation of the early alliance under Samuel de Champlain. Division III turns to Catholic missions, above all the Jesuit mission to the Wendat, and to the epidemics that accompanied it. Division IV examines the destruction of Wendat communities in Huronia in the late 1640s and the long reconstruction that followed. Division V, the heart of the chapter, explains how a new alliance network took shape in the western Great Lakes. Division VI assesses the limits of French authority and concludes with the Great Peace of 1701.

The evidence for this history is uneven and must be read critically. Much of the written record was produced by French observers: Champlain's published narratives, the annual Jesuit Relations, the letters and reports of governors and intendants, and notarial and administrative records. These sources describe Indigenous speech, politics, and belief through French ears and for French purposes. They must be compared with archaeology, with Indigenous oral histories and community scholarship, with linguistic evidence, and with the treaty records and pictographic signatures through which Indigenous diplomats made their own marks on the documentary record. Where this chapter paraphrases, it says so, and it does not attribute invented words to Indigenous leaders.

The chapter also takes care with boundaries. The Haudenosaunee, whose wars with the Wendat and the French shaped nearly every development described here, are treated fully in Chapter 47; the fur trade as an economic system is the subject of Chapter 46; the broader demography of epidemic disease belongs to Chapter 40; and comparative Native Christianity is examined in Chapter 54. Here the organizing unit is the political relationship, not the commodity or the battle. The question throughout is how Indigenous nations negotiated their place in a colonial encounter whose outcome was not yet determined.

Historical Context

Students arriving at this chapter should bring several earlier developments with them. Chapter 25 introduced the Indigenous societies of the Northeast Woodlands, and Chapters 26 and 27 examined the Haudenosaunee world and the Great Lakes and Anishinaabe worlds on the eve of colonization. Chapter 38 placed the Atlantic voyages beginning in 1492 within a wider transformation of Indigenous America, and Chapter 40 surveyed the introduced diseases whose effects are touched on only briefly here. For the purposes of this chapter, three background conditions matter most.

First, the St. Lawrence Valley and the Great Lakes were not empty when Europeans arrived. Maize-growing Iroquoian-speaking peoples had lived in towns along the St. Lawrence in the sixteenth century, as French explorers of the 1530s and 1540s recorded, and these communities had largely disappeared from the valley by the time Champlain arrived in 1603, a change whose causes archaeologists and historians still debate and which may have involved warfare, shifting alliances, and disease. By the early seventeenth century the valley was used principally by Algonquian-speaking peoples, including the Innu and Algonquin nations, while populous agricultural nations, the Wendat and their neighbors, occupied lands between Georgian Bay and Lake Ontario, and the Five Nations of the Haudenosaunee lived south of Lake Ontario.

Second, Europeans had been trading along the Atlantic coast and the Gulf of St. Lawrence for generations before any permanent French settlement. Fishing crews, whalers, and traders exchanged European goods, including metal tools and cloth, for furs at seasonal sites such as Tadoussac. These exchanges flowed into existing Indigenous networks and altered them, often well in advance of European settlers. French merchants and the French crown understood that fur-trade profits depended on access to inland Indigenous suppliers, and that access depended on diplomacy.

Third, the French crown's colonial strategy in the early seventeenth century relied on chartered companies that held trade monopolies in return for a commitment to settle and to support missions. These companies were chronically undercapitalized, and their settlements were small. Colonial population grew slowly: Quebec remained a modest settlement for decades, and the French population of the colony in the 1660s was still counted in the low thousands. A colony so small could not conquer the country that it claimed. Its survival depended on partnership with larger Indigenous populations, and its expansion depended on those partners' willingness to carry French traders, missionaries, and officials into their territories and to protect them there.

The chapter's chronology also intersects with the Haudenosaunee wars of the seventeenth century. The Five Nations were not merely enemies of French allies. They were a confederacy with their own interests, alliances, and economic strategies, including relations with the Dutch and later the English. Chapter 47 examines these wars and their causes directly. Here they appear as a condition shaping the choices of the Wendat, Algonquian-speaking peoples, and the French.

Section 45.1

The Great Lakes as an Indigenous World

The phrase "Great Lakes" names a physical system, but the region was also a human one. The lakes, rivers, forests, wetlands, and shorelines supported communities with different economies, languages, and political traditions, connected by marriage, trade, shared ceremony, and war. When Samuel de Champlain reached the St. Lawrence in 1603, he encountered the northeastern edge of this system. Over the following century the French came to know its western reaches as the pays d'en haut, the upper country, a French term for a region that its own inhabitants understood in entirely different ways.

Three broad clusters of nations matter most to the early seventeenth century. The first comprised Algonquian-speaking hunting, fishing, and trading peoples of the northern forests and rivers. Along the lower St. Lawrence and the Saguenay lived the Innu, whom the French called Montagnais. Along the Ottawa River and its tributaries lived the Algonquin nations, including the Kichesipirini of Allumette Island, and further west the Nipissing around the lake that bears their name. These peoples moved seasonally, hunted and fished extensively, and maintained long-distance exchange relationships with their agricultural neighbors.

The second cluster comprised the Iroquoian-speaking agricultural nations who lived in large towns south of Georgian Bay and along the shores of Lake Ontario and Lake Erie. The Wendat, whom the French called Hurons, were a confederacy of nations whose towns concentrated in a compact territory sometimes called Wendake or, in French sources, Huronia. Near them lived the Tionontaté or Petun to the west, the Attiwandaronk or Neutral to the south and east, and the Erie. The term Huron is a French exonym of uncertain and probably unflattering derivation; the people's own name, Wendat, is used throughout this chapter, with Huron appearing where the sources or established place-names require it.

The third cluster comprised the Haudenosaunee: the Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga, and Seneca nations, who lived in the country south of Lake Ontario and who held a ceremonial and political union that Chapter 26 describes in detail. Further west lived the nations that came to be called Anishinaabeg, among them the Odawa, Ojibwe (including the Saulteaux of Sault Sainte-Marie), and Potawatomi; and still further, the Menominee and Ho-Chunk around Green Bay and the Illinois, Miami, and others around Lake Michigan and the upper Mississippi. Their histories are developed in Sections 45.22 to 45.27.

Treating these peoples collectively as "the Indians of the Great Lakes" would reproduce the French colonial habit of grouping very different societies under convenient labels. They spoke languages from different families, disagreed about land, trade, and war, and followed different political customs. The Wendat lived in palisaded towns of longhouses and relied on maize agriculture. The Innu, Algonquin, and Odawa often lived in smaller seasonal communities. Understanding the French–Indigenous relationship means beginning with this diversity.

Historical Vocabulary
Pays d'en Haut, Alliance, and Confederacy

Pays d'en haut ("upper country") was the French name for the interior west of Montreal and the Ottawa River, covering the Great Lakes and beyond. It was a colonial administrative and conceptual category. Indigenous nations used their own names for their homelands, and the boundaries of the pays d'en haut reflected French travel routes more than Indigenous political geography.

Alliance, in this chapter, refers to a relationship of mutual obligation between political communities, usually expressed through ceremony, gifts, speeches, kinship terms, and shared military or commercial commitments. It differs from a modern treaty alliance with codified clauses; it was renewed through repeated acts of reciprocity and could lapse if obligations were neglected.

Confederacy describes a union of distinct nations that coordinate decisions on shared matters while retaining their own councils and identities. The Wendat and the Haudenosaunee were confederacies of this kind. The term should not suggest centralized states with a single ruler.

Potential misunderstanding: French sources frequently called allied leaders "subjects" of the king or spoke of Indigenous "obedience." Indigenous leaders generally understood themselves to be allies, kin, or partners, which is a different relationship.

Section 45.2

Lakes, Rivers, Portages, and Indigenous Transportation Networks

Long-distance travel in the seventeenth-century Northeast was overwhelmingly a matter of water. The St. Lawrence River drains the lakes toward the Atlantic and formed the principal artery between the Gulf and the interior. At Montreal, the Lachine Rapids interrupted travel and made the island a natural transfer point. From there, travelers could ascend the Ottawa River, a route that avoided Lake Ontario and the Haudenosaunee country to its south. The Ottawa led, by portages and small tributaries such as the Mattawa, to Lake Nipissing and the French River, which flows into Georgian Bay on Lake Huron. This corridor was the principal link between the St. Lawrence and Wendake and, later, between Quebec and the western lakes.

Farther west, Lake Huron connects through the Straits of Mackinac to Lake Michigan and, through the St. Marys River and the rapids at Sault Sainte-Marie, to Lake Superior. Green Bay and the Fox and Wisconsin rivers led toward the Mississippi watershed, while portages near the southern end of Lake Michigan connected to Illinois country rivers. Lake Ontario and Lake Erie, south of this principal corridor, lay along the northern edge of the Haudenosaunee and Neutral territories and were more dangerous for French travelers during periods of conflict.

The technology that made this system practical was the birchbark canoe, built by Indigenous craftspeople. These vessels were light enough to be carried over portages, strong enough to carry large loads in calm water, and repairable with materials found along the way. Their construction relied on knowledge of birch, cedar, spruce roots, and resin that French newcomers did not possess. The French learned to use canoes, but for decades they depended on Indigenous paddlers, builders, and guides to do so. Knowledge of routes was equally specialized: which portage avoided which rapid, where game and fish could be found, which villages offered food, and how weather and ice affected passage.

Control of a corridor was a political resource. The Kichesipirini Algonquin on Allumette Island in the Ottawa River were situated at a point where travelers had to portage past rapids, and they were able to regulate passage and negotiate payments of a kind that French observers sometimes described as tolls. Other communities exercised similar influence over portages, fisheries, and crossing points. Such control did not require walls or armies. It rested on local knowledge, the willingness of allied neighbors to support one's claims, and the dependence of travelers on provisions and assistance.

French maps of the period, including Champlain's own, depict this geography, but they derive from Indigenous information and filter it through French conventions. Historians use them as evidence of what the French learned and claimed, not as accurate depictions of Indigenous territorial boundaries.

Section 45.3

Indigenous Political Authority, Councils, Kinship, and Diplomacy

French observers frequently searched for a ruler, chief, or king with whom to negotiate, and they often misjudged the structure of authority they encountered. In the Great Lakes world, political authority was typically dispersed among councils, lineages, clan systems, and leaders whose influence depended on persuasion. Decisions on major matters such as war, peace, or alliance were made through discussion, with leaders seeking consensus among those whose participation was needed. A leader who spoke beyond the community's agreement could find that no one followed.

Among the Wendat, political life was organized at several levels: households and clans, villages with their own councils, nations within the confederacy, and a confederacy council that coordinated matters of common concern. Clan membership passed through the mother, and women held significant authority over households, agricultural land, and, through senior women, the selection of village leaders. Leaders were commonly divided by function: some concerned with civil and diplomatic affairs and others with war, a distinction French sources recorded in terms such as peace chiefs and war chiefs, with the usual caution that these labels are French translations.

Among the Algonquian-speaking nations of the northern forests, political communities were often smaller, built around extended families and seasonal bands that came together for fishing, hunting, trade, or ceremony. Leadership depended heavily on reputation, generosity, and effectiveness as a hunter, mediator, or war leader. Further west, Anishinaabe communities likewise relied on kin-based leadership and on clan or totemic affiliations that cut across communities, a feature that made clan relationships important in diplomacy between otherwise separate groups. The Haudenosaunee confederacy combined clan matrons, hereditary titles, and a council structure of its own, described in Chapter 26.

Diplomacy followed forms whose details varied but whose logic was widely shared. Councils opened with ceremonial greetings and speeches. Gifts accompanied nearly every significant statement: belts of wampum, trade goods, tobacco, food, or the exchange of prisoners and individuals. Gifts did not buy agreements; they authenticated speech, expressed respect, and obligated the receiver to reciprocate. The calumet ceremony, using a ceremonial pipe, became central in the western lakes and the Mississippi valley, where it signaled peace, hospitality, or alliance. Condolence rituals, in which a party offered words and gifts to ease the grief of a bereaved group, allowed communities to resolve killings without escalating to feud, and they became an essential part of the diplomatic vocabulary. Kinship terms also structured politics. Allies addressed one another as father, brother, or uncle, and the term chosen implied particular obligations, with a "father" expected to be generous and protective rather than domineering.

These traditions did not form a single "Indigenous system." Haudenosaunee, Wendat, Anishinaabe, Illinois, and Miami diplomacy shared features but differed in ritual, symbolism, and expectation. The French, unfamiliar with each, learned them by trial and error, and by relying on interpreters and on missionaries and traders who spent time living among allies.

Section 45.4

Indigenous Commerce and Interregional Relationships before the French

Exchange between Indigenous communities in the Great Lakes region long predated European goods. Agricultural societies produced maize, beans, squash, sunflower, and tobacco. Northern hunting and fishing peoples supplied furs, dried fish, meat, and products of the boreal forest. Shell, copper, stone, and other materials moved over long distances, as earlier chapters have described, particularly in Chapter 20's discussion of Hopewell exchange and Chapter 36's continental synthesis. These exchanges were embedded in relationships of kinship, alliance, and mutual obligation, and they were shaped by the different resource bases of different environments.

The relationship between the Wendat and their northern Algonquian-speaking neighbors illustrates the pattern. The Wendat, with extensive cornfields, produced surpluses that northern peoples, whose hunting grounds could not support agriculture, wanted. The Nipissing and other northern communities supplied furs, fish, and other products, and in return received maize and other Wendat goods. Wendat traders also traveled widely, and Wendat communities hosted visiting traders and diplomats. The Wendat confederacy's position, near the junction of several travel corridors and surrounded by neighbors of different economies, gave it an unusually influential role in regional exchange.

European goods entered this system before there were permanent French settlements. Metal kettles, knives, axes, and cloth reached the interior through Indigenous intermediaries and sometimes became valued as gifts, status goods, or practical tools. Some Indigenous leaders and communities profited from their position between Europeans and interior suppliers; others found that older trade relationships were disrupted by rivalry over access. The arrival of European goods did not simply replace Indigenous exchange. It was integrated into existing structures and often intensified competition among Indigenous groups seeking to control the new flow. Chapter 46 treats the fur trade as an economic system; this chapter focuses on what the trade meant for alliances.

The commercial setting helps explain why the French and Indigenous leaders each sought the other. French merchants wanted furs and needed Indigenous partners to supply them. Indigenous nations wanted metal tools, weapons, and other goods, and sought allies who could provide them reliably. But both parties understood that trade was not purely economic. Exchange between allies required ceremonial gifts, kinship-like obligations, and reciprocal assistance. A partner who traded but declined to ally was an unreliable partner, while a partner who allied could be trusted to keep trading.

Historical Debate

French Empire or Indigenous Alliance Network?

The question: How should historians characterize French power in the Great Lakes in the seventeenth century? Was New France an empire that extended authority into the interior, or was it better understood as a participant in, and dependent on, an Indigenous alliance network that it did not control?

Interpretation A — A French colonial empire with Indigenous collaborators: Some historians emphasize the institutions of the French crown: the appointment of governors, intendants, and military officers, the licensing of trade, the construction of posts, the efforts to regulate commerce, and the formal claims to sovereignty. On this view, the French state progressively extended its reach, and Indigenous allies played important but ultimately supporting roles.
Interpretation B — An Indigenous-directed network with a French participant: Other historians emphasize that in the pays d'en haut, French personnel were few, supplies were limited, and the colony could not impose its will. French officials negotiated, gave gifts, and responded to Indigenous demands, and the decisions on which routes to use, which wars to join, and which French traders to admit were frequently made by Indigenous leaders. Richard White's The Middle Ground (1991) presented an influential argument that French and Algonquian peoples created shared diplomatic practices, while later scholars such as Michael Witgen have stressed the continuing primacy of Indigenous political order. Gilles Havard has shown how French officials adapted to Indigenous diplomatic customs.

Evidence: Both views draw on the same governors' correspondence, mission records, treaty documents, and archaeological evidence. They differ in the weight given to formal institutions versus actual capacity to act.

Points of disagreement: Disagreement concerns interpretation and emphasis more than facts: how much authority is implied by French claims, how mutual the exchange of cultural practices was, and whether the French position should be described as dependence, partnership, or a limited form of influence. The answer may also vary by place and time, since the French position in Quebec in the 1660s differed from that at Michilimackinac in the 1690s.

Why it matters: The framing affects how students understand who made decisions, whose interests drove events, and whether Indigenous peoples are treated as actors or as background.

Student task: Select two events described later in this chapter, and explain how each interpretation would account for them. Then identify which kinds of evidence would best help to decide between the interpretations.

Division II — Champlain and the Formation of the Early French–Indigenous Alliance

Sections 45.5–45.10 · Approximately 1603–1625

Section 45.5

Tadoussac, 1603: The French Enter Indigenous Diplomacy

Tadoussac, at the mouth of the Saguenay River where it meets the St. Lawrence, had been a gathering place for trade and diplomacy before the French arrived. It lay at a junction of routes linking the interior to the Gulf and had hosted European fishing and trading ships for decades. In the spring of 1603, Samuel de Champlain, then a geographer and observer, accompanied a French trading expedition led by François Gravé du Pont to the St. Lawrence. At Tadoussac they encountered an assembly of Indigenous people that included Innu, Algonquin, and Maliseet participants, and a leader whom Champlain names Anadabijou.

The gathering was not organized for the French. Champlain's account describes a celebration following a military success against the Iroquois, a term the French used for the Haudenosaunee and which, in this context, referred most directly to the Mohawk. The wider background was a long-running conflict among nations of the St. Lawrence valley and the Mohawk, in which the Algonquian-speaking nations and their allies sought to protect trade and hunting grounds and to resist Mohawk pressure. The French were invited to attend, they were entertained according to the host's protocols, and speeches were exchanged. Champlain reports that the leaders expressed willingness to have the French as friends and allies and to allow them to settle on their lands.

What this meant requires careful interpretation. In the French telling, the meeting inaugurated an alliance and sanctioned a French presence. For the Innu and their allies, it may have meant something narrower and more conditional: acceptance of the French as trading partners and potential military supporters against a shared adversary, within a relationship that would have to be maintained through reciprocal gifts and conduct. There is no evidence that Anadabijou or the other leaders understood themselves to be transferring sovereignty to the king of France, and Champlain's text does not claim they did. Later French writers sometimes presented the Tadoussac meeting as a foundational act of French dominion, but that reading goes beyond the evidence.

The meeting also shows how French newcomers were incorporated into an existing diplomatic system. Their hosts decided who would attend, how the ceremony would proceed, and what was expected of guests. The French brought goods, military capacity, and the prospect of continued trade. The Innu and Algonquin brought knowledge of the land, control of routes to the interior, and the capacity to supply furs. Each side saw advantages. Neither could dictate terms.

Analysis: The account is among the earliest detailed French descriptions of the diplomacy of the St. Lawrence valley. It shows that the French were guests at an Indigenous event, that Indigenous leaders linked alliance to a specific military conflict, and that hospitality and speechmaking were integral to the encounter.

Limitations: Champlain was an outsider who did not speak the relevant languages fluently and relied on interpretation of uncertain quality. His account was written to promote colonization and likely emphasized Indigenous welcome. It reports nothing of what leaders said among themselves, and no Indigenous-authored record of the event survives. Phrases such as permission to settle may reflect French expectations more than precise Indigenous statements.

Perspective: The account reflects a French geographer seeking investment and royal support. Missing are the Innu, Algonquin, and Maliseet understandings of the event, which survive, if at all, only in later oral traditions.

Section 45.6

Quebec, 1608, and the Dependence of the French Settlement

In 1608 Champlain founded a trading post and residence at Quebec, a narrow point on the St. Lawrence where the river constricts and where Algonquian-speaking peoples had long gathered. The location suited French commercial aims, since it lay upstream from Tadoussac and controlled access to the river. It also lay near territory used by Innu and Algonquin communities who had agreed to the French presence, and not far from the former sites of the Iroquoian towns French explorers had described in the previous century.

The settlement was tiny. The first winter was a disaster: sources agree that most of the roughly two dozen men who stayed at Quebec died of disease and hardship, with the commonly cited survivor count in the single digits. The settlement continued through repeated resupply from France, and its population remained small for decades. As late as the early 1630s the colonial population remained only in the low hundreds, and the colony's growth was limited by the company's reluctance to invest in settlement and by the difficulty of persuading French families to emigrate.

Quebec's survival rested on Indigenous partners. The French relied on Innu and Algonquin traders to bring furs to Quebec; on Indigenous people for food, particularly maize and dried foods obtained through trade, and fresh game; on knowledge of how to move across the landscape and survive winters; and on military support in a region where the French were outnumbered. In this relationship, the French contributed European trade goods, a store of metal tools and weapons, and promises of assistance. But they could not afford to alienate their neighbors.

French ambitions and French capacity were different matters. Official claims asserted that the crown had authority over a vast territory, and Champlain and his successors wrote of establishing a colony that would extend French influence and convert Indigenous people to Christianity. But the colony had no means of enforcing its claims. Quebec was a trading post and a base, not a seat of authority over the surrounding peoples. When French leaders spoke of alliance, they were expressing a political necessity at least as much as a political aspiration.

Section 45.7

Champlain's Military Alliances, 1609–1610

In 1609 Champlain joined a war party of Innu, Algonquin, and Wendat allies traveling up the Richelieu River toward the lake that now bears his name. Their opponents were Mohawk warriors. In a clash in July, French arquebuses, wielded by Champlain and companions, killed several Mohawk leaders and helped decide the engagement. A second clash in 1610, near the mouth of the Richelieu, again saw French participation on the side of Indigenous allies.

For the allies, the invitation to the French was strategic. Firearms were new to the region, and their effect on enemies unfamiliar with them could be dramatic in the short term. The French presence promised access to metal weapons and tools that would improve the allies' trade position. Participation in a joint expedition also tested French commitment. Indigenous leaders measured a prospective ally by whether he would take risks alongside them, and a trading partner who refused to fight was a lesser partner.

For Champlain, participation served several purposes. It secured the loyalty of the nations on whom Quebec depended. It supported the commercial interest of his sponsors by maintaining the flow of furs. And it allowed him to demonstrate French usefulness. But it also entangled the French in conflicts that were not of their making. The Mohawk had their own reasons for opposing the Innu, Algonquin, and Wendat, and the French now appeared to them as partisans of their enemies. Some historians have argued that this alignment had consequences for French–Haudenosaunee relations for much of the century, while others caution against reading backward from the later wars to assume that these 1609–1610 clashes made them inevitable. Chapter 47 examines this debate. What matters here is that alliance with the Innu, Algonquin, and Wendat carried military obligations that the French had to honor, and that the early alliance was inseparable from the interests of its Indigenous members.

Section 45.8

The Wendat Confederacy and Champlain's Expedition of 1615

By the early seventeenth century the Wendat were a confederacy of several nations occupying a territory about a few days' travel across, south of Georgian Bay. The French names for four of the principal groups were the Attignawantan (Bear), Attigneenongnahac (Cord), Arendarhonon (Rock), and Tahontaenrat (Deer), the last of whom joined the others at some point after the French first encountered them. Their towns, many of them fortified with wooden palisades, contained multiple longhouses and a large population. Population estimates for the early seventeenth century vary widely among scholars, with commonly cited figures in the tens of thousands, and they are inferential, based on archaeological and documentary evidence. Wendat farmers cultivated maize, beans, and squash on a large scale, and Wendat traders exchanged agricultural products with northern neighbors.

The Wendat sought French alliance for reasons connected to their regional position. They wanted to secure access to European goods and to strengthen their claim to a central role in the trade between the French and the northern nations. They also faced hostility from the Haudenosaunee, and French firearms and assistance were attractive. Wendat leaders came to Quebec in the 1610s to negotiate. Young men were exchanged between communities in a practice meant to build understanding and ensure mutual reliability. The Wendat, in turn, arranged for the French to visit Wendake.

In 1615 Champlain traveled from Quebec up the Ottawa River, across Lake Nipissing, and down the French River to Georgian Bay, reaching Wendake in the summer. He traveled with Wendat guides; the Récollet missionary Joseph Le Caron had gone to Wendake slightly earlier in the same summer, and Champlain found him there. During his stay he visited several Wendat towns, observed their agriculture and ceremonies, and participated in councils at which a campaign was planned against an enemy town in Onondaga country. The expedition set out in the autumn. Champlain joined a force of Wendat and allied warriors, who besieged a fortified town. The assault failed. Champlain was wounded, the attackers withdrew, and the Wendat allies, who had expected more help than the small French contingent provided, were disappointed. Champlain wintered in Wendake before returning to Quebec in 1616.

The 1615 journey tells us as much about Wendat strategy as about Champlain. The Wendat leaders brought the French into a campaign of their own devising, in pursuit of their own aims, and they measured their guest by his conduct. The visit also gave Champlain and later French writers their first detailed information about Wendat society, though his account, like other French descriptions, reflects the limits of a short visit by a non-Wendat speaker. Archaeologists have since added crucial information about Wendat towns and settlement patterns, correcting or confirming aspects of the written record.

Section 45.9

Indigenous Guides, Interpreters, Travelers, and Political Intermediaries

Behind the alliance stood individuals who made communication, travel, and mutual understanding possible. Indigenous guides and paddlers were indispensable. They selected routes, handled canoes, hunted and fished for the party, negotiated with communities along the way, and determined where it was safe to travel. When Champlain journeyed up the Ottawa River in 1613, he relied on Algonquin guides and was received by Kichesipirini leaders at Allumette Island, who controlled the route and whose goodwill was essential. His 1615 passage to Wendake likewise depended on Wendat canoemen.

The French also sought interpreters, and they developed a practice, shared by many Indigenous nations, of exchanging young people between communities to learn languages and customs. In 1610 Champlain sent a young Frenchman, Étienne Brûlé, to live with Algonquin hosts, and Brûlé subsequently lived among the Wendat, learning their language and becoming an intermediary. In the same exchange a young Wendat man, Savignon, traveled to France with Champlain in 1610 and returned with him in 1611, an exchange of young people that sources describe as intended to build mutual understanding. Brûlé's later life illustrates both the value and the risks of this role. He traveled widely, reached regions beyond Wendake, and served as an interpreter and guide. He later, according to French sources, was killed by Wendat people around 1633, under circumstances that remain obscure and about which the sources are not reliable. His career indicates the personal dimension of diplomacy: his position depended on relationships and trust rather than on any formal French authority.

Indigenous intermediaries were at least as important and far less likely to appear in French records. Wendat, Algonquin, and Innu women and men served as translators, hosts, spouses, and guides whose names often went unrecorded. Many Indigenous leaders themselves traveled to Quebec and, in a few cases, to France, to negotiate directly. Their journeys show that mobility and the ability to communicate across cultures were Indigenous skills, not European monopolies.

Language fluency mattered more than any formal territorial claim. A French trader who spoke Wendat, Algonquin, or an Anishinaabe language could negotiate, mediate disputes, and understand rumor and opinion, while an official who relied on translators was vulnerable to mistranslation and manipulation. French authorities came to value the knowledge of interpreters and traders, and later governors would rely on men such as Nicolas Perrot to represent them in councils. That reliance underlined the central point of the early alliance: French influence depended on personal relationships and cultural knowledge that were gained through Indigenous hospitality.

Section 45.10

The Obligations of Alliance: Gifts, Speeches, Councils, and Reciprocity

An alliance in the Indigenous diplomatic tradition was neither a contract nor a subordination. It was an ongoing relationship maintained through ceremonies, exchanges of gifts, and behavior. Indigenous leaders expected French partners to provide protection when requested, material support in the form of trade goods and gifts, diplomatic responsiveness, which meant attendance at councils and thoughtful replies to speeches, and generosity. The French governor was addressed as a father, and Indigenous leaders understood that role as carrying obligations to provide for and defend his children, not authority to command them.

French officials, by contrast, often held a hierarchical conception of the relationship. In French law and political thought, the king held sovereign authority, and Indigenous allies were treated as friends who owed deference. Governors sometimes spoke in councils as if allies could be ordered to fight or to make peace. Allies rejected such claims in practice by declining to join campaigns, making separate peace arrangements, or pursuing their own trade. Misunderstandings were common and persistent, and they became a recurring source of tension.

Gift-giving was the hinge of the relationship. The annual presents the French distributed at Quebec, Montreal, and later at western posts were not simply bribes or commerce. They were required expressions of the alliance, and a governor who failed to provide them risked offending allies. As the colony's population and administrative apparatus grew, the cost of maintaining alliances became a significant item in colonial budgets. The need for such expenditure was a constant reminder of who depended on whom.

The obligations of alliance were concrete and recurring. Allied leaders who traveled to Quebec, Trois-Rivières, and later Montreal for the summer trading gatherings expected to be received with speeches, food, and presents, and to leave with their own words answered in kind. From the governorship of Charles Huault de Montmagny (1636–1648), Wendat and other allied leaders used the title Onontio, commonly glossed as “great mountain,” for him and later governors of New France. This title emerged after the earliest Champlain-era agreements and was thereafter applied to his successors. The title carried the kinship expectations described above: an Onontio was a father who protected and provided, and a governor who was stingy, absent, or evasive could be reminded that he was failing in the role. Renewal mattered as much as the original agreement. Promises were restated at each council, belts and presents re-confirmed them, and an alliance that was not renewed in this way was understood to have weakened, whatever a French document might say about its permanence.

Division III — Catholic Missions and the Transformation of the Wendat World

Sections 45.11–45.16 · Approximately 1620s–1648

Section 45.11

Récollets, Jesuits, and the Catholic Missionary Project

The French crown's colonial charters bound trading companies to support the conversion of Indigenous people to Catholicism, and missionaries accompanied the colony from early in its history. The Récollets, a reformed branch of the Franciscans, arrived at Quebec in 1615 and soon sent members to Indigenous communities. Their efforts, which included work among the Innu, Algonquin, and Wendat, were constrained by small numbers, limited funds, and the difficulty of learning languages and surviving in unfamiliar surroundings. Their results were modest.

The Society of Jesus, which had considerable influence at the French court and extensive experience of overseas missions, entered the colony in 1625. Its work was interrupted when English forces under the Kirke brothers captured Quebec in 1629; the colony was returned to France by treaty in 1632, and the Jesuits returned to a mission field from which the Récollets were, for the time being, excluded. During the 1630s the Jesuits developed an extensive program that included missions among the Innu and Algonquin, a reserve at Sillery near Quebec established in 1637 for Christian converts, and, above all, a sustained mission among the Wendat beginning in 1634 under Jean de Brébeuf and other priests.

The missionary program was supported by the colonial government and the Company of One Hundred Associates, which received the colony's trade monopoly in 1627 and was obliged to support religion. It was also supported by devout patrons in France, whose donations helped to fund missions and to create institutions such as the Ursuline convent and a hospital at Quebec. Missionaries understood their task as saving souls, and many believed that conversion required reshaping Indigenous social and ceremonial life. They also understood that their own survival depended on Indigenous toleration. In Wendake, the Jesuits lived as guests of Wendat hosts, subject to the decisions of councils and the goodwill of village leaders.

The two missionary orders worked in different institutional circumstances. The Récollets were a mendicant order with few resources and small numbers, dependent on the trading company for passage and support, and some of them favored encouraging Indigenous people to settle near French habitations and adopt French language and farming, an approach that had little success among nations who had no reason to relocate. One of them, Gabriel Sagard, lived among the Wendat in 1623–1624 and later published a description and a Wendat word list that remain valuable evidence. After the restoration of the colony in 1632, the Jesuits held the mission field in Canada, and they drew on the larger funds and wider networks of their order. Their practice was to live in Indigenous communities, study languages for years, and concentrate their efforts on nations with large populations, such as the Wendat. They also supported institutions at Quebec: the Ursuline sisters and the Hospitalières, who arrived in 1639, taught girls and cared for the sick, and Indigenous people were among their pupils and patients. These institutions shared in the missionary project, but they depended, like the priests, on the willingness of Indigenous families to send children and to seek care.

Historical Vocabulary
Mission, Conversion, and the Jesuit Relations

Mission refers both to the missionary enterprise and to the particular stations, residences, and churches that carried it out. A mission could be a priest living in a longhouse, a chapel in a village, or a fortified center such as Sainte-Marie.

Conversion is the adoption of a new religious identity. In this chapter, it covers a spectrum of experiences, from sincere adherence, to selective adoption of some Christian practices, to nominal acceptance for strategic or social reasons, to rejection. Historians avoid assuming that conversion is either wholly sincere or wholly instrumental, and note that one individual may have had mixed motives.

Jesuit Relations are the annual reports sent by Jesuit superiors in New France to their superiors in France, published from 1632 to 1673. Collected in a nineteenth-century edition by Reuben Gold Thwaites (73 volumes with English translations), they are the principal written source for the Wendat in the 1630s and 1640s.

Potential misunderstanding: The Relations were not neutral ethnography. They were written to inspire donations and support in France and often emphasized success, suffering, and providential interpretation.

Section 45.12

The Wendat Encounter with Christianity

Wendat religion, as reconstructed from missionary reports, archaeology, and later Wendat oral tradition, was a complex system of belief and practice. It included accounts of creation involving the figure Aataentsic, a sky woman who fell to earth, and her grandsons, accounts of spirits associated with the natural world, a rich ceremonial life centered on healing, feasting, and dreams, and an elaborate treatment of the dead. Every decade or so, a community would gather the remains of its dead in a great ceremony known as the Feast of the Dead, which reburied them in a common ossuary and reaffirmed links among communities. Brébeuf described a major Feast of the Dead in 1636, and archaeological work has confirmed the scale of such ossuaries.

When the Jesuits arrived, they did not enter a religious vacuum or a community with no means of evaluating newcomers. Wendat leaders were accustomed to hosting visitors, evaluating spiritual claims, and incorporating new ideas. Many welcomed the priests as potentially powerful figures, associated with French goods and French alliance. Some saw them as sources of useful knowledge, such as literacy, clocks, or healing. Others regarded them with suspicion because they condemned core Wendat practices: they objected to dream interpretation, feasts for the dead, and customs they considered immoral, and they refused to participate in some ceremonies. The priests also insisted on exclusive loyalty to Christianity, which contrasted with the Wendat tendency to treat religion as plural and adaptable.

Responses varied across communities and over time. In the first years, the Jesuits baptized very few adults, mostly dying people, and the missionaries themselves worried that their presence was tolerated for diplomatic reasons rather than from spiritual interest. Over the course of the 1630s and 1640s, a minority of Wendat people converted, and by the later 1640s Christian communities existed in several towns, most notably at Ossossané and in Sainte-Marie's vicinity. The Relations record Wendat converts who displayed deep devotion, including leaders who spoke publicly in favor of the faith, and others who stopped practicing under pressure from kin. Estimates of the number of Wendat Christians by 1648 vary in the sources and scholarship and should be treated with caution because the Jesuits measured conversion by baptism, which did not necessarily indicate belief or practice.

Interpretations of this evidence differ. Older missionary histories described heroic evangelization, while some twentieth-century accounts emphasized cultural destruction. Current scholarship tends to stress the agency of Wendat decision-makers. People converted for a mix of reasons that could include spiritual conviction, political alliance with the French, hope for protection or healing during epidemics, and kinship ties to other converts. Others refused for equally varied reasons: loyalty to ancestral practices, doubts about the priests, or anger at the epidemics that accompanied their arrival. This chapter treats neither conversion nor refusal as reducible to a single motive.

Section 45.13

Languages, Translation, and the Jesuit Relations

Missionary work required language, and Jesuit linguists devoted years to learning Wendat, an Iroquoian language of considerable grammatical complexity. Brébeuf and others compiled dictionaries, grammars, and catechisms, and they translated Christian prayers and doctrines into Wendat. These works, some of which survive, are valuable linguistic sources and have been used in modern language revitalization work. They also show how translation shaped meaning. Rendering concepts such as sin, grace, heaven, or God into Wendat required choosing terms that carried their own cultural associations. Some translations worked, while others produced confusion or unintended implications, which Wendat listeners were quick to notice and debate.

The Jesuit Relations were a distinctive form of writing that combined mission reports, ethnographic description, and edifying narrative. Each year the superior in Quebec compiled reports from missionaries in the field, edited them, and sent them to France, where they were published for a devout and wealthy audience. They described Indigenous societies at length, including customs, beliefs, politics, and daily life, and they recorded events of the mission: baptisms, deaths, journeys, epidemics, and conflicts. For many aspects of seventeenth-century Wendat life, the Relations are the best surviving written evidence.

Using them as evidence requires care. The authors wrote from a religious perspective that judged Wendat practices by Christian standards, and they sought to demonstrate progress, justify expenses, and attract support. They often reported what they were told by translators or converts, and their understanding of Wendat religion was shaped by their expectations. They tended to emphasize suffering, martyrdom, and providential rescue. Historians therefore compare the Relations with other evidence: with archaeological findings on Wendat towns, burials, and houses; with the writings of other French observers such as the Récollet Gabriel Sagard; with later Wendat oral histories; and with the internal tensions and contradictions in the texts themselves. The Relations also preserve fragments of Wendat speech, argument, and decision-making, recorded imperfectly but invaluable for understanding how Wendat people evaluated the missionaries.

Wendat listeners judged missionary practice on their own terms. The Wendat gave Brébeuf a Wendat name, Echon, a custom of naming that integrated outsiders into a social place and a set of obligations. Priests who performed baptisms of dying people, especially children, aroused fear because the visits often preceded deaths, and Wendat observers drew their own conclusions about the relationship between the rite and the outcome. Written texts raised another question. Wendat councils did not use writing for decisions, and some people suspected that the priests' books and ink carried power that could harm the community, while others valued the ability to send messages. The Jesuits, for their part, learned that speech in council demanded the generous giving of presents to hosts and to whoever agreed to listen, and that missionary arguments gained a hearing only when they were presented in Wendat forms of oratory and accompanied by gifts. The language work of the mission was therefore inseparable from Wendat expectations about courtesy, reciprocity, and public speech.

Section 45.14

Indigenous Women, Religious Change, and Community Debate

Women occupied central positions in Wendat society. They controlled the households of longhouses, organized the cultivation of maize, beans, and squash, distributed the harvest, and held significant influence in clan and village decisions, including, through senior clan women, the nomination of leaders. They also participated in religious life as healers, dream interpreters, and ritual participants. Because descent passed through the mother, the Wendat household was the basic unit of community life, and missionaries who wished to change belief and practice needed the cooperation of its members.

Christian teachings clashed with some Wendat practices in ways that particularly affected households. Missionaries criticized divorce, which was relatively easy among the Wendat, and objected to premarital relationships and polygynous arrangements. They urged converts to abandon certain ceremonies, to refuse participation in feasts connected with healing or the dead, and to separate from non-Christian relatives when they interfered with devotion. Such demands could cut across kinship obligations. A convert might refuse to attend a family funeral feast, which relatives could regard as a rejection of the household. Missionaries in turn interpreted resistance as superstition or diabolical influence.

Women's responses were varied. The Relations record Wendat women who embraced Christianity, took part in prayer and instruction, and were baptized, among them Marie Aonetta, wife of the Wendat leader Joseph Chihwatenhwa, whose own baptism in 1637 and activities before his death in 1640 the Jesuits described at length. Other women resisted, defended ancestral healing practices, and challenged the priests. Some used the new religion strategically to gain access to resources or protection. The sources, written by men who valued piety and obedience, probably underrepresent the range of women's views, especially those of women who rejected the missionaries.

The result was not a simple conflict between Christian and traditional Wendat, but a series of debates within households and villages. Some communities divided over how much to accept; some individuals attended both Christian and traditional ceremonies; and leaders weighed the political benefits of friendship with the French against the social costs of dividing the community. As Chihwatenhwa's example shows, a leader could take a public Christian stance without ceasing to be a Wendat political figure. By the 1640s, this internal debate was intensified by disease and by the threat of war.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Wendat Community during the Mission Era

A Wendat town of the 1630s contained numerous longhouses, each sheltering several related families, enclosed within a palisade that offered defense against attack. Women worked the surrounding fields in the growing season, planting maize, beans, and squash together, and managed the storage and preparation of food. Men fished, hunted, built houses, and traveled for trade and diplomacy, often being away for months. The community's calendar included planting and harvest observances, healing ceremonies, feasts, and games, as well as the larger cycle of the Feast of the Dead.

The arrival of missionaries introduced new elements. A priest might live in a longhouse as a guest, say Mass in a house set aside, instruct children, and treat the sick. Some households welcomed these activities, others tolerated them, and others sought to exclude them. Missions also brought novelties such as crucifixes, bells, and books, which could be viewed with curiosity or fear. At the same time, the epidemics of the late 1630s killed many of the elders who held ceremonial knowledge and many children, altering the demographic and social fabric. Historians reconstruct daily life through archaeology, which shows house forms, food remains, and tools, supplemented by the descriptions of Sagard, Champlain, and the Jesuits, each with their limitations.

Section 45.15

Sainte-Marie among the Hurons, 1639–1649

In 1639 the Jesuits established a central residence, Sainte-Marie, in Wendat territory near the Wye River, a few miles from the main Wendat towns. Parks Canada records its founding in 1639 and its abandonment in 1649. The site was intended as a headquarters for the mission: a place where priests could rest, receive supplies, study languages, and welcome visitors, and from which missionaries could travel to outlying towns. It combined European and Wendat features. A palisaded complex with a church, residences, workshops, a hospital, and farmland stood alongside a section designed to receive Wendat visitors, including longhouses where Indigenous guests could stay and eat.

The Europeans living at Sainte-Marie were not only priests. They included lay assistants known as donnés, who bound themselves to serve the mission, as well as soldiers, craftsmen, and laborers. At its height in the later 1640s, the community numbered several dozen French people. They depended on food supplies from the Wendat, obtained through trade and gifts, as well as on their own gardens and livestock, and on goods carried up the Ottawa River by canoe in convoys that were themselves guided and sometimes paddled by Wendat people. The mission was therefore vulnerable. It could be provisioned only if the route stayed open and the Wendat remained willing partners.

Sainte-Marie served many purposes in addition to its religious function. It was a diplomatic center where French officials and Wendat leaders could meet. It was a commercial point where trade goods were exchanged. It was a source of medical care, however limited, for Wendat who came to the hospital. It was also a symbol of French presence, but one that did not represent French authority over the Wendat. Wendat leaders remained in control of their towns, councils, and decisions, and the mission was accepted because it brought benefits and because opposing it would have damaged the French alliance on which Wendat trade depended.

Archaeological excavation and reconstruction at the site, now operated as Sainte-Marie among the Hurons, have helped historians understand its layout and the mix of European and Indigenous material culture. Evidence of wattle-and-daub construction, European tools, and Indigenous pottery attests to the contacts at the site. The reconstruction also reflects choices about interpretation, and the Wendat perspective is represented through ongoing collaboration with the Huron-Wendat Nation, whose community histories contribute to understanding the site.

The administrative arrangements at Sainte-Marie reflected both Jesuit rules and local conditions. Jérôme Lalemant, who directed the Wendat mission from 1638, oversaw the choice of site and the construction of the compound, and later superiors, among them Paul Ragueneau, managed supply and discipline. The priests lived under the order's rule of obedience, while the donnés, soldiers, and craftsmen worked under mission direction and received their maintenance in return. Wendat people entered the compound in several capacities: as visitors who received food and lodging in the longhouse set aside for them, as patients in the hospital, as laborers or traders, and, for some Christian families, as residents or neighbors who relied on the mission's protection. Their presence was voluntary and could be withdrawn. The mission's food supply illustrates the relationship. Crops grown at the compound and goods brought from Quebec could not feed a large resident population over the long term, so the French bought maize and other provisions from Wendat farmers, mostly women, who therefore had direct influence over the mission's capacity to function. In the final years, as fighting increased, the compound also sheltered Wendat Christians seeking safety, which put a strain on supplies and made it a visible symbol of the alliance between the Jesuits and a portion of the Wendat community.

Section 45.16

Epidemic Disease, Missionary Controversy, and Social Disruption

Between about 1634 and 1640 the Wendat endured a series of epidemics, including, in 1639–1640, a severe outbreak of smallpox. Chapter 40 surveys the introduced diseases of the period in comparative perspective. The disease agents arrived through communities with trade and travel connections to the St. Lawrence, and they spread through densely settled towns where people lived closely in longhouses and gathered for ceremonies. Population estimates are uncertain, but many scholars conclude that the Wendat lost a very large fraction of their population, possibly half or more, over the decade. The losses fell heavily on children and elders, who carried knowledge and authority.

The epidemics were not only a demographic event. They were interpreted and argued over within Wendat communities. Healers tried their customary remedies and ceremonies. Many Wendat noticed that the sickness followed the arrival of the Jesuits, who had asked for baptisms of the dying and who lived in houses where people had died. Some leaders and shamans accused the missionaries of causing the illness through sorcery, the writing of books, or the sacred objects they carried. The Relations record meetings in which Wendat councils debated whether the priests should be expelled or killed, and episodes in which missionaries faced hostility and threats. In some towns, the Jesuits' lives were in danger.

Modern historians are careful with this evidence. The missionaries did not, in any intentional sense, cause the epidemics, and the diseases would likely have spread through Wendat trade and travel networks regardless of any mission. But the Wendat suspicion was a rational response to circumstances: the epidemics coincided with the arrival of foreign people with unusual practices, and Wendat theories of illness included the possibility of human agency. The Jesuits, for their part, interpreted the epidemics as opportunities to save souls and as trials sent by God, and some leaders were persuaded by the fact that Christians did not appear to die at higher rates, or were drawn to baptism as a source of hope.

The disruption affected politics as well. Councils lost leaders and experienced members. Divisions over the missionaries deepened, with some factions drawing closer to the French and others distancing themselves. At the same time, the confederacy faced growing military pressure from the Haudenosaunee. These stresses, rather than any single event, created the conditions in which the catastrophe of the late 1640s would occur.

Wendat responses to the epidemics took many forms. Healers conducted curing ceremonies, councils discussed the possibility that illness was caused by sorcery, and communities debated whether to continue hosting the priests, whose presence some blamed and others defended. Some leaders called for expulsion, while others argued that the French alliance was too valuable to risk. The Jesuits, who sometimes feared for their lives, responded by seeking to demonstrate goodwill, by offering care to the sick, and by arguing that their prayers would help. The outcome varied by town. In communities where Christian leaders such as Chihwatenhwa held influence, the mission gained supporters; in others, hostility intensified. The long-term effect was to deepen divisions that the Haudenosaunee campaigns later exploited, though the surviving evidence does not allow historians to measure how far these divisions weakened Wendat resistance.

Division IV — Wendat Dispersal and the Reorganization of the Great Lakes

Sections 45.17–45.21 · Approximately 1648–1660s

Section 45.17

The Crisis of Huronia, 1648–1649

The destruction of Wendat settlements in 1648–1649 stands among the most consequential events of the seventeenth-century Great Lakes. It followed years of Haudenosaunee–Wendat conflict, in which each side had suffered raids, captivity, and losses, and it came after a decade of epidemic mortality that weakened the Wendat's capacity to resist. Chapter 47 reconstructs the broader causes and course of these wars, including Haudenosaunee motivations such as the pursuit of captives to replace population losses, the competition for trade and hunting territory, and diplomatic and economic relationships with the Dutch. For this chapter, the key point is the effect on the Wendat and on the French alliance.

In July 1648 a Haudenosaunee force attacked the Wendat town of Teanaostaiaé, killing many of its residents and the Jesuit Antoine Daniel. In March 1649 a larger force attacked the towns of Taenhatentaron and Saint-Louis. The Jesuits Jean de Brébeuf and Gabriel Lalemant were captured and killed, and the Relations describe their deaths in terms that the Church later used in canonizing them as martyrs. Wendat defenders resisted, but the attackers, who most accounts identify as principally Seneca and Mohawk, succeeded. Wendat leaders ordered many of their remaining towns burned and abandoned, to prevent them from falling into enemy hands, and thousands of people dispersed.

The collapse was not caused by a single battle or a single factor. Disease had reduced the population and weakened leadership. Wendat people also faced food shortages, since fields were disrupted by fighting and by flight. Political divisions over the missionaries and over strategy may have hampered coordinated defense, although how far they did is debated. French assistance was minimal because the colony was too distant and too weak to send substantial forces. The Wendat alliance with the French, so important in earlier decades, could not protect the confederacy.

The Wendat were not passive in the face of these attacks. Defenders at the targeted towns fought, and the Relations record that a few survivors escaped from the attacked towns to warn neighboring communities, though the exact numbers who escaped and who died are not reliably known. Wendat councils made hard decisions in the following weeks. Faced with the prospect of further attacks, with limited stores of food, and with towns that could not be defended, leaders and families chose among several courses: to abandon villages and scatter into the forest, to seek shelter with neighboring nations, or to move toward the island refuge in Georgian Bay. These were not a single evacuation but many separate decisions made by households and nations under extreme pressure, and they set the pattern for the dispersal that followed.

Cause and Effect

Epidemics, War, and the Dispersal of Wendat Communities

Long-term causes

Regional competition for trade and hunting territory; long-running Wendat–Haudenosaunee hostility; the Wendat's central role in French trade; Haudenosaunee practices of captivity and adoption to replace losses.

Short-term causes

The epidemics of c. 1634–1640; political division over the missionaries; shortages and disruptions of agriculture and travel; reduced military strength.

Immediate trigger and event

Haudenosaunee campaigns of 1648 and 1649 against Wendat towns, followed by Wendat decisions to burn and abandon their remaining towns.

Immediate effects

Deaths in battle and from famine; flight to other nations and islands; loss of the Wendat homeland; abandonment of Sainte-Marie.

Long-term consequences

Wendat communities reconstituted in Quebec, within Haudenosaunee towns, and in the west; shifts of Odawa and other nations into commercial roles; new refugee networks.

Historical complexity

No single factor suffices. Disease, war, famine, politics, and trade pressures interacted, and different Wendat communities experienced the crisis differently.

Historical complexity: The crisis was not inevitable. Different alliances, outcomes of earlier battles, or the absence of epidemics might have produced a different balance. Historians therefore focus on how contingent decisions combined with structural pressures.

Section 45.18

The Abandonment of Sainte-Marie and the Refuge at Christian Island

In June 1649, with the Wendat towns in ruins and Haudenosaunee forces nearby, the Jesuits decided to abandon Sainte-Marie. They burned the complex themselves rather than leave it for others, a decision that Parks Canada notes among the central facts of the site's history. They moved, with a large number of Wendat refugees, to Gahoendoe, an island in Georgian Bay now called Christian Island. There they built a fortified settlement, hoping to create a refuge and to continue the mission.

The winter of 1649–1650 was catastrophic. The island lacked adequate food for the thousands of people who sought shelter there. Fishing, stored crops, and supplies were insufficient, and many refugees were already weakened by earlier privation. Famine and disease killed large numbers. Estimates of the death toll vary, and the Jesuit sources describe thousands of deaths; historians generally treat these figures as approximate. The situation was desperate, and the Jesuits' own food stocks could not feed the community.

In the spring of 1650 the Jesuits and a group of several hundred Wendat people, who chose to go with them, left the island and traveled to Quebec, where they arrived in the summer. Other Wendat people made different choices, and the Jesuit plan to maintain a mission in Wendake ended. The event is often presented as the closing of the Jesuit mission, but for the Wendat it was one episode in a broader pattern of decisions made by families and communities about where to find safety.

The abandonment also had consequences for the French. The destruction of Wendake removed the principal Indigenous commercial and diplomatic partner in the interior and disrupted the flow of furs to Quebec. The loss weakened the colony for years and forced French officials to seek new partners. Because of the French dependence on Indigenous intermediaries, this search could only proceed with the cooperation of other Indigenous nations, which is the subject of the following sections.

The refuge on Gahoendoe was an attempt to preserve a community. The Jesuits and Wendat built a fortified settlement, including a stone fort whose remains have been the subject of later archaeological and heritage study, and the settlement attracted thousands of refugees. Their hope was that the island would offer protection and room to plant. In practice, the planting season had been lost, fishing could not feed so many people, and the surrounding mainland was unsafe for hunting and gathering. As winter set in, people died from hunger and disease, and the survivors faced a choice of whether to remain, to flee to other nations, or to accompany the Jesuits to Quebec. Those who traveled to Quebec in 1650 were a minority of those who had sheltered on the island. Others left in separate parties during and after the winter, and the surviving record does not allow their movements to be traced precisely.

Section 45.19

Wendat Survivors, Migration, and the Reconstruction of Community

The dispersal of the Wendat took several directions. A number of people accompanied the Jesuits to Quebec in 1650, and these families settled first near the town, then on the Île d'Orléans, and later at several sites in the vicinity, moving as security and circumstances required. The refugees occupied a succession of temporary settlements, including the Île d'Orléans and, later, Ancienne-Lorette, and it was only in 1697 that the community established itself at the place now known as Wendake (Jeune-Lorette), near Quebec, where the Huron-Wendat Nation continues today. Wendake was therefore the product of nearly five decades of relocation, not a permanent settlement founded in 1650. Parks Canada notes that the eastern migrations ultimately contributed to this community.

Other Wendat people were incorporated into Haudenosaunee communities. The Haudenosaunee practice of adopting captives and refugees to replace lost population meant that many Wendat people, including entire families, were taken into Seneca, Onondaga, Mohawk, and other communities during the 1650s. The details of how adoption worked, and how adoptees were treated, are discussed in Chapter 47. Historians emphasize that adoption was neither a simple form of captivity nor a complete assimilation: adoptees could rise to positions of influence, and some Wendat communities maintained distinct identities within Haudenosaunee towns for many years.

Still others moved west, as discussed in the next section. Some joined neighboring nations such as the Tionontaté. Wendat people thus lived in multiple places, each with its own relationships and political circumstances. They were separated by distance and conflict but remained connected through kinship ties and memory.

The interpretation that guides this chapter is expressed in the title of Kathryn Magee Labelle's study, Dispersed but Not Destroyed, which traces Wendat political and cultural continuity through the seventeenth century, drawing on Wendat oral history alongside documentary and archaeological evidence. This interpretation rejects the older view that the Wendat were destroyed in 1649 and that Huronia's fall ended their history. It recognizes the severity of the catastrophe, the loss of life, and the loss of a homeland, while insisting that the Wendat survived, reorganized, and continued to shape the region's politics. The existence of the Huron-Wendat Nation at Wendake and of the Wyandot nations descended from western communities is the best evidence of this continuity.

It is useful to distinguish the survivor communities. The group that reached Quebec in 1650 settled first near the town and then, in 1651, on the Île d'Orléans, where they were vulnerable to attack. In 1656 Haudenosaunee warriors attacked the Wendat on the island, killing or capturing many, and the community moved to the Quebec area for safety, beginning the series of relocations that ended in 1697 at Jeune-Lorette. Wendat people who entered Haudenosaunee communities did so under varied circumstances, some as captives, some as refugees seeking safety, and some as members of families that negotiated their reception. Reports suggest that the choices differed among the Wendat nations, with some groups' members more often incorporated into Seneca and Onondaga towns, though the sources are fragmentary. Those who went west, notably Wendat who had taken refuge among the Tionontaté, maintained their own leadership and, as the next section shows, became participants in the politics of the upper lakes. Each of these communities carried forward Wendat language, kinship, and memory, and the later connections among them show that the dispersal created a network of related communities rather than the end of a nation.

Section 45.20

Tionontaté, Wendat/Wyandot, and Western Great Lakes Alliances

The western movements of Wendat and Tionontaté survivors are among the more complicated episodes of the period, and reconstructions differ in detail. In December 1649, the Haudenosaunee attacked the Tionontaté, or Petun, neighbors of the Wendat, and many Tionontaté people left their homeland as well. Groups of Wendat and Tionontaté refugees moved through the northern lakes, seeking shelter among Odawa and other peoples. Over the following years they settled for periods in places including islands near Green Bay, the shores of Lake Superior, and later the Straits of Mackinac, moving again when pressured by hostile neighbors, including the Dakota, or by shortages.

These groups should not be treated as a single unit. Wendat and Tionontaté communities were distinct, had different leaders, and sometimes made different choices. Their later descendants, who came to be called Wyandot, emerged from the combination of these groups and their experience, and the name itself derives from Wendat. By the 1670s a Tionontaté-Wendat community was established at Michilimackinac, where Jacques Marquette founded the mission of Saint-Ignace in 1671, and it became part of the region's diplomatic network. Kondiaronk, whose death in Montreal opened this chapter, emerged from this milieu.

Their reception by Odawa, Ojibwe, and other peoples shaped their fate. The Odawa had been trading partners of the Wendat and relied on their agricultural surplus; they had incentives to shelter refugees. At the same time, refugees consumed resources and disrupted local arrangements. Alliances were negotiated, sometimes uneasily. Marriage ties, shared enemies, and common interests in trade helped bind communities. The French, who had lost their primary partner in the Wendat, found in these relocated Wendat and in their Odawa hosts the nucleus of a new network.

The Tionontaté suffered an attack of their own. In December 1649 the Haudenosaunee struck the town of Etharita, which the French called Saint-Jean, where the Jesuit Charles Garnier was killed, and the Tionontaté abandoned their towns soon afterward. The refugees from the two peoples sometimes lived together and sometimes apart. Reconstructions based on Jesuit and later French sources describe movements to islands near Green Bay, where Wendat and Tionontaté people lived alongside Odawa, and then to the Chequamegon region, where the refugees' numbers and cohesion helped to make the area a center of diplomatic and commercial activity. When tensions with the Dakota increased around 1670, many moved east to Michilimackinac. In this setting, Tionontaté leaders, who had been the junior partners of the Wendat in earlier decades, rose to prominence, and Kondiaronk's career reflects the fusion of Wendat and Tionontaté traditions in new leadership. Their descendants, known as Wyandot, would later reappear in the Detroit region, a development that belongs to later chapters.

Section 45.21

Refugee Communities and the Reorganization of Indigenous Diplomacy

The destruction of Huronia was one part of a wider displacement. In the middle decades of the seventeenth century, the Neutral and Erie peoples were defeated or dispersed, and Algonquian-speaking nations along the Ottawa River and in southern Ontario were pushed from their homes. The reasons for these conflicts, including the economic, diplomatic, and demographic pressures on the Haudenosaunee, are the subject of Chapter 47. Their consequence for this chapter is that thousands of people moved west and north in search of safety, bringing with them their own traditions, networks, and grievances.

The region around Green Bay, Lake Superior, and the Straits of Mackinac became a meeting place for refugees and for the nations that had lived there earlier, including Potawatomi, Menominee, Ho-Chunk, Sauk, Mesquakie (Fox), and Ojibwe communities. Their coexistence was uneasy. Some nations competed for scarce resources, such as fisheries and hunting territory. Others formed new alliances through marriage and shared ceremony. The arrival of populous agricultural refugees changed local economies, and the movement of people and goods created new commercial opportunities that Odawa and other communities used to expand their influence.

The reorganization of alliances changed the balance of power. The Wendat had been the primary Indigenous partner of the French; after their dispersal, Odawa, Ojibwe, and others took on larger roles. French traders, including the explorers Pierre-Esprit Radisson and Médard Chouart des Groseilliers in the 1650s and 1660s, found that they could reach Lake Superior and Green Bay with the cooperation of these nations. Odawa traders, in particular, brought furs from the west to Montreal in large canoe convoys. Their role, examined economically in Chapter 46, made them politically important to the French.

The reconstruction of diplomacy was gradual. After 1650 the French had no large and stable Indigenous partner in the interior, and for several years the trade at Montreal and Quebec fell sharply. Western traders, especially Odawa, then restored the connection. Indigenous flotillas from the upper lakes reached Montreal in the 1650s and returned in subsequent years, and French traders such as Groseilliers and Radisson traveled with Indigenous partners to Lake Superior. The Odawa also tried to protect their intermediary position, according to French reports, discouraging direct contact between the French and more distant nations when it served their interests. The French thus encountered a regional system in which Indigenous nations used refugees' skills, kin ties, and knowledge of routes to build a new network, one that favored the nations best placed to carry goods and to host newcomers.

Division V — The Western Great Lakes and the French Alliance Network

Sections 45.22–45.28 · Approximately 1660s–1690s

Section 45.22

The Anishinaabeg and the Political Geography of the Western Lakes

The nations the French met in the western lakes were not newcomers to the region or dependents of the vanished Wendat. The Odawa, Ojibwe, and Potawatomi spoke closely related Algonquian languages, shared traditions of origin and clan affiliation, and described themselves in overlapping ways as Anishinaabeg, a term Chapter 27 explains. Each, however, was a distinct political community with its own leaders, territories, and interests. The later phrase "Three Fires," sometimes used to describe an Odawa–Ojibwe–Potawatomi relationship, reflects a political understanding whose seventeenth-century form is less clearly documented and should not be projected backward without care.

The Odawa, whose name French writers often connected with trading, occupied the northern Lake Huron region and Manitoulin Island in the early seventeenth century, and many moved west after the pressures of the 1640s and 1650s. They became prominent in carrying goods between the western nations and the French, drawing on long-standing relations with the Wendat and on knowledge of canoe routes. The Ojibwe, concentrated around Sault Sainte-Marie and the southern shore of Lake Superior, held a strategic position at the rapids connecting Lake Superior to Lake Huron; the French called the people of the Sault the Saulteurs. The Potawatomi, who had been pushed or had moved toward Green Bay in the middle of the century, became involved in the region's alliances and later expanded around the southern end of Lake Michigan.

Neighboring peoples shaped their relations. The Menominee and Ho-Chunk (Winnebago) lived around Green Bay, the latter having been among the first people visited by Jean Nicolet, who traveled to the region in 1634. The Dakota lived to the west of Lake Superior, and relations with them alternated between trade and warfare, contributing to the movements of Wendat refugees. The Mesquakie (Fox), Sauk, Mascouten, Miami, and Illinois lived to the south and west. Control of routes and fisheries differed from nation to nation: the Ojibwe at the Sault controlled the whitefish fishery and the passage between lakes; the Odawa carried goods to Montreal; the Wendat and Tionontaté at Michilimackinac offered agricultural produce and diplomatic skill.

Recognizing these nations as independent actors changes the story of French expansion. When a French trader or missionary arrived at Chequamegon, Green Bay, or Michilimackinac, he arrived in territory where decisions about hospitality, protection, and trade were made by Indigenous councils. Those councils decided whether to receive the stranger, how to evaluate his goods and claims, and whether to align with French policies. The French did not so much discover these peoples as enter into negotiated relationships with them.

The three principal Anishinaabe nations pursued distinct goals. The Odawa, concerned with maintaining commercial and diplomatic advantage, used their canoe fleets and kin links to connect the western nations with the French, and they sheltered refugees whose labor and agricultural skill strengthened their communities. The Ojibwe at the Sault regulated passage through the rapids, benefitted from the whitefish fishery that fed their large seasonal gatherings, and traded with northern neighbors to the north and west. The Potawatomi, who had experienced displacement and warfare, moved to Green Bay and its islands in search of security and trade, and they later expanded south along Lake Michigan. These goals sometimes overlapped and sometimes conflicted. The three nations were linked by language and kinship but made decisions through their own councils, and the French encountered them as separate negotiating partners.

Section 45.23

Claude Allouez and the Lake Superior Missions, 1665 Onward

In the early 1660s the western trade had resumed on a large scale, as Odawa and other canoe parties again reached Montreal with furs, and the French renewed their effort to extend missions to the west. In 1665 the Jesuit Claude Allouez traveled with an Odawa convoy to Chequamegon Bay on the southern shore of Lake Superior, a gathering place for refugees and for nations of the region, including Wendat, Tionontaté, Odawa, Ojibwe, and others. There he founded the mission of Saint-Esprit. His journey was possible because Indigenous traders agreed to carry him and because communities at Chequamegon permitted him to live among them.

The mission's position was precarious. Allouez was a guest in a diverse and sometimes tense environment. The communities at Chequamegon included refugees whose own traditions had been disrupted and who had varied responses to a French priest, as well as host peoples with their own leaders and ceremonies. He recorded in the Relations his efforts to learn languages, to preach, to baptize dying people, and to observe Indigenous customs, and he reported both interest and resistance. His reports also record the importance of Indigenous protection: leaders who invited or tolerated him, families who fed him, and councils whose decisions determined whether he could remain. After a few years he left to take up work elsewhere, and in 1669 Jacques Marquette took over the mission.

Relations between the Chequamegon communities and the Dakota deteriorated in the late 1660s, and by around 1670 or 1671 the Wendat and Tionontaté communities, along with Marquette, moved east to Michilimackinac. The mission at Chequamegon thus ended not through failure of conversion alone, but through the movement of the Indigenous communities it served. This pattern recurs: missions followed Indigenous populations, and their success or failure depended on regional politics.

Allouez also traveled to Green Bay, where in 1669 he established a mission, and his journeys across the western lakes helped to establish French contact with nations such as the Menominee, Potawatomi, Sauk, Mesquakie, and Miami. These contacts were not entirely one-directional: nations such as the Miami and Illinois also sent delegations to the French, showing that the extension of relations was a matter of mutual interest. The Jesuits sought Christian converts, but they also served as intermediaries and sources of information for the French government, which valued their reports on trade, politics, and resources.

At Chequamegon Allouez built a chapel in 1665 and spent two years preaching, learning languages, and observing the gatherings that drew visitors from many nations. He recorded the presence of Wendat, Tionontaté, Odawa, and Ojibwe residents and of travelers from farther west. In 1666 a delegation of Illinois visitors reached Allouez at Chequamegon; his descriptions, reported in 1667, conveyed information about a great river and populous nations to the south and stimulated French interest in further journeys. This exchange shows how the mission functioned as a node in a communication network: Indigenous visitors brought information to the priest, and the priest relayed it to colonial authorities who then acted on it. Allouez's reports also testify to the diversity of religious and political positions at Chequamegon, where some residents listened with interest while others, including leaders whose authority depended on ancestral ceremony, opposed him. Although he measured success by baptisms, the larger effect of the mission was to bring French contacts into the information networks of the western nations.

Section 45.24

Sault Sainte-Marie, Saint-Ignace, and the Northwestern Mission Network

Sault Sainte-Marie, at the rapids of the St. Marys River, was a natural gathering place. Ojibwe communities fished the rapids, and travelers between Lake Superior and Lake Huron had to portage or pass nearby. In 1668 the Jesuits Claude Dablon and Jacques Marquette established a mission there. The Virtual Museum of New France and other sources date the foundation to that year. The mission and the French residence that grew around it endured, but its permanence depended on Indigenous consent and on the seasonal movements of its neighbors.

A few years later, in 1671, Marquette moved with Wendat and Tionontaté refugees to the north side of the Straits of Mackinac and founded the mission of Saint-Ignace, at what became known as Michilimackinac. The site was strategic: the straits connected Lakes Michigan and Huron and lay at the center of the western routes. Its population grew in the 1670s and after as Odawa, Wendat, Tionontaté, and other nations made it a residence and gathering point, and French traders and soldiers followed. The Jesuits built a church, a residence, and a school, and some Indigenous people accepted baptism.

The mission network was not simply a French colonial export. Indigenous people determined where missionaries could reside, how long they could stay, and what activities they could pursue. Missions at Sault Sainte-Marie, Green Bay, and Saint-Ignace flourished when local leaders supported them and withered when communities moved or when leaders turned against the priests. The missions also became centers of diplomacy and trade. French traders and officials used them as bases, and Indigenous leaders used them as places to meet French representatives.

The relocation of Marquette's flock to Saint-Ignace reflected the shifting demography of the region. As the Dakota threat affected Chequamegon and as the Haudenosaunee remained a danger in the east, Michilimackinac offered a central position that was relatively secure. The concentration of refugees there created the conditions for the multinational community discussed in Section 45.26.

At the Sault, Ojibwe and neighboring communities gathered seasonally to fish, and Marquette and Dablon established the mission within this pattern. The French residence there functioned as a stopping point for travelers and a place to meet Indigenous people who came to trade or to ask for presents. In 1671 the move to Saint-Ignace occurred when Marquette and Wendat and Tionontaté refugees left Chequamegon, and the Odawa established their own settlements nearby, on and around the straits. The mission's residents included people of different nations who lived in separate villages while sharing the church and, in some cases, intermarrying. Marquette left Saint-Ignace in 1673 to join Jolliet's voyage, and Jesuit successors continued the work, most prominently Étienne de Carheil among the Wendat and Tionontaté in the 1690s. The sequence shows how missions followed Indigenous settlements rather than creating them.

Section 45.25

The Ceremony of 1671 and French Claims to the Interior

In June 1671, at Sault Sainte-Marie, a French agent named Simon-François Daumont de Saint-Lusson conducted a ceremony taking possession of vast interior territories in the name of Louis XIV. He acted on instructions from Jean Talon, the intendant of New France, who wanted to explore for copper and to counter English ambitions, including those arising from the Hudson Bay trade. The interpreter Nicolas Perrot had traveled through the western lakes inviting nations to attend, and French sources, including a Jesuit account by Claude Dablon and Saint-Lusson's own procès-verbal, report that representatives of numerous Indigenous nations attended, with the Jesuit narrative reporting fourteen nations. The numbers are not uniform across the documents: a separately preserved copy of Saint-Lusson's act of possession, published in Edge of Empire, refers to seventeen nations assembled in May 1671, so the counts should not be treated as identical figures for an identical gathering. The exact ceremony date is also reported differently: the contemporary Jesuit account dates it to 4 June, while a number of later published histories give 14 June. Those discrepancies should be attributed rather than silently reconciled.

The ceremony followed European conventions. A cross was raised, a post bearing the royal arms was set up, and Saint-Lusson proclaimed French possession of the lands around the lakes and of territories described as discovered and yet to be discovered. The assembled French shouted acclamations of the king. Jesuits and other French observers participated, and the proceedings were recorded and signed. Indigenous attendees, according to the French accounts, were offered gifts and heard speeches, and some leaders expressed friendship.

What the ceremony meant to its Indigenous audience is much less certain. The surviving documents were written by French observers for a French audience, and they present the nations as assenting to French sovereignty or at least offering no objection. But Indigenous attendees came to a diplomatic gathering in which gift-giving, speeches, and the display of French power were standard features. They had no reason to understand a European legal act of possession as a transfer of their territory, and there is no evidence that anyone in the region changed their conduct as a consequence. For the following decades, Indigenous nations continued to govern their lands, to decide who could enter them, and to demand gifts and respect from the French.

Preparation for the ceremony itself followed Indigenous diplomatic practice. Perrot traveled to the western nations with presents and invitations, a process that depended on his reputation among them and on their willingness to travel to the Sault. The nations that came did so as guests with their own aims: to receive presents, to observe the French, to renew relations, and to demonstrate their own standing among one another. Jesuits present at the Sault, including Dablon and Allouez, added a religious dimension, and the French reports describe both Christian and royal symbols. Participants may have seen the cross and the post as part of an unfamiliar ceremony of friendship and power rather than as a transfer of rights, and the French observers' own accounts note that Indigenous nations came and went as they pleased.

Historical Debate

Sovereignty and the French Ceremony of 1671

The question: Did the ceremony at Sault Sainte-Marie establish French sovereignty over the interior, or was it a performance whose meaning differed radically for French and Indigenous participants?

Interpretation A — A founding act of French claim: The ceremony is read as part of the process by which European powers asserted sovereignty through ritual possession. On this view, it represented a genuine assertion of French imperial ambition, one that later provided the legal basis for French claims against rivals such as England, even if it did not alter Indigenous control on the ground.
Interpretation B — A diplomatic performance with divergent meanings: The ceremony is read as an event in Indigenous diplomatic space. Indigenous participants may have understood it as an alliance gathering, a reaffirmation of friendship, or a display by a potential partner. French claims of sovereignty were assertions addressed primarily to European rivals, and had no immediate effect on Indigenous authority.

Evidence: The principal sources are the procès-verbal and Jesuit narrative, both French. Indigenous accounts of the event have not been preserved in comparable form. Historians infer Indigenous understanding from the conduct of nations in subsequent years and from comparative evidence about Indigenous diplomacy.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement is interpretive. It concerns how to read ritual acts, what weight to give European legal claims in relation to actual power, and how far Indigenous consent can be inferred from presence at a ceremony.

Why it matters: The question bears on the distinction between claims, consent, alliance, and effective control. Later European powers, and later states, relied on such acts to argue for sovereignty, and contemporary legal and historical debates still engage with them.

Student task: List the facts that both interpretations accept. Then identify one additional kind of evidence, such as an Indigenous oral history or later council record, that would help decide between them.

Section 45.26

Michilimackinac as a Multinational Diplomatic Center

By the 1670s and 1680s Michilimackinac had become one of the most important meeting places in the interior. The straits offered abundant fish, a position at the junction of several lakes, and relative security. Odawa communities settled around the straits, Wendat and Tionontaté refugees lived near the mission of Saint-Ignace, and travelers of many nations passed through. French traders, Jesuits, and soldiers resided there, and the French eventually constructed a post, known in the 1690s as Fort de Buade, near the mission. The population fluctuated with the season, since traders gathered in summer and dispersed in winter.

Politically, Michilimackinac was a place where many interests met. Odawa leaders and Wendat/Tionontaté leaders such as Kondiaronk negotiated among themselves, with other western nations, with Haudenosaunee envoys, and with French officials. Decisions about war, peace, and trade were made in councils attended by representatives of multiple communities. The French commandants, who were few in number, had to work through persuasion and gifts. Religious differences complicated matters. Jesuits sought to promote Christianity and to restrict the sale of alcohol to Indigenous people, which they saw as destructive, while French traders, who profited from brandy, and some officials who valued trade, opposed these restrictions. Indigenous people had their own opinions about the matter and some leaders sought to limit alcohol in their communities.

The relationships at Michilimackinac were also shaped by rivalries. Odawa and Wendat/Tionontaté leaders sometimes competed for influence with the French and with other nations. The Haudenosaunee threat in the 1680s and 1690s tested their cooperation, and diplomacy with the Haudenosaunee, including exploratory contacts and separate negotiations, led to disagreements with French officials who wanted the western nations to remain committed to war. The post's garrison fluctuated and in 1696–1697 the French government ordered the western posts closed, a decision that Chapter 46 discusses in relation to a glut of beaver. The closure did not end Indigenous residence at Michilimackinac, since the communities there continued to live and trade, though the French military presence withdrew for a time.

The documentary record of Michilimackinac is uneven but informative. French officials and missionaries wrote reports about the region in the 1680s and 1690s. The Jesuit Étienne de Carheil, who served the Wendat and Tionontaté at Michilimackinac, criticized the liquor trade and the conduct of French soldiers and traders; his detailed surviving complaint to the intendant is dated August 30, 1702, and offers retrospective evidence about conditions in the preceding decade. His complaints reflect a missionary's views and interests, but also illuminate concerns shared by some Indigenous leaders. Nicolas Perrot, who spent years in the west, later wrote a memoir of Indigenous customs and western diplomacy, a retrospective source that must be used with care because it was composed after the events and reflects his own experience and views. Archaeological excavation at Fort Michilimackinac and at the earlier Saint-Ignace site has added evidence about houses, trade goods, and the mix of European and Indigenous material culture. Together these sources show a place where several communities lived close together, negotiated their differences, and responded to French policies according to their own interests.

Historical Vocabulary
The Middle Ground

Definition: In Richard White's influential 1991 book The Middle Ground: Indians, Empires, and Republics in the Great Lakes Region, 1650–1815, the middle ground is a historical situation in which Indigenous peoples and Europeans, neither able to dominate the other, created shared practices of diplomacy and exchange, often through creative misunderstanding of each other's cultures.

Historical context: White's argument applies to the pays d'en haut after the dispersal of the Wendat and into the age of British and American power. It has been widely adopted and widely criticized.

Why it matters: The concept highlights mutual accommodation and the cultural inventiveness of diplomacy, and it challenges narratives of simple conquest.

Potential misunderstanding: Critics argue that the concept understates Indigenous power in some periods, overstates cultural mixing, and cannot be applied equally to all Indigenous–French relationships. In this chapter, it is used as an interpretation to be tested, not as a label that automatically applies to every region or decade.

Section 45.27

The Illinois, Miami, and the Expanding Southern Great Lakes Networks

South and west of Lake Michigan lived nations whose territories joined the Great Lakes to the Mississippi valley. The Illinois, a confederation of related Algonquian-speaking peoples including the Kaskaskia, Peoria, and others, lived in villages along the Illinois River and nearby. The Miami, with the Mascouten and Kickapoo, occupied lands near the southern end of Lake Michigan and the Fox River, shifting in response to warfare and opportunity. These peoples hunted bison on the prairies, farmed maize, and traded widely, and they were drawn into the French alliance system through a mix of invitation and necessity.

In 1673 Louis Jolliet, a fur trader and mapmaker born in New France, and Jacques Marquette set out from Michilimackinac with five other Frenchmen to explore a great river described by Indigenous informants. Their journey illustrates the dependence of French exploration on Indigenous knowledge. Marquette's journal reports that the party relied on information from Indigenous people along the route, and that at a Mascouten village on the Fox River, local leaders provided two guides who led the party across the portage to the Wisconsin River. They reached the Mississippi in June 1673. Further south, they met the Illinois, who received them with ceremony, including the calumet, and with hospitality. They continued downriver, learned from Indigenous people that Europeans were established farther south, and turned back before reaching the Gulf, returning by the Illinois River and a short portage to Lake Michigan.

The voyage was not a solitary European discovery. It followed routes known to Indigenous people for generations and depended on guides, hosts, and diplomatic protocols. The calumet ceremony offered the travelers protection, and their acceptance by the Illinois demonstrates the role of Indigenous diplomatic customs in managing contact. The journey also brought the Illinois and Miami more directly into French diplomatic and commercial calculations. Marquette returned in 1674 to found a mission among the Illinois and died in 1675 on the return journey. In the following years, the explorer René-Robert Cavelier de La Salle sought to build a commercial and strategic network in the region, constructing posts in Illinois country in 1679 and after, with limited success and the pressure of Haudenosaunee attacks on the Illinois in the early 1680s. Indigenous nations, including the Miami and Illinois, welcomed French trade and support against enemies, but they also resisted French attempts to control their commerce.

For the French, the southern networks meant access to the Mississippi, new trade possibilities, and strategic reach toward the Gulf. For the Illinois and Miami, the French were one among several partners and rivals. Their own interests, including defense against the Haudenosaunee and others, shaped how they interacted with French officials, and their decisions would continue to affect the region.

Illinois and Miami leaders made their own political calculations. The Illinois confederation, vulnerable to raids from the east, welcomed French traders and missionaries as potential allies and sources of metal goods and weapons, and they invited Marquette to return. They also sought to keep control of their relations with other nations, and when La Salle established posts in the country, including Fort Crèvecoeur in 1680, Illinois people pursued their own interests in the arrangement, which could not protect them from Haudenosaunee attacks that same year. Later, as French commanders including La Salle gathered allies near the Illinois River, the Miami and Illinois helped form concentrations of population around the French post known as Fort Saint-Louis, an arrangement that served their own need for defense and trade. The Miami, who moved in response to danger and opportunity, balanced French, Haudenosaunee, and other relationships, and French officials recognized that they could neither command nor take their loyalty for granted.

Section 45.28

Indigenous Women, Marriage, Kinship, and the Making of French Alliances

French men in the pays d'en haut rarely lived outside Indigenous social structures. Traders who wintered in Indigenous communities depended on women for food, clothing, moccasins, snowshoes, canoe repair, and knowledge of local resources, and many formed marriages or long-term partnerships with Indigenous women, often according to Indigenous custom rather than Church or colonial law. Such relationships created kinship ties between French traders and Indigenous families. They gave traders access to networks of trade and information, and they gave Indigenous families links to French goods and support.

These relationships should not be reduced to a French strategy. Indigenous women and their families made choices based on their own interests. Marriage could secure a family's access to trade goods and influence, it could cement alliances between communities, and it was embedded in practices of exchange and obligation. Women exercised significant authority in many Indigenous societies, controlling agricultural and household production, participating in councils, and directing the labor of their households. In the western lakes, women's work in preparing hides, processing food, and producing goods for trade made them central to the economy. French men entering these systems did so within Indigenous expectations.

The marriages produced children of mixed ancestry, who grew up in Indigenous communities, in French settlements, or between the two. Families of this kind became part of the social fabric of places such as Michilimackinac and Sault Sainte-Marie, and they influenced later communities, including the Métis nations whose histories are traced in Chapter 52. It would be anachronistic to describe all such families as Métis in the later sense of a distinct people; in the seventeenth century, children's identities depended on kinship, residence, and community recognition. Church and state officials were ambivalent: some considered such marriages as a means of Christianizing and binding allies, others condemned them as immoral and disorderly, and policies varied over time.

Kinship diplomacy extended beyond marriage. Leaders addressed one another with kinship terms, adopted individuals into families, and exchanged people as a way of building trust. Women played roles as speakers, mediators, and bearers of memory, though French records usually mention them only incidentally. Scholars use sources such as mission records, trade accounts, and later oral traditions to recover more of this history.

Women's economic and diplomatic roles were broad. They prepared fish, meat, and maize for travelers, gathered and processed wild rice, tanned hides, made clothing and moccasins, sewed canoes with spruce root and sealed them with resin, and participated in the exchange of goods in their communities. Marriages between Indigenous women and French traders could connect a trader to a household's network of trade partners and also give the woman's family a stake in the trader's success. Women could also speak in councils or influence decisions through male relatives, and they ensured that obligations of hospitality and kinship were met. These unions in the seventeenth-century pays d'en haut should be distinguished from the later development of Métis peoples as distinct communities with their own identities, a process that gathered force in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries and is traced in Chapter 52. In the seventeenth century, the children of such unions were generally raised within their mothers' communities or in French settlements, and the evidence for their social position varies by place and by family.

Everyday Life and Social History

Kinship and Multinational Communities

A settlement such as Michilimackinac was not a single community but a cluster of households with different origins and languages. Odawa families, Wendat and Tionontaté households, French traders, soldiers, and missionaries lived near one another, and many people spoke more than one language. Daily life followed the seasons: the fishing and trading of summer and the dispersal to hunting grounds in winter. Women maintained fields, gathered and processed food, and prepared goods for trade, while men fished, hunted, traveled, and negotiated.

Intermarriage, godparent relationships created through Catholic baptism, shared ceremonies, and trade partnerships connected families across cultural lines. Disputes could arise over alcohol, debts, or the behavior of soldiers and traders, and they were managed through councils and the intervention of kin. The evidence for this social world is scattered across mission registers, official correspondence, archaeological finds at Michilimackinac, and later oral histories, and each category has limits.

Division VI — The Limits of French Power and the Great Peace of Montreal

Section 45.29

French Governors, Western Alliances, and the Limits of Colonial Authority

From the 1660s the French crown took more direct control of the colony, appointing governors, intendants, and a Sovereign Council, and sending troops. Officials wanted to regulate the fur trade, protect the colony from rivals, and expand French influence. In the upper country they faced a set of competing demands. Military security required the allegiance of western nations against the Haudenosaunee and English. Commercial regulation sought to limit unlicensed trade by coureurs de bois, men who traveled to Indigenous communities without permits. The Church, especially the Jesuits, sought to restrict the liquor trade and to expand missions. And Indigenous allies expected gifts, trade, and respect, and acted on their own interests.

Governors such as Louis de Buade de Frontenac, who served from 1672 to 1682 and again from 1689 to 1698, tried to extend French influence through forts, alliances, and the promotion of western trade, while sometimes clashing with the Jesuits and with colonial administrators. In 1681 the crown introduced a system of congés, licenses to trade in the interior, limited in number, in an attempt to regulate the western trade. Enforcement was uneven. The distance of the pays d'en haut from Quebec, the small number of French officials there, and the need for Indigenous cooperation limited what governors could achieve.

The war with the Haudenosaunee in the 1680s and 1690s tested the alliance. Expeditions led by Governor Denonville in 1687 and the devastating raids of 1689, including the attack on Lachine, strained French resources and left the colony dependent on allies for defense and for intelligence. Western nations such as the Odawa and the Wendat/Tionontaté contributed warriors, but they also pursued their own interests, including separate negotiations with the Haudenosaunee and trade with English merchants at Albany when it was advantageous. The episode of 1688, in which Kondiaronk is reported to have ambushed a Haudenosaunee delegation traveling to negotiate, is attested, but its interpretation depends heavily on the polemical account of Lahontan, and accounts differ in emphasis about its diplomatic consequences. Historians do not treat it as the single cause of renewed warfare, which had deeper roots in the competition for trade and territory examined in Chapter 47. What is clear is that western leaders did not accept French direction automatically.

French policy toward the interior also shifted with the economics of the trade. In 1696 Louis XIV ordered the closure of most western posts and the suppression of the congé system, in part because of a surplus of beaver and in part because of concerns about the cost and morality of the western trade, as Chapter 46 explains. The decision alarmed allies and merchants, who feared that English traders would fill the gap. Within a few years officials reversed course, concluding that abandonment would endanger alliances. On 24 July 1701 Antoine de Lamothe Cadillac established Fort Pontchartrain du Détroit, partly to secure the strait against the English and partly to draw western nations into closer French orbit. This recurrent policy instability itself illustrates the limits of French authority: policy was set in Versailles, but outcomes depended on decisions made in Indigenous councils.

Section 45.30

Indigenous Diplomacy and the Great Peace of Montreal, 1701

By the end of the 1690s, exhaustion, shifting alliances, and the prospect of renewed European war encouraged many nations to seek an end to the long conflicts. Haudenosaunee leaders, facing losses and the risk of being caught between the French and English, pursued negotiation, and western nations sought security for their hunting grounds and trade. A general peace of 1697–1698 among the European powers, the Treaty of Ryswick, did not by itself end the Indigenous conflicts, which required separate agreements. Haudenosaunee and French representatives exchanged envoys and prisoners in 1699 and 1700, and in the summer of 1701 hundreds of delegates gathered at Montreal. Chapter 47 reconstructs the military history that led to this point.

The negotiations followed Indigenous protocols. Delegates arrived by canoe, were greeted with ceremony, and participated in councils in which speeches were delivered and wampum belts exchanged. The calumet was smoked, and the symbolism of burying the hatchet and kindling a common fire was invoked. Governor Callière took part in the councils and had to follow the diplomatic forms. Kondiaronk, a leading orator, played a major role, and his illness and death on 2 August, in the midst of the proceedings, made a deep impression on the assembly; the French records describe a public funeral with military honors, and Indigenous delegates participated in mourning.

On 4 August 1701 representatives of the French and of the Haudenosaunee, along with delegates of western and eastern allies, signed the treaty. Commonly cited figures speak of some 1,300 delegates from roughly three dozen nations, though the numbers vary with the sources. The agreement committed the parties to end hostilities, to return prisoners, and to settle future disputes by consultation rather than by force, with the French governor acting as mediator. The Haudenosaunee agreed to remain neutral in any future war between the French and English, a position they sustained for several decades, and arrangements about hunting grounds were reached. Most delegates signed using pictographic marks representing clans or nations, such as animal figures.

The Great Peace did not end all conflict. Disputes among nations continued, French and English rivalry intensified, and subsequent decades saw wars such as the conflicts with the Mesquakie. But the settlement demonstrated that Indigenous diplomacy could produce a broad, lasting accord that served Indigenous interests and that the French accepted on those terms. It also set the stage for the Haudenosaunee policy of neutrality discussed in Chapter 55 and for the continuing negotiations of the eighteenth century.

Chapter Review and Historical Thinking Checkpoint

This final section consolidates the chapter's major arguments about Indigenous sovereignty, diplomatic alliance, missionary encounters, population displacement, and French colonial dependence. Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that French expansion into the Great Lakes in the seventeenth century was possible because Indigenous peoples controlled the political alliances, transportation corridors, commercial networks, and diplomatic relationships on which the French depended. The Great Lakes were an Indigenous world organized by established nations, confederacies, kinship systems, and routes of travel and exchange, and the French entered it as newcomers who needed partners.

The early alliance, formed at Tadoussac in 1603 and extended through Champlain's years, was political and military as well as commercial. Innu, Algonquin, and Wendat leaders sought French support in conflicts with the Mohawk and in the competition for trade, and the French needed their food, knowledge, and protection. Quebec's survival was never secure without Indigenous cooperation. French claims of sovereignty and alliance, though expressed in the language of kingship, were in practice dependent on Indigenous consent.

Indigenous conceptions of alliance emphasized reciprocity, gift-giving, kinship terms, and ongoing obligation, while French officials tended to think in terms of hierarchy and loyalty to the crown. This difference produced persistent misunderstandings, but also a workable diplomatic practice in which French governors learned to act within Indigenous forms.

The Catholic missions, above all the Jesuit mission among the Wendat, brought conversion, resistance, and debate. Wendat communities evaluated missionaries on their own terms, and responses ranged from devotion to hostility. The Jesuit Relations are indispensable but partisan sources that must be read alongside archaeology and Wendat history. Women's roles in households and communities shaped how Christianity was received and how it affected kinship.

Epidemics in the 1630s, together with political division and Haudenosaunee military pressure, produced the crisis of 1648–1649. Wendat towns were destroyed or abandoned, Sainte-Marie was burned, and thousands died or fled. The explanation for this catastrophe lies in the interaction of disease, war, famine, and politics rather than in any single cause.

The result was dispersal, not disappearance. Wendat survivors established communities near Quebec, were incorporated into Haudenosaunee towns, and moved west with Tionontaté people to join the Odawa and other nations. Their descendants, including the Huron-Wendat Nation at Wendake and the Wyandot nations, maintain distinct identities today. Wendat leaders such as Kondiaronk shaped later diplomacy.

The western Great Lakes became the center of a new alliance network in which Anishinaabe, Wendat/Wyandot, Illinois, Miami, and other nations were decisive. Jesuit missions at Chequamegon, Sault Sainte-Marie, and Saint-Ignace followed Indigenous invitation and protection, and Michilimackinac became a multinational center of diplomacy and trade. The 1671 ceremony at Sault Sainte-Marie illustrates the gap between French claims of sovereignty and Indigenous understandings and control.

Indigenous women, through marriage, kinship, and economic roles, were central to the making of alliances in the western lakes, and the families produced by these relationships contributed to later communities. French exploration, as in the Marquette–Jolliet voyage, followed Indigenous knowledge and depended on Indigenous hospitality.

By the 1690s the limits of French authority were evident. Governors struggled to coordinate allies whose interests diverged from French objectives, and royal policy toward the west changed repeatedly. The Great Peace of Montreal of 1701 brought together the French, the Haudenosaunee, and numerous western nations in a settlement shaped by Indigenous diplomatic traditions, demonstrating the continuing importance of Indigenous political systems.

Historians continue to debate how best to characterize this relationship, whether as French empire, Indigenous network, or the middle ground described by Richard White. This chapter has argued that New France did not create the Indigenous political order of the Great Lakes. French colonial ambitions had to operate within it.

Key Terms

Pays d'en haut
The French term for the interior beyond Montreal and the Ottawa River, including the Great Lakes; a colonial category rather than an Indigenous political unit.
Alliance
A relationship of mutual obligation between political communities, maintained through gifts, councils, and conduct rather than by a fixed contract.
Confederacy
A union of distinct nations coordinating on shared matters while retaining separate councils, as with the Wendat and the Haudenosaunee.
Wendat
The people called Huron by the French; a confederacy of Iroquoian-speaking agricultural nations near Georgian Bay.
Innu
Algonquian-speaking people of the lower St. Lawrence and Saguenay, called Montagnais by the French.
Anishinaabeg
Related Algonquian-speaking peoples including the Odawa, Ojibwe, and Potawatomi.
Tionontaté
Iroquoian-speaking neighbors of the Wendat, also called Petun, whose displaced communities merged with Wendat refugees in the west.
Wyandot
The name of nations descended from Wendat and Tionontaté communities that moved west.
Portage
A carrying place where canoes and cargo are moved overland between waterways or around rapids.
Council
A deliberative gathering in which leaders and community members debated and decided matters such as war, peace, and alliance.
Calumet
A ceremonial pipe used in the western lakes and Mississippi valley to establish peace, hospitality, or alliance.
Condolence
A diplomatic practice of offering words and gifts to relieve grief and resolve the consequences of a death.
Kinship diplomacy
The use of kinship terms, adoption, and marriage to structure political relationships and obligations.
Mission
A religious station or the broader enterprise of attempting to spread Christianity.
Jesuit Relations
Annual reports of the Jesuits in New France, published 1632–1673, indispensable but partisan sources.
Donné
A lay assistant who bound himself to serve the Jesuit mission.
Feast of the Dead
A Wendat ceremony in which the remains of the dead of a community were gathered and reburied together, reaffirming social bonds.

The Jesuit headquarters in Wendake, 1639–1649.
Gahoendoe
The island, now called Christian Island, where Wendat refugees and Jesuits sheltered in 1649–1650.
Diaspora
The dispersal of a people from a homeland to multiple locations, with continuing connections.

A European ritual asserting a claim to territory, such as that held at Sault Sainte-Marie in 1671.
Procès-verbal
A formal French written record of an official act or ceremony.
Middle ground
Richard White's term for a zone of accommodation in which Indigenous peoples and Europeans created shared diplomatic practices; an influential but debated interpretation.
Congé
A license authorizing trade in the interior, introduced in 1681.
Coureur de bois
A French trader operating in Indigenous country without an official license.
Intendant
The royal official responsible for administration, justice, and finance in New France.
Michilimackinac
The straits between Lakes Michigan and Huron and the surrounding multinational community, mission, and post.
Great Peace of Montreal
The 1701 treaty among the French, Haudenosaunee, and many western and eastern Indigenous nations ending a long period of conflict.
Exonym and endonym
An exonym is a name used by outsiders (Huron); an endonym is a people's own name (Wendat).

Chapter Reflection

The seventeenth-century Great Lakes show how misleading it can be to read colonial history from the point of view of the colonizer's maps and proclamations. The claims of Champlain, Saint-Lusson, and the crown described a French empire in the interior. The evidence of councils, canoes, marriages, missions, and treaties describes something different: a world in which Indigenous nations decided who could travel, trade, preach, and settle, and in which French officials worked, often anxiously, within Indigenous rules.

The chapter also shows that Indigenous history in this period was not simply a response to the French. The Wendat, Odawa, Ojibwe, Illinois, Miami, and Haudenosaunee had their own political goals, rivalries, and strategies, and they used French goods, weapons, and alliances for their own purposes. The catastrophe that befell the Wendat was a product of Indigenous as well as European history, and so was their survival. The western alliance network that emerged afterward was largely an Indigenous creation to which the French adapted.

These developments shaped what followed. The alliances formed in the seventeenth century became the foundation of eighteenth-century diplomacy, including the contests among Indigenous nations, France, and Britain examined in Chapters 55 to 58. The Haudenosaunee neutrality and the western alliance shaped the balance of power until the Seven Years' War, and Indigenous expectations of reciprocity and respect continued to challenge British and American practice. The Wendat and Wyandot communities continued to appear in the history of the region, as later chapters show.

Finally, the chapter contributes to this textbook's argument that Indigenous nations are not remnants of an earlier North America but continuing political communities. The Huron-Wendat Nation, the Anishinaabe nations, the Illinois and Miami peoples, and the Haudenosaunee remain present, and their seventeenth-century histories of negotiation, dispersal, and reconstruction are part of the continuous histories this textbook traces.
