Epidemics and Demographic Catastrophe

Indigenous Peoples of North America: A Continental History

Chapter Opening

Introduced diseases killed enormous numbers of Indigenous people in the sixteenth century and after. Yet "disease inevitably wiped out Indigenous peoples" is not an adequate historical explanation, because epidemics did not unfold alone: they moved through societies already being transformed by war, enslavement, forced labor, hunger, displacement, and the collapse of systems of care, and Indigenous communities responded to them with knowledge, caregiving, migration, and reconstruction.

In the winter of 1518–1519, Spanish clergy and officials on the island of Hispaniola wrote to the Crown about a sickness that was spreading rapidly among the Indigenous people who remained there. The disease was smallpox, and the letters mark the first securely documented major smallpox epidemic in the Americas. By then, the Taíno population of the island had already been reduced catastrophically by more than two decades of violence, enslavement, forced labor, and social disruption, a history examined in Chapter 38. Smallpox arrived in a society that was already gravely weakened, and it moved through communities in which many of the people who would ordinarily have nursed the sick, planted crops, and prepared food were themselves ill, enslaved, dead, or far from home.

Less than two years later, in 1520, smallpox appeared in the Valley of Mexico during the war between the Spanish-led coalition and the Mexica of Tenochtitlan. Nahua writers who later described the epidemic to the Franciscan friar Bernardino de Sahagún recalled bodies covered in sores, people too pained to move, and households in which no one was able to bring water or food to the sick. Their testimony, examined in this chapter's Primary Source Window, is among the most important Indigenous-authored descriptions of an epidemic from the sixteenth-century Americas. It is also a reminder of the kind of evidence this chapter relies on: accounts recorded some years or decades after the events, by people with their own purposes, translated and preserved through colonial institutions.

Students approaching this subject often arrive with a simple story in mind: Europeans carried germs to a continent whose people had no immunity, and the people died. That story contains an essential truth. Many Indigenous communities had no prior exposure to smallpox, measles, and other infections circulating in Afro-Eurasia, and mortality in some places was staggering. But the simple story also conceals a great deal. It treats the continent as a single disease environment, though epidemics reached different regions at different times and sometimes not for centuries. It suggests that biology alone explains outcomes, though historians have shown that hunger, labor regimes, warfare, enslavement, forced resettlement, and the loss of caregivers repeatedly turned survivable illnesses into catastrophes. And it can quietly relieve human beings of responsibility, since a catastrophe that appears natural and inevitable seems to have no authors.

This chapter therefore asks a more demanding set of questions. Why were the disease environments of the Americas and Afro-Eurasia different, and what does "immunity" actually mean? How did epidemics spread through colonial and Indigenous networks, and what turned an outbreak into a demographic catastrophe? Which diseases can historians identify with confidence, and which identifications are retrospective guesses from vague descriptions? How did repeated epidemics, fertility decline, and social disruption shape long-term population change, as distinct from the number of people who died in a single outbreak? How did Indigenous peoples care for the sick, reorganize their communities, and sustain political life amid mortality? And how do historians count the dead when pre-contact populations were never enumerated?

The chapter pays particular attention to Mexico and the Caribbean, where the documentary record is richest, because they supply the clearest evidence for how epidemics worked and how historians reason about them. It does not retell the conquest of Tenochtitlan, which belongs to Chapter 39, nor the Spanish expeditions and missions of the Southeast and Southwest, which belong to Chapters 41 through 43, nor the Great Lakes, fur trade, and English colonial histories of Chapters 45 through 48. Where those regions appear here, they appear as comparative evidence that epidemic chronology differed from place to place, which connects directly to the argument of Chapter 1 that contact and colonization were staggered, regionally specific processes rather than a single continental event.

The evidence is varied and uneven. It includes Spanish letters, administrative surveys, tribute records, and physicians' observations; Nahua, Mixtec, Maya, and other Indigenous annals, codices, and testimonies; archaeological settlement and cemetery evidence; the study of skeletal remains; tree-ring and other environmental records; and, increasingly, ancient pathogen DNA. Each kind of evidence answers some questions and leaves others open. Students should finish the chapter holding two propositions at once: introduced epidemics were genuinely catastrophic, and the claim that disease alone inevitably destroyed Indigenous peoples is historically inadequate.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Distinguish epidemic mortality from the broader process of demographic collapse, and explain why the two should not be treated as identical.
  2. Describe Indigenous health and disease before 1492, and explain why the Americas were not disease-free even though many acute "crowd" infections of Afro-Eurasia were absent.
  3. Explain acquired immunity, population exposure, and the concept of a virgin soil epidemic, including the limits of that concept as an explanation for Indigenous population loss.
  4. Trace the first securely documented major smallpox epidemics in the Caribbean (1518–1519) and central Mexico (1520), and contextualize them within Spanish colonial networks and the Spanish–Mexica war.
  5. Analyze how disease interacted with alliances, siege warfare, leadership, and logistics, and explain why smallpox did not by itself conquer Mexico.
  6. Evaluate the evidence for multiple introduced diseases, including cocoliztli, and explain the difference between a documented epidemic and a confident modern diagnosis.
  7. Assess how warfare, enslavement, forced labor, hunger, displacement, and the loss of caregivers multiplied the effects of infection.
  8. Explain why fertility decline, household disruption, migration, and repeated epidemics could sustain population loss after an initial outbreak.
  9. Compare regional epidemic chronologies across North America, and evaluate the debate over whether disease spread far ahead of direct European contact.
  10. Describe Indigenous healing, caregiving, political adaptation, and community reconstruction as historical responses rather than passive outcomes.
  11. Critique pre-contact population estimates, identifying the methods, assumptions, and uncertainties behind them.
  12. Identify the strengths and limits of documentary, archaeological, bioarchaeological, environmental, and ancient-DNA evidence for epidemic history.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why is "Europeans brought germs and Indigenous people died" an incomplete explanation of Indigenous population loss, even though it contains real truth?
  2. What does it mean for a population to lack prior exposure to a disease, and what does that concept not explain?
  3. How did human decisions about labor, warfare, resettlement, food, and tribute shape the effects of infectious disease?
  4. How can historians distinguish between a well-documented epidemic and a confident modern diagnosis based on a vague historical description?
  5. Why might the same pathogen cause very different mortality in different communities or in the same community at different times?
  6. Is it more accurate to speak of one continental epidemic wave or of many regional epidemics, and what evidence would help decide?
  7. How did Indigenous communities understand, treat, and respond to epidemic illness, and how did they rebuild political and social life afterward?
  8. Why do estimates of pre-contact Indigenous populations differ so dramatically, and what should a responsible historian do with such figures?
  9. What are the strengths and limits of written accounts, archaeology, skeletal evidence, and ancient DNA for reconstructing epidemics?

Historical Context

Chapter 38 examined how the voyages beginning in 1492 inaugurated a sustained Spanish presence in the Caribbean, where violence, enslavement, and forced labor under the encomienda and related systems devastated the Taíno and neighboring peoples. Chapter 39 examined the Spanish conquest of the Mexica and the Indigenous alliances, resistance, and reorganization that accompanied it. Chapter 40 begins from those histories and asks a different question: what role did infectious disease play, how did it interact with those human-made conditions, and how should that role be described without exaggerating or minimizing it?

Before 1492, the Western Hemisphere was home to a very large and very diverse population living in settings ranging from Arctic hunting camps to the dense urban and agricultural systems of Mesoamerica, the Mississippi Valley, and the Andes, as Volumes I through III have shown. Those societies were not isolated from one another, but for many thousands of years they were not regularly connected with the disease environments of Africa, Europe, and Asia. After 1492, Spanish ships, and later those of other European powers, created a sustained and expanding network of movement across the Atlantic. People, animals, plants, trade goods, and microbes traveled along it, and along Indigenous trade routes, colonial roads, mission systems, slave-trading circuits, and military campaigns beyond it.

Three features of this setting shape everything that follows. First, the Spanish colonial enterprise was not only a process of contact but a system that reorganized labor, residence, and food production. Where it did so intensively, as in the Caribbean and parts of New Spain, the conditions for epidemic catastrophe were created by colonial institutions as well as by pathogens. Second, contact was staggered: Chapter 1 showed that European presence reached different regions in the sixteenth, seventeenth, eighteenth, and even nineteenth and twentieth centuries. Epidemic history therefore has regional chronologies rather than one continental timetable. Third, the evidence is unusually uneven. Central Mexico, shaped by Spanish bureaucracy and by Nahua and other Indigenous literate traditions, produced extensive documentation; most of the rest of the continent did not, so historians must infer epidemic events from fragments, archaeological patterns, and later records.

A final element of context is historiographical. The twentieth century saw sharp disagreement about how many people lived in the Americas in 1491 and how rapidly they declined. Some scholars proposed very large pre-contact populations and extreme mortality; others argued for far smaller figures. More recent work has tended to move away from explanations that treat biology as sufficient and toward analyses that emphasize regional variation, repeated epidemics, colonial disruption, nutrition, displacement, and social conditions. This chapter follows that more complex approach while remaining clear that the mortality of many epidemics was genuinely devastating.

Section 40.1

Epidemics and Demographic Catastrophe: Beyond a Single-Cause Story

The central claim of this chapter is that introduced diseases caused catastrophic mortality in many Indigenous societies, but that demographic collapse was neither automatic, uniform, nor caused by pathogens acting alone. Epidemics unfolded at different times in different regions and became especially destructive where disease interacted with warfare, enslavement, forced labor, displacement, hunger, ecological disruption, repeated exposure, and the breakdown of systems of care. Indigenous peoples responded with their own medical knowledge, caregiving practices, migration, political adaptation, and community reconstruction.

Two analytical distinctions organize the argument. The first separates epidemic mortality, the deaths caused by a particular outbreak, from demographic collapse, the longer decline of a population's size. A population can fall because people die, but also because fewer children are born, because survivors migrate or are removed, because captives are taken elsewhere, because marriages and households are disrupted, and because the adults who produce food and care for dependents are lost. An epidemic may therefore be a catalyst of collapse without being the whole mechanism. The second distinction separates biological susceptibility from social vulnerability. A population without prior exposure to a pathogen is biologically susceptible, but whether an infection becomes a regional catastrophe depends enormously on whether people can feed and nurse the sick, whether communities remain intact, and whether the political and economic order imposes additional burdens.

This approach should not be mistaken for minimizing the catastrophe. Smallpox, measles, and other introduced infections killed very large numbers of people, and in some localities mortality may have approached or exceeded the majority of the population. Nor does an emphasis on colonial conditions make disease unimportant. The point is that disease and colonialism were not separate explanations competing for credit. They were intertwined, and a historian who removes either one misdescribes the other. Recent scholarship has moved decisively away from treating Indigenous depopulation as the biologically determined result of "virgin soil," and toward a picture emphasizing regional variability, repeated epidemics, and the social conditions under which infection occurred.

There is also an ethical and interpretive dimension. Narratives that present depopulation as natural and inevitable can obscure the human decisions, including enslavement, forced labor, the taking of land and food, and the continued collection of tribute from shrinking communities, that shaped mortality. At the same time, narratives that attribute all death to deliberate policy can obscure the real and unintended work of pathogens. Historians generally agree that the best explanations distinguish carefully among these elements. The question of whether particular colonial actions or systems should be classified as genocide is a separate and contested legal and historical question that depends on definitions of intent and on specific evidence; this chapter focuses on explaining mechanisms and does not settle that question by rhetorical shortcut.

Section 40.2

Indigenous Health Before 1492

A common misconception holds that the pre-contact Americas were either a disease-free paradise or a place of constant sickness. The evidence supports neither image. Indigenous peoples experienced infection, injury, parasites, nutritional stress, dental disease, and chronic conditions, as did people everywhere. What they apparently lacked was a particular set of acute infectious diseases, including smallpox, measles, and several others, that circulated in Africa, Europe, and Asia.

The principal evidence comes from bioarchaeology, the study of human skeletal and mummified remains in their archaeological contexts. Skeletons record some conditions: growth disruptions in childhood suggesting nutritional or disease stress, porous lesions associated with anemia, bone changes associated with infection, and chronic infectious diseases such as tuberculosis and treponemal infections. Coprolites and other preserved materials reveal intestinal parasites. Large comparative studies of skeletons from across the Western Hemisphere show considerable variation in health among regions and periods rather than a single pattern.

That variation matters. In parts of eastern North America, for example, studies of burial populations have suggested that the intensification of maize agriculture and the growth of larger, denser settlements were sometimes accompanied by increased signs of physiological stress in some communities, though interpretations differ and the pattern was not uniform. Large settlements such as Cahokia, examined in Chapter 22, and the great cities of Mesoamerica, examined in Chapter 14, demonstrate that dense populations existed in the Americas long before 1492. Density alone, then, does not explain why particular infections did not circulate there, a point developed in Section 40.3.

Some diseases may have had origins or histories that are still debated. The origin of treponemal diseases, including syphilis, is a long-running controversy in which scholars disagree about whether specific forms were present in both hemispheres before 1492, were carried in one direction or the other, or evolved from related ancestral infections. Ancient DNA has also revealed tuberculosis-causing bacteria in pre-contact human remains in South America, a reminder that significant infectious diseases existed in the Americas before European arrival. These findings caution against reducing Indigenous health history to the single story of post-1492 epidemics.

For the study of epidemics, the key point is limited but important. Before 1492, Indigenous peoples had endured and understood illness over many thousands of years and had medical knowledge, practitioners, and caregiving practices suited to their own disease environments, themes developed in Section 40.17. What changed after 1492 was the sudden and repeated introduction of pathogens to which most people had not previously been exposed, into societies simultaneously being reshaped by colonial violence and economic demands.

Section 40.3

Why the Old World and the Americas Had Different Disease Environments

To understand why introduced diseases struck as they did, historians need to explain why smallpox, measles, and similar infections were present in Afro-Eurasia but apparently absent in the Americas. The explanation lies in long histories of connectivity, population movement, and pathogen circulation, not in the biological superiority of any people.

Many acute infectious diseases, including smallpox and measles, spread directly from person to person, produce immunity in survivors, and do not persist in the environment or in an animal reservoir. Such "crowd diseases" can only continue to circulate where there are enough susceptible people, particularly children, entering the population through birth, and enough contact among communities to carry the infection from one to another. Across Africa, Europe, and Asia, large and interconnected populations, cities, markets, armies, pilgrimages, and long-distance trade over many centuries created conditions in which several such diseases became established as recurrent, often childhood, infections.

A popular shorthand explains this by pointing to domesticated animals: Europeans lived alongside livestock, so they acquired many germs. There is a kernel of truth, since many human infectious diseases have zoonotic ancestors, and domestication increased human exposure to certain animal-borne pathogens. But the shorthand is too crude. Not all major human diseases arose from livestock, smallpox among them is generally understood to be related to viruses of other origins, and the transformation of a zoonotic infection into a human-adapted epidemic disease involved long and complex evolutionary processes. Historians and scientists also note that the Americas had domesticated animals, such as dogs, turkeys, and, in the Andes, camelids and guinea pigs, though fewer large herd animals than Eurasia.

Equally important is the history of human movement. Scholars have proposed that the ancestors of Indigenous Americans, who migrated from northeastern Asia as discussed in Chapters 2 and 3, may have left behind some pathogens during a long journey through cold, sparsely populated environments, and that subsequent isolation from Afro-Eurasian disease systems helped keep certain infections out. This "filter" idea is plausible but remains a hypothesis, and it should not be treated as established detail. What is secure is the broad outcome: before 1492, the Western Hemisphere was not regularly integrated into the continuous disease circulation that connected Africa, Europe, and Asia.

The result was a difference in historical disease environments, not in the worth, adaptability, or biological quality of peoples. Indigenous American cities, trade networks, and agricultural systems were large and complex. Had they been connected to the same pathogen pools, their histories of disease might have resembled those of Afro-Eurasia. It was the sudden connection of two previously separate systems, and the circumstances under which that connection occurred, that produced the epidemic catastrophes of the sixteenth century. Europeans, too, suffered from diseases in the Americas, and some tropical infections and parasites afflicted newcomers heavily, though the colonial system generally placed the burdens of mortality on Indigenous and, increasingly, enslaved African populations.

Section 40.4

Immunity, Exposure, and the Problem of "Virgin Soil"

Immunity is often discussed as though it were a simple property that a person or population either has or lacks. In reality, it is a set of biological processes. After infection with many viruses, the immune system retains memory that can allow a faster and more effective response to later exposure. This is acquired immunity, and it is why diseases such as measles and smallpox could settle into patterns of recurring childhood illness in large populations: most adults had survived the infection earlier in life and were protected, and severe epidemics involved mainly newly born, unexposed children.

In a population never previously exposed to a pathogen, no one has acquired immunity, and the infection can affect people of all ages at once. This has several consequences. Many people may fall ill simultaneously, overwhelming the capacity of healthy individuals to care for them. Adults, who are the primary producers of food and caregivers, may be affected alongside children. And, depending on the disease, adults and the very young may face higher risks of severe illness. The lack of previous exposure thus helps explain why some epidemics reached enormous proportions.

Historical Vocabulary
Virgin Soil Epidemic

Definition. A virgin soil epidemic is an epidemic occurring in a population that has had no previous exposure to the causative pathogen, so that few or no people have acquired immunity from earlier infection. The phrase became widely used in historical writing in the second half of the twentieth century.

Historical context. Historians applied the concept to the Americas to describe the impact of smallpox, measles, and other introduced infections. It helps explain why the first epidemics could spread so widely and strike adults as well as children.

Why it matters. The concept describes a real condition, but it is incomplete as an explanation of why Indigenous populations declined so profoundly and for so long. Historians now emphasize that population exposure is only one variable. Outcomes also depended on whether people could feed and care for the sick, whether communities were displaced, enslaved, at war, or overworked, how often epidemics recurred, and what other pathogens were present.

Potential misunderstanding. "Virgin soil" does not mean that Indigenous people had defective or weaker immune systems, and it does not mean that Europeans were biologically immune to everything they carried. Europeans died of smallpox and other diseases too. Present-day immunogenetic research emphasizes complexity and cautions against simple genetic explanations of historical epidemic outcomes. The word "virgin" can also imply an empty or untouched land, which conflicts with the evidence for long-inhabited and densely settled Indigenous societies. Many scholars now prefer more precise phrases such as "populations without prior exposure."

Historians have also debated whether Indigenous Americans had specific genetic characteristics that increased vulnerability to particular infections. Some researchers have investigated the diversity of immune-system genes, noting that populations that passed through narrow demographic bottlenecks might have reduced diversity in some immune-related genes. The research is genuinely interesting but complex, and its historical implications remain uncertain. Genetic factors, if they mattered, would have interacted with the social conditions described in the next section. Responsible interpretation does not leap from a gene-frequency finding to a claim that biology explains the outcome of colonization.

Finally, it is worth emphasizing a basic point of logic. If lack of prior exposure were a sufficient explanation, all unexposed populations would have experienced the same devastation under all circumstances. They did not. The historical record shows significant variation in the severity and timing of epidemics, and it shows survivors rebuilding. This variation is precisely what the social conditions discussed in the rest of the chapter are needed to explain.

Section 40.5

How Epidemics Become Catastrophic

Disease ecology is the study of how pathogens, hosts, and environments interact. For historians of epidemics, it offers a set of questions that explain why identical infections can have very different consequences. How infectious is the disease, and how is it transmitted? How long does an infected person remain contagious, and how soon do symptoms appear? What proportion of the infected die, a figure called the case fatality rate? How many people are susceptible, and how closely are they connected to one another? How are the sick cared for?

Smallpox, caused by the variola virus, spreads chiefly through respiratory contact and also through contaminated materials. After infection there is a delay of roughly one to two weeks before symptoms appear, during which an infected person may travel unknowingly. A person is generally most infectious after the characteristic rash begins to develop. Historical case fatality in Europe is commonly estimated at around one in three for severe forms of the disease, though it varied. Because the incubation period is long enough for travelers to cross an ocean while carrying the infection, but not so long that the infection dies out in transit, large colonial ships carrying a number of people could introduce smallpox if an infected person was aboard and later susceptible hosts were present. Measles is even more contagious, with a shorter incubation period and a different mortality pattern.

Mobility and density shaped spread. Epidemics follow human routes: ports, markets, roads, rivers, armies, labor drafts, mission settlements, and refugee movements. A colonial system that gathered people in mining camps, labor gangs, and relocated towns brought susceptible individuals into close contact, while the flight of frightened communities could carry infection to places that had not yet experienced it.

Care mattered at least as much as the pathogen's biology. Many fatal outcomes in epidemics result not directly from the infection but from dehydration, hunger, untreated secondary infections, or exposure in people too ill to feed themselves. Where families and communities could keep the sick hydrated, fed, warm, and comfortable, mortality was lower. Where entire households fell ill together, there was no one to provide this support, and death from neglect could multiply deaths from the disease. Nahua accounts of 1520 emphasize exactly this: sickness followed by hunger because no one remained to prepare food.

Nutrition and prior health shaped outcomes as well. People weakened by famine, by forced labor, or by displacement from farmland were more likely to die when infected. Drought and food shortage could therefore transform a manageable epidemic into a mass mortality crisis, a pattern central to the cocoliztli epidemics discussed in Section 40.11. This is why historians sometimes use the term syndemic, meaning the interaction of multiple health and social conditions that worsen one another, to describe sixteenth-century colonial settings. The same pathogen could have different results depending on whether a community was well fed, intact, and free to move, or hungry, overworked, and forcibly resettled.

Section 40.6

The Caribbean Before the First Secure Smallpox Epidemic

Spanish sources from the first decades after 1492 report sickness and death among both Spaniards and Indigenous people in the Caribbean. Settlers at early colonies such as La Isabela on Hispaniola suffered from illness, hunger, and the difficulties of an unfamiliar environment. Spanish writers also described declining Indigenous communities, abandoned villages, and the spread of mortality. It is tempting to assume that these reports record the first European epidemics in the Americas and to assign them diagnoses, most commonly influenza or smallpox.

Historians approach this temptation with caution. Early accounts rarely describe symptoms in sufficient detail for a modern diagnosis, and they use general terms such as pestilence, sickness, or plague. Some historians have proposed that an influenza-like epidemic occurred on Hispaniola in 1493, but the evidence is thin, and no consensus supports a confident identification. Other contemporary evidence points to hunger, overwork, and violence as major causes of death. Spanish exploitation of Taíno labor and agriculture, disruption of food production, forced movement, and warfare drove mortality sharply in these years, as Chapter 38 showed.

Historical Vocabulary
Retrospective Diagnosis

Definition. Retrospective diagnosis is the attempt to identify, using modern medical categories, the disease described in a historical source.

Historical context. Sixteenth-century writers worked within humoral and religious frameworks and did not know about bacteria or viruses. They often grouped illnesses by visible signs, by season, or by their effects on communities, and used broad terms. A word such as "pestilence" could cover many diseases.

Why it matters. Some descriptions, such as the pustular eruption of smallpox, are distinctive enough to support strong identification. Many others are not. Reading a modern diagnosis into a vague account can create false certainty about which diseases were present when.

Potential misunderstanding. The fact that a historical description fits a modern disease does not mean that it was that disease. Different illnesses can produce similar symptoms, and one epidemic may involve several pathogens at once. Scientific methods such as ancient DNA can sometimes test a hypothesis, but they identify pathogens in specific individuals, not necessarily the cause of an entire historical epidemic.

The early Caribbean thus teaches two lessons. First, mortality before 1518 was already severe and cannot be attributed to a single identified infectious cause. Second, the absence of a securely documented smallpox epidemic in the first quarter-century does not mean the Taíno were unaffected by sickness. It means historians should be explicit about the limits of what the sources show. Doing so prevents the later, well-documented epidemics from being retroactively used to explain an earlier decline that human actions had largely already produced.

Section 40.7

Smallpox in Hispaniola and the Caribbean, 1518–1519

The first major smallpox epidemic in the Americas that the documentary record securely establishes began in Hispaniola in late 1518. Spanish officials and clergy on the island reported the illness to the Crown in the winter of 1518–1519, describing it as viruelas, the Spanish term for smallpox, and noted heavy mortality among the Indigenous people. The identification of the disease rests on contemporary descriptions that are distinctive enough, and on the pattern of its spread, to command wide agreement among scholars.

How the virus reached the island is not documented in detail. The most plausible explanation is that it was carried on a ship from Spain or from another point in the Atlantic network, which by then linked Iberia, the Atlantic islands, West Africa, and the Caribbean. The expanding transatlantic slave trade, which brought enslaved Africans to the Caribbean in increasing numbers, formed part of that network, and smallpox circulated in Africa as well as Europe. Once in Hispaniola, the infection spread along the routes of colonial settlement and labor and then to neighboring islands, including Puerto Rico and Cuba, in 1519.

The epidemic struck a population that was already reduced. By the late 1510s, Taíno communities had endured the Spanish conquest of the island, the encomienda system of forced labor and tribute, enslavement, and the disruption of agriculture, and many Indigenous people had been moved to mines and work sites away from home. Population estimates for the Taíno before 1492 vary enormously, from figures in the tens or hundreds of thousands to millions, because no census exists and the main sources, such as the Dominican friar Bartolomé de las Casas, wrote with strong rhetorical and political aims. What is clear is that by 1518 the surviving population was far smaller than it had been in 1492, and the smallpox epidemic added to a collapse that was already well advanced.

Spanish writers claimed very heavy losses, and some later accounts suggested that a large fraction of the remaining Indigenous population died. Students should treat those figures cautiously. The documentary evidence is stronger about the catastrophic character of the epidemic than about numbers. The same period also saw Spanish slave raids that removed Indigenous people from other islands, including the Bahamas, whose Lucayan population was largely eliminated through enslavement and removal. These examples show that human action and infection operated at the same time, and that "disease" alone cannot explain the decline of Caribbean Indigenous populations.

Nor did the Taíno disappear. Indigenous people survived on the islands, intermarried, and contributed to Caribbean cultures, and Taíno heritage remains an important subject of contemporary identification and research. The Caribbean epidemic is best understood as a disaster within a longer process of colonial transformation, and as the opening of a pattern that would recur: contagion moving through the arteries of Spanish colonization into new regions.

Section 40.8

Smallpox Reaches Central Mexico, 1520

Chapter 39 explained the military and political history of the Spanish–Mexica war. This section explains the epidemic that occurred within it. In the spring of 1520, a Spanish expedition under Pánfilo de Narváez, sent from Cuba to confront Hernán Cortés, landed on the Gulf Coast of Mexico. Spanish sources report that smallpox was present among members of that expedition, and that it began to spread among Indigenous communities, including the coastal Totonac town of Cempoala. Through the networks of travel, trade, tribute, and alliance that connected the coast to the highlands, the infection moved inland.

By the autumn of 1520, smallpox was ravaging the Valley of Mexico. Nahua accounts, preserved in the Florentine Codex and other sources, describe an illness that produced sores or pustules across the body, caused such pain that sick people could not move, and left survivors with permanent scars and, in some cases, blindness. They describe the epidemic's social effects: whole households sickened together, the dead went unburied, and hunger followed because food could not be prepared. Spanish writers, including the Franciscan Toribio de Benavente, known as Motolinía, later counted smallpox first among the great afflictions of New Spain.

The epidemic overlapped with the conflict that followed the Spaniards' expulsion from Tenochtitlan in mid-1520. Cuitláhuac, who had become ruler of Tenochtitlan, died during the epidemic late in 1520 and was succeeded by Cuauhtémoc. Beyond particular individuals, the epidemic removed many leaders, warriors, priests, specialists, and heads of households from the communities of the valley, and it affected the Mexica and their enemies and allies alike. Indigenous allies of the Spaniards, such as the Tlaxcalans, also suffered illness and loss.

Spanish expeditions and settlers spread infection further, to Guatemala and beyond. The Kaqchikel Maya annals, written by Indigenous authors in the sixteenth century, record a devastating pestilence in the early 1520s that scholars frequently, though not unanimously, associate with smallpox. Smallpox also appears to have moved into the Andes before the arrival of Spanish forces there. These later movements fall outside this chapter's primary geographic focus, but they illustrate how a single introduced disease could traverse regions through Indigenous as well as colonial networks.

Section 40.9

Disease Changed the War, but Disease Did Not Conquer Mexico

The popular claim that smallpox conquered the Aztecs, or that Cortés won because of germs, is among the most persistent oversimplifications in the history of the Americas. Smallpox affected the war, but the evidence does not support the idea that it determined the outcome by itself.

What the epidemic did was significant. It killed and incapacitated leaders and fighters, disrupted households, interrupted food supplies, and added to the strains on the cities of the valley during a critical period. It weakened the Mexica at the moment when they were reorganizing after the expulsion of the Spaniards. The death of Cuitláhuac shortened a reign and forced a transfer of leadership in wartime. These effects are well supported in the sources.

Yet the war's course depended on much more. As Chapter 39 explained, the Spanish-led campaign depended on the participation of thousands of Indigenous allies, including Tlaxcalans, Texcocans, and others who had their own reasons to oppose Mexica domination. The siege of Tenochtitlan in 1521 involved naval and land operations on the lake system, the cutting of causeways and water supplies, fierce urban fighting, and the interplay of Spanish decisions and Indigenous political fractures. The Mexica resisted vigorously and for months. Smallpox also afflicted the Spaniards' allies and affected the same communities that supplied the siege.

Overstating the role of disease has costs. It diminishes the agency of Indigenous allies and opponents, who made strategic decisions and fought hard. It treats Spanish success as the result of a biological accident, and so ignores the Spanish use of alliances, resources, and violence. And it can imply that the conquest was effortless. A more accurate formulation is that smallpox was a serious additional source of loss and disruption within a war whose outcome rested on political, military, and logistical circumstances.

It also matters what came after. The consequences of the epidemic did not end in 1521. They continued in the years of Spanish rule, as repeated epidemics, labor demands, and colonial resettlement shaped the demography of central Mexico, themes taken up in the sections that follow.

Section 40.10

Beyond Smallpox: Multiple Introduced Diseases

Smallpox is the best-known introduced disease, but it was neither the only one nor necessarily the most destructive over the long term. Historians identify several additional infections as important in sixteenth- and seventeenth-century epidemics, including measles, influenza, typhus, and others. The identification of each requires attention to the evidence.

Measles is reported in the early 1530s in Spanish and Indigenous sources from the Caribbean and New Spain. It is highly contagious, and in populations without prior exposure it can strike adults and children together. Its complications, including pneumonia and dehydration, are especially dangerous when people cannot be cared for. Influenza, which spreads readily and mutates, is a plausible participant in several epidemics, though its identification in early sources is often uncertain. Typhus, spread by body lice and associated with crowding, poor sanitation, and displacement, can accompany war, forced labor, and congregated populations. Other infections, including mumps and diphtheria, may also have circulated, and the introduction of malaria and yellow fever into the tropical Americas, through the transatlantic trade, transformed disease environments in lowland regions, particularly where enslaved Africans and European settlers arrived in numbers.

Sources of the period often did not specify diseases. Spanish chroniclers and Indigenous annalists wrote about plague, pestilence, fevers, or "sickness" that struck whole regions. Some writers named specific diseases when symptoms were distinctive. Others recorded several outbreaks as a series of "plagues," as Motolinía did when he enumerated ten afflictions of New Spain, which included smallpox, warfare, famine, and forced labor and taxation alongside it. This enumeration is itself revealing: a contemporary observer did not separate disease from the human-made burdens of colonial society.

For historians, the practical lesson is to match confidence to evidence. Smallpox in 1518–1520 can be identified with high confidence. Measles in the early 1530s is widely accepted. For many other epidemics, historians describe an outbreak of an unidentified or uncertain disease and consider several candidate pathogens. This restraint is not hesitation for its own sake. It acknowledges that sixteenth-century disease categories do not map neatly onto modern ones, and that multiple infections could co-occur, producing complex clinical pictures.

Section 40.11

Cocoliztli: The Great Mexican Epidemics of 1545 and 1576

Two of the most severe epidemics in the history of colonial Mexico occurred in the mid-sixteenth century. The first began in 1545 and continued into 1548 or later in some regions, and the second struck in 1576 and continued for several years. Nahuatl sources called these epidemics cocoliztli, a word meaning pestilence or sickness, and the term has come to designate these events in the scholarly literature.

Historical Vocabulary
Cocoliztli

Definition. A Nahuatl word meaning sickness or pestilence, used in sixteenth-century Nahua sources and now by historians for the great epidemics of 1545–1548 and 1576–1581 in New Spain.

Historical context. Spanish and Indigenous writers recorded the epidemics as catastrophic events. Contemporaries described high fever, bleeding, and other symptoms, and described heavy mortality, particularly among Indigenous people.

Why it matters. Cocoliztli is a major case study in epidemic history because it combines extraordinary mortality, strong documentation, environmental stress, and unresolved scientific debate about the cause.

Potential misunderstanding. The word names an epidemic event in the sources; it does not by itself identify a single disease. Whether one pathogen or several caused cocoliztli remains debated.

Contemporary descriptions emphasize high fevers, bleeding from the nose or other sites, jaundice, and rapid death, with symptoms reported to differ from those of smallpox. In 1576, the royal physician Francisco Hernández, who was in New Spain to study its natural history and medicine, observed the epidemic and recorded his observations; he is reported to have conducted autopsies. These descriptions have inspired decades of retrospective diagnosis, with candidates including typhus, typhoid or other enteric fevers, a viral hemorrhagic fever, and others. No identification has won unanimous acceptance.

The epidemics occurred during severe environmental stress. Tree-ring evidence from Mexico indicates a prolonged period of drought across the mid-sixteenth century, which may have reduced harvests, caused food shortages, and increased the vulnerability of populations. The drought did not by itself cause the epidemics, but it plausibly worsened their effects. In addition, Indigenous communities were by then subject to heavy Spanish tribute demands, labor obligations, and, in some regions, resettlement. Mortality estimates for the epidemics are very uncertain and vary widely, though scholars agree that millions of people may have died across New Spain in the two great episodes combined, with Indigenous populations suffering most.

Science and History

Ancient DNA and the Epidemic of 1545

Recent advances in ancient-DNA analysis allow researchers to search for the genetic traces of pathogens in the teeth and bones of people who died centuries ago. At Teposcolula-Yucundaa, a Mixtec town in the Mixteca Alta of Oaxaca, archaeologists excavated a cemetery associated with the epidemic of 1545. Analysis of skeletal remains from this site identified DNA from Salmonella enterica, a bacterium that includes the lineage that causes enteric fever, in some of the individuals tested.

What the finding shows: It demonstrates that at least some people buried at this site were infected with this bacterium. It provides a scientifically testable candidate for one pathogen in the epidemic, and it illustrates how ancient DNA can bring new evidence to a long-standing historical question.

What it does not show: It does not prove that a single Salmonella infection explains the entire cocoliztli epidemic across New Spain. Individuals sampled represent a small number from one site. Other pathogens may have been present, and ancient-DNA methods detect only what they are designed to find and what has survived. Nor does the finding on its own establish how the bacterium arrived. The historical context of drought, displacement, and tribute remains essential for explaining why the epidemic was so lethal.

  1. Why is detecting a pathogen in some individuals different from identifying the cause of an entire epidemic?
  2. How might historical documents and ancient-DNA evidence complement one another?
  3. What other kinds of evidence would strengthen or challenge the Salmonella hypothesis?
Section 40.12

War, Forced Labor, Enslavement, and Hunger as Disease Multipliers

The most important correction that recent scholarship has made to older, disease-only narratives is the demonstration that infection and colonialism worked together. People forced from their farms, crowded into labor regimes, deprived of food, compelled into mines or missions, or separated from caregivers were more vulnerable to illness, and sick people in such settings were less likely to recover.

Warfare was one multiplier. Campaigns disrupted planting and harvesting, destroyed food stores, and displaced civilians. Sieges, such as that of Tenochtitlan, produced hunger and crowding. Armies moved infection from place to place. Enslavement was another. Spanish slave raids in the Caribbean and, later, on the mainland removed people from communities, broke up families, and transported captives in crowded conditions that favored disease. Forced labor under the encomienda and related systems exacted tribute in goods and labor from Indigenous communities, and in some regions, including mining zones, drew workers away from their fields and families. Hunger, whether produced by war, drought, or the diversion of labor, weakened bodies and reduced resistance.

Tribute is a revealing case. Indigenous communities owed goods and labor to encomenderos and the Crown, often calculated according to earlier counts of population. When epidemics killed large numbers of people, the remaining survivors frequently still owed the same assessments, which fell more heavily on each person. Reassessments were made, often after petitions, but they typically lagged behind losses. Indigenous officials, friars, and some Spanish administrators documented these burdens. In this way, a demographic shock became an economic and social crisis that further reduced the capacity of communities to feed and care for themselves.

Resettlement policies could magnify risks as well. In central Mexico, Spanish authorities carried out programs of congregación in the second half of the sixteenth century, concentrating scattered populations into new, planned towns. The programs were intended to facilitate administration, taxation, and religious instruction. Where they gathered people more densely, they could enhance transmission, and they separated communities from their fields and lands. Indigenous communities often resisted, negotiated, or adapted them.

Cause and Effect

Why Epidemics Became Demographic Catastrophe

Long-term causes

Separation of the Americas from Afro-Eurasian disease circulation, so that many people lacked acquired immunity to introduced infections; the creation of a sustained Atlantic network after 1492; and Spanish colonial systems of labor, tribute, and resettlement.

Short-term causes

The arrival of infected people by ship, the movement of armies and slave traders, drought and food shortage, and the pressure of war, labor drafts, and tribute obligations on already stressed communities.

Immediate trigger

The introduction of a specific pathogen, such as smallpox in 1518 and 1520, into a community with many unexposed people, where many fell ill at the same time.

Event or transformation

Simultaneous illness overwhelmed caregiving, interrupted food production, and left sick people to die of dehydration, hunger, and secondary infection.

Immediate effects

High mortality among adults and children, the loss of leaders, elders, and specialists, disrupted households, and hunger after the epidemic.

Long-term consequences

Reduced fertility and household formation, repeated epidemics, continuing tribute burdens, resettlement, and prolonged demographic decline, followed in many places by reconstruction and survival.

Historical complexity: The sequence "pathogen, then death" omits the conditions that determined how many fell ill at once, whether they were cared for, and whether survivors could recover. Different colonial regimes, environments, and community responses produced different outcomes. No single factor, whether biology, drought, or Spanish policy, is sufficient on its own.

Section 40.13

Mortality Was Only Part of Demographic Collapse

When historians speak of demographic collapse, they mean a sustained decline in population, which is not the same as the number of deaths in an epidemic. A population changes through births, deaths, and migration. Depopulation can therefore occur through several mechanisms, and epidemics may affect all of them.

Fertility is one. Severe illness, hunger, and disruption reduce the number of births. Women who are ill, malnourished, or widowed may be less likely to conceive or carry pregnancies to term, and infants and young children are especially vulnerable to the diseases and food shortages that accompany epidemics. Marriage and household formation can fall when many adults die or when families scatter. Loss of the older generation removes people who ordinarily care for children, pass on knowledge, and manage household resources. A cohort of children born into a hungry, bereaved community may be smaller and may face greater risks, producing effects that last for a generation or more.

Migration and captivity matter as well. People fled epidemics, resettled under colonial policy, were taken as captives, or left for labor in distant places. Some moved to other Indigenous communities, to Spanish towns, or to areas beyond colonial control. Counts of "disappearance" in a particular town may therefore reflect movement as much as death, a point that historians of colonial censuses emphasize. In other cases, forced removal and enslavement did reduce the number of people living in their home communities, regardless of mortality from illness.

Economic and social disruption affects survival indirectly. When epidemics killed farmers, fields went untended and harvests failed. When labor drafts removed men, women and elders carried heavier burdens. When community leaders and ritual specialists died, the organization of food storage, water management, and mutual assistance suffered. Each of these effects could reduce a community's ability to withstand later shocks.

For these reasons, the phrase "population decline" cannot simply be equated with people dying from infection. The distinction also helps explain why some populations recovered while others did not: where communities retained land, leadership, and kin networks, and where colonial demands were less intense, recovery was more likely. It also helps explain why some Indigenous populations in Mexico began to grow again in the seventeenth century, though they did so from a vastly reduced base.

Section 40.14

Repeated Epidemics and Cumulative Vulnerability

A single epidemic was rarely the whole story. Central Mexico experienced smallpox in 1520, measles in the early 1530s, the first great cocoliztli epidemic in the mid-1540s, and the second in the late 1570s, along with other outbreaks. Each of these episodes struck communities that were still rebuilding households, leadership, and food systems after the last.

Repeated epidemics produced cumulative vulnerability. An epidemic of one disease might leave survivors with acquired immunity to that pathogen, but a new pathogen could then arrive. Children born after one outbreak might lack immunity to the next. Survivors of one epidemic might be weakened by hunger or illness and thus more vulnerable to the next. Recurrent crises prevented the accumulation of the household and community resources, such as stored food, trained specialists, and a balanced age structure, that make recovery possible.

This pattern helps explain why the demographic decline of central Mexico continued for decades. Estimates of the pre-contact population of the region vary enormously, from several million to more than twenty million, as Section 40.19 explains, but most researchers agree that it fell to roughly one to two million by the early seventeenth century, a decline of at least several-fold and possibly far more. Recovery began afterward, though uneven and affected by later epidemics.

Section 40.15

Did Epidemics Race Ahead of Europeans?

A major question in the history of North America concerns the geography of epidemics. In regions far from early Spanish centers, did introduced diseases travel ahead of Europeans through Indigenous trade and diplomatic networks, so that the first Europeans to arrive encountered populations already devastated? Or did epidemics generally follow sustained contact? The answer shapes how historians interpret both the first European accounts of Indigenous societies and the scale of continental population decline.

Historical Debate

Did Disease Spread Far Ahead of Direct European Contact?

The question: Before the establishment of sustained European settlement in many parts of North America, did epidemics move across the continent along Indigenous networks, reducing populations dramatically before Europeans arrived to record them?

Interpretation A — Epidemics often preceded Europeans: Some scholars, influential from the 1960s and 1980s onward, argued that smallpox and other diseases spread widely from early Spanish centers, producing very large population losses in the interior before direct contact. On this view, the first European descriptions of Indigenous societies in some regions record communities already shattered, and early population levels must therefore have been much higher than the numbers implied by later sources. Proponents pointed to expeditions, such as Spanish incursions into the Southeast in the sixteenth century, as possible sources of infection.
Interpretation B — Regional epidemics tied to sustained contact: Other scholars, drawing on archaeological, documentary, and bioarchaeological research, argue that such a continent-wide model is poorly supported. They contend that clear evidence of major epidemics tends to be associated with sustained contact, missions, slave trading, or trade, and that population losses in many regions occurred later than the earlier model proposed. Some research on regions such as the Southeast and California does not find evidence of enormous sixteenth-century collapse before sustained colonial presence. Researchers favoring this view emphasize multiple regional epidemics rather than one continental wave.

Evidence: The debate involves written accounts that are sparse for the sixteenth-century interior, archaeological studies of settlement size and abandonment, patterns of burials, and assumptions about how diseases spread. Both sides note that the same evidence can be read differently, since settlement change can result from many causes, including warfare, climate, and political reorganization. Evidence for specific pathogens in specific places remains limited.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns chronology (when and where epidemics occurred), causation (whether depopulation resulted from disease alone), scale (how large pre-contact populations were), and the interpretation of archaeological evidence.

Why it matters: The answer affects how historians estimate pre-contact populations, how they read early European descriptions, and how they understand the colonial role in producing epidemic conditions. If disease raced ahead of Europeans, then European arrival can seem like the aftermath of a natural catastrophe. If epidemics generally followed contact, then colonial networks, missions, and slave trading become more directly implicated.

Student task: Select a region mentioned in this chapter and identify what kinds of evidence would be needed to determine whether a major epidemic occurred there before sustained European contact. Explain what each kind of evidence could and could not show.

The current consensus, to the extent one exists, favors a more regionally differentiated picture. Some areas may have suffered disease before direct, sustained European presence, through Indigenous networks connected to Spanish settlements or through the effects of slave raiding. But across much of North America, major population losses appear to have followed sustained contact. The better model is multiple regional epidemics with distinct timing, not a single wave sweeping the continent.

Section 40.16

A Continent of Different Epidemic Chronologies

Because later chapters examine regions in detail, this section offers only a comparative survey whose purpose is to emphasize variation in timing. The central point is that epidemic history followed the geography of contact, trade, missions, and slaving, which differed across North America.

In the Caribbean and Mesoamerica, as the preceding sections show, the first major epidemics occurred in the late 1510s and 1520s, and recurring epidemics followed through the sixteenth century. In the Southeast, Spanish expeditions and missions of the sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries, discussed in Chapter 41, brought sustained contact in some areas, while major epidemics in the interior became more prominent later, in the context of the English colonial Indigenous slave trade of the late seventeenth century, a subject connected to Chapter 53. In the Southwest, Spanish colonization beginning in the late sixteenth century, examined in Chapters 42 and 43, was followed by epidemics in the Pueblo world in the seventeenth century, intertwined with mission labor, drought, and raiding.

In the Northeast and Great Lakes, early Atlantic contacts, fishing, and trade preceded sustained settlement. Evidence points to devastating epidemics in coastal New England in the late 1610s, the cause of which remains debated, and to serious epidemics among Huron-Wendat and neighboring peoples in the 1630s, in the context of French missions and the fur trade discussed in Chapters 45 and 46. These epidemics shaped the era of intense Haudenosaunee warfare examined in Chapter 47 and the English colonial encounters of Chapter 48.

Other regions experienced epidemics much later. In California, the Spanish mission system began in 1769, and research does not support a large sixteenth-century epidemic collapse before sustained colonial contact; severe demographic losses are associated with missions, and, later, the Gold Rush, as Chapter 70 examines. On the Northwest Coast, maritime contact began in the late eighteenth century, and major epidemics occurred in the decades that followed, including a devastating smallpox epidemic in 1862. On the Plains, a smallpox epidemic in 1837 devastated peoples of the upper Missouri River, including Mandan villages, in the era of the American fur trade. Some Arctic and Subarctic peoples experienced epidemics only in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries.

These examples confirm the argument of Chapter 1: contact and its consequences were staggered. They also show that epidemics were not confined to the sixteenth century. They recurred wherever new connections and colonial pressures opened regions to introduced pathogens, and they interacted with the particular economic and political relationships of each region.

Section 40.17

Indigenous Healing, Caregiving, and Adaptation

Indigenous peoples were not passive in the face of epidemics. They drew on long-standing systems of medical knowledge, caregiving, and community organization, and they adapted them to unprecedented circumstances.

Nahua medicine provides a well-documented example. Healers, called ticitl in Nahuatl, practiced herbal medicine, bone-setting, bathing, and ritual healing, and Nahua communities used sweat baths, known as temazcalli, in therapy. In 1552, Martín de la Cruz, an Indigenous physician, composed a manuscript on medicinal plants at the Colegio de Santa Cruz de Tlatelolco, which Juan Badiano translated into Latin; it records Nahua knowledge of treatments for numerous conditions. Spanish observers and physicians, including Francisco Hernández, collected information from Indigenous healers, and Spanish medicine in New Spain drew on Indigenous plant knowledge.

It would be a mistake to ridicule Indigenous healing because it did not operate through germ theory. Neither did European medicine of the sixteenth century, which rested on humoral theory and used remedies such as bloodletting and purging, sometimes harmful to patients. Neither tradition possessed effective treatments for smallpox. What both could offer was comfort, nursing, and practices that sometimes reduced suffering, and what mattered most was whether people were able to provide care at all.

Communities also responded socially and spatially. People fled from infected settlements, though flight could spread infection. They isolated households, relocated to other villages, or sought refuge in remote areas. Religious and ritual practices provided meaning and consolation: epidemics were interpreted through Indigenous understandings of cosmic order and obligation, and through the Christian teachings of missionaries, whose explanations sometimes competed with Indigenous ones. Spanish institutions also played a part. Hospitals were founded for Indigenous people in New Spain, including those established by Vasco de Quiroga, a Spanish official and later bishop, and by religious orders, and Indigenous people worked in them, though many of these institutions were overwhelmed during major epidemics. The relationship between epidemic experience and religious change is considered further in Chapter 54.

Everyday Life and Social History

Caring for the Sick in a Household Under Strain

For most people, an epidemic was experienced within a household and a neighborhood. In a typical Nahua or Mixtec household, several generations lived together or near one another, and caring for a sick relative fell mainly to family members, often women. Care included keeping a person warm, giving water and food, cleaning and bathing, and preparing remedies. When several household members fell ill at once, this labor could not be performed, and the sources emphasize that people died of thirst and hunger as well as illness.

Death also created burdens: preparing and burying the dead required labor and ritual attention, and the number of deaths could exceed the capacity of survivors to perform customary rites. Children left without parents were taken in by relatives, neighbors, or, in some cases, Spanish households or religious institutions. Surviving adults had to farm, pay tribute, and meet labor obligations with fewer hands. Historians reconstruct these experiences from Indigenous testimonies, Spanish accounts, wills, and community petitions, using them cautiously, since the written record usually reports elite and clerical perspectives more fully than the experiences of ordinary families.

Section 40.18

Epidemics, Political Change, and Community Reconstruction

The loss of leaders, elders, and specialists changed how communities were governed and how knowledge was transmitted. Rulers died during epidemics, as Cuitláhuac did, and succession could become contested or be redirected. Elders who held memory of ritual, genealogy, land boundaries, and agricultural practice were especially at risk in some outbreaks. Specialists in healing, craft, and ceremony, and their apprentices, might die together. Such losses affected alliances, ceremonial calendars, and the ability of communities to negotiate with colonial authorities.

But disease produced change, not disappearance. Communities incorporated survivors from other settlements, reorganized villages, rebuilt kin networks, and developed new political institutions. In central Mexico, the Indigenous altepetl, the local ethnic state and community, continued under Spanish rule, and municipal councils, called cabildos, brought together Indigenous elites and Spanish institutional forms. Nahua and Mixtec nobles negotiated with Spanish authorities over tribute, labor, land, and succession, using petitions, lawsuits, and alphabetic writing. Indigenous writers produced annals, land records, and testaments that preserved community memory.

Section 40.19

Counting the Dead: Population Estimates and the Demographic Debate

How many people lived in the Americas before 1492, and how many died? These questions have long been disputed, and the range of estimates is wide. For the entire Western Hemisphere, published estimates have ranged from under ten million to well over one hundred million. A widely cited estimate by the geographer William Denevan, published in the early 1990s, proposed about 54 million, but other scholars have argued for much lower or much higher numbers. For North America north of Mexico, proposed figures have ranged from roughly one million to eighteen million, with many recent estimates clustering in the low millions. For central Mexico, estimates for 1519 range from several million to more than twenty million.

The reasons for such differences are methodological. No census existed. Historians must reconstruct populations from indirect evidence, and each method has weaknesses.

  • Colonial counts and tribute records. Spanish surveys and tribute assessments, particularly in central Mexico, provide figures for tributaries, but they may undercount people who were exempt, hidden, or absent, and the relation between tributaries and total population must be assumed.
  • Church and parish records. Baptismal and burial registers become available only after sustained mission or parish organization, and often after major losses.
  • Archaeological settlement data. Counts of houses, sites, and settlement areas can be converted into population estimates using assumptions about household size and occupation, which are uncertain.
  • Backward projection. Some estimates project backward from later counts using assumed rates of decline, a method highly sensitive to the assumptions chosen.
  • Early European descriptions. Chroniclers' statements about numbers were often rhetorical, partisan, or based on limited observation.

Historians therefore treat such figures cautiously. A responsible chapter should not depend on one spectacular percentage, such as the assertion that ninety percent of all Indigenous people in the Americas died. Some places experienced losses on that scale or greater; others did not, and the timing varied enormously. What can be said with confidence is that many regions suffered severe population loss, that central Mexico and the Caribbean provide the best-documented cases of catastrophic decline, and that the details of magnitude remain debated. Using ranges and acknowledging disagreement is more accurate than presenting a single number as settled.

The counting of the dead is also an ethical question. Numbers can make catastrophe comprehensible, but they can also abstract it. Indigenous communities and scholars emphasize that behind the figures lie families, languages, local knowledge, and political communities, and that survivors and their descendants continue to bear and remember these losses.

Section 40.20

How Do We Know? Documents, Archaeology, Bioarchaeology, and Ancient DNA

The evidence for epidemics is rich in some respects and surprisingly limited in others. Written accounts are the strongest evidence that major epidemics occurred. Letters, chronicles, administrative surveys, tribute lists, court records, and Indigenous annals describe sickness and mortality in ways that make the catastrophic character of events like the smallpox epidemic of 1520 and the cocoliztli epidemics unmistakable. In New Spain, the Crown's questionnaires of the late sixteenth century, known as the Relaciones Geográficas, collected information from local communities, including accounts of population change and epidemics, and are a valuable source. Each kind of document reflects the perspective and purposes of its creator, and none provides systematic statistics for most regions.

Archaeology provides evidence of settlement change, abandonment, and burial practices, but identifying a particular epidemic archaeologically is difficult. Abandoned villages can result from many causes. Mass graves may indicate epidemic mortality, but they may also reflect warfare or other events, and the dating of a burial to a specific year or outbreak is often impossible. Recent reviews have noted that, despite strong documentary evidence for smallpox mortality in the Americas, archaeologists have not securely identified smallpox mass cemeteries, which shows how difficult it is to link specific burials to specific diseases.

Bioarchaeology examines skeletal remains for evidence of health, nutrition, and disease, and can reveal patterns of stress in populations over time. Many acute diseases, however, kill too quickly to leave marks on bones, so skeletons often underrepresent them. Environmental records, such as tree rings, provide information on drought and climate. Ancient-DNA analysis can recover pathogen DNA from teeth and bones and has transformed the study of past disease, as the Salmonella finding connected to the 1545 epidemic demonstrates. Its limits are those described earlier: it detects pathogens in specific individuals when preservation allows, and results must be interpreted in historical context. The field is developing rapidly, and new findings may revise current interpretations.

Conducting this research raises ethical questions. Studying ancestral remains involves the consent and authority of descendant communities, as Chapter 1 discussed and as the discussion of repatriation in Chapter 125 will examine. Many Indigenous nations participate in setting research priorities and in interpreting results, and collaborative research is increasingly important. Historians and scientists therefore combine methods: documents, archaeology, bioarchaeology, environmental evidence, genetics, and Indigenous testimony and memory. No single source is decisive, and the most reliable conclusions emerge when independent lines of evidence converge.

Section 40.21

Chapter Review and Historical Thinking Checkpoint

This section gathers the chapter's summary and review materials. Students should be able to explain both central propositions of the chapter: introduced epidemics were genuinely catastrophic, and the claim that disease alone inevitably destroyed Indigenous peoples is historically inadequate.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that introduced diseases caused catastrophic mortality in many Indigenous societies, but that demographic collapse was neither automatic, uniform, nor caused by pathogens acting alone. It began by distinguishing epidemic mortality from demographic collapse and biological susceptibility from social vulnerability, distinctions that allow historians to explain why the same pathogen could have different results in different circumstances.

The chapter then corrected the misconception that the pre-contact Americas were disease-free. Indigenous peoples experienced infection, parasites, nutritional stress, and chronic disease, and their societies possessed medical knowledge and caregiving practices. What many apparently lacked was exposure to smallpox, measles, and other acute infections circulating in Africa, Europe, and Asia, a difference rooted in the histories of connectivity and pathogen circulation, not in the biological quality of any people.

Acquired immunity and the concept of a virgin soil epidemic helped explain why the first epidemics could strike adults and children together and overwhelm communities. But the concept is incomplete: it does not mean Indigenous people had defective immune systems, it cannot explain variation in outcomes, and it leaves out the social conditions that determined whether infection became catastrophe. Disease ecology, including transmission, case fatality, mobility, nutrition, and care, supplied a fuller framework.

The first securely documented major smallpox epidemic occurred in Hispaniola in 1518–1519 and spread to neighboring Caribbean islands, striking a Taíno population already devastated by enslavement, forced labor, and violence. Earlier reports of sickness in the Caribbean cannot be confidently diagnosed. In 1520 smallpox reached central Mexico during the Spanish–Mexica war, and Nahua accounts record its symptoms, its social effects, and the hunger that followed. Yet disease did not conquer Mexico: Indigenous alliances, siege warfare, political fractures, and Spanish decisions determined the war's course.

Other introduced diseases, including measles and probably influenza and typhus, followed, and the great cocoliztli epidemics of 1545 and 1576 devastated New Spain amid drought, forced labor, and tribute. Ancient-DNA evidence of Salmonella enterica at Teposcolula-Yucundaa illustrates both the promise and the limits of new science: it shows infection in individuals but does not prove that a single pathogen explains the whole epidemic.

The central analytical argument was that war, forced labor, enslavement, hunger, and resettlement multiplied the effects of infection. Tribute obligations calculated on earlier population counts placed heavier burdens on survivors, and congregación could concentrate people in new settings. Demographic collapse also included fertility decline, disrupted households, migration, and captivity, and repeated epidemics prevented recovery through cumulative vulnerability.

Regionally, epidemic chronologies varied. The Caribbean and Mesoamerica experienced epidemics in the early sixteenth century, while the Southeast, Southwest, Northeast, Great Lakes, California, the Northwest Coast, the Plains, and the Arctic experienced major epidemics at later and differing times, generally tied to sustained contact, missions, slaving, and trade. The debate over whether epidemics raced ahead of Europeans has moved toward a model of multiple regional epidemics rather than a single continental wave.

Indigenous peoples responded with medical knowledge, caregiving, flight, ritual, and community reorganization. Healers such as the Nahua ticitl and authors such as Martín de la Cruz show a living medical tradition. Political life changed as leaders and elders died, but altepetl, cabildos, petitions, and records show reconstruction and adaptation rather than disappearance.

Finally, the chapter examined the uncertainty of numbers and evidence. Pre-contact population estimates vary enormously because they rest on indirect and assumption-laden methods, and no single percentage should be treated as settled. Written sources provide the strongest evidence of epidemics, archaeology and bioarchaeology offer limited direct identification of specific outbreaks, and ancient DNA is a powerful but carefully bounded tool. Together, these approaches support a picture of catastrophe that is serious, varied, and historically explainable.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it addresses one of the most consequential and most frequently oversimplified subjects in the history of the Americas. The epidemics that followed 1492 transformed Indigenous societies, shaped colonial economies, and altered the demographic geography of the continent. Understanding them accurately requires holding two propositions together: the mortality was catastrophic, and the explanation for it was never biology alone.

The chapter also reveals larger processes. Disease followed networks of connection, and it exploited conditions that human beings created: wars, labor regimes, tribute systems, resettlement, and hunger. It was shaped by the same colonial systems examined in Chapters 38 and 39 and anticipated the different regional histories of Chapters 41 through 48, in which epidemic timing, mission systems, trade, and slaving would interact in distinct ways. It also supports the argument of Chapter 1 that contact and colonization were uneven, regionally specific processes rather than a single continental event.

Equally, the chapter shows that Indigenous peoples were historical actors in the face of catastrophe. They cared for the sick, preserved knowledge, rebuilt households, negotiated with colonial authorities, and continued political and cultural life. The continuities that survived, in communities, languages, medical knowledge, and memory, are central to the larger argument of this textbook that Indigenous nations are continuing political and historical communities rather than remnants of a vanished past.

Finally, the chapter models a way of thinking about historical uncertainty. Numbers are contested, diagnoses are provisional, and new evidence, from archaeology to ancient DNA, continues to refine the picture. Responsible history neither exaggerates what is known nor retreats into doubt. It explains the evidence, names the limits, and treats the lives of the people affected with care.
