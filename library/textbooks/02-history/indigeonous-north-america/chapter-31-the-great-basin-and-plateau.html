The Great Basin and Plateau

Indigenous Peoples of North America: A Continental History

Chapter Opening

In autumn, extended families of Newe, Numu, and other Great Basin peoples climbed into mountain woodlands to harvest piñon cones, while on the Columbia and its tributaries Plateau communities dried and stored the salmon that had returned from the sea. These were not two versions of a single way of life. They were two different Indigenous answers to the same interior landscape of mountains, deserts, and rivers.

The interior West is often imagined, in popular memory, as empty country that travelers crossed on the way to somewhere else: the salt flats and sagebrush valleys between the Rocky Mountains and the Sierra Nevada, and the basalt canyons and rolling grasslands drained by the Columbia. This chapter treats those lands as the homes of many distinct peoples whose histories reach back many thousands of years. It follows the outline's assignment for Chapter 31, which is to examine Shoshonean, Numic, Salishan, Sahaptian, and other communities across the interior West, and it begins Part VI, in which Volume III turns from the nations of the eastern and central continent toward the western, northern, and continental worlds that Indigenous peoples occupied on the eve of colonization.

Two broad regions organize the chapter. The Great Basin is a vast zone of interior drainage in which rivers end in lakes, marshes, or sinks rather than reaching the sea, and in which north-south mountain ranges alternate with arid valleys. The Plateau, by contrast, is organized around great rivers, above all the Columbia and the Snake, and around the fisheries, root grounds, and uplands they sustain. Scholars have long used these two regions as analytical categories, and the line between them is a convenience rather than a wall: Northern Shoshone, Bannock, Northern Paiute, Klamath, and Modoc communities lived where the two merged, and their histories cross the boundary.

The chapter's central argument is that these were not marginal spaces between supposedly more complex societies. Great Basin mobility was an intentional environmental and social strategy that required immense botanical, hydrological, and geographic knowledge, and it sustained dense networks of kinship and reciprocal access. Plateau societies were river-centered, resource-rich, often village-based communities whose fisheries and root economies supported ceremony, trade, and political relationships over long distances. Neither region was isolated. Obsidian, shell, hides, dried foods, baskets, and, later, horses moved along corridors that tied the interior to the Plains, the Southwest, California, the Northwest Coast, and the northern interior.

A second argument concerns names and categories. Just as Chapter 30 showed that the "Southwest" contained Pueblo, O'odham, Diné, and Apache worlds rather than a single Southwestern society, this chapter shows that the Great Basin was not only Numic and the Plateau was not only Sahaptian, and that linguistic families are not political nations. Shoshone, Paiute, and Ute communities speak related Numic languages but were never one political people. Interior Salish speakers on the Plateau are distinct societies, not an appendage of the Coast Salish peoples treated in Chapter 33. The chapter therefore proceeds people by people and place by place, then returns to regional comparison.

The evidence is varied and uneven. Archaeology documents occupation sites, storage facilities, fishing stations, and trade materials across the whole span of human presence. Historical linguistics traces relationships among languages. Oral traditions, which many descendant communities hold to record their presence on these lands since the beginning of time, convey histories that archaeology and linguistics cannot reach in the same way. Written outsider documentation begins unevenly in the late eighteenth century in portions of the Great Basin and becomes substantially more extensive across the Plateau and northern interior in the early nineteenth century, in the journals of explorers and fur traders; to it are added ethnographic notes gathered in the twentieth century from elders whose memories reached back into the nineteenth. Each source type has gaps, and this chapter keeps its limits visible.

The chapter does not narrate the detailed colonial transformation of the interior, which belongs to later chapters, including those on Plateau and Northwest resistance (Chapter 72) and the Nez Perce War (Chapter 78). It also does not absorb California or the Northwest Coast, which are assigned to Chapters 32 and 33. Its task is to establish the interior West as a functioning, historically dynamic set of Indigenous worlds, so that later losses, resistance, and renewal can be understood against what existed.

Historical Context

Volume I established that human history in western North America is very deep. Chapter 8 examined the early western adaptations of the deserts, basins, and interior West, and Chapter 12 examined long-distance exchange and the emergence of regional worlds. This chapter does not repeat that story. It picks up when archaeological traditions can, with caution, be linked to peoples and languages known from later centuries, and it describes the interior West as it was in the centuries before sustained European contact, with enough background to explain how it came to be that way.

Volume II placed these regions beside the agricultural and urban worlds of the continent. Maize agriculture spread widely across North America, but not everywhere, and not equally. In the interior West, farming was practiced in some places, notably by the Fremont communities of what is now Utah and by Southern Paiute communities with irrigated plots, but across most of the Great Basin and Plateau, abundant wild resources, aridity, short growing seasons, or elevation made dependence on cultivated crops unnecessary or impractical. That is a statement about adaptation, not about capacity. Peoples who did not rely on maize managed plant and animal resources through knowledge, labor, and social arrangements as demanding as those of farmers.

Volume III's preceding chapters turned from the Northeast and Southeast through the Plains before the horse (Chapter 29) and the Southwest (Chapter 30). The interior West sat between those worlds and the Pacific. Its peoples were connected eastward to Plains bison hunters and the Rocky Mountain passes, southward to the Southwest and Mesoamerican-influenced exchange, westward across the Sierra Nevada and Cascades to California and the Northwest Coast, and northward along the Columbia and Fraser systems to the northern interior. Chapter 34 treats the Subarctic, and Chapter 36 will synthesize these connections as a continental system.

Two cautions govern the chapter. First, most detailed descriptions of social life come from ethnographic work conducted in the 1920s through 1950s, often with elders recalling practices from decades earlier, after epidemics, dispossession, and economic disruption had altered communities. Such sources are invaluable but cannot be read as photographs of fifteenth-century life. Second, the word "eve" in the volume title is relative. Different parts of the interior experienced European goods and diseases at different times, and some peoples did not meet Europeans or Americans in person until the early nineteenth century. The chapter therefore describes the interior West as a reconstructed system on the eve of colonization, using later evidence cautiously.

Section 31.1

The Interior West as an Indigenous World

The interior West was never a single place, and its Indigenous inhabitants did not think of it as one. The people who now use the terms Great Basin and Plateau for scholarly purposes lived in specific valleys, along specific rivers, and around specific lakes, each with its own names, stories, and obligations. These are modern regional labels imposed by outsiders and later adopted as geographic and anthropological categories. "Great Basin" originated as a nineteenth-century geographic term, associated with John C. Frémont's recognition of the region's interior drainage, while anthropologists later formalized the Great Basin and the Plateau as culture-area categories used to organize a great deal of variation. They are useful when handled carefully and misleading when treated as natural or fixed.

The Great Basin, by the common hydrological definition, covers roughly 200,000 square miles of interior drainage across what are now Nevada, western Utah, and parts of Oregon, Idaho, California, and Wyoming. Its rivers, including the Humboldt, Walker, Carson, Truckee, and Bear, flow into lakes and sinks rather than the ocean. Its landscape alternates between mountain ranges that catch precipitation and valley floors that are dry, salty, or marshy. The Plateau, in the usage this chapter follows, centers on the Columbia River drainage, including the Snake, Spokane, Okanogan, Yakima, Clearwater, Salmon, and Fraser systems, between the Rocky Mountains and the Cascades. It extends north into what is now British Columbia, where Interior Salish peoples such as the Secwépemc and Syilx have long lived. Modern borders cut across this region, and so do the homelands of many nations.

The two regions looked toward one another through a long gradient. Northern Shoshone and Bannock communities lived along the Snake River Plain, Northern Paiute communities lived in southeastern Oregon, and Klamath and Modoc communities lived around the lakes and marshes of the Klamath Basin. These peoples belonged to both worlds. The chapter's organization therefore moves from the Basin to the Plateau, but it keeps returning to the zones where they overlap, because the borders between them were crossed constantly for marriage, trade, harvest, and war.

Understanding what these societies were before sustained colonization is part of understanding what is at stake in those contemporary struggles.

Section 31.2

From Ancient Western Adaptations to Historically Identifiable Peoples

Chapter 8 examined the early adaptations of the desert West, so this section does not retell them. It addresses a methodological problem: how can historians move from archaeological sequences stretching back more than ten thousand years to the named peoples of the last several centuries? The answer is, in part, with caution.

The archaeological record shows long continuity of human presence and substantial change within it. In the Great Basin, dry caves such as Danger Cave and Hogup Cave in Utah and Lovelock Cave in Nevada preserved plant remains, baskets, sandals, and other perishable items that are rare elsewhere, allowing researchers to reconstruct diet and technology over many millennia. Lake and wetland margins provided reliable resources during wetter periods, and as the climate shifted, communities adjusted their use of these places. On the Plateau, rockshelters such as Marmes on the Palouse River and sites along the Columbia and Snake document early occupation, followed by a long sequence in which riverine fishing, root processing, and eventually pithouse villages became increasingly prominent. Sites near The Dalles show salmon use by about nine thousand years ago, although most archaeologists think that the large-scale storage fishery that dominated later Plateau economies took shape more gradually over the following millennia.

The relationship between such archaeological patterns and later peoples is a matter of inference. Artifact styles, house forms, and subsistence patterns do not by themselves reveal the language spoken or the political identity held by their makers. Archaeologists use the term "archaeological culture" for recurring assemblages, but the label describes material patterns, not nations. The Fremont complex in the eastern Great Basin, for example, shows maize farming, distinctive clay figurines and pottery, and rock art, and it disappears from the archaeological record around 1300 CE. Scholars still debate whether Fremont people moved away, merged with neighbors, adopted foraging, or were ancestors of later communities in ways the archaeological evidence cannot settle. Descendant communities often hold their own histories of continuity, and those accounts deserve weight alongside scientific reconstruction.

Recent ethical and scientific developments have made this question concrete. The ancient individual from the Columbia Plateau known to scholars as Kennewick Man and to the Columbia Plateau tribes as the Ancient One, dated to roughly eight to nine thousand years ago, was the subject of long legal and scientific controversy. A 2015 genomic study found that he was more closely related to present-day Native Americans than to any other living population, and in 2017 a coalition of Columbia Plateau tribes reburied him. A similar case in the Great Basin, the Spirit Cave remains in Nevada, were repatriated to the Fallon Paiute-Shoshone Tribe in 2016 after genomic analysis. These cases, treated further in Chapter 125, show that questions about ancient ancestry are also questions about present-day authority and responsibility.

Section 31.3

The Great Basin Environment and the Logic of Mobility

The Great Basin's defining feature is aridity organized by elevation. The Sierra Nevada and Cascades block moisture from the Pacific, leaving the valleys to the east dry, while the many north-south ranges rise high enough to receive snow and rain. Precipitation varies sharply by year and by place. A valley floor may be salt-encrusted while a mountain slope a few miles away carries piñon-juniper woodland, and a spring or creek may support wetland plants and animals in an otherwise dry basin. Lakes were larger and more numerous during wetter periods and have shrunk in drier ones, which reshaped human options over thousands of years.

This environment rewarded knowledge of timing and place. Seeds ripened at different times across the landscape, piñon crops varied greatly from year to year and from range to range, fish spawned in particular streams, waterfowl gathered in particular marshes, and large game moved according to snow and forage. A family or band that stayed in one location year-round would have faced regular shortages. A family that knew where and when resources would be abundant, and that had relationships permitting access to those places, could live well. Mobility in this setting was not wandering. It was scheduled and informed movement among known places, with caches of stored food supporting the winter.

Historical Vocabulary
Seasonal Round

Definition. The annual pattern by which a community moves among resource areas, harvesting different foods at different seasons and returning to winter residences and stored provisions.

Historical context. The seasonal round was fundamental to both the Great Basin and the Plateau. In the Basin, it often involved dispersal of families across many resource zones followed by aggregation at winter camps or at major harvests. On the Plateau, it often involved movement between river winter villages, fishing stations, root-digging grounds, and mountain berry fields, with each community holding rights to particular places.

Why it matters. The seasonal round organized labor, kinship, ceremony, and territory. It is the practical link between environmental knowledge and social organization.

Potential misunderstanding. The phrase "nomadic" is often applied carelessly to interior peoples. A seasonal round follows a repeating, knowledge-based pattern within recognized territories and relationships, which is different from aimless movement. Nor was the round fixed: droughts, poor harvests, intermarriage, trade opportunities, and later horses led communities to adjust their routes.

The scientific term sometimes used for these economies is "foraging," and descriptions of Great Basin peoples as hunter-gatherers are accurate as far as they go. They become misleading if they imply that the people merely took what nature offered. Interior peoples managed their environments through selective harvesting, the transport and replanting of seeds and roots in some places, the use of fire in some habitats, and the construction of dams, ditches, weirs, and traps. In Owens Valley, for example, Paiute communities diverted streams to irrigate stands of wild plants, a practice documented in the 1930s and confirmed by later research. That ethnographic record, discussed further in Section 31.8, is a reminder that the boundary between foraging and agriculture is a modern analytical line, not an Indigenous one.

Finally, mobility had social meaning. It spread risk, allowed communities to be flexible in size, and let families meet seasonally for marriages, ceremonies, trade, and information exchange. A band that gathered for a piñon harvest or a communal rabbit drive also reinforced alliances and arranged marriages. The Great Basin's apparent sparseness of permanent settlement therefore conceals a dense social geography, in which relationships operated across hundreds of miles.

Section 31.4

Numic Languages, Migration, and the Numic Expansion Debate

Most of the Great Basin's historically documented peoples speak languages of the Numic branch of the Uto-Aztecan family, the same larger family that includes languages spoken in the Southwest and Mexico (see Chapter 1 and Chapter 14). Linguists divide Numic into three subgroups. Western Numic includes Northern Paiute and Mono. Central Numic includes Shoshone, Comanche, and Timbisha. Southern Numic includes Southern Paiute (including Chemehuevi) and Ute. These languages are closely related, and their relatively shallow divergence relative to other North American language families suggests to many linguists that their common ancestor was spoken more recently than the ancestors of, for example, the Salishan or Algonquian families.

Historical Vocabulary
Numic and the Language Family

Definition. "Numic" names a branch of Uto-Aztecan languages spoken across the Great Basin and adjacent regions. The word is a modern scholarly label derived from a common root in these languages meaning "person" or "people," and it should not be mistaken for a self-designation of any one nation.

Historical context. Numic speakers call themselves by many names, including Newe (Shoshone), Numu (Northern Paiute), Nuwuvi (Southern Paiute), and Nuche (Ute). The language classification groups them; their political and social identities separate them.

Terminology note. Older scholarship used "Plateau Shoshonean" (and "Shoshonean" more broadly) as a linguistic designation for these languages. That label has been largely superseded by "Numic" in modern scholarship, and Shoshonean and Numic should not be read as separate language families. The assigned scope of this chapter, which names both terms, refers to the same body of communities.

Why it matters. Treating a language branch as a nation would erase real political and cultural differences and would repeat the older error of describing all interior peoples as one "Digger" or "Snake" people, categories imposed by outsiders and often used contemptuously.

Potential misunderstanding. Linguistic relationship does not mean shared government, shared economy, or unvarying friendship. Related peoples have been allies, trading partners, and rivals.

The traditional account of Numic dispersal, associated with the linguist Sydney Lamb's 1958 argument, proposes that Numic speakers originated in the southwestern Great Basin, perhaps near Death Valley and the southern Sierra, and spread north and east across the Basin beginning roughly a thousand years ago, replacing or absorbing earlier populations whose languages are unknown. Archaeologists connected this model to the appearance of certain artifacts, such as small Desert Side-notched arrow points and a utilitarian brownware pottery, which they associated with Numic expansion. The resource-based model advanced by Robert Bettinger and Martin Baumhoff in 1982 proposed an economic mechanism: Numic groups may have gained a competitive advantage through greater reliance on labor-intensive small-seed processing than the hypothesized pre-Numic populations, who are modeled as relying more heavily on large game, thereby supporting larger populations in marginal habitats.

Historical Debate

Did Numic Speakers Spread Across the Great Basin, and If So, How?

The question: The Numic languages are strikingly similar across a very large area, which implies recent spread from a common source. But what does the archaeological and genetic record say about who moved, when, and with what effects on the peoples already there?

Interpretation A — Population replacement or large-scale migration: Some scholars argue that the linguistic uniformity, together with certain artifact patterns that appear after about 1000 CE, indicates the movement of Numic-speaking populations from the southwestern Basin. They stress the shallow time depth of the languages and the lack of any evidence of earlier languages surviving in the region.
Interpretation B — Language shift, local continuity, and gradual spread: Other scholars argue that archaeological evidence for sharp population change is weak, that artifacts such as arrow points and pottery spread through exchange and imitation, and that a language can spread without large-scale replacement of people. They also point to mixed genetic signals and to regional variation in the timing of apparent change.

Evidence: Linguistic reconstruction (relative similarity of the languages), archaeological distributions of artifacts and settlement patterns, radiocarbon chronologies, and ancient and modern DNA. Each line has limits. Linguistic dating is uncertain, artifacts do not speak languages, and genetic ancestry does not equal language or identity.

Points of disagreement: Chronology, mechanism (migration versus language shift), and interpretation of artifact evidence. Some of the disagreement also concerns whether archaeological assemblages can be linked to specific languages at all.

Why it matters: If Numic peoples were recent arrivals who displaced earlier inhabitants, the Basin's history looks like one of replacement. If continuity and gradual spread were more important, it looks like one of long local adaptation and cultural change. The question also bears on Indigenous accounts of origin and presence, which many descendant communities hold to extend to the beginning of time, and on how scholars respond to those accounts.

Student task: Identify one kind of evidence that would strengthen Interpretation A and one that would strengthen Interpretation B. Then explain why scholars might treat the debate as unresolved rather than choosing a winner.

This textbook treats the Numic expansion as a debated model rather than a settled fact. It is clear that Numic languages are closely related and widely spread, that earlier archaeological traditions existed in the Basin, and that many aspects of the transition remain contested. It is not clear how many people moved, in which directions, or how they interacted with those already present. As in Chapter 30's discussion of the Athabaskan migrations, the responsible course is to present what scholars think, mark what remains uncertain, and avoid imposing a single narrative.

Section 31.5

Shoshone Worlds

The Shoshone, whose own name for themselves is Newe, occupied one of the largest territories of any Numic-speaking people, from the Snake River Plain in the north through central Nevada and western Utah to the Rocky Mountain foothills of Wyoming. Outsiders often distinguish Northern, Western, and Eastern Shoshone, although the divisions are modern conveniences and communities had their own names and identities. Differences in environment produced very different ways of living under a shared language.

Western Shoshone communities in the central Great Basin lived primarily in small family groups that moved among valleys and ranges, harvesting piñon in autumn, seeds and roots in spring and summer, and game throughout the year. Their territories included the Ruby, Reese River, and Snake Valleys, and their neighbors included Northern Paiute, Goshute, and Ute communities. Closely related Timbisha Shoshone communities lived in the Death Valley region, combining valley-floor and mountain resources across a wide vertical range. Northern Shoshone communities along the Snake River and its tributaries had access to salmon and other fish, camas, and bison hunting in the mountain valleys and across the Continental Divide, and they were closely linked with Bannock communities. The Lemhi Shoshone of what is now central Idaho met the Lewis and Clark expedition in 1805 and supplied horses essential to its crossing of the Rocky Mountains.

Horses transformed some Shoshone communities and not others. Evidence for Shoshone horse ownership by the early eighteenth century is substantial, and Northern and Eastern Shoshone groups developed mounted bison hunting, long-distance travel, and expanded trade, as discussed in Chapter 49. Western Shoshone groups in the arid core of the Basin had fewer incentives and fewer pastures, and many continued earlier patterns of mobility on foot. A group of Shoshone speakers also moved southeast onto the southern Plains and became the Comanche, whose rise is treated in Chapter 50. These developments show that Shoshone history cannot be summarized as one pattern.

Connections eastward and westward were constant. Shoshone communities traded with Plains peoples such as the Crow and Blackfoot (hunters, trading partners, and rivals by turns), with Plateau peoples such as the Nimiipuu and Salish, and with Ute and Paiute neighbors. Marriage ties spread access to resources across communities. These relationships were political as well as economic, and they allowed families to draw on kin networks during local shortages. The Shoshone world, in short, was a network rather than a bounded unit.

Section 31.6

Paiute, Ute, Goshute, and Related Numic Communities

The Northern Paiute, or Numu, occupied a very large area stretching across eastern Oregon, western Nevada, southwestern Idaho, and parts of eastern California. Their communities took names from characteristic foods or places, such as the cui-ui eaters of Pyramid Lake (Kuyuidökadö) and the tule-root eaters of the Stillwater and Lovelock marshes. Pyramid Lake provided cui-ui, a large lake sucker found nowhere else, along with Lahontan cutthroat trout, while the Humboldt and Stillwater wetlands provided waterfowl and plant foods. In the north, Wadatika bands in the Harney Basin gathered roots, seeds, and game. The Bannock, who speak a Northern Paiute language, lived along the Snake River with Northern Shoshone, and they were among the earliest mounted peoples of that region.

The Southern Paiute, or Nuwuvi, lived across southern Nevada, southwestern Utah, northern Arizona, and parts of the Mojave Desert. Their bands included the Kaibab, Moapa, Shivwits, and Las Vegas peoples, and the closely related Chemehuevi of the lower Colorado. Southern Paiute communities combined foraging with small-scale irrigated farming of corn, beans, and squash along streams such as the Virgin River and at springs. Spanish accounts from the Domínguez-Escalante expedition of 1776 describe irrigated fields among the Southern Paiute, and later ethnographic accounts confirm cultivation. This is a reminder that the boundary between farming and foraging was permeable, and that Southern Paiute economies were shaped by interaction with Ancestral Puebloan and Fremont traditions and with the Colorado Plateau's neighbors (see Chapters 18 and 30).

The Ute, or Nuche, lived in the mountains, canyons, and basins of what is now Colorado and eastern Utah, with a number of named bands such as the Mouache, Capote, Weeminuche, Uncompahgre, Yampa, and Uintah. Their territory straddled Basin, Plateau, and Rocky Mountain environments, and they hunted deer, elk, and, in mountain parks, bison. Contact with the Spanish in New Mexico gave the Ute early access to horses; Ute peoples were among the earliest Indigenous peoples of the interior West to acquire Spanish horses. By the late seventeenth century some eastern Ute groups were mounted, while adoption spread unevenly among other Ute communities during the eighteenth century. The Pueblo Revolt of 1680 (Chapter 44) disrupted Spanish herds and is one of several circumstances that put horses into Indigenous hands. The Ute also traded and raided in the borderlands of New Mexico, including participation in the captive trade discussed in Chapter 53.

The Goshute (Kutsipiuti), whose language is a dialect of Shoshone, lived in the desert valleys west and south of the Great Salt Lake. Their environment was among the driest of any inhabited part of the Basin, and their economy depended on very precise knowledge of springs, seeds, rabbits, and small game, including insects and rodents, along with piñon from the surrounding ranges. This is the landscape that nineteenth-century travelers sometimes described as barren. That judgment says more about outsiders' expectations than about the Goshute, whose communities sustained themselves there for many centuries.

These Numic communities illustrate the chapter's point that shared language does not mean shared political life. Northern Paiute communities were generally small and flexible, Southern Paiute bands were linked by kinship and local irrigation, and Ute bands became, after horses, mobile and politically assertive societies with significant roles in the borderlands. Geography, neighbors, and economic opportunity explain these differences better than language does.

Section 31.7

Washoe and the Diversity beyond the Numic World

The Washoe, or Wá·šiw, are essential to any account that treats the Great Basin as more than a Numic story. Their homeland centers on Lake Tahoe, which they call Dá'aw (the lake), and on the Carson, Truckee, and Walker river valleys of the eastern Sierra Nevada. The Washoe language is not Numic and is not closely related to any of its neighbors. Linguists classify it as a language isolate, although some have proposed a distant relationship to the Hokan languages of California, a hypothesis that remains contested. The Washoe thus provide a case in which the language map of the Basin margins does not match the cultural map built around Numic expansion.

Washoe seasonal movement used the full range of the eastern Sierra: summer at Lake Tahoe and in high mountain meadows, where they fished, gathered roots and berries, and hunted; fall at lower elevations east of the crest, where they harvested piñon; and winter in valley settlements where stored foods sustained them. Washoe families fished the Truckee and Carson rivers for spawning trout and suckers, and they traveled west across the mountains to trade with Miwok, Maidu, and Mono communities in California, exchanging items such as piñon nuts, hides, and finely made baskets for acorns, shell beads, and other goods. The Washoe also have a long tradition of exceptionally fine basketry, which later became widely known through twentieth-century weavers.

The Washoe case reinforces that the interior West's diversity included more than the Numic branch. The region's peoples also included the Fremont communities of earlier centuries, whose descendants are debated, and non-Numic communities along the margins of the Great Basin who interacted with Numic neighbors. Along the western edge, Owens Valley Paiute communities and their California neighbors formed a borderland whose detailed treatment belongs to Chapter 32. Along the south and east, Southern Paiute and Ute communities met the Pueblo and other Southwest peoples described in Chapter 30. These borderlands show that boundaries between cultural regions are zones of interaction rather than lines.

This section's larger point is methodological. A history that equates the Great Basin with Numic history would erase peoples like the Washoe and would misread the linguistic and social complexity of the region. Where a single language family dominates, as the Numic languages do across much of the Basin, historians must still ask what earlier populations were present, how they lived, and how their descendants and neighbors understood their own origins.

Section 31.8

Seeds, Pine Nuts, Game, Wetlands, and Great Basin Food Systems

Great Basin food systems were diverse, seasonal, and knowledge-intensive. The most celebrated resource was the piñon pine nut. In years of good crops, families climbed to piñon-juniper woodlands in autumn, harvested cones by knocking them from trees with long poles or gathering them from the ground, roasted them to open the cones, and removed and stored the nuts in caches. Pine nuts are high in calories and fat and can be stored for months, so a good harvest helped carry families through winter. Because piñon crops fail unpredictably in particular ranges, communities needed relationships that would allow them to harvest in other areas when their own failed, which is one reason reciprocal access to territory mattered so much.

Seeds were the staple of many families through the year. Women and girls gathered grass seeds such as Indian ricegrass, sunflower, and many others using seed beaters and burden baskets, then winnowed them on flat trays with hot coals to parch them, and ground them on stone metates. Roots and bulbs, including biscuitroot, bitterroot, and yampa, were dug in spring and summer. Berries, greens, and in the south mesquite pods and agave supplemented the plant diet. Skills in basketry, in twined and coiled forms, were essential to this economy, and the baskets that survive in dry caves are among the finest evidence of the technical achievement of interior peoples.

Animal foods were equally varied. Men and boys hunted deer, mountain sheep, and pronghorn, and they organized communal drives for rabbits and, in some regions, pronghorn. In a rabbit drive, large groups herded animals into long nets of twisted plant fiber, then dispatched them; the rabbit meat was eaten and the fur woven into blankets. Antelope drives used corrals and brush fences, and were often led by a person with ceremonial responsibilities. Waterfowl, fish, and insects supplied additional protein. At Pyramid Lake and Walker Lake, fishing with nets and weirs supported large gatherings. At Stillwater and Lovelock, tule marsh provided food, building material, and the raw material for boats and decoys, as archaeological finds of duck decoys from Lovelock Cave illustrate.

These systems involved management as well as harvest. Owens Valley Paiute communities diverted stream water to irrigate stands of wild seed and root plants, a practice that Julian Steward described in the 1930s and that later field research supported. Communities in various parts of the Basin used fire in some habitats to encourage particular plants and attract game, although the extent and effects of such burning are still studied and vary by place. Storage, in underground pits, caves, and baskets, extended food availability into winter, and food sharing among relatives reduced the risk of local failure. These practices were not simple extractions from a hostile environment but adaptations resting on deep knowledge, and they sustained societies in some of North America's most demanding terrain.

Everyday Life and Social History

The Pinyon Harvest and the Communal Drive

For many Great Basin families, the autumn pine-nut harvest was both an economic necessity and a social season. Extended families, often including several households related by marriage, camped together near stands of piñon, and work was divided by age and gender. Children helped gather fallen cones, older women supervised processing, and young men and older boys hunted nearby. Because many families gathered in one area, the harvest also provided occasions for courtship, ceremony, and storytelling, and for the exchange of news from distant valleys.

Communal drives gave similar occasions on a larger scale. A rabbit drive or a pronghorn drive required many participants, nets, and coordination, and the person organizing the drive held temporary authority recognized by the participants. The meat and hides were distributed among those who took part. Such events show how a society without permanent chiefs or fixed villages could still mobilize collective labor. Historians should remember that the details come largely from ethnographic accounts recorded after major disruption, and communities varied in how they organized such events.

Section 31.9

Families, Bands, Territory, and Political Life in the Great Basin

Older anthropology often described Great Basin societies as organized only at the level of the family, an influential view associated with Julian Steward's 1938 study of Basin-Plateau sociopolitical groups. Steward drew attention to the importance of the family as the basic economic unit and to the flexibility of larger groupings. His work was a major contribution, but later scholars have shown that it understated the extent of political organization above the household and the continuity of territorial rights. Communities in the Basin had leaders, councils, ceremonial specialists, and rules governing access to resources, even when these arrangements did not take the form of centralized states or permanent chiefs.

Kinship was the main framework of social life. Families belonged to larger networks defined by descent and marriage, and these networks structured who could harvest where, who could share food, and who could expect help in a bad year. Marriage often linked families across valleys and sometimes across linguistic boundaries, and it extended access to resources. A family that married into a neighboring community gained the right, and the obligation, to participate in its harvests and its ceremonies. Territorial rights were typically exercised by groups that returned regularly to the same places, such as piñon stands, fishing stations, and root grounds, and outsiders ordinarily sought permission or invitation.

Leadership was situational and based on recognized skill. A respected elder might advise a community on movement, a "rabbit boss" or similar leader might coordinate a communal drive, an antelope specialist might direct a pronghorn hunt, and ceremonial leaders organized dances and rituals. Such leadership commanded respect rather than coercion, and it was generally limited to the task or season at hand. In the Southern Paiute case, local bands linked by kinship and shared resources recognized headmen, and in the Ute case, band leaders took on larger roles after horses made mobility and warfare more important. Women exercised authority within households and in resource management, including food processing, storage, and in some communities ceremonial roles.

Challenging the older stereotype that small-scale organization meant no political institutions is important for both historical and ethical reasons. Outsiders in the nineteenth century often described Great Basin peoples as primitive and gave them derogatory names such as "Diggers," taking the root-digging that sustained families as a mark of inferiority. This vocabulary served the interests of those who wanted the land and denied the existence of Indigenous governance. A history that treats flexible, kin-based political organization as a legitimate political tradition, adapted to a particular environment, corrects the record and shows how Great Basin peoples governed access, resolved disputes, and negotiated with neighbors for centuries before any outside government recognized them.

Section 31.10

The Plateau: Rivers, Mountains, and Interior Networks

Moving from the Great Basin to the Plateau means moving from a land of scarce and scattered water to one organized around great rivers. The Columbia rises in the Rocky Mountains of what is now British Columbia, arcs through the Plateau, and reaches the Pacific; the Snake, Spokane, Okanogan, Yakima, Clearwater, Salmon, and many other tributaries feed it. The Fraser, to the north, drains much of the interior of what is now British Columbia and supports comparable fisheries. These rivers cut through basalt canyons, open into broad valleys, and drop over falls and rapids that concentrated migrating salmon and made certain places exceptional fishing sites.

The Plateau is also a land of vertical zones. River valleys with relatively mild winters lie below sagebrush steppe, grasslands, and open pine forest, and above them are mountain meadows, huckleberry fields, and high game ranges. Rainfall is moderate in the west and heavier in the east, and the Cascades cast a rain shadow across much of the central Plateau. Snow, cold winters, and hot summers shaped seasonal activity. Root-digging grounds, such as camas prairies and the open hills where biscuitroot and bitterroot grew, lay at intermediate elevations, and mountain berry fields lay higher.

Historical Vocabulary
Plateau

Definition. A culture-area label used by anthropologists for the interior Columbia and Fraser drainages and adjacent uplands, home to peoples who speak Interior Salish, Sahaptian, and several other languages and who share river-centered economies and some ceremonial and social patterns.

Historical context. The term was developed in the early twentieth century, and the work of ethnographers such as Verne Ray in the 1930s helped define it. Ray's study of cultural relations in the Plateau remains influential, but scholars now emphasize variation within the region and its interaction with neighboring regions.

Why it matters. The concept helps explain shared patterns such as winter villages, salmon fisheries, and root economies. It can also obscure political and cultural differences among nations.

Potential misunderstanding. "Plateau" is a convenience for scholars. It does not mark a bounded cultural unit, and the people it describes did not use the term.

The contrast with the Basin is important but should not be exaggerated. Plateau peoples also moved seasonally, and Basin peoples also formed regular gatherings. The key difference was the concentrated, predictable, and storable resource of salmon, which allowed Plateau communities to maintain winter villages with substantial stores along rivers, while many Basin communities remained more dispersed. At the same time, Plateau economies were not simply fishing economies. They depended equally on roots, berries, game, and exchange, as the following sections show.

Interior networks bound these societies together and linked them to others. The Columbia carried goods and people between the Plateau and the Pacific; passes through the Rockies connected the Plateau with the northern Plains; and trails across the Cascades and the Blue Mountains linked the interior with the coast and with the Basin. Language communities overlapped these networks, and many Plateau people were multilingual, speaking neighbors' languages and, later, trade jargons. The Plateau was thus both a set of homelands and a hub of communication.

Section 31.11

Interior Salish Worlds

Interior Salish languages form a continuum of related languages and dialects spoken across the northern and eastern Plateau, from the upper Fraser and Thompson rivers in what is now British Columbia through the Okanogan, Columbia, and Spokane river valleys to the mountain valleys of western Montana. They are one division of the larger Salishan family, whose other major division, the Coast Salish languages, belongs to the Northwest Coast and Chapter 33. The two divisions are related but historically and culturally distinct, and Interior Salish peoples developed economies, political relationships, and ceremonial traditions oriented toward interior rivers, uplands, and exchange routes.

The northern Interior Salish peoples include the Secwépemc (Shuswap), Nlaka'pamux (Thompson), St'át'imc (Lillooet), and Syilx (Okanagan) of British Columbia and northern Washington, and the Sinixt (Arrow Lakes) of the upper Columbia. Further south and east lived the Spokane, whose homeland centered on the Spokane River and its falls; the Schitsu'umsh (Coeur d'Alene) of the lake and river country of northern Idaho; the Qlispé (Kalispel and Pend d'Oreille) of the Pend Oreille River and Lake and the Clark Fork; the Séliš (Salish or Flathead) of the Bitterroot and Flathead valleys of Montana; and the Wenatchi, Columbia (Sinkiuse), Methow, and Colville peoples of the middle Columbia and its tributaries. Each was a distinct society with its own territory, stories, and political life.

Winter villages stood along rivers and lakes, with houses that in many communities were semisubterranean pithouses entered by a ladder through the roof, as in the kekuli of Interior Salish peoples in British Columbia; in other places, communities used mat-covered lodges built over shallow pits. In summer, families moved to fishing stations, root grounds, and berry fields. Salmon were the base of the economy where they could be caught: Kettle Falls on the Columbia, a gathering place for the Sinixt, Syilx, Colville, Spokane, and others, was one of the most productive fisheries in the interior, and Spokane Falls on the Spokane River and the Fraser River canyons were comparable. Fishers used dip nets, spears, weirs, and traps, and the catch was dried and stored.

Some Interior Salish peoples also hunted bison. The Séliš and Qlispé, whose territories lay west of the Continental Divide, crossed the mountains to hunt on the Plains, and some oral traditions and scholars suggest that such journeys long preceded horses, while horses later made them more frequent and more dangerous because they brought these peoples into conflict with Blackfoot and other Plains nations. Salish, Kutenai, and Nimiipuu communities also formed alliances for joint hunts and mutual defense. These patterns show that Interior Salish history was oriented as much toward the Plains as toward the Pacific.

Exchange was central. Interior Salish communities traded dried salmon, hides, baskets, and roots for shell, obsidian, and other goods, and they participated in the great trade fairs at Kettle Falls, Spokane Falls, and Wy'am. They intermarried widely with neighbors, so that kinship ties spread across what scholars now treat as linguistic boundaries. Their political life depended on consensus and reputation, and leaders were chosen for ability and trustworthiness. These are not marginal features of a "Coast Salish" world. They define a distinct interior Salish tradition.

Section 31.12

Sahaptian Worlds

Sahaptian languages are spoken across the southern and central Plateau. The family has two branches that are related but not mutually intelligible: Sahaptin, which includes several dialects, and Nez Perce (Nimiipuutímt). Some linguists link Sahaptian to other Plateau languages in a proposed Plateau Penutian grouping, a hypothesis not universally accepted. Whatever the deeper relationships, Sahaptian speakers organized themselves into many politically independent communities, and the family label tells historians little about political unity.

The Nimiipuu (Nez Perce) homeland included the Clearwater, Snake, and Salmon river drainages in what is now Idaho, Washington, and Oregon, including the Wallowa Valley and the canyon country of the Snake. The name "Nez Perce" is a French exonym that has long been used in English; the people call themselves Nimiipuu or Niimíipuu, usually translated "the people." They lived in autonomous villages and bands linked by kinship and by a shared language, took salmon and steelhead from the rivers, and dug camas on prairies such as Weippe. Horses reached the Nimiipuu by the early eighteenth century at the latest, and they became renowned horse breeders, a development that supported travel to the Plains for bison hunts but did not displace the older river and root economy.

The Sahaptin-speaking peoples include the Yakama of the Yakima River and adjacent Columbia country, the Umatilla and Walla Walla of the lower Snake and Columbia region, the Palouse (Palus) of the Palouse River and Snake River, the Wanapum of the middle Columbia, the Klickitat of the Cascade foothills, and the people of the Warm Springs and John Day river country. These communities were, again, distinct nations. Winter villages stood on rivers, and in warmer months families moved to fisheries and root grounds. The Yakama and neighboring peoples held rights to particular fishing places on the Columbia and its tributaries, and these rights, passed down through families, would be central to the treaty negotiations of 1855 treated in Chapter 72.

The ceremonial life of Sahaptian peoples emphasized relationships with the foods that sustained them. First foods ceremonies honored the return of salmon, the appearance of roots, and the ripening of berries, and in many communities the first harvest was shared before general harvesting could begin. This practice linked religious obligation, resource management, and community solidarity. Sahaptian peoples also participated in winter spirit-dance traditions and in the guardian-spirit quests discussed in Section 31.15. Later religious movements, including the Washat tradition associated with the nineteenth-century leader Smohalla, built on these earlier practices, a development treated in Chapter 54.

Politically, Sahaptian communities were autonomous but never isolated. Villages joined for fishing, trading, hunting, and defense, and leaders emerged who could speak for a village or a group of villages, though their authority was based on consensus. Intermarriage extended ties among Sahaptian peoples and with Salish, Chinookan, Cayuse, and Shoshone neighbors. The Nimiipuu, Yakama, and Umatilla all became prominent in the nineteenth century, but the system they inherited was a web of autonomous communities rather than a hierarchy of tribes.

Section 31.13

Southern Plateau and Interior Borderlands

The Plateau and Basin shaded into California, the Northwest Coast, and the northern Rockies, and the peoples living in the borderlands do not fit neatly into either of the chapter's headline language categories. Treating them explicitly corrects the impression that the Plateau consisted only of Salish and Sahaptian speakers.

The Klamath and Modoc peoples lived in the Klamath Basin of southern Oregon and northern California, a landscape of large lakes, marshes, rivers, and volcanic uplands on the border of Plateau, Basin, and California regions. Their languages are closely related, forming the Klamath-Modoc language, and are sometimes linked to a proposed Plateau Penutian grouping. Both peoples sometimes use a term, Maklaks, rendered roughly as "the people." The Klamath Basin was extraordinarily productive: the seeds of the yellow pond lily (wocas) were harvested in great quantity from canoes in the marshes, and the lake suckers known by Klamath names, such as c'waam and koptu, spawned in rivers and were caught in large numbers. Klamath and Modoc communities traveled widely, traded with Columbia River peoples at The Dalles and with Northern Paiute and California neighbors, and have been documented as participants in regional captive trading, a subject treated in Chapter 53. Their later history includes the Modoc War of 1872–1873 (Chapter 72).

The Cayuse lived in the Blue Mountains and the Walla Walla and Umatilla river country. Their language, Cayuse, is now extinct as a first language and is sometimes classified with the neighboring Molala language as Waiilatpuan, a grouping that scholars debate. The Cayuse became known as horse breeders and traders, and their country linked the Columbia with the Snake and the Plains. Their history is intertwined with that of their Sahaptian neighbors, with whom they intermarried extensively, and many Cayuse people came to speak Nez Perce.

The Ktunaxa (Kootenai, Kutenai) of the upper Columbia and Kootenay river country, in what is now British Columbia, Idaho, and Montana, speak a language isolate with no demonstrated relatives. Some Ktunaxa communities lived primarily by fishing and hunting in mountain valleys and lakes, while others crossed the Rockies to hunt bison on the Plains. Their language and their long association with Salish neighbors illustrate how political and economic relationships could bridge profound linguistic differences.

Along the Columbia itself, the Upper Chinookan peoples, including the Wasco and Wishram speakers of Kiksht, lived around The Dalles and Celilo Falls and controlled one of the continent's most important fishing and trade centers. Their position gave them, and their neighbors, access to salmon in extraordinary numbers, and Wy'am, the great falls, became a gathering place where peoples from the Plateau, the Basin, the Plains, and the Pacific coast met to trade and fish. Celilo Falls was submerged in 1957 by the construction of The Dalles Dam, an event treated in Chapters 120 and 135 as part of the long struggle over Columbia River fisheries. This chapter's emphasis is on what Wy'am was: a hub that linked the Plateau to the Coast and made the Columbia a continental artery.

These borderland peoples show that the Plateau was a region of overlapping communities, many languages, and intricate relationships, not a bounded cultural container.

Section 31.14

Salmon, Camas, Roots, and the Plateau Seasonal Round

Salmon were the foundation of Plateau economies where rivers allowed them to run. Chinook, sockeye, coho, and steelhead returned from the Pacific at different times of year, and communities positioned themselves at falls, rapids, and weirs to catch them. Fishers used dip nets on platforms over fast water, spears, gillnets, traps, and weirs built across streams. The fish were split, dried on racks, and sometimes pounded into a nutritious powder and packed into baskets lined with fish skin for storage and trade. A single successful run could supply thousands of pounds of food, which made fisheries the economic anchor for villages, ceremonies, and exchange. Communities that controlled outstanding fishing places, such as Kettle Falls, Spokane Falls, and Wy'am, held places of considerable economic and political importance, and rights to fish at particular stations were inherited within families and managed by community consensus.

Roots were the second pillar. Camas (Camassia quamash) was a prized root, gathered in spring and early summer on wet prairies such as Weippe Prairie, Camas Prairie, and meadows in the Palouse and Walla Walla country. Women dug the bulbs with digging sticks, cooked them in large earth ovens lined with heated stones and covered with vegetation for a day or more, and dried them for storage or trade. Other roots, including biscuitroot (kouse, Lomatium), bitterroot, and wild onion, were dug in sequence from lower to higher elevations as the season advanced. Berries, especially huckleberries in mountain fields, and the inner bark of certain trees supplemented the diet, and game, including deer, elk, mountain sheep, and, for some peoples, bison, supplied meat and hides.

The seasonal round integrated these resources. Winter was spent in villages with stored salmon, roots, and other foods, and with ceremony, storytelling, and craft production. As the thaw approached, families moved to early root grounds, then to the major fishing stations as runs began, then to mountain berry fields and hunting grounds in late summer and fall, returning to winter villages by early winter. Large gatherings occurred at productive places, where fishing, trading, gambling, racing, and marriage-making went on together. The ceremonial calendar followed the round, with first foods rituals and thanksgivings tied to the arrival of salmon, roots, and berries.

These economies were managed. Communities regulated who could fish at particular places and when, allowed the first run of salmon to pass upstream before general harvest in some communities, cultivated root grounds through selective digging and, in places, burning, and observed rules that spread harvesting across the season. Such arrangements reflect environmental knowledge accumulated over centuries and prefigure the treaty-fishing and stewardship questions treated in Chapter 135. In the first years of written documentation, however, outsiders often noted the abundance of these resources more than the systems that maintained it.

Primary Source Window

The Lewis and Clark Journals at Weippe Prairie, September 1805

Creators: Meriwether Lewis and William Clark and other members of the Corps of Discovery · Date: September 1805 · Type: Journal entries written by Euro-American expedition members, later edited and published

Context: After a difficult crossing of the Bitterroot Mountains, in which the party was short of food, the Lewis and Clark expedition descended in late September 1805 to Weippe Prairie, in Nimiipuu (Nez Perce) country in what is now Idaho. The prairie was a camas-digging ground where Nimiipuu families were gathered. Clark, who reached the prairie first with an advance party, and then the rest of the expedition, were met by Nimiipuu people who gave them food and information.

Brief paraphrase of the journals: The journals record that the Nimiipuu gave the hungry travelers dried salmon, camas roots, and berries; that the expedition's members, unaccustomed to a diet heavy in roots and fish after weeks of scarcity, became ill with digestive complaints; that a Nimiipuu leader whom the expedition called Twisted Hair provided geographic information about the rivers ahead; and that the Nimiipuu later cared for the expedition's horses while the party built canoes on the Clearwater River and traveled downstream. (This is a paraphrase and not a quotation of the journals' wording.)

Analysis: The source documents a moment in which the survival of the expedition depended on Indigenous hospitality, foodways, and geographic knowledge. It shows that a Plateau community could provide substantial supplies from stored and gathered foods, that Nimiipuu leaders could share strategic information, and that diplomatic relations with outsiders were conducted on Indigenous terms and in Indigenous space.

Limitations: The journals were written by outsiders who viewed their encounter primarily in terms of the expedition's goals. They do not provide Nimiipuu perspectives on the encounter, motives for hospitality, or understandings of obligation and reciprocity. Communication relied on sign language and a chain of translators, which introduced possible error. Perspectives not represented include those of the Nimiipuu leaders and families who hosted the expedition, the women who dug and cooked the camas, and the neighboring nations whose relationships shaped the encounter. Section 31.15

Villages, Kinship, Leadership, Ceremony, and Social Life

Plateau village life centered on winter communities along rivers, with houses that ranged from semisubterranean pithouses to large mat-covered lodges that sheltered several related families. A winter village might include a few households or many, depending on the place and period, and it was generally the home of people related by blood and marriage. Villages were autonomous but linked to neighbors by kinship, by shared use of resources, and by the obligations of hospitality. Many communities also maintained sweat lodges, which served for purification, healing, and social gathering.

Kinship organized social life. Households were usually extended families, with descent traced through both parents in many communities, so that individuals had rights in more than one family. Marriage was an important political act, forging alliances between families and villages, and it commonly crossed linguistic and ethnic lines. Women controlled food processing, storage, and root grounds in many communities, and they held ceremonial roles; men fished, hunted, and led most trading and diplomatic expeditions. Children learned by participation, taking on tasks appropriate to age, and elders were the principal repositories of knowledge about history, geography, ceremony, and resource management.

Leadership was based on reputation, ability, and consensus. A village or a group of related villages might recognize a headman or several leaders with expertise in fishing, hunting, diplomacy, or war, and others with spiritual authority. Leaders were expected to be generous, and prestige often depended on giving rather than accumulating. Councils of respected adults deliberated over important decisions, and people who disagreed could often move to another village where they had kin. This flexibility limited the concentration of power but also made collective action possible when needed, especially for fisheries, trade fairs, and defense.

Ceremony tied the community to the land and its resources. Many Plateau peoples practiced guardian-spirit traditions, in which young people, often after a period of fasting and isolation in the mountains, sought a relationship with a spirit helper who would give them power and guidance. Winter spirit dances brought people together to sing and to honor those relationships. First foods ceremonies, as noted, marked the start of fishing and gathering seasons. Because sacred knowledge is held by communities and carries protocols about who may share it, students should read ethnographic descriptions as partial accounts and respect community decisions about what is appropriate to publish.

Cause and Effect

The Rise of Village-and-Fishery Systems on the Plateau

Long-term causes

After the end of the last Ice Age, river systems stabilized and anadromous fish runs developed on the Columbia and its tributaries, creating predictable, storable resources. Long familiarity with fishing places, roots, and game provided the knowledge base for later intensification.

Short-term causes

Archaeologists point to developments over the last few thousand years, including improvements in storage technology, root-processing, and fishing gear, population growth, and increased investment in particular places. The exact sequence varies from place to place and remains under study.

Immediate context

No single event triggered the change. Scholars see a gradual process, with fishing, root processing, and storage reinforcing one another as communities committed more labor and ceremony to productive places.

Effects

Larger and more permanent winter villages, inherited rights to fishing stations, expanded trade at falls and fairs, richer ceremonial calendars, and social differences in prestige and wealth, though less extreme than on the Northwest Coast.

Historical complexity: Abundance alone did not produce villages. Communities needed the technology to store food, the social arrangements to allocate access, and the knowledge to manage places over time. Climate variation, disease, warfare, and the movements of neighbors also shaped the pattern. Scholars continue to debate the pace and causes of intensification, and regional sequences do not all match.

Section 31.16

Trade, Diplomacy, Conflict, and the Interior West on the Eve of Colonization

The interior West functioned as a regional system of exchange, diplomacy, and conflict long before sustained colonization. Exchange corridors followed rivers and mountain passes. Obsidian from sources in Oregon, Idaho, and California moved hundreds of miles, and geochemical sourcing of obsidian artifacts allows archaeologists to trace these movements. Shell, including dentalium from the Pacific coast and Olivella beads from California, traveled inland, as did copper and, later, glass beads. Dried salmon, camas, baskets, hides, horses, and foods flowed in return. Marine shells from the coast and bison products from the Plains met in the interior, and the great fairs at Wy'am and Kettle Falls were places where these networks converged.

Trade was entwined with diplomacy and kinship. Exchange partners were often relatives or ceremonial friends, and gifts established obligations that reached beyond the immediate transaction. Intermarriage tied communities across language lines: Nimiipuu and Shoshone families intermarried, as did Salish and Kootenai families, and Sahaptian, Cayuse, and Chinookan families. Peoples shared fishing places, hunting grounds, and berry fields by agreement, and disputes were negotiated by leaders and elders. Interpreters and traders who knew multiple languages played important roles.

Conflict also occurred. Competition for resources, revenge for killings, raiding for goods and captives, and, after horses, mounted warfare led to violence among interior peoples and between them and Plains nations. Captive taking and trade in captives, which occurred in some parts of the interior and especially around The Dalles and the Klamath Basin, are treated in Chapter 53. Conflict between Shoshone and Blackfoot or between Nimiipuu and Plains peoples sometimes ended in alliance and sometimes in long-lasting hostility. Historians should avoid both a picture of constant peace and a picture of perpetual war. The evidence indicates a mixture of negotiated cooperation and episodic violence, shaped by changing conditions.

The horse and introduced diseases altered this system in the eighteenth century, and Chapters 40 and 49 treat those developments more fully. Mounted peoples gained speed, range, and military power, but they also faced new pressures, including competition for pastures and the hazards of travel to the Plains. Epidemics very likely moved through interior trade networks before Europeans arrived in person, as they did elsewhere on the continent, although documentation is thin and demographic estimates are uncertain. The fur trade, beginning with posts such as Spokane House and Fort Nez Percés in the early nineteenth century, brought new goods and new opportunities that Indigenous communities used strategically, as Chapter 46 explains. By then, the interior West was a densely connected set of Indigenous societies with their own histories, relationships, and political traditions.

Multiple Perspectives

What the Seasonal Round Meant: Four Ways of Seeing the Same Pattern

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns documented in ethnography, archaeology, and written sources. They are not quotations from any named individual, and the groups did not hold equal power to define the record.

From the perspective of a Great Basin family: The round was a calendar of obligations and knowledge. Where to be in autumn depended on the piñon crop, on relatives who might host or be hosted, and on stored food. Movement was how the family stayed fed and connected.
From the perspective of a Plateau winter-village household: The round converged on a dependable return: the village, with its stores, its houses, and its ceremonies. Fishing places and root grounds were inherited responsibilities, and the community's standing depended on managing them well and hosting visitors generously.
From the perspective of a nineteenth-century Euro-American traveler or settler: Outsiders often read mobility as aimlessness and root-digging as poverty, and they used terms such as "Digger" that denied Indigenous governance and land tenure. These writers had interests in acquiring land and often wrote from a position of power, though some recorded valuable observations.
From the perspective of a modern tribal cultural-resources office: The round is a living knowledge system recorded in place names, stories, harvesting practices, and ancestral sites, relevant to current land management, water, and fisheries, and to the interpretation of archaeological evidence.
Continuity and Change Over Time

The Interior West from Antiquity to the Eve of Colonization

What Changed

  • Lakes and marshes expanded and contracted, and communities adjusted subsistence and settlement accordingly
  • Fishing, storage, and villages intensified on the Plateau over several thousand years
  • Farming-and-foraging Fremont communities flourished and then disappeared from the archaeological record
  • Numic languages spread across much of the Basin, with timing and mechanism debated
  • Horses, from the late seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, changed mobility and warfare for some peoples

What Stayed the Same

  • Seasonal movement and detailed environmental knowledge remained central to interior life
  • Kinship and marriage continued to structure access to resources and to link communities
  • First foods and related ceremonies connected people to salmon, roots, and berries
  • Autonomous communities with consensus-based leadership persisted as a basic political form
  • Exchange corridors along rivers and passes endured as arteries of communication

Why the pattern matters: Change was gradual in some respects and abrupt in others, and different communities experienced it differently. Mounted Shoshone and Ute bands experienced greater transformation than Western Shoshone families whose economy changed more slowly, while Plateau fishing villages maintained continuity of place over very long periods. Recognizing both patterns avoids treating the interior either as timeless or as endlessly transformed.

Section 31.17

Chapter Review and Historical Thinking Checkpoint

This chapter has described the Great Basin and Plateau as Indigenous worlds with distinct environments, economies, languages, and political traditions, and it has stressed three analytical commitments. First, regions are tools, and the real units of history are peoples and communities. Second, linguistic families are not political nations: Numic, Salishan, and Sahaptian speakers lived in many autonomous communities. Third, evidence in this region is uneven, and historians must distinguish archaeological patterns, linguistic reconstructions, ethnographic accounts, oral traditions, and early written descriptions.

The core insights are these. Great Basin mobility was a deliberate strategy built on environmental knowledge and social relationships. Great Basin political life was flexible and kin-based, not an absence of government. Plateau societies were river-centered and often village-based, with fisheries and root grounds as the basis of economy, ceremony, and exchange. The interior was connected to the Plains, the Southwest, California, the Northwest Coast, and the north by corridors along which goods, people, and ideas moved. Treating these societies as marginal or simple reflects the biases of outside observers, not the historical record.

Students should now be prepared to read the following chapters on California (Chapter 32) and the Northwest Coast (Chapter 33) with an understanding of how the interior connected to them, and to carry the interior's story forward into the chapters on the Plains, the fur trade, and the conflicts of the nineteenth century.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that the Great Basin and Plateau were densely interconnected Indigenous worlds, not empty spaces between other regions. The Great Basin, defined by interior drainage, mountain ranges, and dispersed water, supported peoples whose seasonal rounds, communal drives, and storage systems made intensive use of piñon, seeds, roots, game, and wetland resources. The Plateau, organized around the Columbia and Fraser systems, supported river-centered communities whose fisheries, root grounds, and winter villages sustained larger, more settled populations.

The chapter has stressed that these regions were not culturally uniform. The Great Basin included Shoshone, Northern and Southern Paiute, Ute, Goshute, and Washoe communities with distinct economies and histories, and the Plateau included Interior Salish, Sahaptian, Klamath-Modoc, Cayuse, Ktunaxa, and Upper Chinookan peoples. Language families helped organize this diversity but did not describe political units. The Numic languages, for example, are closely related but belong to peoples who were never a single nation.

The deep history of the interior is documented by archaeology from roughly eleven to twelve thousand years ago, with some earlier claims still debated, and with cave sites in the Basin and river sites on the Plateau preserving rich evidence. The relationship between ancient archaeological traditions and later named peoples is a matter of inference, and cases such as the Ancient One and Spirit Cave show that those inferences have present-day ethical and legal significance, which Chapter 125 examines.

The debate over the Numic expansion illustrates how historians work with incomplete evidence. Linguistic similarity suggests recent spread, archaeological patterns suggest change after about 1000 CE, and genetic and cultural continuity complicate any account of simple replacement. This textbook treats the expansion as a debated model and not as settled fact, consistent with its approach to other migration questions.

Great Basin political life was based on kinship, reciprocal access to territory, and leadership tied to particular tasks and seasons. Older descriptions of Basin peoples as lacking political institutions reflected outsiders' assumptions and sometimes served the interests of those who sought their land. Plateau communities, with inherited fishing places, village-based leadership, and consensus decision-making, developed different but comparable political traditions, shaped by the particular resources they managed.

Salmon, camas, and other roots formed the foundation of Plateau economies, and the seasonal round integrated them into an annual cycle of movement, harvest, storage, and ceremony. First foods ceremonies and other practices linked ritual and resource management, and the great fisheries at Kettle Falls, Spokane Falls, and Wy'am were also centers of trade and diplomacy. The Primary Source Window on Weippe Prairie in 1805 showed how outsiders' journals document this system while leaving Indigenous perspectives largely unrecorded.

Exchange, intermarriage, diplomacy, and conflict tied the interior to neighboring regions. Obsidian, shell, hides, dried foods, and, later, horses moved along river and mountain corridors, and horses and introduced diseases altered the interior system in the eighteenth century. Warfare and captive trade occurred, but so did long-lasting alliances. The picture that emerges is of a regional system with its own dynamics, in which Indigenous peoples made strategic choices about movement, trade, and alliance.

Finally, the chapter has emphasized continuity and change. Change occurred in lake levels, fisheries, languages, and technologies, and horses and diseases brought new pressures, but kinship, seasonal knowledge, first foods ceremonies, and autonomous community life endured. These continuities are the foundation for the interior nations' later resistance, treaty making, and modern struggles over land, water, and fisheries, treated in Chapters 72, 78, 120, and 135.

Key Terms

Great Basin
A region of interior drainage in western North America, in which rivers end in lakes, marshes, or sinks rather than reaching the sea, and which was home to Numic, Washoe, and other peoples.
Plateau
A scholarly culture-area label for the Columbia and Fraser river interior, home to Interior Salish, Sahaptian, and other peoples with river-centered economies.
Interior drainage
A hydrological condition in which water flows to internal lakes and sinks instead of the ocean.
Seasonal round
The annual pattern of movement among resource areas and winter residences, based on detailed environmental knowledge and social agreements about access.
Numic
A branch of the Uto-Aztecan language family that includes Shoshone, Northern and Southern Paiute, Ute, Comanche, and other languages.
Language isolate
A language with no demonstrated relationship to any other, such as Washoe (with a contested possible Hokan link) and Ktunaxa.
Sahaptian
A language family of the southern Plateau comprising Sahaptin dialects and Nez Perce, related but not mutually intelligible.
Interior Salish
A division of the Salishan language family spoken by peoples of the Plateau, distinct from the Coast Salish languages of the Northwest Coast.
Numic expansion
The debated model that Numic languages spread across the Great Basin from a southwestern source, beginning roughly a thousand years ago.
Fremont
An archaeological label for farming-and-foraging communities of the eastern Great Basin, c. 400–1300 CE, whose relationship to later peoples is debated.
Piñon
A pine whose nuts were a major autumn food of many Great Basin peoples, with crops varying unpredictably from range to range.
Camas
A bulb-forming plant whose roots, cooked in earth ovens, were a staple of Plateau economies.
Communal drive
A coordinated hunt in which large groups herded rabbits or pronghorn into nets or corrals, requiring temporary leadership and shared labor.
Weir
A barrier placed across a stream to channel fish into traps or to places where they can be caught.
Pithouse
A semisubterranean dwelling used as a winter house by many Plateau peoples.
Winter village
A relatively permanent river settlement where a Plateau community lived on stored foods through the winter.
Reciprocal access
The arrangement by which communities allowed one another to harvest in their territories, usually through kinship or negotiated obligation.
First foods ceremony
A ritual honoring the first harvest of salmon, roots, or berries, linking religious obligation and resource management.
Guardian spirit
A spiritual helper sought by many Plateau individuals through fasting and solitude, giving personal power and guidance.

A route along rivers or passes along which goods, people, and ideas moved between regions.

A major fishery and trade center on the Columbia River, submerged by The Dalles Dam in 1957.

The convention of describing a society as it was observed by ethnographers, which may not reflect earlier conditions.

A proposed grouping of Sahaptian, Klamath-Modoc, and some other Plateau languages whose validity is debated.
"Digger"
A derogatory outsider term applied to Great Basin and other western peoples, reflecting contempt for root-digging economies and denial of Indigenous governance.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it corrects a long-standing habit of treating the interior West as a pass-through. The Great Basin and Plateau were homes to peoples who developed some of the continent's most sophisticated knowledge systems for living with scarcity and abundance, and who built networks that linked a continent. Understanding them on their own terms changes the questions historians ask, shifting attention from what these regions lacked to what their peoples knew, managed, and exchanged.

The chapter also reveals larger processes that recur across this textbook: the difference between linguistic classification and political identity, the interplay of environment and social strategy without environmental determinism, the interaction of multiple kinds of evidence, and the importance of treating oral tradition and scientific reconstruction as partners rather than rivals. These lessons will shape the study of California in Chapter 32 and the Northwest Coast in Chapter 33, whose peoples were connected to the interior through the corridors this chapter has traced.

The continuities described here, in kinship, seasonal knowledge, first foods, and community autonomy, survived the pressures of the nineteenth century, and they inform the interior nations' modern assertions of treaty rights, water rights, and cultural authority. The later chapters on Plateau resistance, the Nez Perce War, treaty fisheries, and water sovereignty draw directly on the world described here, and they cannot be understood without it.

Finally, the interior West connects to the book's continental argument. Indigenous North America was not a patchwork of isolated cultures but an interconnected system of regions, linked by rivers, passes, trade, marriage, and diplomacy. By the time Chapter 36 synthesizes that system, students will have seen the interior as one of its most important parts.
