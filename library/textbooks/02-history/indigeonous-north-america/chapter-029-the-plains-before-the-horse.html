The Plains Before the Horse

Indigenous Peoples of North America: A Continental History

Chapter Opening

Long before a mounted warrior on a fast horse became the most familiar image of Indigenous North America, communities along the Missouri River and its tributaries were raising maize, beans, squash, and sunflowers in fortified riverside towns of a hundred or more earthlodges, while other Plains peoples, traveling on foot with dogs as their only draft animals, organized disciplined communal hunts that could bring down dozens of bison at once. The horse would later transform this world dramatically. It did not create it.

Ask most students, and many adults, to picture "the Plains Indians," and a single image usually appears: a mounted warrior in feathered regalia, chasing a herd of bison across an open grassland, living in a portable tipi, moving constantly with no fixed home. That image is not simply incomplete. It describes a historical world that did not yet exist for most of Plains history, a world made possible only after horses, reintroduced to North America by Spanish colonization, spread northward across the continent beginning in the seventeenth century and reorganized Plains life during the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. The full story of that transformation belongs to Chapter 49. This chapter asks a different and, in some ways, more fundamental question: what did Plains Indigenous life actually look like for the many centuries before horses arrived?

The answer surprises many students. Along the Missouri River in present-day North and South Dakota, and along other major river systems stretching from the Canadian prairies to central Texas, Indigenous peoples built substantial, long-lived farming communities. Some of these villages housed several hundred to well over a thousand people behind ditches, palisades, and bastioned fortifications. Their residents cultivated gardens of corn, beans, squash, and sunflowers using digging sticks and bone hoes, stored enormous quantities of dried food in deep earthen pits, and built large, earth-covered log houses that could shelter extended families and their belongings through brutal northern winters. These were not isolated outposts. They were connected by trails and waterways to other villages, to mobile hunting peoples who shared the grasslands with them, and, through chains of exchange, to trade networks reaching toward the Rocky Mountains, the Gulf Coast, the Great Lakes, and the Southwest.

At the same time, bison already stood at the center of Plains economic and spiritual life long before any Plains person rode a horse. Pedestrian hunters, working in coordinated groups, used knowledge of animal behavior, landscape, and weather to drive bison into natural traps, over cliffs, into human-made pounds, or into deep snow, where mounted pursuit was unnecessary and, in many terrains, would have been impossible even had horses been available. Village farmers themselves often undertook organized bison hunts during the summer and fall, combining agriculture and hunting within a single annual cycle rather than choosing between them. Meanwhile, mobile hunting peoples who depended more heavily on bison traveled extensively on foot, using dogs to haul belongings on wooden frames called travois, and maintained their own networks of trade, alliance, and seasonal rendezvous with farming villages.

This chapter asks how historians and archaeologists reconstruct this earlier Plains world, given that written documentation from Europeans and their descendants arrived on the Plains much later than it did along the Atlantic coast, and given that oral tradition, archaeological evidence, and later ethnographic observation must be combined carefully, without allowing any one source to stand in for what the others cannot show. It asks how village farming, communal bison hunting, river-based settlement, and long-distance trade fit together into functioning regional economies, and how those economies connected Plains peoples to one another and to the wider continent centuries before any Spanish horse set foot on the grasslands.

This is also a chapter about the discipline of historical caution. It would be easy, and inaccurate, to take the peoples documented by European and American observers in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, after horses, epidemic disease, and new trade networks had already reorganized Plains life, and simply project their territories, political alliances, and ways of life backward onto the fifteenth century. This chapter resists that temptation at every turn. It asks what the archaeological record can establish on its own terms, where later historical and oral evidence can responsibly extend that record, and where historians must be honest about the limits of what can be known.

By the chapter's end, students should understand the Plains not as an empty grassland occupied by small bands of wandering hunters, but as a varied, populous, and economically interconnected Indigenous world, a world whose farmers, hunters, traders, and diplomats had already built sophisticated regional systems of settlement and exchange. Understanding that world on its own terms is the necessary foundation for understanding, later in this textbook, just how much the horse would eventually change, and just how much it would leave in place.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why the equestrian, nomadic image of "the Plains Indians" misrepresents most of the history of Indigenous peoples on the Plains.
  2. Describe the environmental diversity of the Plains, including the role of rivers in concentrating timber, water, soil, and settlement within a predominantly grassland region.
  3. Distinguish archaeological traditions, such as the Plains Village tradition, from historically documented nations, and explain the interpretive care required to connect the two.
  4. Analyze the Upper Missouri village world as a case study in pre-equestrian agricultural settlement, fortification, and bison hunting.
  5. Trace the historical relationships among Mandan, Hidatsa, and Arikara communities without projecting their later political alliance backward onto earlier centuries.
  6. Compare village farming societies of the Upper Missouri with Central and Southern Plains village traditions, including Caddoan-speaking communities.
  7. Explain how pedestrian communal bison hunting worked, and why mounted hunting was not necessary for bison to be economically and culturally central.
  8. Describe earthlodge construction, household organization, garden agriculture, and food storage in Plains village communities.
  9. Assess pre-equestrian mobility, including the roles of dogs, travois, and river travel, and explain why the absence of horses did not mean the absence of movement.
  10. Evaluate the Plains as a zone of regional commerce connecting farming villages, mobile hunting peoples, and distant trade networks.
  11. Analyze evidence for local political leadership, diplomacy, and fortification without assuming a single uniform Plains political system or constant warfare.
  12. Synthesize the pre-equestrian Plains world as the historical baseline against which the later transformation produced by horses can be measured.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did Indigenous peoples build interconnected farming, hunting, trading, and river-based societies on the Plains before horses transformed mobility, warfare, hunting, and regional power?
  2. What evidence allows historians to distinguish a fifteenth-century archaeological village from a nineteenth-century historically documented nation, and what are the risks of collapsing that distinction?
  3. Why did substantial agricultural settlement concentrate along river valleys within a landscape popularly imagined as uniform grassland?
  4. In what ways were farming and bison hunting complementary rather than competing ways of life on the pre-equestrian Plains?
  5. How did communities without horses achieve effective seasonal mobility, communal hunting, and long-distance trade?
  6. What does the presence of substantial fortifications at village sites reveal, and what should historians be cautious about concluding from that evidence alone?
  7. How did settled villagers and mobile hunting peoples depend upon one another economically?
  8. Why is it historically inaccurate to describe the pre-horse Plains as empty, static, or undifferentiated?
  9. What should change, and what should remain the same, in how students understand the later equestrian transformation addressed in Chapter 49, once the pre-horse Plains world described in this chapter is properly understood?

Historical Context

Chapter 28 examined how ancient societies of the Southeast became the historically documented Cherokee, Muscogee, Choctaw, Chickasaw, Catawba, and neighboring nations through long processes of continuity, migration, coalescence, and ethnogenesis following the Mississippian transformation. That chapter emphasized that historically documented nations were not simply the unchanged descendants of a single named ancestral people, but often the product of communities regrouping, merging, and reorganizing themselves across generations of upheaval, including the demographic catastrophe that followed European contact.

This chapter turns to a different region and a different set of interpretive problems. The Great Plains, stretching from the boreal forest edge of the Canadian prairie provinces southward through the central United States into Texas and northern Mexico, and from the Rocky Mountain foothills eastward to the tallgrass prairies bordering the Mississippi and Missouri watersheds, encompasses one of the largest and most ecologically varied grassland regions on Earth. Because the region's later history became closely associated with mounted bison hunting, horse-based warfare, and highly mobile equestrian societies, popular understanding of Plains history has often assumed that this equestrian pattern was simply how Plains peoples had always lived. It was not. Horses, descended from animals brought by Spanish colonizers into the Americas, did not become widely available to Plains peoples until the seventeenth century at the earliest, and in many northern and interior regions considerably later, a diffusion examined in full in Chapter 49.

Before that diffusion, the Plains supported a substantial and long-developed Indigenous world organized very differently. Along the Missouri River and its major tributaries, and along other river systems farther south and east, farming communities built permanent and semipermanent villages, cultivated maize, beans, squash, and sunflowers, and combined agriculture with seasonal bison hunting. Other Plains peoples relied more heavily on bison hunting and plant gathering, moving seasonally across the grasslands on foot, their possessions carried by dogs. These two broad patterns of life, farming-and-hunting villagers and more mobile hunting-and-gathering peoples, were not separate, isolated worlds. They interacted constantly through trade, intermarriage, seasonal visiting, and sometimes conflict, forming an interconnected regional system whose full scale is only gradually becoming clear through archaeological research.

This chapter also continues a methodological conversation begun in earlier chapters of Part V. Chapter 25 asked whether archaeological sites could be responsibly assigned to later-named peoples. Chapter 26 asked how oral constitutional tradition relates to archaeological chronology. Chapter 27 examined migration traditions using archaeology and linguistics together. Chapter 28 asked how continuity and ethnogenesis produced the historically documented nations of the Southeast. This chapter asks a closely related but distinct question for the Plains: how much of the historically documented Plains world, recorded by European and American observers mostly after 1700, can be projected backward onto the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, and how much of that later world was itself the product of changes, including the horse, that had not yet occurred?

Section 29.1

The Plains Before the Horse

Few regions of Indigenous North America carry a more fixed popular image than the Great Plains. Mention "the Plains Indians," and most people, inside and outside the classroom, picture a mounted warrior, a portable tipi, a constantly moving band following bison herds across open grassland, and a way of life seemingly as old as the grass itself. That image is drawn almost entirely from the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, after horses had already transformed Plains mobility, hunting, warfare, and political power. Applied to the centuries before roughly 1600, it is substantially inaccurate.

This chapter asks students to set that later image aside and examine the Plains as they existed for the many centuries before equestrian transformation. During this earlier period, examined in detail throughout the sections that follow, the Plains supported farming communities with populations in the hundreds and, in some cases, close to two thousand; fortified settlements defended by ditches, palisades, and projecting bastions; mobile hunting peoples whose pedestrian communal hunts could take dozens of bison in a single coordinated effort; and trade networks that moved specialized stone, agricultural surplus, hides, and other goods across hundreds of miles.

None of this means that the later equestrian Plains world was unimportant or that this chapter should minimize the genuine transformation horses eventually produced. That transformation, examined fully in Chapter 49, was real, rapid in places, and historically significant. But the horse transformed an already complex, populous, and economically interconnected Indigenous world. It did not create Plains civilization from nothing, and it did not turn empty grassland into inhabited land. This chapter's task is to recover that earlier world on its own terms, so that the later transformation can be measured accurately rather than assumed.

Doing so requires combining several different kinds of evidence. Archaeology provides the primary evidence for settlement, agriculture, hunting, fortification, and trade across most of the period this chapter examines, since sustained written documentation of the Plains by outside observers did not begin until long after the period under discussion, and even then arrived unevenly across the region. Oral tradition, carefully used, can illuminate migration, settlement, and cultural practice, particularly where it can be checked against archaeological or linguistic evidence. Later ethnographic observation, recorded by outside observers from the eighteenth century onward, can sometimes illuminate earlier practices, but only with the explicit caution that centuries of change, including the horse, intervened between the period this chapter studies and the period most ethnographic observation actually describes.

Section 29.2

Grasslands, Rivers, and Regional Environments

"The Plains" is often imagined as a single, uniform environment: flat, treeless, and dry. The reality is considerably more varied, and that variation shaped where and how Indigenous peoples settled, farmed, hunted, and traded. The region commonly called the Great Plains stretches roughly from the Canadian prairie provinces in the north to central Texas and parts of northern Mexico in the south, and from the eastern foothills of the Rocky Mountains to the point, differing somewhat by latitude, where shortgrass gives way to taller, moister prairie closer to the Mississippi and Missouri watersheds.

Rainfall, elevation, and soil vary considerably across this range. The northern and central high Plains tend toward shorter grasses and drier conditions, while areas farther east and south, benefiting from greater precipitation, support taller grasses and, in many river corridors, substantial stands of timber. This environmental variation matters directly for this chapter's subject, because it explains why the most substantial pre-equestrian agricultural settlement concentrated specifically along river valleys rather than spreading evenly across the grasslands.

Rivers as Concentrated Resource Zones

Rivers running through the Plains, including the Missouri and its major tributaries such as the Knife, Heart, Cannonball, and Platte, along with more southerly systems such as the Republican, Arkansas, and Canadian Rivers, created narrow but resource-rich corridors within an otherwise more sparsely wooded grassland. River valleys provided water for drinking and irrigation-adjacent gardening, fertile alluvial soils suited to maize, bean, and squash cultivation, timber for construction and fuel in an environment where wood was otherwise scarce, fish and other aquatic resources, and sheltered terrain that moderated the Plains' often extreme temperature swings. These combined advantages made river valleys the preferred locations for permanent and semipermanent village settlement across the Plains, even as bison hunting carried communities well beyond the rivers themselves during particular seasons.

Regional Zones Within the Plains

Scholars commonly distinguish a Northern Plains zone, encompassing the Canadian prairies and the northern United States, where the growing season was shorter and agriculture more constrained; a Central Plains zone, encompassing much of present-day Nebraska, Kansas, and neighboring areas, with a longer growing season supporting more extensive village agriculture; and a Southern Plains zone, extending into Texas and adjacent areas, with its own distinct river systems, climate, and historical trajectory, including increasing interaction with Southwestern and northern Mexican societies examined further in Chapter 30. This chapter attends to all three zones, while recognizing that boundaries between them were never sharp and that peoples, trade goods, and influences moved across them continuously.

Section 29.3

Archaeological Traditions and Historical Peoples

Archaeologists studying the Plains have developed a vocabulary of "traditions" and "phases" to describe patterns in material culture, house form, settlement organization, and subsistence that recur across particular regions and time periods. The most important such classification for this chapter is the Plains Village tradition, a broad term describing farming and hunting communities that built substantial, often fortified, river-valley settlements across much of the Plains beginning roughly a thousand years ago and continuing, with considerable regional variation, into the era of sustained European documentation.

This terminology is useful, but it requires the same methodological caution this textbook has applied throughout Part V. An archaeological tradition describes patterns in material remains: house forms, pottery styles, tool types, settlement layout, and subsistence evidence recovered through excavation. It does not, by itself, establish what language a community spoke, how its members understood their own political identity, or which later historically documented nation, if any, descended from it in a straightforward way. Archaeologists and historians combine several additional lines of evidence, including continuity of settlement location, pottery and architectural style, oral tradition maintained by descendant communities, linguistic evidence, and, where available, early written observation, to propose connections between archaeological traditions and particular historically documented peoples.

The Central Question of This Chapter

This chapter asks a specific version of that broader methodological question: when can archaeologists and historians responsibly connect a precontact Plains village to a historically documented nation such as the Mandan, Hidatsa, Arikara, Pawnee, or Wichita? The answer, as in earlier chapters, is that the connection is often probable, sometimes strong, and rarely absolutely certain for the earliest periods. Continuity of settlement location along particular river stretches, continuity of certain house forms and pottery styles, and the descendant communities' own oral traditions of long occupation together support real historical continuity in many cases. At the same time, village populations relocated, merged, and separated repeatedly over the centuries this chapter covers, meaning that any specific archaeological site cannot automatically be assumed to represent the direct, unchanged ancestors of a single later-named nation.

This caution matters because later written sources, produced mostly by European and American traders, explorers, and officials from the eighteenth century onward, describe Plains peoples using names, territories, and political relationships that had already been shaped by more than a century of epidemic disease, population movement, and, increasingly, horse-based reorganization. Reading those later names and territories backward onto fifteenth-century villages risks obscuring exactly the kind of population movement and reorganization that archaeology reveals.

Section 29.4

The Upper Missouri Village World

The best-studied and most extensively documented pre-equestrian Plains village world developed along the Missouri River and its tributaries in present-day North and South Dakota. Beginning several centuries before sustained European contact, farming communities in this region built substantial, often fortified settlements on terraces above the river, combining intensive garden agriculture with seasonal bison hunting in a single, integrated economy.

These were not small hamlets. Village populations in the Upper Missouri region could number in the hundreds, and some of the larger and later fortified villages, including Huff Village discussed in this chapter's Primary Source Window, are estimated to have housed roughly a thousand or more residents at their peak, with some later Upper Missouri villages estimated at close to two thousand residents, organized into extended-family households occupying substantial earthlodges arranged around open plazas. Villages were typically situated on defensible terraces overlooking the river, with access to garden land in the fertile floodplain below, to timber along the riverbank, and to water for daily use.

An Integrated Farming and Hunting Economy

The archaeological evidence from Upper Missouri villages consistently shows a combined economy rather than a purely agricultural or purely hunting way of life. Garden plots produced maize, beans, squash, and sunflowers in quantities sufficient not only for daily consumption but for storage against winter shortages and, as later sections of this chapter discuss, for trade; later Hidatsa ethnohistorical accounts describe women as the primary garden caretakers, a pattern discussed cautiously in Section 29.8. At the same time, bison remains recovered from village sites demonstrate that hunting, often organized as seasonal expeditions away from the village itself, provided essential protein, hides, and other materials. Huff Village's archaeological record, for example, shows substantial evidence of both farming and bison hunting integrated within a single community's economy, underscoring that these were complementary rather than competing subsistence strategies.

This combined economy allowed Upper Missouri villages to support denser, more stable populations than pure hunting economies typically could, while remaining flexible enough to respond to seasonal and year-to-year variation in both crop yields and bison availability. It also created the economic surplus, discussed further in Section 29.10, that allowed villages to function as trading centers for a broader regional network.

Fortification and Collective Labor

Many Upper Missouri villages, particularly from roughly the fourteenth century onward, were substantially fortified, with ditches, log palisades, and projecting bastions positioned to allow defenders to fire along the length of the palisade wall. These fortifications represented a major investment of collective labor and suggest the existence of community institutions capable of coordinating a workforce for defense, a subject examined further in Section 29.11. The scale of some fortification systems, including the more than two-thousand-foot ditch, palisade, and ten bastions documented at Huff Village, indicates that defense was a serious and sustained concern for at least some Upper Missouri communities during this period, though the chapter is careful, as Section 29.11 discusses, not to overstate what this evidence can establish about the frequency or specific sources of conflict.

Section 29.5

Mandan, Hidatsa, Arikara, and Changing River Communities

The archaeological village world described in Section 29.4 is understood by historians and archaeologists to be ancestral, in substantial part, to the historically documented Mandan, Hidatsa, and Arikara peoples of the Upper Missouri. Establishing this connection responsibly, however, requires the same careful handling of continuity and change that this textbook has applied throughout Part V.

Mandan villages are documented archaeologically along the Missouri River by at least the fifteenth century, built on a tradition of settlement, earthlodge construction, and combined farming and hunting that, in its broad outlines, extended back considerably further. Hidatsa communities have a somewhat different documented history, with oral tradition and archaeological evidence together suggesting migration toward the Missouri River region and settlement among or near Mandan villages over a period extending through subsequent centuries. Arikara communities, speaking a Caddoan language related to Pawnee and Wichita rather than the Siouan languages of Mandan and Hidatsa, moved into the Upper Missouri region from further south over a period that current scholarship continues to refine, bringing with them related but distinct village traditions rooted in the Central Plains world discussed in Section 29.6.

Interaction, Exchange, and Intermarriage

By the time sustained written documentation of the Upper Missouri begins, Mandan, Hidatsa, and Arikara communities were living in close proximity along the river, engaged in extensive trade, intermarriage, and cultural exchange, while maintaining distinct languages, village identities, and, in many periods, distinct and sometimes rival political interests. That later, well-documented configuration developed gradually: Mandan villages were established along the Missouri by roughly 1450, while Hidatsa communities moved westward toward the river in stages across the following century and beyond, and Arikara communities arrived from the Central Plains on their own, separate timeline. Their ancestral communities occupied interconnected Plains and Missouri River worlds and likely interacted at different times during this period of movement and settlement, but the close-proximity, three-nation relationship documented in later sources should not be projected unchanged into the fifteenth century. This pattern of proximity combined with distinctness is important, because it resists two opposite oversimplifications: treating the three peoples as a single undifferentiated "Missouri River culture," and treating them as entirely separate societies with no meaningful interaction before European documentation began.

A Caution About Later Political Unity

In the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, following catastrophic population loss from epidemic disease and the pressures of United States territorial expansion, examined in later volumes of this textbook, Mandan, Hidatsa, and Arikara communities formed a closer political alliance that eventually became formalized as the Three Affiliated Tribes, governed today by the Mandan, Hidatsa, and Arikara Nation. That political alliance is a specific historical development belonging to a much later period than this chapter covers, and this chapter is careful not to project it backward onto the fifteenth or sixteenth centuries. During that earlier period, their ancestral communities occupied interconnected Plains worlds and interacted at different times, while the later close-proximity relationship among all three peoples developed gradually; the three communities maintained separate villages, separate leadership, and, at various points, separate and sometimes competing interests rather than a unified political structure.

Section 29.6

Central and Southern Plains Village Worlds

The Upper Missouri was not the only region of the Plains to develop substantial pre-equestrian village agriculture. Across the Central Plains, in present-day Nebraska, Kansas, and neighboring areas, archaeological evidence documents farming communities ancestral, in important respects, to the historically documented Pawnee and related peoples. These Central Plains village traditions shared important features with the Upper Missouri world, including river-valley settlement, earthlodge or related house construction, combined farming and bison hunting, and participation in regional trade, while also developing distinct local patterns in house form, settlement layout, and material culture.

Farther south, Caddoan-speaking peoples ancestral to the historically documented Wichita and related communities developed village traditions along river systems extending into present-day Kansas, Oklahoma, and Texas. These southern Caddoan-speaking village communities, like their northern counterparts, combined agriculture with bison hunting and participated in trade networks extending both across the Plains and toward the Caddo peoples of the Southeast and the Puebloan and other societies of the Southwest, a borderlands relationship examined further where it becomes relevant to Chapter 30's treatment of the Southwest and Pueblo world.

A Shared Pattern, Not a Single Culture

Taken together, the Upper Missouri, Central Plains, and Southern Plains village traditions demonstrate that substantial agricultural settlement was a widespread feature of Plains life well before European contact, not a phenomenon confined to a single river or a single people. At the same time, students should resist concluding that these traditions were simply regional variations of one underlying "Plains farming culture." Linguistic evidence places Mandan and Hidatsa within the Siouan language family, while Arikara, Pawnee, and Wichita belong to the related but distinct Caddoan family, indicating separate, long historical trajectories even where settlement patterns and subsistence strategies looked broadly similar. Archaeological traditions should not be assigned mechanically to a single historically named people without supporting evidence of continuity in each specific case.

Section 29.7

Bison Hunting Before Horses

No single development has shaped popular misunderstanding of pre-equestrian Plains history more than the assumption that bison hunting required horses. It did not. Bison stood at the center of Plains economic, material, and spiritual life for many centuries before any Plains hunter rode a horse, and pedestrian hunting methods, developed and refined over generations, were capable of taking substantial numbers of animals in a single coordinated effort.

Communal Hunting Methods

Pedestrian communal bison hunting relied on detailed knowledge of bison behavior, landscape, and seasonal movement patterns. In regions with suitable terrain, hunters drove bison toward cliffs or steep drop-offs, a method sometimes called a bison jump, where animals plunging over the edge could be killed or disabled in large numbers at once. In other settings, hunters constructed bison pounds, enclosures built from logs, brush, or natural terrain features, into which bison were driven and then dispatched by hunters waiting at close range. Hunters also took advantage of deep snow, which could slow and trap bison, of natural bottlenecks in terrain, and of controlled burning in some regions to manage grassland and influence bison movement toward preferred hunting areas.

These methods required substantial coordination among many participants: scouts to track herd movement, individuals skilled at imitating bison or wolf behavior to help direct animals toward the trap, and a large labor force ready to process the kill quickly once it occurred. Successful communal hunts could yield dozens of animals at once, far more than individual hunting could typically achieve, and the organization required to carry out such a hunt reflects sophisticated social coordination rather than simple opportunism.

Village Hunters and Dedicated Hunting Peoples

Village farming communities such as those described in Sections 29.4 through 29.6 did not treat bison hunting as a secondary or occasional activity. Seasonal hunting expeditions, often undertaken by substantial portions of a village's population during summer and fall, after spring planting and before fall harvest and winter storage, were a regular and essential part of the annual economic cycle. At the same time, other Plains peoples relied more heavily, though rarely exclusively, on bison hunting and wild plant gathering, moving seasonally across the grasslands to follow herds and to access other resources, and maintaining their own networks of exchange with farming villages, discussed further in Section 29.10.

It is important not to treat "farmer" and "hunter" as fixed, mutually exclusive categories describing entirely separate peoples. Many Plains communities combined both activities within a single annual cycle, and the degree of emphasis on farming versus hunting varied by region, by year, and by the specific resources available in a given river valley or stretch of grassland.

Section 29.8

Earthlodges, Gardens, Households, and Stored Food

Village life on the pre-equestrian Plains centered on the earthlodge, a substantial dwelling constructed from a timber frame covered with layers of willow branches, grass, and packed earth, built around a central support structure and typically featuring a smoke hole above a central hearth and an entry passage designed to reduce wind and cold. Earthlodges were large enough to house extended families, often spanning multiple generations, along with stored food, tools, and, in some communities, space for horses once they became available in later periods. Within the village, earthlodges were typically arranged around open plazas used for ceremony, gathering, and communal activity.

Gardens and Agricultural Labor

Garden plots in the fertile floodplain soils below village terraces produced maize, beans, squash, and sunflowers using digging sticks, bone hoes, and other tools suited to intensive hand cultivation. Later ethnographic descriptions of Hidatsa agriculture, recorded in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries and drawing substantially on the detailed testimony of the Hidatsa farmer Buffalo Bird Woman, describe women as the primary caretakers of garden plots, responsible for planting, weeding, harvesting, and processing crops, with garden produce supporting both household consumption and, as Section 29.10 discusses, exchange with other communities. Archaeology securely documents intensive agriculture, garden tools, storage facilities, and hunting activity at earlier village sites, but it does not by itself identify the gender of the people who performed any particular task. The detailed evidence for women as primary garden caretakers comes chiefly from this later ethnohistorical testimony rather than from archaeological evidence alone. Historians may use that later, well-documented pattern cautiously as a possible guide to earlier practice, but students should understand that archaeology alone cannot establish the gender division of labor in fifteenth-century villages.

Storage and Food Security

Archaeological excavation of Plains Village sites consistently reveals large numbers of bell-shaped or cylindrical storage pits, commonly called cache pits, dug into the ground within or near earthlodges and capable of holding substantial quantities of dried corn, beans, squash, and other foodstuffs. These storage systems allowed villages to carry surplus food through winter months when fresh agricultural production was impossible and hunting could be unpredictable, and they also provided the stored surplus that made trade with visiting peoples possible, discussed further below.

The scale of storage capacity documented at some Upper Missouri village sites, including Huff Village, where archaeological investigation has identified hundreds of storage pits, indicates substantial, sustained agricultural productivity rather than marginal subsistence farming. This evidence directly undermines any assumption that these communities practiced only marginal, precarious subsistence agriculture, though it cannot establish that crop failures or food shortages never occurred.

Section 29.9

Dogs, Travois, Walking, and Seasonal Mobility

The absence of horses on the pre-equestrian Plains did not mean the absence of mobility. Village communities relocated periodically as local timber supplies, soil fertility, or other conditions changed, and both village farmers and more mobile hunting peoples undertook substantial seasonal travel for hunting, trade, and resource gathering.

Dogs served as the primary draft animal before horses, trained to pull a travois, a frame of two trailing poles joined near the shoulders and dragged along the ground, onto which goods, hides, and other belongings could be loaded and transported overland. While a dog-drawn travois could carry far less than a horse-drawn version would later carry, dogs nonetheless provided meaningful transport capacity, particularly when combined with human carriers, and allowed mobile groups to transport tents, tools, dried food, and other essential goods across considerable distances.

Walking as a Skilled, Sustainable Practice

Pedestrian travel across the Plains should not be imagined as slow or ineffective simply because it lacked horses. Communities developed detailed knowledge of trails, water sources, seasonal resource availability, and safe travel routes that allowed substantial distances to be covered reliably on foot. River travel by canoe or other watercraft supplemented foot travel in regions where rivers were navigable, and well-established trails connected villages, seasonal hunting grounds, and trade destinations across the broader region.

Seasonal Movement and Village Relocation

Village communities themselves were not always permanently fixed in a single location across many centuries. Archaeological evidence shows that villages were periodically abandoned and rebuilt nearby, often in response to depleted local timber supplies, changing soil fertility, shifting river channels, or defensive considerations. This pattern of periodic relocation, sometimes over a span of one or two generations, should be understood as a normal feature of village life rather than evidence of instability or failure.

Understanding pre-equestrian mobility in these terms helps students appreciate, later in this textbook, just how significant the horse's arrival would be. Horses dramatically increased the speed, distance, and carrying capacity of overland travel, with major consequences for hunting range, trade reach, and military mobility, examined fully in Chapter 49. But that later transformation built upon, rather than invented, an already functioning system of seasonal movement, trade, and settlement relocation.

Section 29.10

Trade, Exchange, and Plains Commercial Networks

Plains villages functioned not only as farming and hunting communities but as nodes within extensive regional and long-distance trade networks. Agricultural surplus, particularly dried corn, beans, and squash, could be exchanged with mobile hunting peoples for bison products, including hides, dried meat, and other materials, creating a mutually beneficial economic relationship between farming villages and hunting communities that depended on each other's specialized production.

Knife River Flint as Evidence of Exchange

Among the clearest physical indicators of long-distance Plains exchange is Knife River flint, a distinctive, high-quality toolstone quarried from major source and quarry areas in the upper Knife River basin of present-day Dunn and Mercer Counties, North Dakota, roughly fifty to sixty miles west of the Knife–Missouri village region discussed in Section 29.4. Archaeologists have recovered artifacts made from Knife River flint at sites across a very wide area of the northern and central Plains and beyond, demonstrating that this material moved through extensive networks of trade, gift exchange, or other forms of transfer well beyond its immediate source area. The documented movement of Knife River flint provides tangible, material evidence for trade networks that oral tradition and later ethnographic observation also describe in less physically verifiable terms.

Villages as Trading Centers

Upper Missouri villages, in particular, are understood by archaeologists and historians to have functioned as significant trading centers visited by peoples from across a wide surrounding region, exchanging garden produce, bison products, toolstone, pottery, and other goods, including items originating from considerably more distant regions through chains of intermediary exchange. This commercial role helped integrate farming villages and mobile hunting peoples into a single, interdependent regional economy rather than two separate and unconnected ways of life.

This pattern of exchange connects directly to the continental trade systems examined in earlier chapters of this textbook, including the long-distance exchange networks discussed in Chapter 12 and the Hopewell-era exchange described in Chapter 20. The Plains were not an isolated or peripheral region cut off from these broader continental patterns of trade; they were an active participant in them, with their own specialized products, including Knife River flint and agricultural surplus, flowing outward even as goods from distant regions flowed in.

Section 29.11

Leadership, Diplomacy, Warfare, and Fortified Villages

Organizing a village's agricultural labor, communal hunts, trade relationships, and defense required functioning systems of local leadership and decision-making. The precise nature of political authority in pre-equestrian Plains villages is not fully recoverable from archaeological evidence alone, but the scale of coordinated activity these communities undertook, including large-scale fortification construction, organized hunting expeditions, and extensive trade, indicates effective mechanisms for mobilizing collective labor and resolving disputes, likely involving councils, respected elders, and specific ceremonial or war leadership roles that later ethnographic sources describe in more detail for the historically documented Mandan, Hidatsa, Arikara, and Pawnee.

This chapter avoids describing a single, uniform "Plains political system," because the evidence instead suggests considerable local variation, with individual villages functioning as largely autonomous political communities that formed alliances, trade relationships, and, at times, rivalries with neighboring villages and with mobile hunting peoples, rather than being organized under any single overarching regional government.

Fortification as Evidence, Carefully Read

The substantial fortifications documented at villages such as Huff, including ditches extending more than two thousand feet, log palisades, and multiple projecting bastions positioned to allow defenders to cover the length of the wall, demonstrate that at least some communities invested heavily in organized defense. This evidence supports the conclusion that conflict, or the credible threat of conflict, was a serious concern for some Upper Missouri communities during the period this chapter examines.

At the same time, this chapter cautions against overreading fortification evidence. The presence of substantial defensive works does not, by itself, establish how frequently conflict actually occurred, who the specific parties to that conflict were, or whether fortification served primarily defensive purposes, deterrent purposes, or functions related to status and community identity as well as physical security. Archaeologists continue to debate the specific historical circumstances that drove particular episodes of village fortification, and this chapter presents the evidence without assigning it to a single settled interpretation.

Section 29.12

The Plains on the Eve of Equestrian Transformation

By the close of the period this chapter examines, roughly the late sixteenth century, the Plains supported a varied, populous, and economically interconnected Indigenous world. Along major river systems, farming communities lived in substantial, sometimes fortified villages, combining intensive garden agriculture with seasonal bison hunting and participating in regional and long-distance trade networks that moved agricultural surplus, bison products, specialized toolstone such as Knife River flint, and other goods across considerable distances. Beyond the river valleys, mobile hunting peoples organized sophisticated pedestrian communal hunts, traveled extensively using dogs and travois, and maintained their own networks of exchange, alliance, and seasonal interaction with farming villages.

This was not a static world. Villages relocated, grew, and sometimes declined; peoples migrated, as the Arikara movement into the Upper Missouri region illustrates; political relationships among neighboring communities shifted over time; and fortification patterns suggest periods of heightened conflict alongside periods of more settled exchange. But it was, by any reasonable historical standard, a functioning, sophisticated regional system, built through many centuries of Indigenous innovation, exchange, labor, and decision-making, before sustained European influence substantially reorganized the region.

Beginning in the seventeenth century, and accelerating through the eighteenth, horses descended from Spanish colonial stock began spreading northward and eastward across Indigenous trade and exchange networks, reaching Plains peoples well before most of those peoples had any direct, sustained contact with Spanish colonists themselves. That diffusion, and the dramatic transformation in mobility, hunting range, trade, warfare, and political power that followed, is the subject of Chapter 49. Understanding that later transformation accurately requires understanding, as this chapter has attempted to demonstrate, exactly what already existed on the Plains before it occurred: not an empty grassland waiting to be filled by mounted hunters, but an already functioning Indigenous world of farmers, hunters, traders, and diplomats whose achievements the horse would reshape rather than originate.

Historical Vocabulary
Plains Village Tradition

Plains Village tradition is an archaeological classification, not the name of a single people or nation. It describes a broad pattern of settlement, subsistence, and material culture, including river-valley settlement, earthlodge or related house construction, combined farming and bison hunting, and, in many cases, fortification, documented across much of the Plains beginning roughly a thousand years ago. Multiple historically distinct, linguistically unrelated peoples, including ancestors of the Mandan, Hidatsa, Arikara, Pawnee, and Wichita, developed communities that archaeologists group within this broad tradition, while differing considerably in language, specific cultural practice, and historical trajectory.

Why it matters: Treating "Plains Village" as though it named a single ethnic or political group risks exactly the kind of flattening this textbook has warned against since Chapter 1. The term is a useful organizing tool for describing shared economic and settlement patterns; it is not a substitute for the more specific historical and linguistic evidence needed to identify particular peoples.

Historical Vocabulary
Earthlodge

Earthlodge describes a substantial dwelling built around a timber frame and covered with layers of willow, grass, and packed earth, typically featuring a central hearth, a smoke hole, and an entry passage. Earthlodges were large enough to house extended, multi-generational families along with stored food and household goods, and were arranged within villages around shared open plazas used for ceremony and community gathering.

Beyond shelter: An earthlodge was not simply a house. It organized household labor, kinship relationships, and, in many documented communities, specific ceremonial responsibilities associated with particular households or lineages. Understanding the earthlodge as a social and economic unit, not only an architectural form, helps students see Plains village life as organized around durable households rather than loosely associated individuals.

Historical Method

From Archaeological Village to Historical Nation

When can historians responsibly say that a fifteenth-century archaeological village was "Mandan," "Pawnee," or "Arikara"? The answer depends on combining several distinct forms of evidence, no one of which is sufficient alone.

Continuity of location and material culture: When pottery style, house form, and settlement location show a continuous, traceable sequence from an early village site to a later, historically documented community, archaeologists can propose a reasonably strong connection.

Oral tradition: Descendant communities often maintain their own traditions of long occupation, migration, and settlement history, which can corroborate or complicate the archaeological sequence.

Linguistic evidence: Language family relationships, such as the distinction between Siouan-speaking Mandan and Hidatsa and Caddoan-speaking Arikara and Pawnee, provide an independent line of evidence about historical population relationships.

Early written observation: Where early European or American observers recorded village locations, names, and relationships soon enough after a given archaeological period, their accounts can help bridge the gap between archaeology and later, better-documented history, though such accounts must themselves be read critically, as this textbook has emphasized since Chapter 1.

The responsible conclusion: Historians can often say with reasonable confidence that a given archaeological tradition is ancestral, in a general sense, to a later historically documented people, while remaining appropriately cautious about assigning any single specific village site to a specific later-named community without strong corroborating evidence. This chapter follows that standard throughout.

Primary Source Window

Reading the Plan of Huff Village, c. 1450

Site: Huff Indian Village State Historic Site, Morton County, North Dakota · Estimated date of occupation: approximately 1450 · Investigating institution: State Historical Society of North Dakota · Type of source: Archaeological site plan and excavation record

Context: Huff Village sits on a terrace above the Missouri River and is identified by the State Historical Society of North Dakota as a fortified Mandan ancestral settlement dating to roughly 1450. The site preserves the footprint of more than one hundred earthlodge depressions arranged around a central plaza, along with extensive evidence of storage, fortification, and a combined farming and bison-hunting economy.

Reading the plan: The arrangement of more than a hundred house depressions around an open plaza indicates a deliberately organized community rather than an informal cluster of dwellings. The sheer number of storage pits recovered at the site points to substantial, sustained agricultural production capable of supporting a large population, estimated by investigators at roughly a thousand or more residents at the settlement's peak. A defensive ditch running more than two thousand feet, accompanied by a log palisade built from roughly 2,500 posts and ten projecting bastions, represents a very large collective labor investment, suggesting both the existence of community institutions capable of coordinating a major collective construction project and genuine concern for defense. Bison remains recovered throughout the site demonstrate that hunting remained a major economic activity alongside farming.

Analysis: As a primary source, a village plan functions differently from a written document or an oral tradition, but it is no less a form of historical evidence. It can reveal population scale, labor organization, food storage capacity, defensive concerns, and economic strategy with a level of physical, non-verbal precision that other kinds of evidence cannot always provide.

Limitations: A site plan cannot, by itself, reveal the language spoken by Huff's residents, the specific political structure of the community, the identity of any group the fortifications may have been built against, or the individual beliefs, relationships, or experiences of the people who lived there. These limitations do not make the archaeological evidence less valuable; they define the kind of historical questions it can and cannot answer on its own.

Questions for students:

  1. What can the arrangement of houses, storage pits, and fortifications at Huff Village reveal about population size, economic organization, and community planning?
  2. What does the scale of Huff's fortifications reveal about the community's ability to coordinate collective labor, and why does that evidence not by itself tell us exactly how political authority was organized?
  3. What questions about language, politics, and individual experience can a site plan like Huff's not answer on its own?
  4. How might combining this archaeological evidence with oral tradition, linguistic evidence, and later written observation strengthen or complicate conclusions drawn from the site plan alone?
Historical Debate

How Sedentary Were the Pre-Horse Plains?

The question: Should pre-equestrian Plains societies be described primarily as "sedentary village farmers" or primarily as "mobile hunting peoples"? Historians and archaeologists have sometimes framed the pre-horse Plains in terms of this binary, but the evidence suggests a more complicated picture that resists easy classification.

Interpretation A — Emphasize village permanence and agricultural stability: Some scholars emphasize that substantial, long-occupied villages with extensive storage capacity, fortification, and intensive agriculture represent a fundamentally settled way of life, with hunting serving as a seasonal supplement to an agricultural core.
Interpretation B — Emphasize mobility as integral, not supplemental: Other scholars emphasize that even village farming communities undertook substantial seasonal hunting expeditions away from their settlements, that villages themselves relocated periodically, and that a significant portion of the Plains population lived in more mobile hunting-focused communities throughout this period, making mobility a defining rather than secondary feature of Plains life as a whole.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the same archaeological record of village settlement, storage capacity, seasonal hunting evidence, and the broader presence of more mobile hunting peoples across the Plains; they differ in how much relative emphasis to place on settlement versus movement as the defining feature of the region's pre-equestrian economy.

Why it matters: How historians frame this question shapes the baseline against which the later equestrian transformation, examined in Chapter 49, is measured. If mobility was already central to Plains life before horses, then the key change the horse produced was one of scale, speed, and range rather than the introduction of mobility itself.

Student task: Using the evidence on village agriculture, storage, seasonal hunting, and the presence of mobile hunting peoples presented throughout this chapter, construct an argument for which interpretation better accounts for the evidence, or explain why a framework rejecting the sedentary/mobile binary altogether might describe the Plains more accurately than either interpretation alone.

Multiple Perspectives

Four Views of a River-Valley World

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives based on archaeological and ethnohistorical evidence discussed in this chapter. They are not quotations from any specific named individual.

From the perspective of a woman tending a riverside garden plot: The yearly cycle of planting, weeding, and harvesting maize, beans, squash, and sunflowers shapes daily and seasonal life, and the household's stored surplus, kept in carefully maintained cache pits, provides both security through the winter and goods to exchange when visitors from other communities arrive to trade.
From the perspective of a participant in a seasonal bison hunt: Weeks of travel away from the village, coordinated movement with dozens of other hunters, careful reading of wind, terrain, and animal behavior, and the demanding labor of processing a large kill quickly define this portion of the year, which provides meat, hides, and other materials essential to the community's survival and to trade.
From the perspective of a trader arriving from a more distant community: A village like Huff functions as a destination worth a long journey, offering dried agricultural surplus, finished goods, and access to materials such as Knife River flint in exchange for bison products, hides, or other goods carried from a different region, within a network of exchange relationships that may also involve kinship ties or long-standing trading partnerships.
From the perspective of a village member participating in the construction and defense of fortifications: The substantial collective labor required to dig a long defensive ditch, build a palisade, and construct bastions reflects both a real concern for the community's safety and a shared responsibility distributed across many households, coordinated by leaders capable of organizing sustained group effort.
Continuity and Change Over Time

Plains Life Before and After the Horse

What This Chapter Establishes as the Baseline

  • River-valley villages combining intensive agriculture with seasonal bison hunting
  • Pedestrian communal hunting methods capable of taking large numbers of bison at once
  • Mobility achieved through walking, dogs, travois, and, where navigable, river travel
  • Extensive regional and long-distance trade connecting farming villages, mobile hunting peoples, and distant regions
  • Local community institutions capable of coordinating labor for agriculture, hunting, trade, and defense

What Chapter 49 Will Examine as Later Change

  • Dramatically increased range, speed, and carrying capacity of overland travel
  • New possibilities for mounted bison hunting, altering the scale and organization of hunts
  • New forms of mounted warfare, raiding, and territorial expansion among some Plains peoples
  • Reorganization of wealth, status, and political power around horse ownership and horse-based military capacity
  • New and expanded long-distance trade and diplomatic relationships built on top of, rather than replacing, earlier regional networks

Why the pattern matters: Framing the horse's arrival as a transformation of an existing system, rather than the creation of Plains civilization from nothing, allows students to recognize both genuine, significant change and genuine, important continuity, including continued village agriculture, continued importance of river systems, and continued regional trade relationships that long predated horses and persisted, in modified form, long after their arrival.

Cause and Effect

Why Did Large Villages Develop Along Rivers Within a Grassland Region?

Long-term causes

The gradual northward and westward spread of maize, bean, and squash agriculture across North America, examined in Chapter 13, provided the agricultural foundation that Plains communities adapted to river-valley conditions over many generations.

Short-term causes

Specific river valleys offered fertile alluvial soil, reliable water, and locally available timber, resources that were scarce or absent across most of the surrounding grassland and that made particular stretches of river especially attractive for permanent settlement.

Effect

Population concentrated heavily in narrow river corridors rather than spreading evenly across the Plains, producing substantial, sometimes fortified villages with populations in the hundreds to nearly two thousand, while large stretches of surrounding grassland supported lower-density, more mobile hunting-based populations.

Historical consequence

River villages became natural centers of trade, drawing in mobile hunting peoples seeking agricultural surplus and offering, in turn, access to specialized materials such as Knife River flint, reinforcing the economic interdependence between farming and hunting communities described throughout this chapter.

Historical complexity: This pattern should not be read as suggesting that river-valley life was straightforwardly "better" than life farther out on the grasslands. Both patterns represented successful, sustained adaptations to different aspects of the same broader environment, and many communities and individuals moved between the two patterns across a single year or across a lifetime.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Seasonal Year in a Plains River Village

Life in a pre-equestrian Upper Missouri village followed a seasonal rhythm shaped by both agriculture and hunting. In spring, households prepared garden plots in the floodplain below the village terrace, planting maize, beans, squash, and sunflowers using digging sticks and bone hoes, work that later ethnographic sources associate primarily with women, working alongside children and other household members. As crops grew through early summer, portions of the village population, often a substantial share of able-bodied adults, departed on organized bison-hunting expeditions, traveling on foot with dogs carrying supplies on travois, applying the communal hunting methods described in Section 29.7 to take meat, hides, and other materials for the coming year.

Late summer and fall brought harvest, as garden produce was gathered, dried, and stored in cache pits dug within or near each household's earthlodge, work requiring careful management to ensure that stored food would last through the long winter months when neither farming nor reliable hunting could be counted on. Fall also brought trading visitors to the village, exchanging bison products, hides, or goods carried from distant regions for the village's agricultural surplus and access to locally available materials.

Winter life centered on the earthlodge itself, where extended families gathered around the central hearth, relying on stored food, continuing craft production, and maintaining tools and equipment for the coming year. Throughout the year, the village plaza served as a gathering place for ceremony, decision-making, and community life, reinforcing the social bonds that made coordinated agriculture, hunting, trade, and defense possible. This seasonal rhythm, repeated across generations with only gradual change, illustrates how ordinary daily and yearly labor, not only dramatic political or military events, sustained the pre-equestrian Plains world this chapter has described.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has set out to dismantle one of the most persistent stereotypes in the study of Indigenous North America: the assumption that Plains history is, by its nature, horse history. For the many centuries before horses reached the Plains, Indigenous peoples had already built a varied, populous, and economically interconnected regional world, one that the later equestrian transformation would reshape dramatically but did not create.

Along the Missouri River and its tributaries, and along other major river systems stretching from the Canadian prairies into Texas, farming communities built substantial, often fortified villages, cultivating maize, beans, squash, and sunflowers in fertile floodplain gardens and storing significant surplus in deep cache pits. These communities combined agriculture with seasonal bison hunting, demonstrating that farming and hunting were complementary strategies within a single integrated economy rather than separate, competing ways of life.

The chapter examined the Upper Missouri village world in particular depth, using Huff Village as a detailed case study of a fortified, populous, fifteenth-century community combining farming and bison hunting behind substantial defensive works. It traced the historically documented Mandan, Hidatsa, and Arikara peoples back toward this archaeological world with appropriate caution, emphasizing that their later political alliance as the Three Affiliated Tribes reflects developments from a much later period and should not be projected backward onto the centuries this chapter examines.

The chapter also examined Central and Southern Plains village traditions, including ancestral Pawnee and Caddoan-speaking communities related to the Wichita, demonstrating that substantial agricultural settlement was a widespread Plains phenomenon rather than a development confined to a single river system or people, while cautioning against collapsing these linguistically and historically distinct traditions into a single undifferentiated "Plains farming culture."

Bison hunting received sustained attention as a centerpiece of this chapter's argument. Pedestrian communal hunting methods, including drives, jumps, and pounds, allowed coordinated groups to take substantial numbers of bison without horses, and bison already stood at the center of Plains economic and cultural life long before mounted hunting became possible. Village farmers themselves undertook significant seasonal hunting expeditions, further demonstrating the integration of farming and hunting within pre-equestrian Plains economies.

Household and community life was examined through earthlodge construction, garden agriculture, and food storage, revealing substantial, well-organized communities capable of supporting dense populations through careful management of stored surplus. Pre-equestrian mobility was examined through the roles of dogs, travois, pedestrian travel, and river transport, demonstrating that the absence of horses did not mean the absence of effective seasonal movement, village relocation, or long-distance travel.

Trade and exchange received particular emphasis through the example of Knife River flint, whose wide archaeological distribution provides tangible, physical evidence of extensive regional and long-distance Plains trade networks connecting farming villages and mobile hunting peoples in relationships of economic interdependence. Local political leadership, diplomacy, and warfare were examined through the evidence of village fortification, with appropriate caution against overreading what such evidence can establish about specific conflicts or political structures.

Throughout, this chapter has pursued a specific methodological question: how much of the historically documented Plains world, recorded mostly after 1700, can be responsibly projected backward onto the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries? The chapter's consistent answer has been that archaeological evidence, oral tradition, linguistic evidence, and later written observation together support real historical continuity between the pre-equestrian village world and later historically documented peoples, while requiring real caution against assuming that later names, territories, alliances, and ways of life describe the earlier period without substantial modification.

This chapter's final purpose is to establish a historical baseline. Chapter 49 will examine what changed when horses, descended from Spanish colonial stock, began spreading across Indigenous Plains trade and exchange networks from the seventeenth century onward, transforming mobility, hunting, warfare, and political power across much of the region. That transformation can only be understood accurately once the Plains world it transformed, the world this chapter has described, is understood clearly and on its own terms.

A language family including Mandan and Hidatsa, among many other North American languages.
Knife River flint
A distinctive, high-quality toolstone quarried in present-day west-central North Dakota and traded across a very wide area of the Plains and beyond, used as physical evidence of long-distance exchange.
Three Affiliated Tribes
The modern political alliance of the Mandan, Hidatsa, and Arikara Nation, a development belonging to a much later historical period than the pre-equestrian villages this chapter examines.
Pedestrian mobility
Overland movement on foot, supplemented by dogs and travois, characteristic of Plains transportation before horses.
Village agriculture
Intensive garden cultivation of maize, beans, squash, and sunflowers associated with permanent or semipermanent Plains settlements.
Seasonal round
The annual cycle of planting, hunting, harvesting, trading, and other activities that structured the yearly rhythm of Plains village and hunting communities.
Archaeological culture
A grouping of sites sharing similar material remains, used by archaeologists as an analytical tool rather than a direct statement about ethnic or political identity.
Ethnohistory
The use of written, oral, and other historical sources, often produced by outside observers, alongside archaeological evidence to reconstruct Indigenous history.
Regional exchange network
A system of trade and exchange connecting multiple communities across a defined geographic area, demonstrated on the Plains through evidence such as Knife River flint.
Garden complex
The combination of maize, beans, squash, and sunflowers cultivated together in Plains village agriculture.

Major People and Places

Identification & Significance

Huff Village
A fortified ancestral Mandan settlement on the Missouri River, dated to approximately 1450, containing more than one hundred earthlodge depressions and extensive evidence of combined farming and bison hunting, examined in this chapter's Primary Source Window.
The Upper Missouri village world
The complex of farming and hunting communities along the Missouri River and its tributaries in present-day North and South Dakota, ancestral to the Mandan, Hidatsa, and Arikara peoples, examined in Sections 29.4 and 29.5.
Mandan, Hidatsa, and Arikara peoples
Historically documented Upper Missouri nations whose later political alliance as the Three Affiliated Tribes reflects developments from a much later period than the pre-equestrian villages discussed in this chapter.
Ancestral Pawnee and Central Plains village communities
Farming societies of the Central Plains river valleys, discussed in Section 29.6 as an example of village agriculture beyond the Upper Missouri.
Ancestral Wichita and Southern Plains Caddoan-speaking communities
Village farming societies of the Southern Plains, linguistically related to the Arikara and Pawnee, discussed in Section 29.6.
Knife River flint quarry sources
Toolstone source and quarry areas in the upper Knife River basin of present-day Dunn and Mercer Counties, North Dakota, west of the Knife-Missouri village region, whose wide archaeological distribution provides physical evidence of long-distance Plains trade, discussed in Section 29.10.
The Knife River Indian Villages region
A cluster of Upper Missouri village sites studied extensively by the National Park Service, used throughout this chapter as a source of archaeological and ethnohistorical evidence.
The Missouri River
The central river system of the Upper Missouri village world, discussed throughout Sections 29.4 and 29.5 as the backbone of regional settlement, agriculture, and trade.
Double Ditch Village
A large Mandan ancestral village site on the Missouri River estimated at close to two thousand residents at points in its history, illustrating the upper range of Upper Missouri village population alongside Huff Village.
Big Hidatsa and Awatixa village sites
Hidatsa ancestral village complexes documenting the staged westward movement of Hidatsa communities toward the Missouri River discussed in Section 29.5.
Buffalo Bird Woman
A Hidatsa farmer whose detailed early twentieth-century testimony underlies much later scholarly description of women's roles in Hidatsa garden agriculture, discussed in Section 29.8.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has asked students to set aside one of the most familiar and most misleading images in the popular understanding of Indigenous North America: the idea that Plains history begins and ends with the mounted, highly mobile equestrian societies of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. In its place, this chapter has offered a different picture, built from archaeological evidence, careful historical reasoning, and appropriate caution about what later sources can and cannot establish about earlier centuries: a Plains world of substantial farming villages, sophisticated pedestrian hunting, extensive regional trade, and locally organized political life, developed entirely through Indigenous innovation over many centuries before any Plains community possessed a horse.

This matters for reasons that extend well beyond this single chapter. It matters because the equestrian image of Plains life, however iconic in popular memory, has often served to make Plains history seem simpler and more timeless than it actually was, obscuring the depth, complexity, and historical dynamism of village agriculture, communal hunting, and regional trade that long predated horses. Recovering that earlier world does not diminish the later equestrian transformation examined in Chapter 49; it provides the necessary baseline for understanding that transformation accurately, as a reorganization of an already functioning Indigenous system rather than the sudden creation of Plains civilization from an empty landscape.

This chapter also matters methodologically. It has extended the interpretive questions raised throughout Part V, concerning the relationship between archaeological evidence and historically documented peoples, into a new regional context, showing that the same careful standards of evidence, caution about projecting later identities backward, and attention to both continuity and change apply as fully to the Plains as they do to the Northeast, the Southeast, or any other region this textbook examines.

Students who understand the pre-equestrian Plains as a world of farmers, hunters, traders, and leaders, rather than as an empty stage awaiting the horse, are well prepared to understand the dramatic changes examined in Chapter 49 not as the invention of Plains history, but as one chapter, however significant, within a much longer and richer Indigenous story that began many centuries earlier and that continues to shape the identities, communities, and historical memory of Plains nations today.
