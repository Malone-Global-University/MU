Spanish Conquest and Indigenous Mesoamerica

Indigenous Peoples of North America: A Continental History

Chapter Opening

On 13 August 1521 the last organized defense of the island city of Tenochtitlan collapsed. Within weeks, a Spanish captain and a few hundred of his countrymen would be credited in European writing with having conquered an empire. The people of Tlaxcallan, Texcoco, Cempoala, Huexotzinco, and many other communities, who had supplied most of the fighters, food, carriers, and local knowledge, remembered the campaign differently, and had their own purposes in remembering it.

The standard story of the conquest of Mexico is among the most familiar in world history, and it is a story told almost entirely from one side. A Spanish adventurer burns his ships, marches inland with a few hundred men, is welcomed by a ruler who mistakes him for a god, seizes the capital, and in a stroke ends the Aztec empire. Nearly every element of that account has been revised by scholarship over the last half century, and some elements have been discarded. The ships were not burned. The empire was not a unified nation. The Spaniards were not alone, and the Indigenous armies that marched beside them were neither passive instruments nor deceived dupes. The war of 1519–1521 was a Mesoamerican war, fought within a political landscape of rivalry, tribute, and shifting alliance, into which a small group of Europeans inserted themselves with considerable skill, considerable brutality, and considerable luck.

This chapter treats the conquest as a Mesoamerican political and military struggle with four connected tasks, which together define its intellectual responsibility. The first is to explain the conquest itself: the sequence of expeditions, battles, negotiations, and sieges that ended in the fall of Tenochtitlan. The second is to place Indigenous alliances and resistance at the center of that explanation, asking what Totonacs, Tlaxcalteca, Texcocans, and others wanted, what they risked, and what they hoped to gain. The third is to acknowledge the demographic catastrophe that accompanied the war, especially the smallpox epidemic of 1520–1521, without reducing the conquest to a story about disease. The fourth is to follow the reorganization of Indigenous political life in New Spain, showing that the destruction of one capital transformed Indigenous political society rather than extinguishing it.

The chapter builds directly on the preceding chapters of Volume IV. Chapter 14 introduced the civilizations of Mesoamerica and their connections to regions farther north, and this chapter summarizes that background only as far as the war requires. Chapter 38 traced the Caribbean invasion that created the colonial base from which the expeditions to the mainland were launched: the settlements on Hispaniola and Cuba, the slave raiding, the encomienda labor system, and the governors, such as Diego Velázquez of Cuba, who authorized the voyages that followed. Chapter 40 will examine epidemic disease and demographic catastrophe in full; Chapter 42 will follow Spanish expansion into northern Mexico. Chapter 39 holds the ground between: the war that made central Mexico a Spanish colony and the first decades in which Indigenous communities made that colony work on terms they helped to shape.

The evidence for these events is abundant, but it is also tangled. Hernán Cortés wrote letters to the Spanish king designed to justify a campaign that had been undertaken without proper authorization. Bernal Díaz del Castillo, a soldier, wrote his recollections decades later to correct what he saw as the self-serving histories of others. Nahuatl-speaking authors in Tlatelolco, Tlaxcala, and elsewhere produced alphabetic texts and painted histories in the sixteenth century, each shaped by its own community's experience and its own claims on the colonial regime. The most famous of these, Book XII of the Florentine Codex, records the memories of elderly Nahua men gathered decades after the war. Reading these sources against one another, and asking what each author wanted from his audience, is part of the work of this chapter.

The chapter also takes care with names and categories. The people who ruled Tenochtitlan called themselves Mexica, and the term “Aztec” is a later convention with its own history. The political unit that mattered most to ordinary people was usually not an empire but the altepetl, the local ethnic state, and the alliances of 1519–1521 are intelligible only in terms of these units. Indigenous people did not use the labels “Indian” or “Native Mexican” for themselves before the Spaniards imposed such categories, and the idea of a single Indigenous side in the war is therefore an anachronism that the sources themselves contradict.

None of this reduces the violence of the conquest. Cholula, Tóxcatl, the siege of Tenochtitlan, and the branding of captives as slaves were episodes of mass killing and coercion, and the chapter treats them as such. It also does not reduce the Indigenous peoples of the region to victims or allies of the Spaniards. They were political actors whose choices were made under pressure, with imperfect information, and with consequences they could not foresee. Some of those choices, such as the Tlaxcalan decision to ally with Cortés, helped bring down a hated imperial power at the price of a new and unforeseen subordination. Others, such as the Mexica defense of Tenochtitlan, ended in destruction. Both belong to the history of Indigenous North America.

Finally, the chapter insists that the story does not end in August 1521. Spanish campaigns against the Purépecha, the Zapotecs and Mixtecs, and the Maya continued for years and in some cases for generations, and many Mesoamerican polities were never conquered in the sense the Cortés narrative suggests. Within the conquered core, Indigenous elites, councils, and communities negotiated a place in the new colonial order, and the altepetl survived as the basic unit of local government under a Spanish name and a Spanish legal form. Understanding that transformation is as essential as understanding the siege.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why the conquest of central Mexico is best understood as a Mesoamerican coalition war rather than as a victory of a few hundred Spaniards over a unified Indigenous nation.
  2. Describe the political organization of Mesoamerica in 1519, including the altepetl, the Triple Alliance, tribute relationships, and the independent states that stood outside Mexica control.
  3. Trace the sequence of events from the Yucatán expeditions of 1517–1518 through the fall of Tenochtitlan in 1521, identifying the major turning points.
  4. Analyze the motives and actions of the Totonacs, Tlaxcalteca, Texcocans, and other Indigenous allies, and explain how alliance followed from their own political interests.
  5. Assess the role of Malintzin and other interpreters in diplomacy and intelligence, distinguishing historical evidence from later nationalist and gendered stereotypes.
  6. Evaluate competing accounts of the Cholula massacre, the Tóxcatl massacre, and the death of Moctezuma II, distinguishing contemporary testimony from later tradition.
  7. Critique the claim that the Mexica believed the Spaniards were gods, and explain how later narrative traditions shaped that claim.
  8. Explain the military and political significance of the 1520–1521 smallpox epidemic without treating disease as the sole cause of Spanish success.
  9. Compare Spanish and Indigenous sources for the conquest, and explain why Indigenous accounts are plural rather than uniform.
  10. Describe the uneven continuation of conquest after 1521 in Michoacán, Oaxaca, Guatemala, and Yucatán.
  11. Explain how Indigenous communities and elites were incorporated into colonial government through native cabildos, tribute, and encomienda, and identify what continued and what changed.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Who actually conquered Tenochtitlan, and why have historians come to answer that question differently than sixteenth-century Spanish chroniclers did?
  2. Why did Indigenous peoples who had no prior relationship with the Spaniards choose to ally with them, and what did each community hope to gain?
  3. How should historians weigh incompatible accounts of a single event, such as the massacre at Cholula, when each narrator had reasons to shape the story?
  4. Why did the Spanish expulsion from Tenochtitlan in 1520 not end the invasion?
  5. In what ways did epidemic disease change the political and military balance, and in what ways did it not make conquest inevitable?
  6. What does it mean to say that the “Indigenous perspective” on the conquest is plural, and what do the Tlatelolcan, Tlaxcalan, and other sources reveal about that plurality?
  7. Why is the fall of Tenochtitlan a poor stand-in for the conquest of Mesoamerica as a whole?
  8. How did the altepetl survive the destruction of the Triple Alliance, and what changed when it became a colonial corporate community?

Historical Context

By 1519 the Spanish presence in the Americas was a generation old and entirely Caribbean. Columbus's voyages, examined in Chapter 38, had been followed by the settlement of Hispaniola, Puerto Rico, Jamaica, and Cuba, by the collapse of Taíno and other Island populations under forced labor, violence, and disease, and by the rise of slave raiding across the surrounding waters. Governors and settlers on the islands needed new sources of labor and wealth, and they pursued them by sending ships westward and southward. The expeditions to the Yucatán coast in 1517 and 1518 belong to this process. They were not voyages of discovery into the unknown. They were extensions of an existing colonial economy in search of captives, gold, and information.

On the mainland, Mesoamerica was a densely populated region of cities, agricultural systems, long-distance markets, and states, whose history is introduced in Chapter 14. In the central highlands the dominant political fact was the Triple Alliance, a military and tributary partnership among the cities of Tenochtitlan, Texcoco, and Tlacopan formed in the 1420s, in which Tenochtitlan had gradually become the senior partner. Its rulers collected tribute from dozens of conquered communities, from the Gulf Coast to the Pacific slope and the southern highlands. Yet the empire was not a centralized state of the kind later European observers imagined. It governed largely by extracting tribute from existing local rulers, who kept their own institutions, and by threatening force against those who resisted. Several large and powerful neighbors, among them Tlaxcallan and the Purépecha state in the west, remained outside its control.

The Basin of Mexico itself was an extraordinary environment. A chain of shallow lakes supported intensive chinampa agriculture, a dense system of canals and causeways, and some of the largest urban concentrations in the world at that time. Tenochtitlan, founded on an island in Lake Texcoco according to Mexica tradition in 1325, was connected to the shore by causeways and supplied by an aqueduct from Chapultepec. Its sister city Tlatelolco, once independent and absorbed by Tenochtitlan in the 1470s, held the great market. This landscape would shape the campaigns of 1520–1521, in which control of water, causeways, and canoes mattered as much as control of land.

Historians face a limitation in reconstructing this world: Mesoamerican peoples did write, using painted books, glyphic and pictographic systems, and, after the conquest, alphabetic Nahuatl and other languages. But nearly all surviving narratives of the conquest were produced after the fact, many under Spanish supervision or for Spanish audiences. For the events themselves, historians must work with a small number of eyewitness Spanish accounts and a larger body of Indigenous retrospective sources, each requiring critical reading. Section 39.21 takes up this problem directly.

Section 39.1

Rethinking the “Conquest of Mexico”

The phrase “conquest of Mexico” compresses several distinct processes into a single event. It can mean the overthrow of the Triple Alliance capital in 1521, the subjugation of the wider region that Spaniards would call New Spain, or the longer transformation of Indigenous societies under colonial rule. These are different things, separated by years and in some cases by generations, and the habit of treating them as one has produced much of the distortion in popular memory. This chapter distinguishes between the fall of Tenochtitlan, a specific military and political event, and the conquest of Mesoamerica, a patchwork of campaigns, negotiations, and settlements that continued well beyond 1521.

The central argument of the chapter is that the capture of the Mexica capital was achieved by a coalition in which Spaniards were a small but consequential part. The Spanish contingent numbered in the hundreds at the outset and reached perhaps a thousand during the siege, according to the commander's own reports, whose figures should be treated as approximate. The Indigenous forces who fought beside them numbered in the tens of thousands, and no reliable count survives. Those forces were not recruited by Spanish persuasion alone. They represented communities with their own grievances against Mexica tribute demands, their own rivalries with neighboring cities, and their own calculations about what an alliance with the newcomers might gain or cost.

Spanish weapons and tactics mattered. Steel swords and armor, crossbows and firearms, horses, a handful of cannon, and later the brigantines gave the invaders real advantages in certain kinds of combat. But these advantages were not decisive by themselves, and the campaign nearly failed more than once. Spaniards were defeated in battle, expelled from the capital, and driven back to Tlaxcallan within months of entering Tenochtitlan. That they returned was owed to Indigenous allies who chose to renew the alliance, to a smallpox epidemic that struck the Basin of Mexico, to Cortés's political resourcefulness, and to reinforcements arriving from the Caribbean. No single cause explains the outcome, and none made it inevitable.

A second argument concerns the afterlife of the conquest. The destruction of Tenochtitlan ended the Triple Alliance as a political system but did not end Indigenous politics. Local rulers, noble lineages, and community councils were incorporated into the new colonial structure, often adapting their institutions to Spanish legal forms. The result was a transformation, painful and unequal and often coercive, rather than a replacement. This is why the chapter ends not with Cuauhtémoc's capture but with the Indigenous governments of the 1530s and 1540s.

Historical Vocabulary
Mexica, Aztec, and Nahua

Mexica is the name the people of Tenochtitlan and Tlatelolco used for themselves. Nahua refers more broadly to the many peoples of central Mexico who spoke Nahuatl, including the Mexica, the Tlaxcalteca, the Texcocans, and the people of Cholula. Aztec derives from Aztlan, the legendary place of origin in Mexica tradition, and was popularized in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries; it is not a term the Mexica used as a political label, and it is sometimes applied loosely to the whole Triple Alliance or to all Nahua peoples.

Why it matters: Treating “the Aztecs” as a single people obscures the fact that the conquest was fought among Nahua communities as much as against them. This textbook uses Mexica for the people of Tenochtitlan and Tlatelolco, Nahua for the broader language community, and “Triple Alliance” for the imperial partnership, and uses “Aztec” only where an older source or a familiar convention requires it.

Section 39.2

Mesoamerica in 1519: Altepetl, Empire, and Political Rivalry

To understand why the war unfolded as it did, students must grasp how political authority was organized in central Mexico on the eve of the Spanish arrival. The basic unit was not the empire but the altepetl, literally “water-mountain,” a term used for a local ethnic state: a territory with a ruling dynasty, a population divided into subunits, a central temple and market, and a claim to a collective identity and history. An altepetl might be small, with a few thousand people, or large, like Tenochtitlan or Texcoco. Its ruler, the tlatoani (“speaker”), governed with councils of nobles, and the altepetl was typically divided into smaller units, often calpulli, that organized land, labor, and tribute.

Historical Vocabulary
Altepetl

Definition: A Nahua local state, composed of a territory, a people, a ruling house, and a set of subdivisions, with its own history, patron deity, and political identity.

Historical context: The altepetl was the building block of Nahua political life before and after 1521. Empires, alliances, and tributary networks were built from altepetl; they rarely replaced them.

Why it matters: Alliances and rivalries in the conquest were largely between altepetl. The term also helps explain colonial continuity, because Spanish authorities governed through these units.

Potential misunderstanding: “City-state” is a common English translation but can mislead, since many altepetl were rural and included dependent settlements across a wide area.

The Triple Alliance had emerged in the early fifteenth century from a war against Azcapotzalco, a dominant power in the Basin, and Tenochtitlan, Texcoco, and Tlacopan had divided its spoils. Over the following decades the alliance conquered or intimidated a large part of central and southern Mexico. By 1519 the Mexica ruler Moctezuma Xocoyotzin had reigned for about seventeen years. His empire was best described as a tributary network, in which conquered rulers retained local authority in exchange for regular deliveries of goods and labor and for supplying warriors on demand. A tribute list compiled under Spanish supervision in the 1540s, the Codex Mendoza, records the provinces and their obligations, and it remains one of the main sources for the system, though it reflects a colonial moment and a Spanish audience.

Tribute empires create enemies, and the Triple Alliance had many. Communities that had been conquered resented the demands, the occasional seizure of captives for sacrifice, and the garrisons and officials stationed in their territory. The Mexica also engaged in ritual warfare with some neighbors, notably the cities of the Puebla-Tlaxcala region, with whom they staged contests that provided captives and tested their strength. Some historians have argued that these conflicts show Tlaxcallan to have been a deliberately maintained enemy; others see a real strategic rivalry that the Triple Alliance had not managed to resolve. Whichever is correct, by 1519 Tlaxcallan was surrounded by Triple Alliance territory and had endured years of economic pressure, including restrictions on access to goods such as salt and cotton, according to later accounts.

Beyond central Mexico, the political landscape was equally fragmented. The Purépecha state in Michoacán had defeated Mexica armies in the late fifteenth century and remained independent. The Mixtecs and Zapotecs of Oaxaca lived in a mosaic of rival kingdoms, some of them tributaries of the Triple Alliance. In the Maya regions of Yucatán and Guatemala, a number of small and mid-sized polities competed with one another. The point is not to catalog them but to establish that no one in 1519 could have regarded Spanish arrival as an attack on “Mesoamerica.” Different communities encountered the newcomers from different positions of power and vulnerability.

Section 39.3

Before Cortés: Yucatán and the Gulf Coast, 1517–1518

The expedition that Cortés led in 1519 was the third Spanish voyage to the Mexican mainland in as many years, and it depended on information gathered by the first two. In 1517, Francisco Hernández de Córdoba sailed from Cuba with a small fleet, partly to seize captives and partly to explore. The expedition made landfall on the Yucatán coast and was received with hostility. At Champotón the Spaniards fought a pitched battle against a Maya force and suffered heavy losses; Córdoba was badly wounded and died soon after returning to Cuba. Despite the defeat, the voyage showed that the mainland held large towns with stone buildings, organized armies, and gold ornaments, and Governor Velázquez moved quickly to follow it up.

The second expedition, led by Juan de Grijalva in 1518, sailed along the Yucatán coast and then into the Gulf of Mexico. It touched at Cozumel and Champotón, entered the Tabasco river region, and continued north along the Gulf coast to the area of San Juan de Ulúa, where Grijalva's men exchanged goods with local people and met officials who, according to Spanish accounts, were representatives of the ruler of Tenochtitlan. The voyage produced gold, news of a powerful inland empire, and a clearer understanding that Spaniards could trade as well as fight. Pedro de Alvarado, later notorious at Tenochtitlan, served on this expedition and returned to Cuba with reports.

These expeditions matter for two reasons. First, they demonstrate that the conquest drew on accumulating knowledge and that Cortés's expedition was not a leap into the dark. The Spanish captains had maps, an idea of the coast, and prior contacts, and some Indigenous communities had already seen what the newcomers could do. Second, they show the Caribbean origins of the venture: the ships, supplies, financing, and manpower came from Cuba, whose settlers were struggling with the collapse of the island's Indigenous workforce, a process examined in Chapter 38. The search for the next source of labor and wealth was a structural pressure that made further expeditions likely, even though the identity of the commander and the outcome of the venture were not.

Indigenous communities of the coast were also observing and learning. Reports of the strangers, their ships, and their weapons passed from town to town. By the time Cortés arrived in 1519, rulers in the Gulf region and in Tenochtitlan itself had received multiple accounts, and some Maya communities had developed a clear understanding of Spanish intentions. The sources do not allow a detailed reconstruction of the information networks involved, but they leave no doubt that the Spaniards were not unknown, and that Indigenous decision-makers could draw on prior experience when they met them.

Section 39.4

Cortés Leaves Cuba: Cozumel, Tabasco, and Centla

Hernán Cortés was an ambitious Spanish settler in Cuba who had held office under Velázquez and had acquired property and a reputation. In late 1518, Velázquez commissioned him to lead a third expedition, with instructions focused on trade and exploration rather than settlement. The governor soon grew suspicious that Cortés would use the venture for his own purposes and attempted to revoke the commission, but Cortés sailed in February 1519 before the order could be enforced. His fleet consisted of eleven vessels with several hundred men, a small number of horses, and some artillery. Accounts differ on exact numbers, which Spanish narrators tended to inflate or reduce to serve their purposes.

The fleet stopped first at Cozumel, off the Yucatán coast. There the Spaniards learned that a Spaniard named Jerónimo de Aguilar, shipwrecked years earlier, was living among the Maya. Aguilar rejoined the expedition and proved invaluable as a translator of Yucatec Maya. Another castaway, Gonzalo Guerrero, chose to stay with the Maya, where he had married and raised a family, and later Spanish sources describe him as an opponent of the invaders. His story shows that cultural crossing could run in both directions and that some individuals made choices that Spanish writers found hard to explain.

Moving west along the coast, the expedition reached the Tabasco river in March 1519. The Chontal Maya of the region, whose chief town Potonchán lay nearby, had already encountered Grijalva, and now they met Cortés with armed force. The Spaniards landed and fought at Centla. Accounts agree that the fighting was fierce, that Spanish horses and firearms helped, and that the Maya forces suffered heavy casualties. Some Spanish narrators gave the number of Maya warriors as many thousands, a figure that should be treated with caution. After the battle, local leaders sued for peace and gave the Spaniards gifts, including twenty women.

That transfer of women belongs to the history of conquest as well as to the history of diplomacy. In Mesoamerican practice, the gift of women to a victorious or powerful outsider could establish alliance and kinship ties, and the Chontal leaders probably understood it in those terms. For the women themselves, it meant enslavement and sexual exploitation by the Spaniards, who baptized them and distributed them among the officers. One of them, a woman known in Nahuatl sources as Malintzin and in Spanish ones as Marina or Doña Marina, would become the central linguistic and political intermediary of the entire campaign.

Section 39.5

Malintzin, Aguilar, Translation, and Political Intelligence

Malintzin's origins are known only from later and partly contradictory sources. She was born, probably around 1500, in a Nahuatl-speaking region near the Gulf coast, and she was at some point separated from her family and transferred to the Maya-speaking Chontal area, where she learned Chontal Maya. Spanish writers later told dramatic stories about her childhood, and these stories cannot be verified. What is certain is that when the Spaniards received her at Centla, she spoke Nahuatl, the language of Tenochtitlan and its neighbors, as well as a Maya language.

This gave the expedition a means of communicating with the Mexica. Aguilar translated between Spanish and Yucatec Maya; Malintzin translated between that Maya language and Nahuatl; a chain of translation therefore connected Cortés with Mexica envoys. As Malintzin learned Spanish, which she did within months, she began to translate directly. By the time the expedition reached the Mexica court she was an interpreter, negotiator, and cultural mediator at the center of nearly every major encounter. Spanish sources routinely reported that Cortés “spoke” with Moctezuma when in fact he spoke through her. Indigenous pictorial sources reflect her importance: in the Lienzo de Tlaxcala, painted around 1552, she appears prominently beside Cortés in scenes of diplomacy and warfare, depicted as a figure of authority.

Interpretation was not neutral. An interpreter chose words, tone, and emphasis, and she also gathered information about local politics, grievances, and rivalries that informed Spanish decisions. Spanish accounts credit her with warning Cortés of the supposed plot at Cholula, an episode examined in Section 39.9. Whether or not that particular account is accurate, her role as a political adviser is clear. The status she held in the Spanish camp was also unusual. She was enslaved at the outset, and she remained under Spanish control, but over time she gained a recognized position and, after the conquest, became the mother of Cortés's son Martín, born around 1522, and later married the Spanish soldier Juan Jaramillo. She probably died in the late 1520s, though the date is uncertain.

Later memory has not been kind to her. In Mexican nationalist rhetoric from the nineteenth century onward, she was often condemned as la Malinche, a traitor who betrayed her people, a label that persists in popular use. This framing is anachronistic in several ways. It assumes a Mexican national identity that did not exist in 1519, when she was a young enslaved woman among peoples to whom she had no political loyalty. It places on her a responsibility for outcomes shaped by the choices of thousands of Indigenous leaders. And it reflects a gendered pattern in which women are blamed for the political actions of men. Historians now emphasize her agency, her coercion, and her ambiguous position, and Indigenous sources from the sixteenth century do not treat her as a villain.

Multiple Perspectives

Malintzin: How Different Groups Understood Her Role

Note: the viewpoints below are analytical reconstructions of positions documented in sources and scholarship, not quotations.

From the perspective of Cortés and Spanish chroniclers: An invaluable interpreter and informant, sometimes described in religious terms as a divinely guided convert. Spanish authors credited her with services to the Crown but rarely named the coercion that shaped her position or acknowledged how much of the diplomacy depended on her judgment.
From the perspective of Tlaxcalan painters and officials of the 1550s: A prominent participant in the alliance and the war, depicted in the Lienzo de Tlaxcala as an authoritative figure beside Cortés. Tlaxcalans emphasizing their own conquest service had reason to show the full scope of the campaign in which they, and she, had taken part.
From the perspective of nineteenth- and twentieth-century Mexican nationalism: Often a figure of betrayal or, in more recent reinterpretations, a symbol of the violent origins of mestizo identity. These readings reflect modern political projects more than evidence about 1519.
From the perspective of recent historians: An enslaved young woman who became an influential mediator under severe constraint, whose choices should be assessed in light of her limited options and the multi-layered politics of the period, and whose voice has to be recovered from sources that were mostly written by others.
Section 39.6

Veracruz, Cempoala, and the Totonac Alliance

Cortés's fleet sailed up the Gulf coast and anchored near San Juan de Ulúa around Easter 1519. Envoys from Moctezuma arrived with gifts, and the Spaniards, speaking through Malintzin, learned that the Mexica ruler wished to learn their intentions. The envoys returned with Cortés's request for an audience, and Moctezuma's reply, delivered in the following weeks, offered gifts but discouraged a visit. This early diplomacy reveals the Mexica approach: gather information, offer presents, avoid commitment, and observe. It also reveals that Cortés understood the importance of gold, which he used to enlarge his political case in Spain.

A critical legal move followed. Cortés had left Cuba under Velázquez's authority, which was now in doubt. To establish an independent basis for the expedition, he and his supporters founded a municipality, the Villa Rica de la Vera Cruz, and constituted a town council, or cabildo. The council then named Cortés its captain and chief justice, and in doing so claimed to derive his authority directly from the Spanish Crown rather than from the governor of Cuba. The council sent a letter and a large amount of treasure to the king, Charles V, in July 1519 and requested his confirmation. This was a legal device, and a risky one, but it gave Cortés a framework for acting independently and a justification for what followed.

Meanwhile, Cortés took the expedition to Cempoala, a Totonac town and the center of a polity on the Gulf coast that was a tributary of the Triple Alliance. The Totonac ruler, whom Spanish sources call the cacique of Cempoala, complained of Mexica tribute demands and the conduct of Mexica collectors. Accounts agree that, during the Spaniards' stay, Totonac leaders seized Mexica tribute officials and then, with Cortés's encouragement, committed themselves to resisting further payment. The sources conflict on how much initiative came from the Totonacs and how much from Cortés. A plausible reading is that the Totonacs, long resentful, saw in the Spaniards a source of leverage against their overlord, and that Cortés recognized the opportunity and exploited it, including by secretly releasing some of the Mexica prisoners to cultivate Moctezuma's goodwill.

The Totonac alliance supplied the expedition with food, carriers, and warriors, and with information about the road inland. It also tested the newcomers: the Totonacs risked Mexica retribution, and their decision shows a calculation that the Spaniards could provide protection. At about this time Cortés dealt with a faction of his own men who wished to return to Cuba by having the ships stripped and sunk or run aground, so that the expedition could not retreat. The popular image of “burning the ships” is a later embellishment; the point was that he removed the option of leaving, and he kept the ships' materials and crews.

Section 39.7

Tlaxcallan: War Before Alliance

In August 1519 Cortés set out for the interior with several hundred Spaniards and, according to Spanish accounts, a force of Totonac warriors and carriers. The route led through the highlands toward Tlaxcallan, a polity that stood apart from the empire. Tlaxcallan was not an altepetl ruled by a single king but a confederation of several divisions, such as Tizatlan, Ocotelulco, Tepeticpac, and Quiahuiztlan, governed by a council of nobles. Archaeological and documentary study suggests a political system with collective decision-making and a degree of institutional constraint on rulers unusual in Mesoamerica, though historians debate how far to describe it as egalitarian. What is clear is that Tlaxcallan had resisted the Triple Alliance for decades and preserved its independence.

In early September 1519 the Spaniards and their allies crossed the frontier. They were attacked. Over the next two weeks, a series of battles followed in which Tlaxcalan and allied Otomí forces assaulted the column repeatedly. Spanish accounts depict these battles as hard-fought, and Cortés himself reported being hard-pressed. The Spaniards were helped by horses, firearms, and steel in open ground, but they also suffered casualties and illness, and they took a defensive posture on several days. The fighting confirms that Tlaxcallan did not receive the Spaniards as liberators, nor did it regard them as supernatural. It treated them as dangerous strangers, possibly allied with Mexica enemies, and it tested them.

Inside Tlaxcallan there was a real debate. Later accounts, including Spanish narrators and the Tlaxcalan historians who wrote in the sixteenth century, describe leaders divided over whether to continue the fighting or to negotiate. Xicotencatl the Younger, a military commander, favored continued resistance; Xicotencatl the Elder and Maxixcatzin, senior figures, argued that an alliance with the newcomers offered a way to confront the Mexica. The sources do not reveal the full deliberations, and some details come from Tlaxcalans writing decades later with their own purposes. Nonetheless, they present a consistent picture of a polity that weighed options through council debate, rather than yielding to superior force.

The decision to negotiate reflected multiple considerations. The battles had shown the Spaniards to be formidable but not invincible. The Triple Alliance remained the dominant threat to Tlaxcallan's independence, and an enemy of the Mexica could be a useful ally. And the Spaniards, for their part, needed to end the fighting and secure a base. Peace talks followed, and in late September the Spaniards entered Tlaxcallan as allies. The progression from war to alliance matters: the alliance was not an automatic response to Spanish superiority but a negotiated settlement between parties that had tested one another and found a shared interest.

Section 39.8

The Tlaxcalan-Spanish Alliance

The alliance between Tlaxcallan and the Spanish expedition became the keystone of the entire conquest. It supplied the Spaniards with food, shelter, medical care, intelligence, and, above all, manpower. Tlaxcalan warriors fought in every major subsequent campaign. Tlaxcalan porters carried baggage, artillery, and later the dismantled brigantines, and Tlaxcalan women provided cooking and other support work that Spanish accounts rarely credit adequately. Without these contributions the expedition could not have moved beyond the coast, let alone besieged an island city.

What did each side expect? For the Spaniards, Tlaxcallan was a base, a source of soldiers, and an enemy of their enemy. For the Tlaxcalteca, the alliance offered a chance to weaken the Triple Alliance, recover access to goods and territory, and obtain a share in any spoils. They also expected gifts and recognition. Tlaxcalan lords gave daughters to Spanish captains to cement ties; Spanish sources mention that Xicotencatl the Elder gave a daughter to Pedro de Alvarado. Such marriages followed Mesoamerican diplomatic practice, though the Spaniards often regarded the women as concubines, and their status in the Spanish moral world was ambiguous.

The relationship was never one of equals. The Spaniards held weapons and horses that gave them real military leverage, and Cortés did not hesitate to impose his will. Yet the alliance was also not one of subordination. Tlaxcalan leaders acted autonomously, set conditions, and, as events would show, were willing to reconsider when the Spaniards suffered reverses. Later Tlaxcalan documents, including petitions to the Crown, justified their privileges by pointing to the terms of this early alliance, and the Spanish government granted Tlaxcala certain exemptions and titles on this basis. The Tlaxcalans were thus conscious negotiators who sought to secure their long-term interests through a pact with a rising power.

They also took risks. By marching with the Spaniards toward the Mexica capital they exposed themselves to defeat and retaliation, and as the expedition moved into territory dominated by their rivals, Tlaxcalan warriors fought in the vanguard of every engagement. The costs would be severe, in lives lost during the Noche Triste and the siege, in epidemic disease, and in the transformation of Tlaxcallan itself under colonial rule. The alliance achieved some of what its architects wanted, since the Triple Alliance was destroyed, but it also created new dependencies, and later generations of Tlaxcalans would struggle to maintain the privileges won in 1519.

Section 39.9

Cholula: Massacre and Contested Memory

In October 1519 the combined force of Spaniards, Tlaxcalteca, and Totonacs marched toward Cholula, a large city in the Puebla valley and one of the major religious and commercial centers of central Mexico. It was home to a great pyramid and to the cult of Quetzalcoatl and was a pilgrimage destination. Cholula was not a Tlaxcalan ally; it had closer ties to the Triple Alliance, and its people and the Tlaxcalteca were traditional rivals. Tlaxcalan leaders reportedly warned Cortés against the city, while Cholulan leaders and Mexica envoys invited him to visit. The Spaniards entered the city, leaving their Tlaxcalan allies for the most part outside, and were received for several days.

Then, around mid- to late October, the Spaniards attacked. In the course of a single day they and their allies killed a large number of Cholulans, many of them unarmed, in the city's courtyards and streets, and Tlaxcalan warriors, who entered the city, joined in killing and plundering. Cortés wrote that the Cholulans had planned an ambush and that he forestalled it. He reported a death toll on the order of several thousand, and other Spanish writers gave various figures. No reliable count exists. The city was damaged, and its political structure was reorganized under Spanish influence, with Cholula soon professing loyalty to the Spaniards.

The events at Cholula are an example of how the same episode could be narrated in incompatible ways, and they are an ideal case for training historical judgment. The following feature sets out the problem.

Historical Debate

Was There a Plot at Cholula?

The question: Did the leaders of Cholula, possibly in coordination with Moctezuma, plan to ambush and destroy the Spaniards, as Cortés and several Spanish narrators claimed? Or was the massacre a deliberate act of terror, intended to intimidate the Mexica and demonstrate the expedition's power?

Interpretation A — A plot probably existed, or was plausibly believed to exist: Spanish accounts agree broadly that Cholulan leaders were preparing to attack, that unusual preparations were observed, and that Malintzin received information from a local woman. Some historians accept that a Cholulan or Mexica plan of this kind is plausible, since hosting a foreign force inside a city invited consideration of its elimination. They note that Cholulan resources and Mexica interests converged.
Interpretation B — The “plot” was a justification: Others observe that the story appears in sources written by the perpetrators or their sympathizers, that Cortés was already under legal pressure to explain his conduct, and that Tlaxcalan rivalry with Cholula supplied a motive for provoking the attack. Indigenous accounts survive that treat the killing as an unprovoked massacre, though these were composed decades later. On this reading, the Spaniards and their allies used terror deliberately, and the plot story was invented or exaggerated afterward.

Evidence: The principal Spanish accounts (Cortés's letters, Bernal Díaz, and others) differ in details. Nahua sources, including Book XII of the Florentine Codex, are retrospective and also differ in emphasis. No independent contemporary Cholulan record survives that would settle the issue.

Points of disagreement: Disagreement concerns evidence and interpretation, rather than chronology. Both sides accept that a massacre occurred. What remains contested is the intent of the Cholulans, the role of Tlaxcalan allies, and the weight given to sources produced by the killers.

Why it matters: If a plot existed, the massacre can be read as a preemptive defense; if not, it becomes an early demonstration of a strategy of terror. The answer also shapes how historians read other Spanish claims of Indigenous treachery, which recur in the sources.

Student task: Identify what kind of evidence would be needed to determine whether a plot existed, and explain why the sources we have cannot supply it. Then discuss whether the question of the plot should alter our judgment of the killing of unarmed people.

Whatever the truth about intent, the effect of Cholula on the political situation was significant. Messages reached Tenochtitlan, and other communities drew conclusions about the risks of resisting or of hosting the invaders. Moctezuma's response was to continue diplomacy, and the Spaniards and their allies were permitted to approach the capital.

Section 39.10

Moctezuma II, Diplomacy, Omens, and the “Gods” Myth

Moctezuma Xocoyotzin faced a difficult decision. For months he had received reports from the coast and from the interior, and his policy was cautious: gather information, offer gifts, discourage a visit, and avoid a confrontation that might encourage his own tributaries to revolt. Spanish writers later explained this caution as fear or religious confusion. A more plausible explanation is that Moctezuma was evaluating a new kind of political actor, with unfamiliar military technology and an uncertain relationship to rival powers, at a time when the Triple Alliance had other concerns, and when the empire's own subjects were already showing signs of restiveness.

Policy debates within the Mexica court are not directly documented. Later Nahua narratives, notably Book XII of the Florentine Codex, describe a series of omens that preceded the Spanish arrival: comets, a burning temple, a weeping woman, and other portents. These accounts were set down decades after the conquest by authors who were explaining a catastrophe. Historians treat them as an Indigenous narrative tradition, shaped by hindsight, that expresses how survivors understood what had happened. They are not contemporary evidence that Mexica leaders were paralyzed by supernatural fears.

The most persistent claim in popular tradition is that the Mexica believed Cortés to be the returning god Quetzalcoatl, and that this belief explains Moctezuma's reception of the Spaniards. This claim is poorly supported. It appears first in Spanish writings produced after the conquest and in some Nahua texts of the same period, when the conquest needed explaining and when Spanish missionaries had an interest in portraying Indigenous religion as susceptible to Christian providence. Cortés's own letters do not describe any such belief, and Mexica terms for the Spaniards, as recorded in Nahuatl, such as teotl, could be applied to people or things that were awe-inspiring or sacred, and do not necessarily mean “god” in a European sense. Many historians now regard the Quetzalcoatl narrative as a later construction, and in any case Moctezuma's actions are better explained by ordinary diplomatic calculation.

Moctezuma's decision to admit the Spaniards to Tenochtitlan has been judged a blunder in hindsight. Several explanations are possible, and they are not mutually exclusive. He may have wanted to observe them closely and keep them under surveillance. He may have seen them as potential allies against rivals, or as a force that, if admitted and then contained, could be controlled more easily than if left to roam among restive tributaries. He may have underestimated them. The sources do not permit certainty, and it is important to remember that he could not know how events would unfold.

Section 39.11

Entering Tenochtitlan, 8 November 1519

On 8 November 1519 the Spaniards and their allies entered Tenochtitlan by the causeway from Iztapalapa. Spanish eyewitnesses were astonished by what they saw: a city rising from the water, connected to the shore by broad causeways with bridges that could be raised, filled with canoes, canals, temples, and plazas. Cortés and Bernal Díaz both wrote about the experience, comparing it to the cities of European romance. Estimates of Tenochtitlan's population vary widely among modern scholars, but it was clearly among the largest cities in the world at that time, and the surrounding Basin of Mexico supported a very dense population.

The city must be understood as a functioning metropolis with its own institutions, and not merely as the stage for Cortés. Its core was the ceremonial precinct with the Templo Mayor, dedicated to Huitzilopochtli and Tlaloc, surrounded by palaces of the ruling dynasty and priestly colleges. The city was divided into districts organized around calpulli, each with its own temple, school, and market, and governed by officials appointed by the ruler. Its food came in part from the chinampas, the raised agricultural plots in the lake, and in part from tribute, and its markets, especially the great market at Tlatelolco, drew vendors and buyers from across the region. Spanish observers marveled at the variety and order of the market, though they also recorded features that shocked them, including the ritual killings at the temples.

Moctezuma received the Spaniards at the edge of the city. Both Spanish and Nahua accounts describe the meeting, although they differ in details, and the primary source feature in Section 39.21 compares them. Moctezuma lodged the visitors in a palace complex near the center of the city that had belonged to his father, Axayacatl. The Spaniards and their allies, now numbering perhaps several thousand with Tlaxcalan and other contingents, were thus lodged inside a city of far greater size than their own force, with causeways that could be cut, and they depended on the goodwill, or the strategic calculation, of their host.

Section 39.12

The Captivity of Moctezuma and the Spanish Occupation

Within days of arrival, Cortés took an extraordinary step: he seized Moctezuma and held him under guard in the Spanish quarters. Spanish accounts justify the act with reference to an attack on Spanish soldiers on the coast, in which several Spaniards were killed, which Cortés presented as proof of the ruler's treachery. Whatever the pretext, the effect was to convert a diplomatic visit into a hostage situation. For Cortés, who had no authority to be in Tenochtitlan and a very small force relative to the city, the captive ruler was both protection and instrument.

The following months are among the least clear in the history of the conquest. Spanish sources report that Moctezuma continued to rule, summoned tribute, and instructed his officials, and that he acknowledged the sovereignty of Charles V in a formal ceremony. Cortés demanded gold and valuables, and the Spaniards collected and melted a large amount of treasure. Nahua accounts describe the Spaniards as intrusive and violent, and describe them searching for hidden treasure in the palaces. How far Moctezuma governed freely and how far he governed under duress is uncertain, but the arrangement was clearly unstable.

The Mexica political system itself did not rest on one man. Moctezuma was tlatoani, but he governed with a council, and key roles such as the cihuacoatl, a senior official, and the leaders of the military orders and calpulli remained active. Members of the Mexica nobility, including his brother Cuitláhuac and other relatives, observed the situation with growing alarm. The Spaniards also used their time to explore, to map the city, and to gather information about its defenses. They placed a chapel and cross in a temple, an act that provoked resentment among the Mexica priests, according to Spanish accounts.

Elsewhere in the Basin, rival factions in Texcoco quarreled over succession, and some Texcocan nobles, whom the Spaniards courted, would later become important allies. At the same time, tributary communities across the empire were watching. A ruler held hostage by a few hundred foreigners was a ruler whose authority could be questioned, and the Spaniards' ability to hold the situation in balance depended on the continued uncertainty of Mexica elites about what to do. That uncertainty ended when events outside the city forced Cortés to leave.

Section 39.13

Narváez, Tóxcatl, and the Collapse of Accommodation

In April 1520 a second Spanish force landed on the Gulf coast. It was led by Pánfilo de Narváez, sent by Governor Velázquez with instructions to arrest Cortés and take control of the expedition. The force was larger than Cortés's, with several hundred men and a significant number of horses and guns. Cortés took part of his garrison and marched to the coast, leaving Pedro de Alvarado in command in Tenochtitlan with a small force. In late May he surprised and defeated Narváez near Cempoala in a night attack, then persuaded most of the defeated men to join him. The result was a reinforced Cortés, though the reinforcements brought something else with them, as Section 39.16 explains.

Meanwhile, in Tenochtitlan, tension had been rising. The festival of Tóxcatl, honoring Huitzilopochtli and Tezcatlipoca, was approaching, and the Mexica nobility asked Alvarado for permission to hold it. Spanish accounts say Alvarado, fearing an attack, or having been told of a plot, struck first. Nahua accounts describe the Spaniards closing the exits of the sacred precinct and attacking the dancers, drummers, and celebrants, many of them nobles and warriors, who were unarmed. Numbers are unreliable, but the killing was extensive, and the effect was to shatter whatever trust had existed.

The Mexica rose in force. They attacked the Spaniards' quarters, cut off supplies, and besieged the garrison, which was saved from collapse only because Moctezuma, still in custody, was used to restrain his people, at least for a time. Cortés returned in late June with his reinforced army, and his entry into the city was allowed, probably because the Mexica wished to trap the Spaniards inside rather than let them escape. The Spaniards found themselves besieged in their own lodging.

The massacre at Tóxcatl broke the political accommodation that had existed in the city. Before it, the Mexica elite had been divided between those favoring cautious engagement and those hostile to the intruders. After it, the hardliners prevailed. Alvarado's action is sometimes attributed to personal violence or panic, and both Spanish and Nahua sources agree that he bore responsibility. Cortés, in his letters, defended his lieutenant. The consequences were far larger than any individual decision, since they ended Moctezuma's usefulness as a go-between and placed the Spaniards in a position they could not hold.

Section 39.14

Moctezuma's Death and the Noche Triste

Moctezuma died in late June 1520, and the cause is disputed. Spanish accounts say that he was struck by stones thrown by his own people when he tried to speak to them from a rooftop, and that he died of his wounds days later. Nahua accounts, notably in the Florentine Codex, say that the Spaniards killed him and his fellow captives before leaving the city. Both versions have motives: the Spanish account placed responsibility on the Mexica and exonerated Cortés, while the Nahua account blamed the Spaniards and, implicitly, preserved the ruler's dignity. Historians cannot resolve the question with certainty, though most agree that his authority had collapsed before his death. The Mexica selected his brother Cuitláhuac as the next tlatoani.

Cortés recognized that the position was untenable. The Mexica had cut the bridges in the causeways, access to food and water was limited, and the garrison was under constant attack. On the night of 30 June to 1 July 1520, the Spaniards and their Tlaxcalan and other allies tried to escape along the Tlacopan causeway. They were discovered, and canoes carrying Mexica warriors attacked the column from the lake while others pressed from behind. The Spaniards, weighed down by gold they were carrying, were caught at the gaps where bridges had been removed, and many drowned or were killed. The episode is known as the Noche Triste, the “sad night,” in Spanish tradition.

Spanish losses numbered in the hundreds, and Indigenous allied losses were larger, though the figures vary greatly among sources and cannot be settled. Cortés lost much of his artillery, many horses, and the treasure he had gathered. The Spaniards who survived retreated eastward, wounded and exhausted. From the Mexica perspective, this was a major victory, a successful expulsion of invaders who had occupied their city for months. It is a reminder that the story of the conquest includes a decisive Indigenous military triumph, and that the outcome was not predetermined.

The Noche Triste also exposed the structure of the Spanish force. Many of the dead were Tlaxcalan and other Indigenous allies who had borne the brunt of the rear guard and had fewer escape routes. Spanish narratives emphasize Spanish losses and name individual captains, while allied dead were often recorded only in aggregate. This pattern of unequal remembrance runs through the sources, and Section 39.20 returns to it.

Section 39.15

Retreat, Otumba, and the Renewal of the Coalition

The retreat from Tenochtitlan was a march of several days around the northern edge of the lake system, harassed by Mexica pursuers. Around 7 July 1520, at Otumba, the Spaniards and their allies faced a large Mexica and allied army blocking the way. In the battle that followed, the column was nearly overwhelmed, and according to Spanish accounts Cortés and a few horsemen made a charge that killed a commander and broke the enemy's resolve. This episode is widely told in the Spanish tradition as an example of Spanish courage; historians observe that the details are impossible to verify and that the Mexica forces were probably also exhausted and may have broken off the pursuit for other reasons.

The Spaniards reached Tlaxcallan on about 8 July. Here a crucial question arose. The Spaniards were weak, many were wounded, and their expedition had been mauled. Some Tlaxcalan leaders, including Xicotencatl the Younger according to the sources, urged an end to the alliance, and the expedition's fate lay in Tlaxcalan hands. The Mexica sent envoys proposing that Tlaxcallan join them against the Spaniards, offering relief from tribute pressure. The Tlaxcalan council debated and, led by Maxixcatzin and Xicotencatl the Elder, chose to maintain the alliance, though on terms that were probably renegotiated. They gave shelter and care to the Spaniards, who remained for several weeks to recover.

Why did the Tlaxcalteca renew the alliance when the Spaniards were at their weakest? The sources suggest several reasons: enduring hostility toward the Mexica, the expectation that the Spaniards could be useful again, the belief that Mexica victory would restore pressure on Tlaxcallan, and the commitments made in 1519. Their decision, like their original alliance, was an act of political judgment. Cortés also demonstrated flexibility, promising them tribute exemptions and a share of conquered territory, promises that would shape later Tlaxcalan claims.

Cause and Effect

Why the Expulsion of 1520 Did Not End the Invasion

Long-term causes of Indigenous opposition to the Triple Alliance

Decades of tribute demands, military campaigns, and political pressure on tributaries and independent neighbors had created widespread resentment, which gave the Spaniards a reservoir of potential allies.

Short-term causes

The Tóxcatl massacre, the collapse of the Spanish position in Tenochtitlan, and the Spaniards' flight forced them back on the support of those already allied.

Immediate effects

Heavy Spanish and allied losses, the loss of treasure and artillery, but also a Mexica failure to destroy the retreating force or to win over Tlaxcallan.

Long-term consequences

The renewal of the alliance on revised terms, the building of a larger coalition, reinforcements from the Caribbean, and the arrival of smallpox allowed the campaign to resume in 1521.

Historical complexity: No single factor explains why the invasion resumed. The decisions of Tlaxcalan and other leaders, Mexica strategic choices, disease, and Spanish adaptability all mattered, and none by itself would have been sufficient. The war could have ended in 1520.

During the second half of 1520 Cortés rebuilt his forces. He campaigned in the Tepeaca region, where he and his allies defeated local forces allied with the Mexica and founded a Spanish settlement, and he began to systematically attack communities in the zone between Tlaxcallan and the Basin. In this phase Spanish forces branded captives from rebellious communities as slaves, a practice with Caribbean precedent, and the slave-taking would recur later in the conquest, a subject treated in Chapter 53. Reinforcements also arrived from the Caribbean, including men and horses from ships that had landed on the Gulf coast, and these strengthened the force considerably.

Section 39.16

Smallpox, 1520–1521: A Military and Political Shock

Smallpox, a disease to which the people of the Americas had no prior exposure, reached central Mexico in 1520. Spanish accounts attribute its introduction to a member of Narváez's expedition who carried the disease to the Gulf coast, from where it spread inland along trade and travel routes. Nahua sources describe a great sickness, marked by skin eruptions, that struck the Basin in the second half of 1520. The Nahua term, translated in Spanish sources as huey zahuatl, “great rash,” refers to this epidemic.

Its political effects were immediate. Cuitláhuac, who had succeeded Moctezuma and led the expulsion of the Spaniards, died of smallpox after a reign of only several months, and he was succeeded by his young cousin or nephew Cuauhtémoc. Other leaders and nobles died as well. The epidemic killed large numbers of ordinary people, disrupting households, food production, and the military mobilization on which the empire depended. Nahua accounts describe a society in which people were too sick to tend to the ill or bury the dead, with serious effects on food supply and civic life.

These effects must be described with care. The epidemic struck Mexica and Spaniards' allies alike, and it probably weakened Tlaxcalan and Texcocan communities as well as those of the Triple Alliance. It did not strike only one side. It also did not by itself cause the fall of Tenochtitlan, since the city resisted for months after the epidemic had peaked. What the disease did was alter the balance: it removed leaders at a critical time, reduced the capacity of the Mexica to rally their tributaries, and added to the pressures of the siege. Historians caution against treating smallpox as an autonomous agent that conquered Mexico, because that account erases the human decisions, alliances, and military actions that gave disease its political effects.

The full dimensions of the demographic catastrophe in Mesoamerica, including the transmission of disease, the scale of mortality, subsequent epidemics, and regional variation, are the subject of Chapter 40, and the figures that circulate in popular accounts are the subject of considerable scholarly dispute. For the purposes of this chapter, the point is that the epidemic was part of the political and military history of the conquest, and that Indigenous communities experienced it as a catastrophe at the same time as they were fighting, allying, and negotiating.

Section 39.17

Building the Final Coalition: Texcoco, Tlaxcala, and the Brigantines

At the end of December 1520 Cortés returned to the Basin of Mexico, entering Texcoco, the eastern partner of the Triple Alliance. Texcoco was in turmoil. The death of its ruler Cacama, who had been taken into Spanish custody earlier, and rival claims to succession had divided the nobility, and a faction led by Ixtlilxochitl, a Texcocan prince with his own ambitions, was willing to support the Spaniards. The city thus became the base for the campaign. Its shore position, its resources, and its status as a core city of the Triple Alliance made its defection a major political blow to Tenochtitlan.

Over the following months Cortés and his allies worked to isolate the Mexica capital. Spanish and allied forces attacked and, through a mixture of force and negotiation, gained the allegiance of communities around the lake. Some, like Chalco, were hostile to the Mexica and joined readily; others were coerced. The Mexica, under Cuauhtémoc, attempted to hold their tributaries and to send out their own forces, with mixed success. By early 1521 the campaign had become a struggle for control of the lake and its shores.

The key technical innovation was the brigantine. Cortés's carpenter Martín López oversaw the building of thirteen small, shallow-draft sailing vessels, intended to dominate the lake and defeat the Mexica canoe fleet. The vessels were constructed at Tlaxcallan, using timber and labor supplied by Tlaxcalans, and then dismantled and carried overland, in pieces, to Texcoco, where they were reassembled and launched in April 1521. Spanish accounts credit Tlaxcalan porters in the thousands with this work, and while the numbers cannot be confirmed, the logistical effort was enormous. It could not have been accomplished without a large and willing Indigenous labor force and a functioning supply system.

This logistics system is easily overlooked. An army of tens of thousands of men needs food, water, shelter, and transport, and the sources suggest that Tlaxcalan and other Indigenous authorities supplied much of it, along with the labor to dig a canal at Texcoco, build bridges, and move materiel. In effect, the campaign was an Indigenous military operation to which the Spaniards contributed key weapons, commanders, and tactics. Later Spanish writers, who credited the achievement to Cortés and his captains, minimized this contribution, and recovering it is part of the work of modern scholarship.

Section 39.18

The Siege of Tenochtitlan and Tlatelolco

The siege began in late May 1521. Cortés divided his force into several divisions, each assigned to one of the causeways, and used the brigantines to control the lake. A crucial early step was cutting the aqueduct that brought fresh water from Chapultepec, which reduced the city's supply. The Mexica responded by digging wells and by attacking the Spanish positions in sorties, and they used canoes and traps on the causeways to inflict losses. In late June, in one of the most serious Spanish reverses of the siege, a Spanish column was drawn into an ambush on a causeway, and dozens of Spaniards were captured and sacrificed, shaking Spanish morale and nearly ending Cortés's own command.

Spanish accounts often describe the siege as a Spanish triumph, but the numerical reality was different. Cortés reported commanding several hundred Spaniards, with numbers rising to near a thousand as reinforcements arrived, alongside very large numbers of Indigenous allies, which he estimated in the tens of thousands, and some later accounts estimated much higher. The Indigenous forces came from Tlaxcallan, Texcoco, Chalco, Huexotzinco, Cholula, and many other communities, and they were often organized under their own commanders. They fought for their own reasons, many of them settling scores with the Mexica, and their participation meant that the siege was in large part a war of Nahua communities against Tenochtitlan.

The Mexica and Tlatelolca defended their city with remarkable determination. They adapted their tactics to the brigantines, defended their houses street by street, and used the canals and canoes to harass the besiegers. Still, the combination of blockade, disease, and starvation weighed heavily. The Spaniards and allies gradually advanced along the causeways and into the city, systematically destroying houses to fill canals and clear lines of attack, a tactic that caused extensive destruction. As the Mexica lost territory, the surviving population was compressed into a shrinking area around Tlatelolco, where the market district became the final redoubt.

Everyday Life and Social History

The City Under Siege: Households, Hunger, and Survival

For the civilians of Tenochtitlan and Tlatelolco, the siege was a prolonged ordeal. Surviving Nahua narratives, written down decades later, describe the loss of water, the search for food, and the sheer density of people crowded into the final districts. They speak of people eating whatever they could find, of the sick and wounded without care, and of women and children among the dead. These accounts reflect the memory of survivors and cannot be read as exact records, but they are consistent with what is known of the effects of blockade and disease on a dense urban population.

The siege also fell unequally. Nobles and warriors bore particular burdens as defenders and leaders and as the targets of Spanish and allied attacks. Women were responsible for much of the household labor under extreme conditions and were also at risk of capture. After the fall of the city, many survivors were enslaved or taken as servants by Spanish and allied conquerors, and others fled to nearby communities, where they placed further pressure on resources. Allied Indigenous communities that supplied the army also suffered casualties and dislocations that rarely appear in Spanish accounts. The siege thus belongs to social history as well as military history, because it altered households, labor, and community structure across the Basin.

The violence of the final weeks was extreme. Spanish accounts, including Cortés's, describe the slaughter of civilians by allied forces and claim that Cortés attempted to restrain them, though this claim cannot be verified and has been viewed skeptically by some historians, who note that the Spaniards also participated in and directed the destruction. The numbers of the dead are uncertain, and estimates vary widely. What is clear is that the siege resulted in a very large loss of life and the near-total destruction of a major city.

Section 39.19

Cuauhtémoc and 13 August 1521

Cuauhtémoc, a young noble in his twenties by most accounts, became tlatoani in late 1520 after Cuitláhuac's death. He inherited a city under blockade and a diminished empire, and he led its defense for the remainder of the siege. Spanish sources credit him with refusing offers of surrender and with maintaining resistance when the case seemed hopeless. Nahua accounts, particularly from Tlatelolco, emphasize his leadership and also record debates and tensions among the defenders. The historical figure is difficult to separate from later legend, since nineteenth-century Mexican nationalism made him a symbol of Indigenous resistance, and the evidence for his personal conduct is thin.

On 13 August 1521, in the final assault on Tlatelolco, the defense collapsed. Cuauhtémoc attempted to escape by canoe across the lake and was captured by a Spanish brigantine commanded by García Holguín. Spanish accounts record that he was brought before Cortés and spoke of his resistance and its failure. Whether the exact words they attribute to him were spoken is uncertain, since they appear in sources written by Spaniards and filtered through interpretation. The capture ended organized resistance in the city.

The date should be understood precisely. It marks the fall of the Triple Alliance capital, a decisive political and military event. It does not mark the end of the conquest of Mesoamerica, nor the end of Indigenous political life in Tenochtitlan. In the weeks and months that followed, the Spaniards and their allies occupied the ruins, and the survivors were dispersed. Cuauhtémoc remained in Spanish custody. He was tortured by Spaniards searching for hidden treasure and was later taken on Cortés's expedition to Honduras, where he was executed in 1525 on charges of conspiracy.

Section 39.20

Who Conquered Tenochtitlan? Indigenous Allies and Competing Memories

The question in the title is not rhetorical, and it has no single answer. In the Spanish narrative that dominated European histories, Cortés conquered Tenochtitlan with a small band of Spanish soldiers. That narrative was reinforced by the Cartas de relación, which Cortés wrote to promote his claims, and by later histories, such as the one by Francisco López de Gómara, Cortés's chaplain, who had not been present. In these accounts, Indigenous allies appear as auxiliaries to a Spanish victory.

Indigenous communities told the story differently, and for good reason. Under Spanish rule, rewards were distributed according to service: encomiendas, tribute exemptions, titles, and privileges went to those who could demonstrate that they had served the Crown. Spaniards compiled formal petitions of service, called probanzas or relaciones de méritos, and Indigenous communities and nobles composed their own. The Tlaxcalans were especially active, and the Lienzo de Tlaxcala, a large painted narrative made around 1552 and known today through later copies, presents the conquest as a joint enterprise in which Tlaxcalans were conquerors in their own right. Tlaxcala's leaders used this version in petitions to the Crown, and the city received privileges that reflected its claims.

Texcocans likewise emphasized their contributions, and writers descended from Texcocan nobility, such as the historian Fernando de Alva Ixtlilxochitl, in the early seventeenth century, wrote accounts that credited their ancestor's alliance with Cortés as essential. These accounts are valuable but are shaped by family and community interest. Other communities, including Cholula, Huexotzinco, Chalco, and Cempoala, made their own claims, and several of them complained that Spanish chroniclers and later Tlaxcalan historians had neglected them.

What emerges is a contested memory in which each group sought recognition and each version served present needs. This does not mean that all versions are equally accurate, but it means that the issue of who conquered Tenochtitlan was itself a political question in the colonial period, and the answers given reflect the interests of the people who gave them. The most defensible historical conclusion is that the city fell to a coalition in which Indigenous participants were numerically dominant and strategically indispensable, and in which Spaniards provided specific military technologies, leadership, and a legal and political framework that allowed the coalition to cohere. The Spanish claim to sole credit was largely a product of the colonial system that Spaniards built afterward.

Section 39.21

How Do We Know? Indigenous and Spanish Histories of the Conquest

The principal Spanish sources include Cortés's five Cartas de relación, written between 1519 and 1526 to the king and designed to justify his actions; Bernal Díaz del Castillo's Historia verdadera de la conquista de la Nueva España, written decades later by a participant who sought to correct other accounts and credit the common soldiers; the history by Gómara, based on Cortés's own information; and a critique by Bartolomé de las Casas, who condemned Spanish violence in his Brevísima relación of 1552. Each has strengths and limits. Cortés was an eyewitness but wrote to protect himself. Díaz was an eyewitness but wrote from memory long after. Las Casas was a polemicist.

Indigenous sources are equally varied. The Florentine Codex, compiled by the Franciscan friar Bernardino de Sahagún with Nahua collaborators at the Colegio de Santa Cruz in Tlatelolco, contains in Book XII a conquest narrative based on testimony from elderly Nahua men, gathered in the mid-1550s and revised in 1585. It is written in Nahuatl and illustrated with images, and it is the most extensive Indigenous account of the conquest. Its perspective is largely that of Tlatelolco, a community with its own grievances against Tenochtitlan as well as against the Spaniards. The Annals of Tlatelolco, an early Nahuatl text, also records a Tlatelolcan view. The Lienzo de Tlaxcala presents a Tlaxcalan perspective. Maya, Mixtec, Zapotec, and other sources add further angles.

These sources do not agree, and they should not be expected to. A Tlatelolcan author might emphasize the failures of Tenochtitlan's leadership; a Tlaxcalan painter might emphasize alliance and service; a Spanish soldier might emphasize hardship and heroism. The phrase “the Indigenous perspective” is therefore misleading, because it implies a single viewpoint. Students should instead think of a plurality of Indigenous perspectives, each shaped by community, status, time of writing, and audience. Reading them against one another, and against Spanish sources, allows a more complete if still partial reconstruction.

Primary Source Window

Two Accounts of the First Meeting of Cortés and Moctezuma, 8 November 1519

Creators: Hernán Cortés (Spanish, writing in 1520) and Nahua informants of Tlatelolco recorded with Bernardino de Sahagún (Nahuatl, mid-1550s, revised 1585) · Types: A letter to the king, and a retrospective narrative gathered from elderly eyewitnesses and community members.

Context: Cortés's second letter was written after the Noche Triste to justify his conduct and persuade the Crown that his actions served royal interests. Book XII of the Florentine Codex was produced about thirty-five years after the events, during a period of Spanish ecclesiastical supervision, by Nahua scholars and their elders who remembered, or had heard, what had happened.

Paraphrase of Cortés's account: Cortés describes being met at the edge of the city by Moctezuma, borne in a litter and attended by lords, and exchanging greetings and gifts. He presents the encounter as a courteous reception that opened the way to a formal acknowledgment of Spanish royal authority, and he emphasizes the ruler's generosity and the splendor of his surroundings. (Paraphrase; not a quotation.)

Paraphrase of the Nahua account: The Nahua narrative describes Moctezuma and his nobles greeting the strangers with gifts and speeches, and it portrays the Spaniards' behavior as intrusive and acquisitive, emphasizing their interest in gold and their weapons. It conveys unease and uncertainty, and reflects the knowledge of the later disaster. (Paraphrase; not a quotation.)

Analysis: The two accounts agree on the basic fact of the meeting and the exchange of gifts, and they diverge in tone and emphasis. Cortés presents courtesy and submission, which supported his claim that Moctezuma had recognized Spanish authority. The Nahua account, composed after the conquest, presents the encounter as the beginning of a catastrophe.

Limitations: Neither account is a neutral record. Cortés was writing to defend himself; the Nahua narrators were reconstructing events decades later, under Spanish supervision, and may have been influenced by what they knew had followed. Neither can tell us what Moctezuma privately thought.

Perspective: The first is the perspective of the invading commander; the second is that of survivors of a conquered community. Missing are the voices of Totonac, Tlaxcalan, and other allies who were present and the voices of ordinary Mexica people.

Section 39.22

The Conquest Did Not End at Tenochtitlan

The idea that Mexico was conquered in 1521 is geographically false. Mesoamerica extended far beyond the Basin of Mexico, and the fall of Tenochtitlan was followed by decades of campaigns, negotiated submissions, rebellions, and colonial settlement whose chronology differed region by region. This section surveys three major examples.

In Michoacán, the Purépecha state, centered on Tzintzuntzan, had remained independent of the Triple Alliance and had received Mexica envoys seeking help in 1520, when the Purépecha ruler, the cazonci, declined to join them. After the fall of Tenochtitlan, a Spanish expedition under Cristóbal de Olid in 1522 led the cazonci Tangaxoan II to submit to Spanish authority without a major battle. Michoacán then entered a new colonial phase, in which Spanish officials, encomenderos, and missionaries reorganized settlements and landscapes. The state's subjugation was incomplete and violent, and in 1530 the cazonci was executed by Nuño de Guzmán, a Spanish official, in a notorious episode.

In Oaxaca, Spanish expeditions in the early 1520s entered a world of Mixtec and Zapotec kingdoms. Some rulers allied with the newcomers, others resisted, and the region saw a mixture of warfare and diplomacy. Cortés received a large grant of Oaxaca territory in the late 1520s, which he used to build a personal domain, and Spanish settlement concentrated in the valley around the town later called Antequera. Indigenous rulers and communities continued to maintain local authority within the colonial system, and Mixtec and Zapotec elites left rich documentation, including painted histories and alphabetic texts, of their strategies.

In Guatemala and the Maya regions, Pedro de Alvarado led a Spanish campaign beginning in 1524, accompanied by thousands of Nahua and other Mexican allies. The K'iche' were defeated in 1524, and the Kaqchikel initially allied with the Spaniards before rebelling against their exactions. Resistance continued in the highlands and in the lowlands. In Yucatán, Francisco de Montejo and his son of the same name began campaigns in 1527, and Spanish control of the northern peninsula was only consolidated in the early 1540s with the founding of Mérida. Some Maya polities remained independent much longer; the Itza kingdom of Nojpetén in the Petén lowlands was not conquered until 1697.

This pattern shows that the conquest was a long process, with Indigenous allies from central Mexico serving as colonizers and settlers in other regions, and with local polities negotiating or resisting for years. The Nahua and Tlaxcalan soldiers who marched with Alvarado, for example, often settled in the regions they helped conquer, and their descendants preserved this history in petitions and chronicles. The northern frontier, with its different dynamics of mission, mining, and warfare, is the subject of Chapter 42.

Section 39.23

From Altepetl to New Spain: Indigenous Politics After Conquest

After 1521, the Spaniards built a new political order on the ruins of the old one, and they did so with extensive Indigenous participation. Cortés decided to rebuild Mexico City on the site of Tenochtitlan, and the Spanish city rose over the old ceremonial center, with a gridded Spanish core and Indigenous districts around it. In October 1522 Charles V named Cortés governor and captain general of New Spain, as the territory was now called, and the Crown gradually introduced its own institutions: a high court, the Audiencia, in 1528, and a viceroy, Antonio de Mendoza, in 1535. Mendicant friars, beginning with the Franciscans in 1524, undertook conversion, a process examined in Chapter 54.

Spanish rule depended on Indigenous communities and elites. The Spaniards lacked the numbers to govern directly, and they relied on existing local rulers to collect tribute, supply labor, and keep order. Cortés and later officials distributed Indigenous communities in encomienda, a grant that entitled a Spaniard to tribute and labor from a community in exchange for a duty of protection and instruction in Christianity. The system, transplanted from the Caribbean, was exploitative and often brutal, and Indigenous people resisted, petitioned, and litigated against it. Tribute, which had existed before the conquest, was transformed into payments in goods, money, and labor under Spanish rules, and in some cases was increased.

Historical Vocabulary
Native Cabildo (cabildo indígena)

Definition: A town council of the Spanish type, with a governor, mayors, and councilmen, established in Indigenous communities in New Spain from roughly the 1520s and 1530s and increasingly common by the middle of the sixteenth century.

Historical context: The native cabildo gave existing altepetl and their ruling lineages a form that Spanish law recognized. Offices were often filled by members of traditional noble houses, though officeholding and rotation sometimes departed from earlier practice.

Why it matters: It was the means by which Indigenous communities governed themselves under Spanish rule and negotiated with colonial authorities.

Potential misunderstanding: The cabildo was neither a purely Spanish imposition nor an unchanged continuation of earlier government. It was a hybrid, shaped by both.

Indigenous political life continued within and beside the Spanish city. Even in Mexico City, an Indigenous government of Tenochtitlan survived: the former capital was divided into four districts, given Christian names such as San Juan Moyotlan and Santa María Cuepopan, with Indigenous governors and councils, and Tlatelolco retained its own government. The first governors included former Mexica nobles who had cooperated with Cortés, and later governors, such as Diego de Alvarado Huanitzin in 1538, came from the old royal lineage. These officials dealt with the Spanish authorities, litigated over land and tribute, and sponsored church building and local festivals. Elsewhere, altepetl such as Tlaxcala, Cholula, and Texcoco developed their own cabildos and secured Spanish recognition of their privileges, with Tlaxcala receiving particularly favorable treatment because of its wartime role.

The costs were severe. Epidemics, the subject of Chapter 40, devastated Indigenous populations in the decades after the conquest. Forced labor, tribute demands, land seizures, and enslavement of captives caused further hardship. Conversion campaigns attacked Indigenous religious institutions and ritual practice, though many Indigenous people adapted Christian forms to their own purposes. Indigenous elites gained new roles but also faced pressure from Spanish officials and from commoners who resented their privileges. The system was unequal, and Spaniards held the power to enforce it.

Continuity and Change Over Time

The Altepetl Before and After 1521

What Changed

  • The Triple Alliance and its tributary network were dismantled, and the altepetl now answered to Spanish authorities rather than to Mexica overlords.
  • Rulers became governors and councilmen in Spanish-style cabildos, and Christian names and offices were added to their titles.
  • Tribute and labor were redirected to encomenderos, the Crown, and the Church, and were increasingly reckoned in money and Spanish categories.
  • Populations declined sharply in the following decades, and communities were sometimes relocated or consolidated.

What Stayed the Same

  • The altepetl itself persisted as the basic unit of local government, identity, and territory.
  • Noble lineages often retained leadership, and the Nahuatl language remained the language of local politics and, increasingly, of alphabetic writing.
  • Communities continued to use calpulli or similar subdivisions for tribute and labor, and maintained their own histories and claims to land.
  • Indigenous leaders continued to negotiate, litigate, and petition within the colonial system.

When and how: Change was uneven, beginning immediately after 1521 and continuing through the century. Elites and urban communities experienced the transformation most directly, while rural communities often experienced greater continuity in daily life, until epidemics and relocation disrupted them. The pattern matters because it explains why Indigenous communities remained historical actors after the conquest, and why the story of Indigenous North America does not end in 1521.

The transformation of Indigenous political society after 1521 was therefore a story of constrained continuity. The Triple Alliance was gone, and the Mexica empire with it. But altepetl, noble houses, and community councils endured, reshaped into corporate entities within a colonial system. Indigenous leaders learned the Spanish legal system and used it, and the Nahuatl-language records they produced are today among the richest sources for the period. The conquest of 1519–1521 inaugurated a long era of negotiation, resistance, and adaptation, whose later chapters appear throughout this textbook, including in Chapter 141, which follows Indigenous Mexico into the present.

Section 39.24

Chapter Review and Historical Thinking Checkpoint

This section consolidates the chapter. It begins with a summary, then provides review materials, source and map exercises, discussion and writing assignments, a library research project, and a closing reflection.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that the Spanish conquest of central Mexico was a Mesoamerican political and military struggle rather than the achievement of a few hundred Europeans against a unified “Aztec nation.” The Triple Alliance governed through tribute and the threat of force over a landscape of altepetl, and its demands had created resentment among tributaries and enmity among independent neighbors. That structure gave a small foreign force room to maneuver, and it explains why Indigenous peoples took part in the war on both sides.

The expeditions of 1517 and 1518 supplied Spaniards with information, and Cortés's 1519 expedition drew on Caribbean resources and legal improvisation. At Centla the Spaniards acquired Malintzin, whose command of Nahuatl and Maya languages, and whose political judgment, made sustained diplomacy possible. Her position, as an enslaved woman who became an indispensable mediator, shows how coercion and agency coexisted, and her later reputation shows how modern politics reshapes memory.

Indigenous alliances were the decisive structure of the campaign. The Totonacs of Cempoala saw leverage against Mexica tribute collectors. Tlaxcallan, an independent collective polity, fought the Spaniards before deciding after internal debate that alliance served its long-standing opposition to the Triple Alliance. Each of these decisions was an act of political judgment, and Tlaxcalan support in particular supplied the manpower, food, and logistics that made the invasion possible.

Violence was central to the campaign. The Cholula massacre of October 1519 remains contested in its causes, with sources that cannot settle whether a plot existed. The Tóxcatl massacre of May 1520 destroyed the accommodation inside Tenochtitlan. The siege of 1521 killed very large numbers of people and destroyed much of the city. These events were carried out by Spaniards and by Indigenous allies, and responsibility should be assigned precisely.

The war was not a continuous Spanish advance. The Spaniards entered Tenochtitlan as guests, became captors, and were expelled in the Noche Triste, a major Mexica military success. The Tlaxcalan council's decision to renew the alliance in July 1520 kept the invasion alive. This reversal is a reminder that the outcome was contingent. Claims that the Mexica took Cortés for a god are poorly supported and reflect later narrative traditions, and Moctezuma's choices are better understood as diplomacy under uncertainty.

Smallpox arrived in 1520 and killed leaders and ordinary people, disrupting households and mobilization. The disease was a significant element of the war, but it did not conquer Mexico alone, since it struck several communities and the siege required months of fighting by a large coalition. The fuller demographic story belongs to Chapter 40. The brigantines, built at Tlaxcallan and carried to Texcoco by Indigenous labor, and the defection of Texcocan factions, gave the coalition the means to blockade the island city.

The fall of Tenochtitlan and Tlatelolco on 13 August 1521 ended the Triple Alliance capital but did not end the conquest of Mesoamerica. Campaigns in Michoacán, Oaxaca, Guatemala, and Yucatán continued for years, and some Maya polities remained independent until the late seventeenth century. Indigenous allies often served as colonizers in these campaigns, and their claims to service produced a contested memory of who had conquered Tenochtitlan.

The sources for these events are plural. Cortés wrote to justify himself, Díaz to correct others, and Las Casas to condemn. Nahua sources such as the Florentine Codex and the Annals of Tlatelolco and Tlaxcalan sources such as the Lienzo de Tlaxcala record different communities' memories, shaped by their own purposes. “The Indigenous perspective” is therefore a plurality, and reading these accounts against one another is the core method of the chapter.

Finally, the chapter has shown that Indigenous political life was reorganized rather than erased. Altepetl, noble lineages, and councils survived as native cabildos and corporate communities within New Spain, even as encomienda, tribute, epidemic disease, forced labor, and conversion campaigns imposed severe costs. The result was constrained continuity: Indigenous communities remained historical actors, negotiating, litigating, and adapting within a colonial order they did not choose. The chapters that follow trace how that order developed in the north and in the Southeast, and how Indigenous peoples continued to shape it.

Constructing a Source Dossier on a Contested Episode of the Conquest

This project asks you to build a source dossier on one contested episode, comparing how Spanish and Indigenous sources represent it.

  1. Choose an episode. Options include the Cholula massacre, the Tóxcatl massacre, the death of Moctezuma, the Noche Triste, or the capture of Cuauhtémoc.
  2. Collect sources. Using library resources, locate translated excerpts from at least one Spanish source and at least one Indigenous source, such as the Florentine Codex, the Annals of Tlatelolco, or the Lienzo de Tlaxcala.
  3. Analyze each source. Identify authorship, date, audience, purpose, and perspective, and note what each source includes and omits.
  4. Compare and evaluate. Identify points of agreement and disagreement, and consider what additional evidence could resolve the differences.
  5. Write your findings. In a 4–6 page essay, present your conclusions, noting where the evidence is strong, where it is uncertain, and where scholars disagree.

Library resources to use: Translated editions of Cortés's letters, Díaz, and the Florentine Codex; scholarly studies of Nahua historical writing; and the MGU History Library's reference materials on Mesoamerican and colonial Mexican history.

Chapter Reflection

The conquest of central Mexico is often told as the story of a few men and their extraordinary luck. This chapter has told it as the story of many peoples making decisions under pressure. That change in perspective is not a matter of fashion. It follows from the evidence, which shows Totonac, Tlaxcalan, Texcocan, Mexica, and other leaders weighing risks and opportunities, and acting on their own judgments about what would serve their communities.

The chapter also shows how a single event can be remembered differently by those who experienced it. The Cholula massacre, the death of Moctezuma, and the question of who conquered Tenochtitlan have no single agreed account, and the disagreement itself is historical evidence about interests, communities, and the colonial order that followed. Learning to read such disagreement is a skill that serves well beyond this chapter.

The war's consequences shaped everything that followed in the Spanish Americas: the establishment of New Spain, the extension of the encomienda, the arrival of missionaries, the epidemics examined in Chapter 40, and the northward movement of Spanish colonization examined in Chapter 42. Yet the Indigenous communities that survived the conquest did not vanish into the new order. They governed through native cabildos, litigated in Spanish courts, wrote in Nahuatl, and remembered their own histories. Their persistence is the context for the chapters that follow in this volume, and for the long history of Indigenous nations that this textbook traces to the present.
