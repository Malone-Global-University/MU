The Covenant Chain and Northeastern Diplomacy

Indigenous Peoples of North America: A Continental History

Chapter Opening

In July 1754, delegates from seven British colonies gathered at Albany to meet Haudenosaunee leaders whose predecessors had been negotiating with that town’s officials for nearly a century. The Haudenosaunee came with grievances, and the colonists came needing their help.

At Albany in the summer of 1754, Haudenosaunee representatives confronted colonial delegates who hoped to preserve a diplomatic relationship that had outlived numerous governors and imperial wars. The relationship was known through the image of a chain. It could link political communities, but it could also become tarnished when one party failed to honor what had been promised. The meeting was therefore not simply a plea for military support: it was an attempt to determine whether a long-standing system of reciprocal obligations remained trustworthy.

Behind that meeting stood a century of changing relations. Dutch traders and Mohawk diplomats had established patterns of exchange and negotiation on the upper Hudson. English officials inherited those connections after the conquest of New Netherland in 1664, but they did not inherit uncontested authority over Indigenous peoples. Through councils, gifts, wampum, and negotiations involving neighboring colonies, the partners gradually created what later historians call the Covenant Chain. No single ceremonial declaration can capture the changing membership or meaning of that system.

The central participants included the Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga, and Seneca nations, later joined within the Confederacy by the Tuscarora. Their decisions did not always coincide. Nor were the English colonies a single actor: New York merchants, Pennsylvania proprietors, and the governments of Maryland and Virginia pursued different aims. The chain brought people together because its procedures created opportunities for them to negotiate even when the underlying political interests remained incompatible.

Land made those differences increasingly urgent. Agreements that some negotiators understood through kinship, protection, and regulated use appeared in colonial law as deeds transferring territory. Other Indigenous nations—among them Lenape, Mahican, Shawnee, and residents of the Ohio Country—could find their rights debated by people who had not obtained their consent. Understanding the chain therefore requires asking both what it enabled for the Haudenosaunee and what it could permit colonial officials and powerful neighbors to claim about others.

The evidence is unusually rich but not transparent. Colonial secretaries recorded speeches after interpretation, and later editors printed records drawn from surviving manuscripts. Wampum belts carried treaty memory but were not word-for-word transcripts. Land deeds asserted rights whose validity could be challenged. Indigenous historical traditions provide perspectives that colonial documents often omit. The chapter treats each source as evidence of a historically situated claim, not as an automatic description of political reality.

This chapter follows the diplomatic relationship from Dutch antecedents and the post-1664 English regime through the council diplomacy of the 1670s, the settlement of 1701, the intercolonial conferences of 1722 and 1744, and the crisis that led to Albany in 1754. Its argument is that the Covenant Chain was durable because it was continually renegotiated; influential because the Haudenosaunee retained political agency; and contested because the same arrangements could sustain peace or facilitate assertions of authority over other peoples.

Chapter 55 introduced the wider continental landscape of Indigenous diplomacy. Here the focus narrows to one unusually documented but regionally specific relationship, without treating it as a model for every Indigenous society. Chapter 57 will examine the subsequent Seven Years’ War. This chapter ends at its threshold, when neither the outcome of imperial rivalry nor the future of Indigenous alliances had been decided.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Define the Covenant Chain as an evolving diplomatic system rather than a single treaty.
  2. Trace its development from Dutch–Mohawk diplomacy through the 1664 transition and the 1677 negotiations.
  3. Distinguish confederal decisions from initiatives by individual Haudenosaunee nations.
  4. Explain the roles of wampum, speeches, gifts, and renewal ceremonies.
  5. Analyze the work of Albany officials, interpreters, and intermediaries.
  6. Assess the effect of the French wars and the Great Peace of Montreal (1701) on the English alliance.
  7. Evaluate relations with Mahican, Lenape, Shawnee, and Ohio peoples, including disputed Haudenosaunee claims.
  8. Interpret the 1722, 1744, and 1754 proceedings as evidence, noting their limitations.
  9. Explain why the 1737 Walking Purchase and the Lancaster land cession were contested.
  10. Assess the chain’s achievements and contradictions on the eve of war.

Guiding Historical Questions

  1. How did the Covenant Chain emerge from existing Indigenous political relationships rather than English decision alone?
  2. Who had authority to negotiate for the Haudenosaunee, and how far did it extend?
  3. How was a relationship maintained when no single document could secure it?
  4. Why did the Haudenosaunee and colonial officials understand the chain differently?
  5. Did neighboring Indigenous peoples accept Haudenosaunee claims over their lands?
  6. How did the chain reinforce, and how did it limit, Indigenous political independence?
  7. Why was the chain strained by 1754 without being abandoned?

Historical Context

Long before European settlement, the Haudenosaunee nations maintained political relations through councils, kinship responsibilities, condolence, and the institutions associated with the Great Law of Peace. The original five nations were linked through a Confederacy whose center of deliberation lay at Onondaga, while each nation also maintained its own communities and political priorities. The precise chronology of the Confederacy's founding remains a subject in which Indigenous tradition and scholarly reconstruction must be distinguished. Its sovereign institutions did not depend on colonial recognition.

During the seventeenth century, fur exchange and conflict altered the political geography of the Northeast. The Dutch traded at Fort Orange near Mohawk country, while French officials and their Indigenous allies developed networks around the St. Lawrence and Great Lakes. The Mohawk position near the Hudson corridor gave them particular access to colonial markets. The Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga, and Seneca faced distinct pressures and choices, and warfare or resettlement could affect each nation differently. The Confederacy's political relationships were extensive but did not amount to uncontested rule over every neighboring people.

English expansion introduced more colonial centers. New Netherland passed to English control in 1664 and became New York; Pennsylvania was founded in the early 1680s; Virginia, Maryland, and the New England colonies pressed claims that intersected with existing Indigenous territorial arrangements. No European government controlled all routes or negotiations in the interior. The need for food, trade, safe passage, and Indigenous allies made diplomacy essential to colonial survival and expansion even as growing settlement threatened the basis of earlier agreements.

Documents preserved under the headings 'Indian treaties' or 'covenants' do not always represent the consent of every affected Indigenous nation. Colonial officials often preferred a single recognized Indigenous speaker, especially when seeking land. Haudenosaunee representatives could use such recognition to expand influence, but Lenape and other communities might dispute their right to speak for local residents. The distinction between diplomatic authority and ownership of land will recur throughout the chapter.

For students, the central method is comparative source reading. A council minute identifies a public record shaped by translation and colonial record keeping. A wampum belt or oral tradition preserves other forms of political memory. A legal deed captures what the signatories and drafters represented, not necessarily a legitimate transfer from all residents of the country described. The narrative evaluates these materials against one another rather than treating any one as self-sufficient proof.

Division I — Origins of the Covenant Chain

Section 56.1

The Covenant Chain as a Diplomatic System

The Covenant Chain was not a political object that could be signed once, filed away, and thereafter assumed to work. It was a changing framework of councils, agreements, expectations, ceremonial protocols, and practical services linking the Haudenosaunee Confederacy with the government of English New York and, eventually, other colonies and Indigenous communities. Its history is inseparable from disputes about whose territory was protected, which leaders could speak for whom, and what obligation followed from a previous council. Its durability arose not from a single constitution but from the repeated work of making a relationship believable.

The metaphor of a chain conveyed a relationship between partners who were supposed to hold together. Speakers described links that might rust when grievances accumulated and could be brightened when the parties renewed their obligations. This imagery connected diplomatic ceremony with a test of conduct: an ally needed to make amends for injuries, supply promised goods, investigate violence, and keep avenues of communication open. A polished chain was therefore a demand for reliable behavior, not merely a declaration of friendliness. Wampum belts and strings accompanied particular messages, and the parties used them to recognize that words imposed responsibilities.

There was an asymmetry in how the relationship was understood. English governors could invoke imperial sovereignty or describe Native people as subjects receiving protection. Haudenosaunee negotiators generally approached English officials as representatives of another polity whose assistance could be secured through reciprocal obligations. Their councils might adopt diplomatic language familiar to Europeans without thereby endorsing every implication of European law. Equally, the Haudenosaunee could assert wider rights over neighbors that those neighbors did not recognize. Neither the independence of a participant nor the justice of every claim should be presumed simply because it appeared in a council record.

Haudenosaunee governance preceded the Chain. The original five constituent nations—Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga, and Seneca—had their own councils, communities, and traditions of confederal decision-making. The Onondaga council fire occupied a central place in relations among these nations; the Mohawk, nearest the Hudson corridor, frequently conducted early discussions with Albany. A Mohawk delegation did not necessarily possess the authority to alienate Seneca territory or settle an issue binding every nation. Colonial officials might find it convenient to infer a single Indigenous counterpart, but the diplomatic reality was more complex.

Historical Vocabulary — Covenant Chain

Definition

A network of recurring diplomatic relationships, most prominently among the Haudenosaunee and English, later British, colonial governments.

Historical context

Chains were invoked to signify continuing obligations to maintain peace and cooperation. Their precise membership and interpretation changed over time; no single founding document explains all later uses.

Why it matters

The metaphor reveals why treaties required repeated renewal and why failure to keep a promise could become an accusation of allowing the chain to tarnish.

Potential misunderstanding

The Covenant Chain was not the Haudenosaunee Confederacy's internal constitution, nor proof that all allied Indigenous communities became Crown subjects.

The colonial partner was likewise divided. The governor of New York issued commissions and directions, yet Albany magistrates, traders, and commissioners carried out much of the routine business. The governments of Pennsylvania, Maryland, Virginia, and New England later entered negotiations while competing with each other over trade, land, and jurisdiction. A promise attributed to 'the English' could have been made by one governor, one merchant, or a group of commissioners whose political capacity was limited. A central task for historians is determining which institution made a commitment and who could realistically enforce it.

The chain's geographical reach shifted over time. At first the relationship centered on the Hudson–Mohawk corridor and the diplomatic consequences of conflicts involving southern New England and the mid-Atlantic. As European settlements expanded, negotiations connected Albany with the Susquehanna, Delaware, Chesapeake, and Ohio regions. For many of the Indigenous residents of those places, the Covenant Chain did not represent a consensual league to which they had freely attached themselves. It could be a route to mediation and security, or a forum in which other powers asserted a right to speak about their lands.

This history calls for precision about terminology. 'Treaty' could mean a formal recorded agreement, but diplomacy also consisted of speeches, visits, presents, messages, and periodically renewed understandings. 'Alliance' could imply military aid or peace, commercial access, passage, and dispute settlement; these meanings did not always coincide. 'Protection' might refer to assistance from an ally or to a hierarchical arrangement that threatened the independence of someone being protected. The words must be explained in the context of particular negotiations rather than given one timeless definition.

Finally, the Chain should be studied through results as well as rhetoric. Could parties travel safely? Did commissioners secure the goods they promised? Were deaths investigated and compensation accepted? Did a treaty prevent unauthorized settlements, or were settlers free to ignore it? Did an alleged dependent nation agree to the status attributed to it? Answers vary across decades. The most useful question is not whether the Covenant Chain was simply successful or unsuccessful, but how its participants repeatedly made and unmade their political obligations.

Section 56.2

Mohawk–Dutch Diplomacy and the Fort Orange World

The Dutch period established practical precedents for later Anglo-Haudenosaunee diplomacy without constituting the Covenant Chain in its mature form. Fort Orange, founded near the upper Hudson River in the early seventeenth century, drew merchants and Indigenous visitors into a commercial zone connected to the Mohawk River, the interior woodlands, and the St. Lawrence routes. The Mohawk held a strong geographical position east of the other Haudenosaunee nations and near important trading approaches. They could influence access to markets and to the intermediaries who connected Dutch manufactures with inland producers of furs and food.

For Mohawk communities, commercial exchange was inseparable from political security. Metal implements, cloth, guns, ammunition, and other European goods became desirable for multiple reasons, but trade also brought disputes about prices, credit, violence, and the movement of rival traders. Mohawk leaders assessed whether access to Dutch goods would strengthen their people in contests with neighboring nations. The Dutch, for their part, had limited capacity to control the countryside and depended on Indigenous partners to move commodities and maintain routes. Neither side operated from a position of complete independence or unchallenged dominance.

Dutch officials did not all behave alike. Local traders, the West India Company, the patroon's representatives at Rensselaerswyck, and colonial governors could pursue conflicting priorities. Arent van Curler became an important intermediary through prolonged contact with Indigenous communities and an acquired reputation for fair dealing. Later diplomatic references to 'Corlaer' made his name part of a political vocabulary associated with the colonial governor at Albany. The endurance of this name demonstrates how Indigenous negotiators could transform a personal relationship into a broader office-like expectation of conduct.

The diplomacy was not a compact among men alone. Households supported traveling parties; Indigenous women cultivated food, maintained kin relationships, and shaped community decisions that affected trade and war. Dutch and Indigenous families sometimes developed relationships across cultural boundaries, while interpreters and traders became familiar with ceremonial expectations. Much of this social infrastructure is harder to recover than the speeches of prominent men because clerks wrote down proceedings that served colonial administration. Nevertheless, treaties would have been impossible without those who provided information, food, transport, and translations.

War and epidemic loss framed this commerce. The conflict known in some older literature as the Beaver Wars involved multiple Indigenous nations and shifting European relationships. Haudenosaunee decisions cannot be explained by a single desire for furs; security, mourning, captives, kinship replacement, and rival claims over access to hunting territories also mattered. Dutch merchants had commercial interests in violence without fully directing it. Mohawk diplomatic agency was visible both when their leaders sought partners and when they resisted demands inconsistent with their own strategic interests.

Haudenosaunee traditions of the Two Row Wampum describe distinct peoples traveling together without interfering in each other's political and cultural life. The Onondaga Nation associates the tradition with early Dutch contact and a 1613 agreement. Historians debate the documentation and dating of a particular early treaty and belt; the absence of universally accepted colonial paperwork should not erase the independent standing of Indigenous tradition. A textbook must distinguish the meaning and continuing use of the tradition from claims about a precisely dated, completely verified seventeenth-century instrument.

The English takeover in 1664 thus occurred in an already negotiated landscape. Mohawk leaders had expectations about the behavior of traders and officials, and familiar routes drew them to Fort Orange regardless of the flag flying there. The new government needed to learn these expectations or risk losing access to trade and political support. At the same time, English claims of imperial succession did not automatically carry the consent of the nations that had bargained with the Dutch. The political relationship had to be remade in council.

The Dutch antecedents also point to the limitations of retrospective founding stories. Some later accounts imagine the Chain as if it began with an uncontested promise of mutual respect; others treat it as a legal submission to a European monarch. Both flatten a messy historical process. Earlier relations supplied people, routes, institutions, and metaphors; the specific Covenant Chain emerged through successive negotiations shaped by the wars and political problems of the later seventeenth century.

Section 56.3

English New York and the Negotiations of 1664

In 1664 an English expedition took New Netherland, and the Dutch settlement of New Amsterdam became New York. Fort Orange was renamed Albany. For English officials, changing the administration of a colony did not secure the Mohawk Valley, the Great Lakes approaches, or diplomatic cooperation among the Five Nations. English military resources in the interior were limited. The new government inherited commercial connections and practical vulnerabilities rather than uncontested authority over the Indigenous peoples whose paths crossed Albany.

Mohawk leaders confronted a different problem. A partner whose merchants and administrators had become familiar was replaced by officials who claimed an inheritance of Dutch rights. Would the English supply comparable trade goods, protect established relations, and hear the complaints that came to Albany? Would they intervene in wars with Indigenous neighbors? Would commercial advantages be lost to competing networks? These were issues of concrete political economy, not merely an abstract question of recognizing a European ruler. Continued trade might be attractive even when an English narrative of conquest was not.

Diplomatic meetings in Albany in 1664 therefore carried multiple meanings. English spokesmen could frame arrangements as confirmation of authority under the duke of York. Haudenosaunee leaders could regard those exchanges as new assurances of friendship and access to essential supplies. The records produced by the colonial administration preserve some terms and speeches, but not every discussion within the visiting delegations. Later English claims of an unbroken covenant stretching back to Dutch times were themselves political assertions, useful in persuading Native audiences that English officials would honor inherited obligations.

Renaming places did not erase prior diplomatic geography. The Mohawk River still connected communities to Albany, while routes from Onondaga and the western nations required multiple stages of Indigenous communication. Mohawk representatives might speak first because they were nearest; farther nations learned developments through councils and messengers. Colonial expectations of centralized representation could therefore produce recurrent confusion. An Albany agreement could be meaningful, but whether it bound every Haudenosaunee community required investigation rather than assumption.

The mid-1660s were not a time of unbroken stability. The English still faced competition from the Dutch, who briefly recovered New York in 1673 before English rule was restored in 1674. Colonial factional politics affected who governed and the reliability of promises. For Indigenous diplomats, this instability offered chances to renegotiate trade and protection but also raised doubts about whether one set of colonial officials could bind its successors. Maintaining a relationship required more than a ceremonial pledge at the first change of government.

English expansion into Indigenous territories elsewhere complicated New York's position. New England colonies had their own histories of conflict, dispossession, and diplomacy with Algonquian peoples. The Chesapeake involved Susquehannock, Haudenosaunee, and southern Indigenous networks whose disputes could be carried north through envoys. A governor wishing to present New York as the principal intermediary needed to convince many parties that Albany was a useful council fire. That claim grew through practice, not through the administrative act of 1664.

Trade regulation also exposed divergent assumptions about political authority. English officials sought to license commerce and impose colonial rules, whereas Indigenous buyers and suppliers judged a market by availability, price, safety, and the behavior of individual traders. A treaty could promise fair dealing without guaranteeing that merchants or settlers would obey. Complaints about fraud, alcohol, or illegal land dealings eventually threatened the Chain because the apparent alliance could not be separated from what ordinary people experienced in market transactions.

The 1664 transition is therefore a turning point, not an absolute beginning. It brought a new colonial government into established Indigenous diplomatic and commercial systems, prompting a new series of councils. The durable Covenant Chain would take form in the following decades as parties addressed war, peace, the claims of neighboring communities, and the burdens of sustaining repeated political promises.

Section 56.4

Forging and Renewing the Chain, 1670s–1680s

The negotiations of the 1670s occurred amid the consequences of war in New England and continuing conflict around the Chesapeake. King Philip's War, fought primarily in 1675–1676, devastated numerous communities, displaced people, and altered the relationship between the English colonies and the Native nations of southern New England. Haudenosaunee leaders confronted opportunities and dangers as colonial governments sought their assistance or neutrality. The diplomatic network centering on Albany expanded because several colonies needed an intermediary with the capacity to influence Indigenous political relationships beyond their own frontiers.

Events identified collectively as the negotiations of 1677 should not be collapsed into a single council. An Albany conference in the spring involved regional participants and New York's administration, while later proceedings included Maryland's emissary Henry Coursey. Maryland documents show that Coursey was empowered to pursue peace with the Haudenosaunee and address relations involving Susquehannock people and other communities. The instructions themselves reveal how colonial governments imagined a negotiator's task: to determine who was under whose protection, seek an end to violence, and build arrangements across several jurisdictions.

The July discussions preserve especially important images of binding commitments with a chain. In an account published by the Onondaga Nation, the Onondaga speaker Carachkondie was recorded making peace and presenting a broad wampum belt. The record of this statement matters not because it fixes a universal constitutional founding date but because it documents diplomatic metaphor in a concrete negotiation. The words reached us through colonial writing and translation, and a historically responsible account must consider the parties and record-making circumstances before treating them as a transparent transcript.

The metaphor served different ends. For English officials a chain could suggest a durable framework for regulating relations among colonies and Indigenous nations, perhaps one they could claim to administer. For Haudenosaunee representatives the image could emphasize mutual obligation, public confirmation, and the capacity to repair an injured relationship. It allowed a peace to be announced without describing every community as a subordinate of one governor. New participants could be attached to an existing relationship, but their consent, their standing, and the precise obligations attached to them were always subject to negotiation.

Governor Edmund Andros played a significant role in developing New York's claims to diplomatic centrality. Yet he had to work through local officials and the practical politics of Albany. Maryland and neighboring colonies had their own territorial and commercial priorities, while Haudenosaunee people had reasons not to accept broad restrictions on movement. Requests to halt attacks, return prisoners, and establish safe routes implicated the interests of Indigenous communities that might not be present in Albany. Agreements made there were therefore starting points for further political work rather than final settlements.

During the 1680s additional conferences and treaties were shaped by violence involving French forces, Haudenosaunee nations, and western Indigenous allies of New France. English diplomats invoked their relationship with the Five Nations to secure trade and support, but their capacity to supply soldiers or restrain other colonies was limited. Haudenosaunee leaders used their connections to obtain goods and cultivate political options. The Chain did not eliminate war; it created a framework in which war and diplomacy were repeatedly discussed and contested.

Accounts of the material from which the Chain was made—iron in some retellings, silver in later language—should be treated as historically situated images. Silver implied a relationship that could be polished and kept bright, and that would tarnish through neglect. Rather than establishing a precise and immutable sequence of diplomatic metals, the historian should examine when speakers used these images and what action they demanded. The metaphor mattered because a relationship was assumed to need maintenance, repair, and public recognition.

From this perspective the late seventeenth century produced an institution in motion. A set of negotiations originally focused on trade and security became an avenue for intercolonial treaty making and for Haudenosaunee claims about relations with neighboring nations. The Chain was strong enough to offer a shared political language but not so fixed as to settle whether one party could bind another or whether protection authorized control. Those unresolved questions would shape its subsequent history.

Division II — The Political Machinery of the Covenant Chain

Section 56.5

The Haudenosaunee Confederacy and Diplomatic Representation

The Haudenosaunee Confederacy was not a European-style monarchy with a single official authorized to dispose of every nation's land or direct every community's military affairs. Its political order linked the Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga, and Seneca nations through councils, shared protocols, and recognized offices of leadership, while preserving important spheres of local and national decision-making. The Tuscarora subsequently joined this political world. Understanding this arrangement is essential to understanding why colonial documents sometimes overstate the unity or reach of 'the Five Nations' or 'the Six Nations.'

At Onondaga the central council fire provided a setting for confederal deliberation. Questions brought before the Confederacy could be discussed through procedures grounded in consultation and consensus. Chiefs or sachems held titled responsibilities, and their standing depended on social and political relationships that Europeans did not always understand. Clan mothers played recognized roles in the selection and accountability of certain leaders. Political authority therefore involved matrilineal kinship, community acceptance, and institutional responsibilities rather than the simple power of an elected governor to issue orders.

Councils were also differentiated by subject. Decisions concerning war might involve military leaders whose authority arose in particular circumstances; peace and treaty matters could be advanced through established diplomatic speakers. Messengers carried propositions among towns and nations, where they might be discussed, amended, or rejected. An envoy sent to Albany could have been instructed to hear proposals rather than make irrevocable commitments. Colonial impatience with deliberation must not be mistaken for the absence of a political procedure. Time spent consulting other communities was itself a feature of authority.

The Mohawk occupied a distinctive position because of proximity to Albany and established relations with the Dutch. Their diplomats often opened conversations, carried news, or presented claims that English officials interpreted as representing all the Five Nations. Yet Seneca interests in the western Great Lakes or Onondaga priorities concerning the French were not necessarily identical to those of eastern Mohawk villages. The Oneida and Cayuga also had their own local and regional considerations. When the historical record reports an agreement by 'the Iroquois,' it is often necessary to ask which nation and which delegates actually participated.

Demographic and religious changes further complicated the picture. Warfare, epidemic losses, adoption, and relocation reshaped communities. Some Haudenosaunee Catholics lived in settlements near the St. Lawrence and maintained relationships with their homelands while taking part in French diplomatic circuits. Their ties could complicate simplistic categories of pro-French and pro-English factions. Diplomacy depended on people whose commitments were formed through kinship, settlement, faith, commerce, and security rather than by one fixed international alignment.

The chain included not only relations between a confederal center and a colonial capital but also the question of whether a council could bind neighboring Indigenous peoples. Haudenosaunee negotiators sometimes asserted authority derived from conquest, protection, or older alliances. Colonial negotiators had incentives to treat such assertions as jurisdiction over broad territories because they preferred dealing with an identifiable grantor of land rights. Lenape and other communities did not invariably accept this representation. A historian must distinguish a claim of authority, the colonial recognition of that claim, and the consent of residents whose lives would be affected.

Political representation also changed over time. Successive sachems inherited titles but occupied them in new circumstances; speakers developed reputations for judgment and skill; William Johnson and colonial administrators learned to seek the help of leaders who could persuade other communities. Neither Haudenosaunee nor English delegations were timeless institutions free of internal debate. The very durability of the Chain depended on adapting procedures to change without conceding that adaptation erased earlier principles.

A rigorous reading of council records therefore asks three levels of questions: which named people spoke, whom they claimed to represent, and whether the resulting obligation was carried out by those supposedly bound. This approach recovers the political diversity that a phrase such as 'the Six Nations agreed' can obscure. It also exposes an enduring paradox of the Covenant Chain: an alliance that recognized Haudenosaunee influence could be used by outsiders to overstate their control over other Indigenous societies.

Section 56.6

Polishing the Silver Chain: Wampum, Kinship, and Treaty Renewal

In the Covenant Chain, an agreement became meaningful through public action. Delegations arrived, received welcome, offered condolence, listened to earlier grievances, and spoke in formal sequences. Wampum strings or belts could accompany specific propositions and responses. The material exchange helped identify a message as diplomatic and made its commitments memorable. This was not a writing system transcribing every word, and it should not be treated as a decorative substitute for a colonial deed. It was an element of a complex oral, ceremonial, and institutional process.

Condolence could precede substantive negotiations because death and injury threatened the social conditions necessary for listening. The language of wiping tears, clearing throats, and opening ears marked an effort to restore communication after grief or conflict. Such practices linked diplomacy with the experience of communities affected by war, including losses that colonial clerks might reduce to a brief statement of sympathy. A council could not simply proceed as though violence had no moral and political consequences. Repairing relationships was part of the work of making peace.

The metaphor of polishing or brightening a silver chain conveyed a recurring obligation. Silver might appear magnificent but would dull if left unattended. Speakers could use this image to complain that allies had neglected trade, protection, or restitution and to insist that officials act before the relationship broke. Renewal was therefore not merely ceremonial repetition; it was a means of pressing for redress. Gifts were not necessarily bribes or tribute. They might signify commitment, compensate for harm, enable long journeys, or sustain reciprocal obligations between political communities.

Kinship names likewise carried political content. The 'brother' relationship could emphasize reciprocal dignity even when powers were unequal. 'Father' and 'children' could denote protection and generosity in one setting, but English officials might read paternal language as governmental superiority while Indigenous participants demanded a father's continuing duty to assist. Formal vocabulary shifted over time and across nations. The same address might be acceptable in a council and contested later if the expected provisions did not arrive. One must trace usage rather than decree that each term had a universal meaning.

Ceremony could also protect against misunderstandings. A speaker sometimes repeated a proposition through an interpreter to establish that the intended meaning had been received. Wampum exchanges gave parties visible occasions to accept, reject, or qualify particular points. At Lancaster in 1744, the printed minutes show repeated rounds of speech, interpretation, present giving, and responses. These procedures may look slow to readers accustomed to instantaneous signatures, but they recognized the stakes of committing peoples and lands. The sequence of interactions is evidence of institutional sophistication.

Diplomatic renewal had a material cost. Delegations needed food, lodging, safe travel, gifts, interpreters, and time away from work and family. Colonial administrators sought budgets for presents; merchants could profit by supplying goods; Indigenous hosts provided the resources that made conferences possible. Poor-quality goods could insult recipients, just as delayed payments undermined the words of a colonial governor. An alliance was maintained by political economy as well as oratory, and disputes over these expenses could affect the ability of authorities to act credibly.

A recurrent source problem is the difference between a preserved belt and a documented event. The surviving wampum object may have a complex history of custody and interpretation. The written council account may name a belt but provide no image of it; a later tradition may explain a belt through events not documented by contemporary colonial secretaries. Neither source should automatically eliminate the other. Their evidentiary contributions differ: the object may sustain memory and ceremonial meaning, while the record can help establish the institutional circumstances of a particular meeting.

The chain's ceremonies thus reveal a conception of political obligation that was durable precisely because it was revisable. A dispute could lead to a new conference; a grievance could require more wampum or compensation; a new colony could be included by negotiated terms. This strength also contained a danger. The capacity to attach additional participants could tempt colonial officials to claim that the relationship authorized transactions with Indigenous communities that had never expressly consented.

Section 56.7

Albany as a Center of Indigenous–Colonial Diplomacy

Albany became an indispensable diplomatic center because it stood near the meeting of the Hudson and Mohawk corridors. From here delegations could reach settlements along the Mohawk Valley, the Onondaga council fire, and through Indigenous routes the Great Lakes and St. Lawrence networks. The town served as a market for European manufactures and furs, a place to obtain news, and a venue in which Indigenous leaders could compel colonial officials to hear grievances. Its political importance was geographical, commercial, and ceremonial at the same time.

New York's governors relied heavily on commissioners of Indian affairs, many drawn from Albany's merchant families, to handle correspondence and meetings. These officials knew traders, languages, and routes better than distant governors but were not disinterested civil servants in the modern sense. Commercial interests could intersect with public duties, and representatives criticized their willingness or capacity to prevent fraud. An official who secured favorable trade for his associates might still damage a diplomatic relationship if visiting delegations felt that promised protections had not been honored.

The Albany council fire did not replace Onondaga's internal importance. Rather, the relationship involved two centers with different standing. Colonial governments recognized Albany as a convenient place to negotiate, while Haudenosaunee councils at home determined whether representatives' proposals were acceptable. The distance between centers meant that decisions could take weeks or months to circulate. Colonial records that describe a swift 'agreement' may not reveal the additional deliberation necessary before a decision was implemented in towns beyond the Mohawk Valley.

Geography magnified the work of interpreters. Indigenous envoys had to travel through waterways and paths affected by seasonal weather, warfare, changing settlement, and the hospitality of communities along the way. European commissioners depended on the knowledge of guides and traders. A route open one year might be dangerous in another; a village that welcomed travelers might later refuse passage because of a dispute. Control of the road was therefore a political asset, and council speeches frequently connected peace with the restoration of safe paths between peoples.

The town's commercial markets encouraged recurring visits even during diplomatic crises. Mohawk visitors could negotiate purchases or debts while discussing English protection and war news. The 'Albany commissioners' were themselves a changing body. Political struggles in New York affected appointments, mandates, and relationships with figures such as William Johnson. When a new official promised reforms, established merchants might resist alterations to the trade or negotiating process. By the 1750s Haudenosaunee criticism targeted not only abstract British policy but identifiable officials and practical abuses. This helps explain why the crisis of 1753 involved disputes over who was trusted to speak and act for New York.

Regional participation expanded the meaning of Albany. Maryland or Virginia diplomats did not necessarily use the same language or possess the same priorities as New Yorkers. Pennsylvania intermediaries brought questions about Lenape lands and the Susquehanna. English descriptions of a single 'alliance' could conceal negotiations among the colonies themselves over who would provide gifts, bear military costs, or benefit from land agreements. Haudenosaunee delegates could use these rivalries, but intercolonial competition also made promises difficult to enforce.

Albany is therefore best understood as an institutional crossroads rather than the capital of an Indigenous empire or a place where Europeans dictated every outcome. Its importance arose from repeated encounters between multiple authorities. To reconstruct the Chain one must follow what happened beyond the council house: the flow of goods, the conduct of settlers, the decisions at Onondaga, the experiences of neighboring communities, and the willingness of colonial assemblies to finance agreements their commissioners had announced.

Section 56.8

Interpreters, Political Obligations, and the Management of Disputes

The Covenant Chain depended on intermediaries who could convert claims into understandable proposals across languages and political systems. Interpreters were not neutral instruments. They carried knowledge of idioms, personal relationships, and the customary order of speeches. A term like 'ownership' or 'protection' could be rendered through an expression whose social implications differed from those assumed by a colonial lawyer. Skilled translators could help parties recognize the problem; less careful translation could make a dispute appear settled while leaving the essential disagreement untouched.

Intermediaries included traders, missionaries, adopted residents, couriers, and Indigenous diplomats who knew several languages. Their authority came partly from reputation: could a messenger travel safely and deliver a speech accurately? Could a trader keep promises about payments or credit? Could an official be trusted to relay an unwelcome complaint to a governor? A single failed message might provoke renewed conflict. Conversely, repeated contact could create lasting expectations that survived an individual's death and were transferred into political titles or offices.

Conrad Weiser's career illustrates the complexity of such work in the Pennsylvania diplomatic world. He lived for a period among the Mohawk as a young man, learned languages and diplomatic practices, and later worked as a Pennsylvania interpreter and negotiator. His usefulness to colonial officials did not make him the author of Indigenous decisions. He mediated them, sometimes with significant influence over the form in which those decisions appeared in written minutes. Historians must examine both his expertise and his position within colonial interests.

The management of violent incidents was one of the Chain's recurrent tasks. When a traveler was killed, a merchant robbed, or a raid launched, the injured party might demand that responsible people be surrendered or that compensation be provided. Indigenous councils could respond by distinguishing the conduct of individuals from the commitments of a whole nation, while colonial authorities might demand collective punishment. A diplomatic settlement required deciding whether to treat the violence as a private wrong, an intercommunity injury, or evidence of a broken alliance.

Everyday Life — The Work Behind a Council

A diplomatic visit required weeks of travel, food, shelter, clothing, interpreters, wampum, and goods for exchange. Families supported envoys during their absences; women maintained agricultural work and influenced the standing of leaders; boatmen and messengers carried information along routes whose security depended on other communities. These activities were central to diplomacy even when official minutes named only prominent speakers.

Albany merchants could profit from provisioning visiting delegations. Poor goods, insufficient compensation, and exploitative trading practices could convert a ceremonial assurance into resentment. Council records should therefore be read beside commercial accounts, petitions, household histories, and evidence of travel.

Gifts and restitution also traveled through households. Women prepared food, maintained fields and domestic stores during absences, and sustained relationships of marriage and kinship through which traders and visitors obtained hospitality. Clan mothers' responsibilities in Haudenosaunee political life extended to the standing of titled leaders, although colonial summaries often record only male speakers. Children and dependents could experience diplomacy through altered access to food and goods, relocation, or the dangers created when a path between communities became unsafe.

For colonial officials, paperwork was another form of diplomatic labor. Clerks created journals, preserved copies of treaties, listed presents, and sent reports to governors and imperial ministries. These texts can be exceptionally informative about dates, attendees, proposed terms, and the official version of outcomes. But a clerk rarely recorded every private conversation or Indigenous disagreement. A later historian who reads a published transcript must ask whether it is an original journal, a fair copy, a printed selection, or a nineteenth-century editorial compilation.

Disputes over land made these problems acute. A colonial secretary could record the signature or mark of certain leaders, while the community living on the land might not have been present, informed, or empowered to resist. An English official might insist a deed settled ownership forever; Indigenous participants might have thought a payment addressed a particular relationship or limited claim. The historian should investigate the exact terms, signatories, residence and authority of the signatories, and later reactions before describing a 'sale' as an uncontested transfer.

In this sense the Chain was not maintained by governors and sachems alone. Its effectiveness rested on a distributed social infrastructure involving travelers, households, clerks, suppliers, and people who repaired harm after negotiations failed. The apparent stability of a diplomatic system often depended on ordinary actions beyond the formal council, which is why the everyday consequences of agreements are as revealing as the agreements themselves.

Division III — The Chain and the Northeastern Balance of Power

Section 56.9

The Covenant Chain during the French–Haudenosaunee Wars

The late seventeenth century tested the Covenant Chain through overlapping wars involving the Haudenosaunee, New France, and Indigenous nations allied with French traders and missionaries. The Confederacy did not fight these conflicts as the obedient auxiliary of an English governor. Its leaders considered security, access to hunting lands, trade supplies, captives, diplomatic standing, and the survival of their communities. English officials often wished to use the Five Nations as a barrier against French expansion, but that expectation was not identical to Haudenosaunee political objectives.

Mohawk towns lay closest to Albany and thus could receive English messages and trade goods more readily than western Haudenosaunee settlements. Seneca and Onondaga leaders, however, confronted the French and their Indigenous allies across different geographic fronts. Their decisions depended on local threats and opportunities. Collective councils could pursue a shared course, but individual communities sometimes opened channels of communication that others distrusted. A diplomatic narrative that calls the Confederacy simply pro-English obscures these patterns of negotiation and divergence.

Governor Frontenac's policies in New France combined diplomacy with military action, and French officials tried to detach Haudenosaunee nations from English support. Haudenosaunee diplomats used the prospect of peace with France to demand better English assistance, while English administrations urged them not to settle without consultation. The Chain thereby became a contested instrument. One party might invoke the obligation to help an ally in war; the other might reply that promised supplies had not arrived or that peace was necessary for survival.

The 1680s and 1690s produced severe destruction in Haudenosaunee country, including French campaigns against settlements and crops. The effects of war were not restricted to battles. Agricultural losses could threaten winter food supplies; families faced bereavement and captivity; kinship networks mediated adoption and the return of prisoners. These conditions put pressure on leaders to find alternatives to continuous conflict. Negotiations over peace were therefore also decisions about demographic recovery and community reconstruction.

European policymakers sometimes understood an alliance as implying exclusive military obedience. Haudenosaunee envoys could instead treat English promises of military aid as one of several reciprocal duties that could be judged by performance. When English assistance seemed inadequate, negotiating separately with the French could be a political necessity. Such moves did not require repudiating every aspect of the Chain. They reflected the possibility of simultaneous relationships, each with its own stated terms and obligations.

The Catholic Haudenosaunee communities along the St. Lawrence complicated colonial assumptions. Some maintained personal and family ties with their original nations while living within the orbit of New France. Their religious affiliations did not erase their political or kinship interests. Diplomats and military leaders therefore operated amid multiple channels of influence that cannot be summarized by two rigid European blocs. Earlier chapters examined religious change and inter-Indigenous warfare; here those developments matter because they altered who could carry news, claim kin, and negotiate peace.

English colonial divisions further undermined expectations of a unified wartime alliance. New York merchants depended on commerce, while other colonies calculated frontier threats differently. London could set imperial objectives without supplying the goods that visiting delegations expected. Colonial proclamations about friendship thus had to be compared against concrete performance. If an English governor insisted that the Five Nations fight France, Indigenous leaders could ask what the promised relationship actually yielded to their people.

By the turn of the eighteenth century, the strategic logic of seeking peace with both European powers had become increasingly attractive. This did not mean the Confederacy as a whole ceased to have rivals or that every community welcomed an agreement. Rather, exhaustion, political calculation, and an altered balance among western Indigenous nations encouraged negotiations. The significance of the French wars for the Chain lies in revealing its flexibility and limits: it could frame obligations but could not dictate the outcome of Haudenosaunee diplomacy.

Section 56.10

The Great Peace of Montreal and the Reorientation of Diplomacy, 1701

The Great Peace of Montreal in 1701 was a major restructuring of Indigenous and French diplomatic relations in the Great Lakes and St. Lawrence world. Delegates from numerous Indigenous nations gathered with representatives of New France to end hostilities and create procedures for handling future disputes. Haudenosaunee representatives participated alongside former enemies. The agreement emerged from extensive negotiations among Indigenous political actors; French officials could not have imposed it simply by decree. The French governor's role as mediator was important, but it did not make Indigenous parties passive recipients of a European peace.

This settlement affected the Covenant Chain precisely because the Haudenosaunee did not abandon their English connections. They sought to preserve relations with Albany while accommodating peace with France and French-allied nations. What historians describe as a strategy of neutrality was a set of negotiated choices, not permanent withdrawal from all conflict. The Haudenosaunee had interests in trade routes, prisoners, former hunting areas, and the security of particular settlements. Neutrality helped keep diplomatic alternatives available when exclusive dependence on one power could be costly.

The Albany proceedings of 1701 must be distinguished from the Montreal settlement. On July 19, 1701, the document often called the Nanfan deed purported to surrender an extensive western tract associated with Haudenosaunee beaver-hunting claims to the English Crown while reserving continued hunting and British protection. Its language was unmistakably legalistic and made broad assertions of conquest, sovereignty, and hereditary rights. It was not the same instrument as the peace reached at Montreal, and it cannot simply be treated as evidence that every community occupying the described country surrendered its territory.

The Nanfan deed reveals the politics of translation between legal systems. Haudenosaunee signatories could have viewed an English royal claim as a diplomatic means of resisting French forts and asserting protection for their hunting interests. English officials could treat the deed as an imperial territorial title. Those understandings were not necessarily identical. Moreover, the claims reached places inhabited or used by other Indigenous nations whose political consent was not established by the wording of an Albany document. The document is strong evidence for what certain parties represented at Albany, not conclusive proof of all the rights it asserted.

The treaty's territorial descriptions invite critical reading. Boundaries were presented through lakes, rivers, passes, and places rendered into colonial spellings. Their apparent precision should not be confused with a reliable survey of a stable political frontier. Regional histories were marked by wars, displacement, shared hunting practices, and competing claims. A description covering hundreds of miles can tell us much about geopolitical ambition while telling us relatively little about the governance of each village within the area named.

The relationship between the 1701 peace and the Chain was therefore deliberately plural. French envoys could claim that Haudenosaunee friendship with Montreal should limit attacks, while English officials could claim that the deed and older alliance assured British protection. Haudenosaunee diplomats could invoke both arrangements to resist demands for exclusive obedience. This balancing required continuing councils, because neither European government accepted every implication of Indigenous independence. A change in the European balance might threaten the practical viability of the arrangement.

The agreements were also experienced differently within the Confederacy. Western communities had immediate concerns involving French and Great Lakes Indigenous neighbors; eastern Mohawk communities maintained intensive commercial contact with Albany. Catholic relatives near Montreal provided another channel. The peace did not erase those interests, but it gave leaders new grounds to pursue travel, trade, and a reduction in violence. Whether it worked for a particular town had to be measured through local experience rather than a diplomatic label alone.

For the Chain, 1701 was not an ending. It showed that the alliance could coexist with a separate peace, provided the parties could tolerate divided loyalties and ambiguous claims. It also introduced territorial documents that later colonial advocates would use expansively. The same arrangements that extended Indigenous political options could become instruments for asserting European title over lands whose residents had not agreed to such a transformation.

Section 56.11

Mahican, Schaghticoke, and Southern New England Relations

The Covenant Chain reached beyond its most visible Anglo-Haudenosaunee participants. Mahican communities, whose homelands included parts of the Hudson Valley, had long dealt with Dutch and English traders, neighboring Indigenous peoples, and colonial authorities. Their position was not reducible to that of clients waiting for Haudenosaunee permission. They possessed their own political histories and claims to land, even as warfare, settlement, and diplomacy altered the conditions under which they could exercise them.

Schaghticoke developed as a place of refuge and intercommunity relations in the upper Hudson region, including people displaced by the wars and colonial pressures of southern New England. Residents came with distinct histories and kinship ties. A council record that names 'the Schaghticoke' or 'the River Indians' may group people whose affiliations and political objectives varied. Refugee settlement could create new possibilities for protection and trade, but it also invited disputes about authority, land access, and who spoke for the community in colonial councils.

English officials sometimes sought to draw southern New England peoples into an arrangement managed through Albany. Such diplomacy served the colonial wish to restrain frontier warfare and protect travel, but Indigenous participants could have different motivations: avoiding a rival's raids, recovering prisoners, securing a home for displaced families, or establishing access to markets. Haudenosaunee leaders likewise might regard the extension of their peace arrangements as a means of influence or responsibility. The question was always whether those whose interests were being negotiated accepted the terms.

The politics surrounding King Philip's War are essential background. Communities that had supported, opposed, or tried to avoid the fighting did not emerge from it in equivalent positions. Some refugees sought security through new relationships; others remained distrustful of the English colonies that had dispossessed them. Haudenosaunee diplomacy could offer a route to peace without undoing the losses caused by the conflict. Treating all postwar arrangements as the creation of one homogeneous 'Indian alliance' would conceal differences in power and experience.

The Mahican and related communities also drew attention to the geography of protection. An Albany promise might have been made by a New York official, while a conflict took place in territory claimed by Massachusetts or Connecticut. Who would intervene? A council could proclaim a road safe, yet local settlers could refuse access or threaten the travelers. That gap between regional diplomatic language and dispersed colonial enforcement became a recurring weakness of the Chain. Its significance was often measured by whether particular people could continue to occupy and use their lands.

Relations between the Haudenosaunee and neighboring Algonquian peoples could include friendship, rivalry, adoption, intermarriage, trade, and claims of superiority. No one formula captured them all. Some neighboring spokespeople accepted particular arrangements with the Confederacy; others resisted a description of dependence. Colonial administrators could strategically amplify Haudenosaunee claims when they wanted a single negotiator to impose peace or release land. Historians should therefore locate each agreement in a regional political context rather than infer jurisdiction from the presence of a Haudenosaunee speaker.

Contemporary records also leave important silences. Colonists wrote down council speeches primarily when an issue concerned land, war, or trade; the experiences of women, displaced households, and people who disagreed with a named speaker were seldom recorded directly. Oral traditions, community histories, land records, and later petitions can complicate the official narrative. For students, this is an opportunity to see how a diplomatic system appeared different to a colonial commissioner, a Haudenosaunee delegate, and an Algonquian family negotiating its security.

The northeastern expansion of the Chain illustrates both its usefulness and its danger. A network of alliances could reduce violence and provide forums for claims; the same network could permit powerful participants to define the standing of less powerful communities. The result was a regional diplomatic order whose boundaries were less stable than colonial descriptions of a great alliance might imply.

Section 56.12

Pennsylvania, Lenape, Susquehanna, and the Mid-Atlantic Diplomatic World

Pennsylvania's establishment in the early 1680s brought a rapidly growing colony into the diplomatic world of the Susquehanna and Delaware valleys. William Penn's government promoted negotiation and purchase as means of obtaining land, yet such transactions occurred in places where multiple Indigenous peoples maintained distinct political interests. Lenape communities, Susquehannock survivors and descendants, Shawnee migrants, Haudenosaunee leaders, and colonial officials all participated in a changing regional order. The Covenant Chain became increasingly important because English authorities hoped to reach arrangements extending across those networks.

The importance of the Susquehanna corridor lay in its combination of settlement, commerce, migration, and political geography. Roads and waterways connected the lower river to the interior, while growing Pennsylvania farms and trading establishments increased pressure on Indigenous territory. Colonial agents frequently treated land deeds as resolving questions of possession. Indigenous communities might distinguish rights of residence, hunting, shared use, and diplomatic protection in ways that a blanket English title could obscure. Consequently, the same transaction could be understood as a limited arrangement by some parties and an irreversible sale by others.

Haudenosaunee influence in the region rested partly on warfare and political relationships formed during the seventeenth century. Diplomats could claim that conquest or protection entitled them to negotiate about neighboring peoples. Their standing, however, was contested. Lenape communities were not merely a dependent appendage of the Confederacy, and the fate of Susquehannock peoples cannot be understood through a single English category. A fundamental distinction must be maintained between a Haudenosaunee assertion, colonial willingness to accept it, and a neighboring nation's agreement to be represented.

Pennsylvania officials found such assertions useful because colonial expansion required apparently legitimate land titles and a means of restraining frontier conflict. Negotiating with a recognized Haudenosaunee speaker could appear simpler than consulting several different communities. But simplification could come at the expense of local rights. A deed's existence tells historians what certain people represented at a particular moment; it does not settle the validity of their authority over every family or settlement in its described area. This issue becomes central in the Walking Purchase and the councils of 1742.

The regional system also depended on people who moved among societies. Traders, interpreters, and messengers translated more than language. This work could create trust while also generating dependence. A skilled intermediary might be required for negotiations that participants could not otherwise undertake, giving that person influence over the wording of promises. Traders could encourage negotiations that protected commerce, while government officials sought agreements to advance provincial priorities. Indigenous leaders evaluated the usefulness of these figures in light of local interests. Thus even a council that seemed to be about land could also be about which communications route would remain open for years to come.

The expansion of the Chain did not produce a single agreed map of northeastern sovereignty. Pennsylvania, New York, Maryland, and Virginia possessed different colonial charters and conflicting political ambitions. Indigenous nations had their own territorial systems and practices. Colonial boundary claims could be expansive on paper yet unenforceable without Indigenous cooperation. When Pennsylvania enlisted Haudenosaunee assistance in disputes with Lenape residents, it exploited the gap between colonial legal aspirations and the complicated political standing of communities on the ground.

By the early eighteenth century, the Covenant Chain had become a means by which colonial governments sought to regulate relations far beyond Albany, and Haudenosaunee leaders could use the same system to assert their own influence. The benefits were real: recurrent councils, mediation, commercial openings, and the possibility of peace. The costs were also real when the diplomatic claims of a powerful neighbor were converted into deeds that deprived another Indigenous people of its homeland.

Division IV — Expansion, Land, and the Intercolonial Treaty System

Section 56.13

The Tuscarora and the Changing Six Nations Confederacy

The Tuscarora entered the northern Haudenosaunee world amid violence and displacement following wars in Carolina in the early eighteenth century. Tuscarora communities were themselves politically diverse, and their relationship with the original Five Nations developed over years rather than through one universally decisive ceremony. Some people remained in the South, while others migrated northward, often toward Oneida country. Their movement demonstrates that confederacies were not immobile constitutional relics: they could adapt to population change, kinship claims, and the needs of people seeking refuge and political security.

The wars in Carolina from 1711 onward combined colonial settlement pressure, Indigenous rivalries, violence in trade and enslavement, and decisions by different Tuscarora communities. They should not be reduced to an inevitable migration caused by the expansion of one colony. For those who moved north, incorporation into a wider Haudenosaunee political framework could offer protection, connections to related peoples, and access to established councils. For the Confederacy, receiving newcomers could enlarge communities while requiring negotiation over residence, political voice, and social responsibilities.

The expression 'Six Nations' increasingly entered colonial records, but nomenclature did not reveal the entire internal constitution. The Tuscarora became acknowledged as a sixth nation without automatically inheriting precisely the same council positions as the original five. Their political participation, local decision-making, and relations with the Oneida evolved over time. Claims that they 'joined in 1722' may summarize a useful period but risk suggesting a single, fully documented transition. The student should distinguish historical process from administrative convenience in recording a date.

The Tuscarora case also reveals the limits of treating Indigenous migration as a loss of political agency. Moving families could reconstruct settlements, preserve language and kinship, and seek relations that made survival more secure. Women and households sustained that transition through agriculture, marriage networks, and the incorporation of people into new communities. A diplomatic record concentrating on sachems and treaties rarely captures those decisions, but they mattered for the ability of migrants to participate in collective politics.

Colonial officials encountered the Confederacy at the very moment its composition was changing. Documents might still speak of the Five Nations, sometimes out of habit or because a specific agreement named representatives of the original five, even after the Tuscarora had begun participating in Haudenosaunee political life. A primary record titled 'Treaty with the Five Nations' is therefore not necessarily proof that the Tuscarora had no relationship to the Confederacy, nor is a later reference to six nations proof that their rights in every council were identical.

The incorporation of Tuscarora communities intersects with but differs from the expansion of the Covenant Chain. The former concerned the political and social organization of an Indigenous confederacy; the latter concerned relations with colonial governments and associated Indigenous nations. English officials might use the same number-based label for both, but an Indigenous nation's relation to Onondaga was not the same as its relation to Albany. Confusing the two would reproduce the error of treating Haudenosaunee sovereignty as an extension of the British alliance.

The changing Confederacy also complicates how historians interpret territorial claims. Migrants might gain shelter and rights through kinship and agreement without automatically transferring their previous homelands into the jurisdiction of every other member nation. Likewise, the adoption of refugees did not make displaced communities merely instruments of a confederal central authority. Land, residence, and diplomatic representation were negotiated in distinct ways and at different scales.

By the early 1720s, the concept of the Six Nations had become increasingly meaningful in the politics of northeastern North America. Its historical importance lies not in a precise date alone but in the adaptation of Indigenous institutions under intense colonial pressure. That adaptability would help the Confederacy maintain influence even as the Covenant Chain expanded into intercolonial treaty making and new controversies over authority.

Section 56.14

The Great Treaty of 1722 and the Expansion of Intercolonial Diplomacy

The 1722 Albany negotiations illustrate the enlargement of the Covenant Chain beyond a bilateral New York–Haudenosaunee relationship. Representatives of New York, Virginia, and Pennsylvania assembled with the Five Nations and Mahican participants to address security, the movement of warriors, violence, and diplomatic obligations across provincial borders. Governor Alexander Spotswood of Virginia personally participated, demonstrating the importance colonial leaders attached to a settlement that could affect the Chesapeake and interior. The records show not a single instant of agreement but a sequence of speeches, replies, and attempted clarifications.

The documentary record also requires careful identification. The National Archives–linked digitization identifies a 1722 treaty involving governors and the Five Nations, while a published collection preserves additional proceedings and speeches, including those involving the Mahicans. These related textual witnesses should not simply be treated as interchangeable. A later certified copy of an earlier treaty, an eighteenth-century manuscript, and a nineteenth-century printed edition may preserve somewhat different framing. In a source window, the document's editorial history is part of its meaning.

For Virginia officials, the question of frontier passage was urgent. Haudenosaunee war parties had moved through areas where Virginia claimed responsibility for Indigenous allies and colonial settlements. Colonial leaders wanted rules defining places warriors should not cross. In the recorded answer to Spotswood of September 12, 1722, Five Nations speakers addressed the boundary associated with the River Kahongoronton and the mountain ridge. The speech also responded to Virginia's proposed penalties and offered assurances that people encountered would be treated peacefully. The details matter because they reveal negotiation rather than unconditional compliance.

The boundaries in the record were not modern international borders and should not be plotted as exact lines without identifying uncertainty in historical place names. The reference to a river, mountain ridge, and movement of persons functioned as a political effort to reduce the risk of violence among people following familiar routes. Colonial laws threatened severe punishment for those who crossed certain boundaries, but an agreement could not ensure that every traveler knew or obeyed them. Indigenous representatives had reasons to seek clear and reciprocal rules instead of surrendering freedom of movement entirely.

The presence of Mahican participants and references to neighboring peoples expose the system's multilateral character. An agreement among an English governor and Haudenosaunee representatives could affect communities not signatory to every proposition. Indigenous delegates could seek assurances that their own families, travelers, and allies would not be attacked. The emerging framework was thus a set of negotiated jurisdictions and practical obligations, not a blueprint for one unified government.

Wampum and speech remained central to the proceedings. Participants explained past injuries, confirmed willingness to live in peace, and debated how obligations should be carried forward. The printed record includes the names and formal titles of officials and preserves Indigenous replies through colonial clerks and interpreters. It can therefore reveal the public language of negotiation with unusual detail while leaving hidden the private consultations and community-level deliberations that made the replies politically possible.

The 1722 settlement also marked the widening colonial investment in Albany diplomacy. A Virginia governor traveled a substantial distance for negotiations hosted through New York's established institutions. Pennsylvania's participation connected the northern alliance to land and travel concerns farther south. It would be wrong, however, to infer that all three colonies subsequently followed identical policies or paid their obligations equally. Their assemblies, trading interests, and land ambitions could diverge, making collective assurances difficult to enforce.

For students the key analytical contrast is between the appearance of a single 'great treaty' and the underlying work of maintaining several relationships. The documents preserve a moment when colonial governments and Indigenous nations tried to reduce violence through explicit procedures, territorial language, and renewed commitments. Their significance rests as much in the problems they could not permanently settle as in the obligations they formally recorded.

Section 56.15

Pennsylvania Land Disputes and the Crisis of Indigenous Representation

Land pressure exposed some of the deepest contradictions of the Covenant Chain. Pennsylvania officials increasingly relied on documents and diplomatic relationships to obtain territory that Lenape communities occupied, cultivated, and used according to their own rights and obligations. Colonial proprietors treated the production of legal deeds as a method of turning contested claims into saleable property. Indigenous inhabitants could object that a deed was false, misunderstood, signed by unauthorized people, or not intended to transfer the rights asserted by colonial officials. A recognized Haudenosaunee role in regional diplomacy did not resolve these conflicts.

The Walking Purchase of 1737 became a particularly consequential case. Pennsylvania proprietors invoked an earlier and disputed document that purported to describe land by the distance a person might walk in a day and a half. Colonial organizers arranged a rapid, measured route that expanded the area claimed far beyond what many Lenape understood to have been contemplated. The problem was not merely the speed of the walkers. It concerned the authority and authenticity of the document, the meaning of the original arrangement, and the use of colonial power to make a contested claim effective on the ground.

The effects fell on communities whose livelihoods depended on the land. Farms, villages, hunting and gathering areas, sacred places, and routes were not interchangeable spaces on a map. Dispossession could force relocation toward the Susquehanna and beyond, disrupting kinship and patterns of political obligation. Some affected families resisted or remained as long as possible; others rebuilt elsewhere. The phrase 'the Lenape lost land' is accurate only as a beginning: a textbook must explore how colonial decisions remade households and political communities over time.

Pennsylvania officials sought Haudenosaunee support against Lenape resistance. At Philadelphia in 1742, Canassatego spoke in a manner that supported Pennsylvania's position and ordered Lenape representatives to depart from disputed lands. The colonial record includes humiliating kinship and gender language about the Lenape, which later writers have sometimes treated as proof of a permanent and universally recognized political subordination. That interpretation is unsafe. The speech was situated in a particular negotiation and reflected the interests and claims of the parties represented, not the uncontested consent of all Lenape communities.

The 1742 council raises a question about the practical meaning of protection. If a powerful Indigenous confederacy asserted the right to speak for neighboring people, could that relationship serve their security or facilitate their displacement? Haudenosaunee leaders had legitimate interests of their own, including alliances and claims shaped by prior wars. Colonial officials had strong incentives to interpret those claims expansively. Lenape people had their own relationships to land and were not passive objects of decisions made by others. A historically serious account must retain all three perspectives.

Interpreters and council procedure helped transform political rhetoric into apparent legal results. Conrad Weiser, familiar with Haudenosaunee protocol and trusted by Pennsylvania officials, took part in the work of explanation and translation. Printed minutes preserve the sequence of official propositions but not every private concern expressed by Lenape residents. The source can demonstrate what Canassatego and the governor represented; it is less reliable as a total account of the rights of people living in villages hundreds of miles from the meeting.

This episode also qualifies simple depictions of the Covenant Chain as a defensive alliance against European encroachment. It certainly supplied Indigenous negotiators with leverage and occasions to seek concessions. But it could also be used to create a colonial market in alleged Indigenous title, with documents formalizing dispossession. The process was not reducible to European trickery alone or to uncomplicated Haudenosaunee domination. It involved conflicting strategies, unequal access to written law, and disputed recognition of authority.

The Walking Purchase and Philadelphia council foreshadowed the even larger conflicts surrounding Lancaster in 1744. Both cases forced the question of representation: who could convey rights to territory, and whose interpretation would be enforced? Neither a council speech nor a deed can answer that question without examining the affected communities, the boundaries claimed, the history of negotiations, and the later conduct of the colonial governments.

Section 56.16

The Treaty of Lancaster, 1744: Canassatego and the Struggle over Territory

The Lancaster treaty council of June and July 1744 was a major episode in the Covenant Chain's intercolonial expansion. Delegates of the Six Nations met Pennsylvania's governor George Thomas and commissioners from Maryland and Virginia, with Conrad Weiser serving as interpreter. The printed proceedings, published in the colonial era and preserved in modern transcriptions, record multiple sessions over several days. This chronology matters: the negotiation did not consist of one simple trade of land for goods. Participants exchanged speeches, investigated claims, considered boundaries, delivered presents, and returned to points on which agreement was not immediate.

Pennsylvania acted as a diplomatic convener, linking the other colonies to a relationship that had developed through Albany and earlier councils. Maryland commissioners wanted a resolution to Haudenosaunee claims over lands already settled within the colony. Virginia representatives wished to clarify claims reaching toward western territory and to deal with disputes associated with frontier warfare and the movement of Indigenous people. Haudenosaunee delegates wanted recognition of their political standing, compensation, reciprocal assistance, and respect for the procedures through which they made commitments. None of these interests can be reduced to an abstract commitment to peace.

The first meetings demonstrate the importance of diplomatic method. Governor Thomas welcomed participants and presented wampum, and Canassatego confirmed his understanding of the governor's words through the interpreter before responding. The insistence on receiving and answering belts was not empty formality. It gave the participants a recognized way to verify propositions, show respect, and signal readiness to negotiate substantive disagreements. The printed text accordingly provides evidence not only for what was said but for how a political agreement was made possible.

Disputes over land dominated substantial portions of the proceedings. The Haudenosaunee asserted that some lands in Maryland and Virginia had been possessed through earlier conquest or political relations; the colonial commissioners disputed aspects of these claims and pressed for written releases. Maryland agreed to compensation for a release of claims, while discussions of boundaries involved rivers, forks, mountains, and the outermost settlements. The precise lines mattered to colonial officials seeking a legally defensible territory. For Haudenosaunee speakers, the negotiations also concerned relationships among political communities and the recognition of previous claims.

The council's exchanges demonstrate that English legal categories were actively debated. Gachradodow, speaking in a later session, challenged a premise of British authority by emphasizing distinct peoples' laws and denying that the Great King had conquered the Six Nations. This speech is especially useful for students because it directly contradicts a simplistic narrative of Indigenous subjection. Its surviving wording nevertheless passed through interpretation and colonial print. It demonstrates a public argument recorded at Lancaster; it does not provide unrestricted access to every layer of the speaker's private intention.

Conrad Weiser's role was crucial, and his presence should not be made invisible. The record shows officials asking him to relay propositions and participants checking whether the meaning had been understood. He was more than a dictionary between languages: he understood the ceremonies and political histories on which discussions depended. At the same time, he served Pennsylvania's government. His translation made communication possible while embedding the surviving record in the colonial institutions that commissioned and printed it. The form of compensation also deserves analysis. Goods were displayed, inspected, and evaluated, with discussion of their quality and value. Such exchange could signify the seriousness of an agreement in Indigenous diplomatic practice, while for colonial officials it became evidence of purchase or release. Students should not assume that one side necessarily failed to understand what was offered. Rather, the parties could consciously negotiate within different systems while disagreeing about the full reach of the obligation. After the council, colonial authorities often preferred interpretations that maximized their territorial rights.

Virginia's western claims proved especially consequential. Later advocates cited the 1744 negotiations to justify expansive territorial assertions. Yet the printed proceedings did not settle all questions concerning land west of the mountains, and Indigenous communities in the Ohio Country were not simply represented by the Haudenosaunee delegates at Lancaster. The deed's force in colonial legal arguments must therefore be distinguished from the actual political rights of Shawnee, Lenape, Wyandot, Miami, and other peoples occupying and using disputed territory.

Lancaster was also a forum for discussion of injury, trade, and relations with more distant Indigenous nations, including the Catawba and Cherokee. The record shows that the Chain's reach was not confined to the particular land releases for which the conference later became famous. When speakers addressed safe roads, past violence, and the duty to keep a council relationship bright, they connected local grievances to an intercolonial system of obligations. Ignoring those issues would reduce the meeting to a real-estate instrument and miss its wider diplomatic purpose.

Canassatego's remarks about unity have attracted later attention because eighteenth-century colonists increasingly debated how their governments might coordinate. The suggestion that the Haudenosaunee example influenced colonial constitutional thinking has been interpreted in differing ways, and one cannot infer a direct institutional genealogy from a single reported speech. In this chapter the more immediate significance lies in the speakers' effort to defend political unity and reciprocal authority within a negotiation that involved several English colonial administrations.

Lancaster thus exposes the Covenant Chain's central ambiguity. It enabled sustained multilateral diplomacy; Indigenous leaders could insist on recognition and payment; colonial governments had to employ established councils rather than simply announce decisions. At the same time, the agreed language could support later colonial territorial assertions far larger than the political consent demonstrated by those present. The central historical question is not just what the treaty said but how different parties later deployed its terms, and which Indigenous communities bore the consequences.

Division V — Crisis, Renewal, and the Approach of Imperial War

Section 56.17

The Covenant Chain and the Ohio Country

The Ohio Country was not an unoccupied frontier waiting to be allocated among European governments. It contained communities with their own political authorities, migration histories, economic connections, and understandings of land. Lenape and Shawnee migrants joined or established settlements across the region, while Wyandot, Miami, and other nations pursued diplomatic and commercial strategies shaped by the Great Lakes and upper Ohio river systems. Their interests frequently differed from the claims made about them by Virginia land speculators, Pennsylvania traders, French officers, or Haudenosaunee representatives.

By the middle of the eighteenth century, the Ohio Country linked competing routes of commerce and diplomacy. British traders moved inland from Pennsylvania and Virginia, while French officials sought control of communications between the St. Lawrence and Mississippi watersheds. Indigenous communities could profit by trading with more than one market and by retaining the capacity to move between political relationships. Their choices did not make them neutral empty space between empires. Rather, they helped determine whether imperial projects could function beyond a small number of forts and trading posts.

The Haudenosaunee entered these contests through claims grounded in earlier warfare, hunting rights, and relationships with neighboring nations. Such claims could become useful when colonial officials sought a recognized party with whom to negotiate a land release. But the residents of the Ohio Country had not universally authorized the Confederacy to sell their land. The political difficulty arose when colonial governments treated one kind of regional influence as a transferable title over all the territory in question. The discrepancy between claim and consent became a major source of conflict.

The Lancaster settlement in 1744 supplied material for expansive Virginia interpretations of western rights. Haudenosaunee and Virginia participants did not necessarily agree about the geographic reach of their transaction, and Ohio nations had their own positions. Traders and land companies meanwhile promoted claims that far exceeded settled colonial jurisdiction. A student reading subsequent Virginia demands should ask which provision of Lancaster was being cited, what land was described, and whether the people actually using the territory recognized any such sale.

At Logstown in 1752, Virginia representatives negotiated in a place where Haudenosaunee-associated spokespeople and Ohio residents confronted the practical meaning of earlier treaties. The conference exposed diverging interpretations of land and protection. Colonial officials hoped to turn ambiguous claims into clear opportunities for settlement; Indigenous representatives sought trade, security, and recognition of political interests. The record must be read as a negotiation among unevenly represented parties rather than proof that one decision had settled the entire Ohio Valley's political geography.

The Ohio Towns also had histories of their own diplomatic assemblies and intercommunity consultation. Mingoes, including Haudenosaunee people resident in the Ohio region, did not necessarily speak with the authority of the Onondaga council in every matter. Some towns balanced French and British approaches or resisted outside interference. Other communities regarded the assertion of a distant confederacy's power over their lands as a threat. Consequently, even the term 'Six Nations influence' must be explained at the scale of particular people and places.

European efforts to use the Covenant Chain in the Ohio Country revealed the system's geographical limitations. Albany commissioners could relay treaties and messages, but they could not guarantee safe passage or local compliance beyond their reach. Colonial settlement pressure converted uncertain claims into demands for actual land, while French fort-building and British commercial expansion raised the stakes. Indigenous diplomats had incentives to preserve alternatives but increasingly faced outsiders unwilling to accept those choices.

The Ohio Country thus provides a test of the Chain's legitimacy. It was an effective diplomatic network in certain relationships, but not a universal institution with unquestioned jurisdiction across the northeastern interior. Its use in western land negotiations could reinforce Haudenosaunee political standing while simultaneously undermining the sovereignty of neighboring nations. Those contradictions help explain why the crises of the 1750s cannot be reduced to rivalry between two European crowns.

Section 56.18

Protection, Dependency, and the Limits of Haudenosaunee Authority

The language of protection helped the Covenant Chain hold together, but it also generated difficult claims about dependency. A powerful nation could offer another community refuge, military assistance, or access to diplomacy. The recipient might accept help while understanding that its own political identity remained intact. The protector might describe the relationship through kinship or precedence, claiming a right to speak in certain matters. Colonial officials could then translate that claim into a legal hierarchy that neither Indigenous party understood in exactly the same way.

Haudenosaunee claims of authority over neighboring peoples were historically significant and cannot be dismissed as mere colonial invention. The Confederacy had exercised military power, negotiated settlements, incorporated captives and migrants, and formed relations with multiple nations. Its speakers could credibly assert an influential position in regional diplomacy. But influence did not automatically establish exclusive ownership of all lands inhabited by those peoples. The move from military superiority or protective relationship to permanent alienable title was especially problematic when made in colonial legal categories.

Lenape reactions to Pennsylvania's land policies demonstrate the difficulty. Some Haudenosaunee leaders presented Lenape communities as dependent, while affected Lenape peoples maintained claims grounded in residence, use, community history, and their own political authority. Neither a governor's endorsement nor a dramatic council speech can be treated as proof that all Lenape people accepted subordination. The rights of a nation must be distinguished from what a third party says that nation possesses or has relinquished.

The same problem appeared among Mahican communities, southern New England refugees, and peoples of the Ohio Country. Political relationships changed with war, resettlement, marriage, trading opportunity, and access to protection. The labels 'tributary,' 'subject,' 'ally,' and 'child' might describe different arrangements in different documents. A historian should avoid replacing local histories with one neat map of a Haudenosaunee empire. The territories and peoples connected to the Chain did not form an administrative state controlled from Albany or Onondaga.

Definition

Protection could refer to mutual or unequal arrangements of assistance; dependency was a claim about the standing of another community, not a fixed legal fact applicable everywhere.

Historical context

Haudenosaunee leaders sometimes asserted authority over neighboring peoples, and British negotiators could extend those assertions into land law.

Why it matters

A statement that one nation protected another does not by itself authorize sale of that nation's land.

Potential misunderstanding

Colonial terms such as 'subject,' 'conquest,' and 'cession' do not establish whether affected Indigenous inhabitants accepted a particular relationship.

Question

Did the Covenant Chain primarily enlarge Haudenosaunee diplomatic autonomy, or did it enable colonial manipulation and territorial dispossession?

Interpretation emphasizing Indigenous leverage

Repeated councils, reciprocal obligations, trade access, and the ability to negotiate with competing powers show significant Haudenosaunee strategic agency.

Interpretation emphasizing colonial appropriation

Land deeds, administrative favoritism, and colonial reliance on broad Haudenosaunee claims could draw the relationship toward settler expansion.

The British colonies also had uneven power. English officials sometimes relied on Haudenosaunee influence to mediate disputes because they lacked the soldiers or political legitimacy to act alone. Their recognition of Confederacy claims could therefore be a strategic choice rather than an impartial judgment about Indigenous sovereignty. Conversely, colonial commissioners could exploit divisions within Haudenosaunee councils, favor particular speakers, or secure signatures that did not reflect broad agreement. The resulting deed might strengthen colonial expansion while leaving Indigenous relations more strained.

Assessing whether the Chain increased or reduced Indigenous autonomy requires distinguishing participants. For some Haudenosaunee leaders the alliance secured goods, recognition, and additional negotiating forums. For certain neighboring communities it could offer peace or protection. For others it created a route through which their land became the subject of distant negotiations. A consequence beneficial to one nation could be harmful to another. No single verdict that ignores these differences adequately captures the historical record.

Scholarly discussions of the Covenant Chain have sometimes used imperial metaphors to describe Haudenosaunee power, while other studies emphasize consensus, alliance, and negotiations among neighboring nations. Both approaches raise useful questions, but neither vocabulary should determine the evidence in advance. The historian must ask how political claims were made, accepted, rejected, or enforced and how the outcome differed across regions. An enduring alliance does not necessarily imply an integrated empire; a confederacy with political autonomy could still participate in coercive relations.

By the 1750s these tensions were severe. Colonial settlers sought more land, governors sought stable frontiers, and Indigenous towns sought recognition of rights that formal deeds could ignore. The Chain could still bring representatives together, but council participation could no longer guarantee justice or even agreement about who had standing. Its apparent success in multiplying diplomatic links had produced competing understandings of what those links authorized.

Section 56.19

The Mohawk Crisis and the Fracturing of the Chain, 1753

The crisis of 1753 emerged from years of dissatisfaction with New York's management of its Haudenosaunee relationships. Mohawk leaders complained about land encroachment, trade abuses, insecure protection, and officials who did not respond adequately to earlier petitions. These were not minor complaints about ceremonial etiquette. They concerned whether the British alliance could still defend community rights and support political independence when colonial settlers and merchants were pursuing objectives contrary to Indigenous interests.

Hendrick Theyanoguin was a prominent Mohawk speaker associated with the movement to demand reform. His speeches are often remembered through an assertion that the Covenant Chain was broken. The statement should be located in the context of deliberate diplomatic pressure rather than treated as an irrevocable termination of every agreement. To declare an alliance injured was to demand conduct that could repair it, and continued negotiation indicates that leaders were still assessing whether the relationship could be made useful again.

William Johnson had developed relationships with Mohawk and other Haudenosaunee leaders through residence, commerce, and personal diplomacy in the Mohawk Valley. He offered a channel to the colonial government that was different from the Albany commissioners. Indigenous diplomats could assess Johnson's reliability and seek his help, while rival colonial interests disputed the administration of trade and treaty business. The crisis thus involved political authority within New York as well as relationships between the colony and the Confederacy.

The Albany commissioners' commercial entanglements were a source of distrust. A merchant official might benefit from a profitable trading system while failing to prevent exploitative transactions or encroachment on Indigenous land. Mohawk complaints reflected the gap between council promises of fair treatment and the conditions encountered by people visiting markets or defending farms. Without attention to those practical matters, a narrative of 'breaking the chain' sounds like a symbolic dispute divorced from its social causes.

The midcentury crisis unfolded amid competition with New France. French authorities sought diplomatic and military partnerships in the interior, and some Haudenosaunee communities maintained alternative contacts. English officials feared the loss of Native allies, but fears of French influence did not explain away Indigenous grievances. It was entirely possible for Mohawk leaders to criticize British administration because they wanted a more effective relationship rather than because they had secretly committed themselves to the French.

Colonial records describing Hendrick's speeches require close reading. The surviving version may be filtered through interpreters, scribes, and a later editor. A printed line can reveal what officials believed a speaker said, but its exact form should not automatically be quoted as if an audio transcript survived. A historian should compare the setting, the persons addressed, the subsequent efforts to negotiate, and later official reactions. The meaning of a dramatic phrase becomes clearer when linked to action over the following months.

Johnson's efforts to restore dialogue demonstrate that the Chain still held value. Councils could renew expressions of friendship and promise examination of grievances, but repairing words alone could not settle disputed land purchases or reform the commission system. Haudenosaunee leaders faced decisions about whether to trust new assurances, how far to press their claims, and whether competing colonial powers offered useful alternatives. The practical question was not whether an abstract alliance existed but whether officials would honor it under pressure.

The crisis of 1753 is therefore best understood as a warning produced by the Chain's own language of reciprocity. Leaders could demand that a tarnished chain be polished precisely because they possessed a recognized tradition of mutual obligations. The incident prepared the setting for the Albany Congress of 1754, where intercolonial representatives were forced to listen to complaints that New York had not resolved. It did not make subsequent imperial war inevitable; it revealed how fragile the diplomatic foundation of British strategy had become.

Section 56.20

The Albany Congress of 1754 and the Covenant Chain on the Eve of War

The Albany Congress of 1754 brought together delegates from seven British colonies in an effort to coordinate colonial policy amid increasing competition with France and strained relations with the Haudenosaunee. It met in June and July and included formal exchanges with Indigenous representatives, but its proceedings also reveal extensive discussions among colonial commissioners concerning their own political disagreements. The event was therefore not a single treaty meeting. It combined efforts to repair the Covenant Chain, planning for intercolonial cooperation, and negotiations about land at a moment of growing uncertainty.

The location was deliberate. Albany had long been an essential council place, and colonial authorities hoped that its history would lend legitimacy to fresh assurances. Haudenosaunee participants had reasons to attend as well. They could present complaints directly to multiple colonial governments, seek gifts and reforms, and test whether promises of assistance were credible. Attendance did not require that the Confederacy as a whole accept all imperial aims. Nor did the delegates present necessarily represent every Indigenous community whose land was subsequently discussed.

Hendrick Theyanoguin and other spokesmen brought forward grievances about land, liquor, trade abuses, and inadequate protection. The colonial record preserves formal exchanges in which officials promised to inquire into particular complaints and renew the relationship. Some delegates connected the apparent decline of Haudenosaunee confidence to the conduct of Albany commissioners. Such statements were useful to colonial reformers as well as to Indigenous participants; they served different agendas. The teacher should invite students to separate the grievance itself from the political uses made of it by those recording the proceedings.

The records also show that the so-called River Indians and other communities were not equally visible in the commissioners' planning. Debate over who would provide food and presents for visitors reveals an institutional hierarchy: the conduct of a council depended on which colonial government recognized an obligation toward particular participants. Indigenous people who had long attended Albany negotiations could be left out of formal arrangements when colonial officials disagreed about responsibility. This detail shows that diplomacy was not simply high rhetoric but a dispute over resources and recognition.

Primary Source Window — Albany Congress Proceedings, 1754

Context and source type

Proceedings of the June–July 1754 Congress, preserved in a nineteenth-century edited compilation of colonial papers, include formal meetings with Haudenosaunee leaders and internal sessions of the colonial delegates.

Source: Albany Congress proceedings and treaty materials.

Editorial paraphrase — not a direct quotation

Haudenosaunee representatives raised concerns about land, liquor, fair dealing, and protection. Commissioners proposed renewed assurances and debated methods of coordinating colonial management of Indigenous affairs.

Analysis

The record distinguishes Indigenous complaints from colonial policy proposals, showing where interests overlapped and where they diverged. It also documents differences among colonial governments over the treatment of other visiting Indigenous communities.

Limitations and perspective

Speeches were translated and recorded through colonial intermediaries; not all affected Ohio and Susquehanna communities participated. Land purchases associated with July 6 and July 11 must be examined separately rather than assumed to be one treaty.

The British colonial representatives debated a proposed union to coordinate defense, trade, western land purchases, and relations with Indigenous nations. Benjamin Franklin played a significant role in drafting the plan discussed at the congress. The proposal envisioned an imperial structure with a president-general and council empowered in matters including Indian affairs and western settlement. It was not implemented as proposed. Its significance for this chapter lies in how colonial leaders viewed Indigenous relations as a central problem of governance, even while designing institutions that would expand colonial authority.

The language of the intercolonial proposal exposes its own assumptions. Commissioners argued that private land purchases and disorderly commerce had damaged relations, yet proposed stronger centralized supervision of Indigenous trade and land acquisitions. Such reforms might have restrained particular abuses but would also have concentrated power in British colonial institutions. Haudenosaunee support for better enforcement of agreed obligations should not be confused with consent to every constitutional arrangement British delegates considered among themselves.

Land transactions conducted during the congress intensified existing controversy. A Pennsylvania proprietary purchase dated July 6, 1754, involved a substantial Susquehanna-area tract and was negotiated amid claims about which lands the Six Nations would or would not convey. A separate purchase dated July 11, associated with Connecticut's Susquehanna Company, asserted rights involving the Wyoming region. These were different agreements with different claimants, and neither should be merged into the Congress's diplomatic proceedings as though it were one uncontested settlement. Their validity and effect were contested by other Indigenous and colonial interests.

The separate land dealings reveal the Chain's political contradictions sharply. Colonists meeting to restore an alliance could also obtain deeds from selected Indigenous representatives. The implications for Lenape, Shawnee, and other resident or interested communities were not determined simply by the presence of Haudenosaunee negotiators. Pennsylvania and Connecticut interests competed over territory, while Haudenosaunee political authority itself was a subject of dispute. The same occasion that publicly promised friendship could generate new grounds for distrust.

Even the official attendance lists and speeches need interpretation. Not all Haudenosaunee member nations sent equal numbers of representatives, and some communities maintained alternative connections with French settlements and missions. The congress's minutes were produced by colonial clerks, later compiled and published, and they privilege the agenda of the colonial commissioners. They remain indispensable for reconstructing meetings and proposals, but the reader must resist treating the printed record as a complete collective Indigenous account of the event.

The Albany Congress did not end the Covenant Chain. Diplomacy continued afterward, and Indigenous leaders still possessed alternatives and leverage. Nor should the meeting be written as the inevitable first act of a preordained British victory. In 1754 the future of the interior remained contested, colonial governments struggled to coordinate, and Indigenous nations assessed the costs of cooperation, neutrality, and resistance. The agreements and failures of Albany were historically consequential precisely because different outcomes remained possible.

By following the Chain from Dutch antecedents through Albany, Montreal, Lancaster, and the crisis of 1754, students can see that diplomatic institutions survived through repeated negotiation while developing serious contradictions. The Haudenosaunee secured recognition and bargaining opportunities, colonial officials gained access to alliances and territorial claims, and neighboring nations sometimes found their rights debated without their effective consent. The chain's endurance was real; its capacity to guarantee just relationships or prevent imperial pressure was not. That tension prepares the ground for Chapter 57's examination of war without replacing that chapter's military and political narrative.

Chapter Summary

The Covenant Chain emerged from early Indigenous–Dutch trade and diplomacy and the renegotiation of relationships after English authorities took New Netherland in 1664. It was not a one-time treaty or an extension of colonial authority over the Haudenosaunee Confederacy. Its membership, purposes, and obligations developed through successive councils.

The political institutions of the Haudenosaunee were distinct from the chain. Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga, Seneca, and later Tuscarora communities maintained internal processes of consultation and government. Mohawk diplomatic prominence at Albany did not automatically make every Mohawk decision binding on the other nations.

Wampum, condolence, speeches, gifts, and kinship metaphors gave agreements public form. The instruction to polish a silver chain expressed a political expectation that parties would act to repair injured obligations. Those institutions required interpreters, food, travel, household support, and other practical work.

The Albany conferences of 1677 and subsequent renewal councils developed a diplomatic network across the colonies. The July proceedings associated with Maryland envoy Henry Coursey are especially valuable evidence for chain imagery, while the existence of several different conferences cautions against assigning one uncontested founding date.

Conflict with New France exposed the limits of exclusive alliances. The Great Peace of Montreal in 1701 enabled the Haudenosaunee to pursue peace and strategic choices while maintaining English relations. The Nanfan deed executed at Albany the same year was a separate legal instrument whose expansive land claims require critical scrutiny.

The relationship widened to include neighboring Indigenous peoples and additional English colonies. Its reach could provide mediation and security, but power remained uneven. Mahican, Schaghticoke, Lenape, and other peoples possessed their own political histories and did not necessarily recognize the standing attributed to them by powerful neighbors.

The 1722 Albany treaty proceedings show how questions of travel, peace, and jurisdiction connected New York, Virginia, Pennsylvania, and Indigenous political communities. The Tuscarora's incorporation into the Haudenosaunee Confederacy illustrates Indigenous institutional change that must not be confused with expansion of the colonial alliance.

The 1737 Walking Purchase and the Philadelphia meeting of 1742 exposed the dangers of treating contested Haudenosaunee claims as authorization to convey Lenape land. The records preserve important speeches but cannot substitute for the consent of communities whose homelands were at stake.

Lancaster in 1744 joined several colonial governments and Six Nations representatives in detailed deliberation about territory and political obligations. Its proceedings reveal the importance of interpreters, reciprocal forms, and Indigenous challenges to claims of British conquest, even as later colonial readings promoted expansive land claims.

The Ohio Country and the Mohawk crisis of 1753 exposed growing pressures on the chain. The Albany Congress in 1754 attempted to restore confidence but also coincided with competing land purchases and colonial plans for consolidated authority. The Covenant Chain remained important, yet its mechanisms could not resolve all disputes over protection, territorial rights, and representation.

Chapter Reflection

The history of the Covenant Chain turns on an apparent paradox: a durable diplomatic institution could endure precisely because its obligations were open to renegotiation. Yet that flexibility allowed participants to attach very different meanings to the same formal expressions of peace. A chain that required continual polishing was both a promise and a recurring admission that promises could be broken.

The Haudenosaunee Confederacy retained its own political institutions and substantial negotiating capacity through decades of imperial rivalry. Its influence did not amount to unquestioned authority over all neighboring nations. The practical histories of Lenape, Mahican, Shawnee, and Ohio communities show why sovereignty must be studied at several scales rather than assigned solely from the language of a colonial deed.

The wealth of surviving council records makes this chapter unusually documentable, but their abundance should not create false certainty. Speeches preserved through interpretation and colonial editing illuminate public propositions. They also leave out much of the deliberation, household experience, and political dissent that made agreements meaningful or controversial.

In 1754 neither a complete diplomatic collapse nor a settled European conquest was predetermined. Indigenous leaders still made choices among alliances, trade routes, and strategies of resistance or accommodation. The next chapter examines how those choices changed during the Seven Years’ War, while this chapter closes by establishing the political institutions and historical contingencies through which they were made.
