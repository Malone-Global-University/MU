Indigenous Slavery, Captivity, and Adoption

Indigenous Peoples of North America: A Continental History

Chapter Opening

Captivity did not always mean enslavement, adoption did not erase the violence of capture, and the existence of slavery in some Indigenous societies before European colonization did not make colonial slavery a simple continuation of older practices. Each of those statements is true, and the history of the continent cannot be understood without holding all three together.

In the early morning of February 29, 1704, a force of French soldiers and Indigenous warriors attacked the English frontier town of Deerfield, Massachusetts. The attackers killed dozens of residents and took more than one hundred people north toward New France. Among the captives was seven-year-old Eunice Williams, daughter of the minister John Williams. Her father was eventually redeemed and returned to New England, where he published an account of his captivity. Eunice was not. She was taken to the Mohawk community of Kahnawake near Montreal, baptized as a Catholic, and in time married a Kahnawake man. She visited her relatives in Massachusetts on several occasions but never permanently rejoined them. Her case is among the best known in the history of North American captivity, and, for that reason, it is also one of the most misleading when treated as typical.

It is misleading, first, because it concerns an English child, and the documentary record preserves far more about English captives than about the Indigenous people who made up the great majority of those held in bondage across the continent between 1500 and 1821. It is misleading, second, because it describes adoption, a process in which a captive was incorporated into a family and community, while many other captives in the same period were killed, ransomed, sold, held as household servants, shipped across the Atlantic, or compelled to labor in missions and colonial households. A Pawnee girl purchased in Montreal, a Tuscarora man sold through Charleston to a Caribbean planter, a Chumash or Kumeyaay worker confined within a Franciscan mission, and a Haida captive taken in a coastal raid and held in a chief's household all experienced loss of kin and homeland, yet the legal, social, and economic systems that held them were profoundly different from one another.

This chapter is a comparative continental history of those systems. It asks how Indigenous societies governed warfare, captivity, incorporation, servitude, and enslavement before and during European colonization, and how colonial empires and settler societies transformed those institutions. It examines Haudenosaunee mourning wars, the Indigenous and French slave trades of the Great Lakes and the St. Lawrence, English enslavement in New England, the Carolina slave trade, Spanish imperial labor law and its evasions, the genízaro communities of New Mexico, Comanche and Apache captivity, slavery among Northwest Coast peoples, mission labor in California, and the entangled histories of Indigenous and African-descended people in the Southeast. The chronological emphasis falls on approximately 1500 to 1821, with earlier evidence used to understand precolonial institutions.

The evidence for this subject is uneven and often disturbing. Historians draw on military correspondence, mission registers, parish and notarial records, court proceedings, trade accounts, captive narratives, archaeology, linguistic evidence, and Indigenous oral histories. Most of the surviving written sources were produced by captors, purchasers, priests, and officials rather than by the captives themselves, and the published narratives that survive are those of people who were released, who escaped, or who were able to tell their stories in a language and genre that colonial readers valued. A responsible chapter must therefore ask continually whose voice a source preserves and whose it omits.

The chapter also resists two opposite temptations. One treats Indigenous societies as universally benevolent toward captives, imagining adoption as a gentle alternative to European slavery. The other measures every Indigenous nation against a single model of "slave-holding," as though all societies practiced one institution and differed only in degree. The evidence supports neither view. Some societies, such as certain Northwest Coast communities, maintained hereditary slavery with property status and enduring exclusion. Others, such as the Haudenosaunee, often incorporated captives into kin groups after violence and coercion. Still others combined practices, or changed them over time under the pressure of epidemic loss, commercial opportunity, and imperial demand.

The chapter belongs to Part IX of this textbook because captivity and slavery were part of the new geopolitics of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. Indigenous nations negotiated alliances, traded, raided, and defended their territories within systems that increasingly tied their political choices to European empires and Atlantic markets, a subject also treated in Chapters 46, 50, 51, and 55. At the same time, captivity shaped households, languages, kinship systems, and identities from the St. Lawrence to the Pacific. The chapter's governing aim is to distinguish these institutions carefully, to recover the experiences of the people who lived within them, and to show how they shaped the sovereignty and survival of Indigenous communities beyond 1821.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Distinguish captivity, ransom, adoption, incorporation, servitude, forced labor, and slavery, and explain why historians must not treat them as identical institutions.
  2. Describe the evidence for captive-taking and unequal status in selected precolonial Indigenous societies while avoiding the claim that every nation practiced slavery.
  3. Explain the relationship among warfare, bereavement, demographic loss, and captive adoption in Haudenosaunee and other Eastern Woodlands communities.
  4. Trace how Indigenous captive-taking, French alliances, and colonial legal claims produced the Panis slave system of New France.
  5. Analyze how English colonial warfare and law enabled Indigenous enslavement in New England and the Carolina trade, and assess the regional consequences of the Tuscarora and Yamasee Wars.
  6. Compare Spanish imperial law with colonial practice, distinguishing slavery from encomienda, repartimiento, debt servitude, and mission labor.
  7. Explain the formation of genízaro communities and why incorporation into colonial society did not guarantee equal standing.
  8. Compare Comanche, Apache, Navajo, Ute, and Pueblo captive systems in terms of age, gender, ransom, and kinship.
  9. Evaluate Northwest Coast slavery as an institution linked to hereditary rank, property, and household production, and assess how maritime trade altered it.
  10. Distinguish California mission labor and confinement from chattel slavery while recognizing their coercive character.
  11. Interpret African-Indigenous relationships in the Southeast, including refuge, kinship, and the adoption of racial slavery by some Indigenous slaveholders.
  12. Assess the evidentiary limits of captive narratives, mission registers, court records, and oral history, and evaluate how historians compare Indigenous and colonial slaveries.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. What distinguished captivity, ransom, adoption, coerced labor, and enslavement, and why do those distinctions matter for historical interpretation?
  2. How did different Indigenous societies decide who could be incorporated, who could be exchanged, and who could be killed?
  3. In what ways did colonial markets and imperial law transform existing Indigenous practices without simply replacing them?
  4. Why did the same word, such as Panis or genízaro, describe people from many different nations?
  5. How did women and children shape, and how were they shaped by, systems of captivity?
  6. What forms of freedom were available to captives, and how did Indigenous, French, English, and Spanish institutions define freedom differently?
  7. How should historians read sources produced almost entirely by captors, purchasers, and officials?
  8. Was Indigenous slavery in colonial North America best understood as a single system, a set of interacting systems, or a series of regional histories?

Historical Context

By 1500, many Indigenous societies already had long histories of warfare, raiding, and the movement of people between communities. Archaeological evidence from sites such as Crow Creek in South Dakota, where a large number of people were killed around the early fourteenth century, shows that organized violence was a feature of some pre-contact regions, though archaeologists differ about its causes and about what happened to the survivors. Captive-taking was not universal, nor was it always economically motivated. Its meanings depended on kinship structures, political authority, ritual obligations, and the availability of labor.

European colonization altered this landscape in several ways. Epidemic diseases, examined in Chapter 40, killed large numbers of people and created demographic pressures that affected how communities treated outsiders. European demand for furs, deerskins, laborers, and servants created new commercial incentives. Firearms and horses, examined in Chapters 46 and 49, changed the scale and mobility of warfare. And the legal systems of France, England, and Spain, each with its own doctrines about conquest, just war, property in persons, and the status of Indigenous people, created different categories into which captives could be placed.

The three European empires also differed in how they treated enslavement. Spanish law, from the New Laws of 1542 onward, restricted the enslavement of Indigenous people while permitting forms of compulsory labor and a number of exceptions. French officials in the Great Lakes relied on Indigenous allies and often received enslaved people as diplomatic gifts, until a 1709 ordinance in New France recognized the legal ownership of Indigenous and Black slaves. English colonies developed statutes and customs that permitted the enslavement of captives taken in what they called just wars and of people purchased from Indigenous traders, while also building, by the later seventeenth century, increasingly racialized codes of slavery centered on Africans.

Students should keep in mind that Indigenous nations were not passive in these developments. Some leaders sought captives to rebuild their communities after epidemics. Some traded captives to acquire firearms and goods. Some sought to rescue relatives from captivity, or to use ransom and exchange as instruments of diplomacy. Others were themselves victims of raids, sold, and sent far from their homelands. Frequently the same community occupied several of these positions at different times, and it is a central methodological commitment of this chapter that Indigenous peoples must not be classified permanently as either perpetrators or victims.

Section 53.1

Indigenous Captivity and Slavery as a Continental Historical Problem

The vocabulary of captivity is treacherous because English words such as "captive," "prisoner," "slave," "servant," and "adopted relative" cover institutions that Indigenous, French, Spanish, and English societies did not define in the same way. A captive, in the broadest sense, is a person seized by force and held by those who seized him or her. Captivity describes the condition of being taken; it does not by itself specify what happens next. A captive might be killed, held for ransom, exchanged in diplomacy, put to work in a household, married into a community, adopted into a family to replace a deceased relative, sold to another community, or sold to Europeans. Slavery is a narrower and more specific condition. In its fullest form, historians use it to describe a status in which a person is treated as property, can be bought, sold, or transferred, is excluded from the kinship and political community of the owner, and often transmits that condition to descendants.

These distinctions matter because Indigenous societies did not form a single category with regard to captivity. In some communities, those taken in war were quickly absorbed into kin networks and, once adopted, could hold recognized positions as sons, daughters, mothers, or siblings. In others, captives remained outsiders, could be bought or sold, and had no claim on the protections of kinship. In still others, both patterns coexisted, depending on a captive's age, sex, skills, and the preferences of the household or leader who held them. Historians have also emphasized that the line between incorporation and servitude could be blurred. A person might be adopted and also required to perform heavy labor; a person called a "slave" in colonial records might in practice live as a member of an Indigenous household.

European colonization did not introduce captivity to North America, but it altered it. Colonial empires created markets that valued Indigenous captives as laborers, and they developed legal categories to define who could be owned. At the same time, Indigenous nations used captives in diplomacy and trade, sometimes giving them as gifts to confirm alliances, sometimes selling them for firearms and other goods. The resulting systems were hybrid and regionally distinct. In the Great Lakes, for example, French officials received enslaved people through Indigenous alliance networks. In the Southeast, English traders supplied weapons on credit to Indigenous partners who then raided other Indigenous communities. In New Mexico, Spanish settlers purchased or ransomed captives at trading fairs and baptized them as household dependents. Each of these systems depended on Indigenous participation and on colonial institutions, and none can be explained by either alone.

Historical Vocabulary
Captivity, Slavery, Adoption, and Servitude

Definition. Captivity is the condition of being seized and held by force. Slavery is a status in which a person is treated as property and, characteristically, is socially excluded and subject to sale or transfer. Adoption is the formal incorporation of an outsider into a kin group, often with an assigned kin role. Servitude is a condition of compulsory or obligated labor that may be temporary, may be regulated by law or custom, and may not involve ownership of the person.

Historical context. Indigenous languages used terms that did not map neatly onto these English categories. Colonial administrators often used "slave" for any coerced dependent, while Indigenous speakers might have distinguished among a captive awaiting a decision, an adopted relative, a purchased worker, and a hereditary dependent.

Why it matters. Using one term for all forms of captivity obscures real differences in property status, heredity, kinship, and opportunities for freedom.

Potential misunderstanding. Adoption is not the opposite of coercion. A captive adopted into a family might have been taken violently, separated from relatives, and compelled to accept a new identity. At the same time, not every captive was a slave in the sense of being owned, and the existence of slavery in some Indigenous societies does not mean that colonial slave systems merely continued older practice.

The chapter's method follows from these cautions. It treats captivity, slavery, and adoption as institutions to be compared region by region and period by period. It does not suppose that a universal Indigenous slavery existed, nor that those practices called slavery were everywhere the same. It also declines to romanticize. Whether a captive was adopted, ransomed, sold, or enslaved, the initial act was the violent removal of a person from relatives and homeland, often accompanied by the killing of others. Historians who study these histories must acknowledge both the diversity of institutions and the consistent presence of violence and loss.

Section 53.2

Indigenous Institutions of Warfare, Captivity, and Social Inequality before European Colonization

Reconstructing precolonial captive institutions requires care, because the written sources for most of North America begin only after European contact and are shaped by European observers. For earlier periods historians rely on archaeology, oral tradition, linguistic evidence, and the retrospective testimony recorded in early colonial sources. Each is limited. Archaeology can document violence, burial treatment, and patterns of labor or settlement, but it can rarely establish the legal status of a person, and the same skeletal evidence can be interpreted in different ways. Oral traditions preserve sophisticated historical knowledge but are organized for community purposes, and early European observers often described Indigenous practices in categories they already understood.

In the Eastern Woodlands, archaeological evidence of fortified towns, palisades, and mass burials indicates that warfare was a recurring feature of life in some regions in the centuries before sustained European contact, particularly after about 1300 CE. Some skeletal assemblages show evidence of violent death, and the demographic profiles of a few such sites have prompted discussion about whether women and children were taken alive. At Crow Creek on the Missouri River, for example, the remains of several hundred people who were killed in the early fourteenth century include fewer young women than might be expected, which some researchers have interpreted as evidence that young women were taken captive, though this inference remains an interpretation rather than a certainty. Early seventeenth-century European sources, including Jesuit accounts and Champlain's writings about the lower Great Lakes and the St. Lawrence, describe the capture, torture, adoption, and killing of enemies among Iroquoian and Algonquian-speaking peoples. These sources were written by outsiders and must be read critically, but they agree with later Indigenous testimony in showing that captive-taking was woven into diplomatic, ritual, and kinship obligations.

The significance of captive-taking depended on how a society organized kinship and political authority. In matrilineal societies such as the Haudenosaunee, clan matrons played central roles in decisions about whether a captive should replace a deceased relative. In ranked societies, such as those of the Northwest Coast, status was tied to inherited names, privileges, and the control of resources, and enslaved people could be integrated into the economy of a high-ranking household without acquiring kin status. In societies organized around small mobile bands, the capture of women and children might serve to recruit members and spouses. In more centralized chiefdoms of the Southeast, as described in later Spanish and French sources, captives might be assigned to labor, ritual, or exchange, though what the evidence permits historians to say about precolonial practice remains limited.

Scholars of the Southeast have argued that many Indigenous societies there recognized forms of captivity in which outsiders were taken in warfare and might be put to work, exchanged, adopted, or killed, and that such captives were in many cases integrated over time rather than held as a permanent hereditary class. Christina Snyder's work in particular has emphasized that Indigenous captivity was often linked to kinship and to concepts of honor and obligation, and that its character changed as English commercial slavery and, later, racial slavery created new categories. In contrast, on the Northwest Coast, the evidence for slavery as an established, hereditary institution before European contact is stronger and is discussed in Sections 53.13 and 53.14. The contrast illustrates why generalization is dangerous: the same continent contained societies in which captive-taking produced kin, societies in which it produced property, and societies in which it produced both at once.

Section 53.3

Mourning, Retribution, Ransom, Adoption, and Incorporation

Among many Eastern Woodlands peoples, the death of a person created an obligation not only of grief but of restoration. A household or lineage that had lost a member might seek to replace that person, restore the balance of the community, and satisfy the claims of the dead. In the language of historians, this complex is often called the mourning war, a term developed in the scholarship of Iroquoian societies and applied, with qualification, to others. The term describes a set of practices in which war parties sought captives who might be adopted to take the place of the deceased or killed to satisfy grief and obligations of vengeance. It is an analytical convention, not a term used uniformly by all communities, and it should not be taken to imply that mourning was the sole cause of war. Other motives, including territorial defense, trade competition, and diplomatic alliance, operated alongside it.

The fate of a captive was not automatic. Early European observers wrote about prisoners being compelled to run a gauntlet, being beaten, or being subjected to torture and ritual killing. They also described captives who were welcomed into households, given new names and kin terms, and who lived as members of their adopting families. Historians interpret these contrasting outcomes as parts of a single process in which the captive's position was uncertain until a decision was reached by the community or by the family claiming him or her. Age, sex, and the identity of the captor and the captive's own demeanor mattered. Adult men, whom warriors often regarded as dangerous and as a source of vengeance, were more likely to be killed; women and children were more likely to be adopted, though exceptions were common.

Adoption transformed a captive's legal and social position, but it did not erase the coercion by which the person had been taken. Captives were often required to abandon their original names and kin relations, learn a new language, and adapt to new norms under conditions of vulnerability. They were commonly watched closely, especially in the early period, and could be killed if they attempted to flee. Some adoptees became deeply committed to their new communities; some remained ambivalent; some were forcibly returned to their original societies later and resisted leaving. Such cases do not prove that adoption was benign or that it was coercive in every case. They show that the same institution could produce profoundly different personal outcomes, and that the line between acceptance and constraint was often blurred.

Ransom and exchange offered alternatives. A captor's family might accept goods or other considerations in return for a captive's release, and communities frequently used the return of captives as part of treaty settlements. Ransom could be an instrument of diplomacy, but it could also encourage captive-taking, since a prisoner with kin able to pay or negotiate had a market value. The relationship between mourning, adoption, ransom, and commerce was therefore not static. As colonial governments and merchants began to pay for captives, and as epidemic losses grew, existing practices were reshaped by new incentives. The resulting mixture of kinship obligation and commercial exchange is a recurring theme in the sections that follow.

Section 53.4

Reconstructing Captive Histories: Sources, Silences, and Scholarly Disagreement

The history of captivity is, to an unusual degree, a history of absent testimony. The people most affected by captive-taking and slavery rarely left written accounts, and those who did were not representative. Surviving evidence tends to come from the people who held or purchased captives, who recorded them in registers, wills, inventories, baptismal books, court proceedings, and correspondence. A French priest recording the baptism of an enslaved Indigenous child in Montreal, a Spanish official listing captives exchanged at a trading fair, and a New England magistrate ordering the sale of prisoners each created records that serve the purposes of administration, religion, or law, and that treat captives as objects of record rather than as speakers.

Captive narratives, a popular genre in the English-speaking world beginning in the seventeenth century, are valuable but must be handled with caution. Narratives such as Mary Rowlandson's account of her 1676 captivity, John Williams's account of his 1704 captivity, or the later narrative of Mary Jemison, who was adopted by Seneca women in the 1750s and whose life story was published in 1824 through the writing of a white editor, tell us a great deal about specific individuals. But they were shaped by authors, editors, and audiences, and they were published precisely because they were unusual. Those who died in captivity, those who chose to remain, and those whose languages or circumstances prevented publication are largely absent. Historians therefore avoid treating published narratives as typical.

Other sources supplement and sometimes correct them. Mission and parish registers allow quantitative analysis of baptisms, marriages, and deaths, and in some cases record an enslaved person's nation of origin, though often through colonial labels that may not reflect the person's own identity. Court records capture moments when captives or their descendants challenged their status, though only those who gained access to a court and recorded testimony. Archaeological work on mission sites and trading posts reveals diet, labor, and living conditions. Indigenous oral histories, preserved by communities, may record the loss of relatives, the formation of new settlements, and the memory of captive-taking from perspectives that colonial records omit. Their use requires respect for community protocols about who may speak for the past, as discussed in Chapter 1.

Scholars also disagree about interpretation. Some emphasize the continuity between older Indigenous practices and colonial-era slavery, while others stress transformation, arguing that European commercial demand turned older kin-based captivity into commodity exchange. Some debate whether "slavery" is an appropriate term for societies in which captives were eventually incorporated, while others note that a narrow definition risks excluding the experiences of people who were exploited and treated as property. Estimates of the number of Indigenous people enslaved are uncertain, because records are fragmentary and categories were fluid. This chapter adopts the following practices. It uses specific terms where the sources permit, notes when colonial labels such as Panis or genízaro do not reflect a single nation, gives estimates as ranges, and acknowledges that the experiences of many captives are accessible only through the records of those who held them.

There is also an ethical dimension. The documentary record of Indigenous slavery is, in large part, a record of violence and dispossession, and the descendants of enslaved and captive people remain present in many communities, including those of genízaro heritage in New Mexico, Pequot and other New England Native families, and Native communities whose ancestors were removed through the slave trade. Historians have a responsibility to present these histories accurately, to avoid sensationalism, and to recognize that the people whose lives survive chiefly in records of coercion were individuals with families, languages, and agency. The sections that follow try to meet that responsibility by identifying specific people and communities wherever the evidence allows.

Section 53.5

Haudenosaunee Mourning Wars, Captive Adoption, and Community Reconstruction

The Haudenosaunee nations, whose political institutions are treated in Chapter 26 and whose seventeenth-century wars are treated in Chapter 47, offer the best-documented case of captive adoption in North America, and the case most often cited, sometimes too simply, in discussions of Indigenous captivity. Here the section's purpose is not to retell the wars but to examine how captive-taking functioned within Haudenosaunee communities and how it changed as epidemic disease, fur-trade competition, and European alliances reshaped the world in which they lived.

In the 1630s and 1640s, epidemic diseases struck Iroquoian-speaking communities of the lower Great Lakes and the Mohawk valley with devastating effect, a history discussed in Chapter 40. Haudenosaunee towns lost large numbers of people, including elders, leaders, and the kin on whom households depended. In those conditions, the obligation to restore the dead took on added urgency. Haudenosaunee war parties, operating against the Wendat, Neutral, Petun, Erie, and Algonquian-speaking neighbors, as well as against French settlements and their Indigenous allies, brought back prisoners in substantial numbers. French Jesuit observers, who lived among some of these communities and wrote voluminous reports, described prisoners being led into Haudenosaunee villages, subjected to ritual violence, and then, in many cases, assigned to families. Some Jesuits claimed that adopted outsiders constituted a very large share of some towns in the later seventeenth century. Such statements are impressions rather than censuses, but they are consistent with other evidence that adoption contributed significantly to demographic recovery.

Decisions about captives were made within households and clans. Among the Haudenosaunee, whose clans were matrilineal, clan mothers and senior women held recognized authority over the fate of individual prisoners. A woman who had lost a son, brother, or husband could claim a captive to fill the vacancy, and a captive so claimed might receive the dead person's name and a place in the household. Those not claimed, or those assigned to ritual violence, could be killed. Historians have emphasized that this authority gave women a role in warfare that is sometimes missed in accounts focused on male warriors. It also meant that outcomes depended on particular households and moments rather than a uniform policy.

The captives incorporated through these processes included Wendat families whose own communities had been scattered after the Haudenosaunee attacks of 1649 and 1650. Many Wendat sought refuge elsewhere, including at Jesuit missions and with other Indigenous nations, while others joined Haudenosaunee communities as adoptees. The experience was not uniform: some captives integrated; some held an ambiguous status; some retained their language or religion for years; some fled to French settlements. At the same time, the Haudenosaunee themselves experienced losses to French and Algonquian raids and were sometimes captured. The Jesuit Isaac Jogues, for example, was captured by Mohawks in 1642 and held before he escaped with Dutch assistance, and Pierre-Esprit Radisson later reported being adopted by a Mohawk family in the 1650s. These accounts survive because they were written by Europeans who left, and they should be read as such.

It would be a mistake to treat all Haudenosaunee nations as following an identical policy at every moment. The Mohawk, geographically closest to Dutch and English traders at Albany, were differently positioned from the Seneca in the west. Some communities, particularly those with large numbers of Christian converts, developed distinctive relationships with the French; the migration of Haudenosaunee Catholic converts to mission villages such as Kahnawake created communities that were at once Mohawk and tied to New France. Policy also changed. By the later seventeenth century, Haudenosaunee leaders were increasingly engaged in diplomacy with the English through what became known as the Covenant Chain, discussed in Chapter 56, and with the French. The settlements of 1701, in which Haudenosaunee representatives made peace with the French and their Indigenous allies at Montreal and affirmed ties with the English at Albany, ushered in a period of neutrality in the imperial wars. The Montreal settlement included provisions for the return of prisoners, an indication that the exchange of captives was a recognized element of diplomacy.

Captive-taking continued after 1701, though on different scales and with different participants. Raids on English frontier towns such as Deerfield in 1704 involved Kahnawake and other Indigenous fighters acting alongside French forces, and the captives taken, including Eunice Williams, were often adopted by Catholic Mohawk families. Later in the eighteenth century, Seneca and Delaware communities in the Ohio country and western Pennsylvania took captives during the Seven Years' War (examined in Chapter 57) and Pontiac's War (Chapter 58). When British authorities, notably Colonel Henry Bouquet in 1764, compelled several Ohio communities to surrender captives, observers recorded that some of the people being returned, particularly those who had been taken as children, were reluctant to leave families they now regarded as their own. English commentators often interpreted this reluctance as a sign of captivity's strange power; Indigenous communities understood it as a consequence of adoption.

Section 53.6

Indigenous Slavery in New France: The Great Lakes, Panis, and Colonial Markets

French colonization along the St. Lawrence and in the Great Lakes depended on alliances with Indigenous nations, a subject treated in Chapters 45 and 46. These alliances were sustained by gifts, by shared military action, and by the exchange of goods, and they also included the circulation of captives. Brett Rushforth's research has shown that the presentation of captives to French officials and traders was woven into Indigenous diplomatic customs. In the societies of the Great Lakes and the upper Mississippi valley, giving a captive to an ally could confirm friendship and could assign the recipient responsibility for the captive's life. French officials, for their part, were drawn into this system and frequently found that refusing the gift risked offending allies, while accepting it created owners, and eventually a market.

The people who became enslaved in New France came from many nations. The largest group came to be called Panis in French records. The word derived from the name of the Pawnee, a Caddoan-speaking people of the central Plains, and it was used in the early eighteenth century to designate enslaved Indigenous people originating in lands west and southwest of the Great Lakes. It was not confined to Pawnee individuals. Canadian historical scholarship has shown that the designation also covered captives from other nations, including the Osage, Missouria, and others, and that it became, in effect, a legal and social category for enslaved Indigenous people in the colony. This is a clear example of a colonial label that conceals the people it names: a woman recorded as Panis in a Montreal register might have been Pawnee, Osage, Meskwaki, or from another nation, and her own name and community often went unrecorded.

How were such captives obtained? The sources point to several channels. Some were taken in raids by Illinois, Miami, Odawa, Ojibwe, and other peoples on neighboring societies, and brought east through trading networks to French posts. After the French and their Indigenous allies fought the Meskwaki (Fox) in the wars of the 1710s through the 1730s, many Meskwaki captives were seized and enslaved. Other captives were taken by nations of the Mississippi and Missouri valleys, such as the Osage, who traded or exchanged them with intermediaries. French traders and officials sometimes purchased captives directly. A person might pass through several hands, across hundreds of miles, before arriving in a household at Montreal, Quebec, Detroit, or another settlement.

The legal status of these captives was uncertain until 1709, when the intendant Jacques Raudot issued an ordinance declaring that Panis and Black people purchased or to be purchased belonged to their owners as slaves. The ordinance was a local administrative decision rather than a royal edict, and the Crown never enacted a comprehensive code for slavery in Canada, but colonial courts and notaries treated it as authoritative. It reflected labor shortages in a colony where settlers complained about the cost and scarcity of workers, and it responded to a situation in which enslaved Indigenous people were already being held. Recorded cases show that enslaved people were bought, sold, bequeathed, and mortgaged. They were baptized, given French names, and sometimes buried in parish cemeteries; the colony's records show their children being born in bondage, and also show owners who freed some of them.

Section 53.7

New England: The Pequot War, King Philip's War, and Indigenous Enslavement

Margaret Ellen Newell's research has demonstrated that Indigenous slavery was an integral part of early New England colonial society. English settlers in Massachusetts, Plymouth, Connecticut, and Rhode Island enslaved Native people from the 1630s onward, often as an outcome of warfare, and Native servants and slaves worked in households, on farms, and in coastal trades. Historians who once treated slavery in New England as marginal and African-centered have revised that picture to include the Indigenous people who made up a significant share of the region's dependent labor, especially before the eighteenth century.

The Pequot War of 1636 to 1638 was the first major conflict in which English colonists enslaved large numbers of Indigenous captives. The war grew out of tensions among the Pequot, neighboring Indigenous nations, Dutch and English traders, and the expanding English settlements of the Connecticut River valley. In May 1637, English forces under John Mason and John Underhill, accompanied by Narragansett and Mohegan allies, attacked a Pequot fortified town near the Mystic River and set it on fire. Many of those killed were noncombatants; estimates of the dead range from several hundred upward. Over the following months, survivors were hunted, captured, and distributed. English leaders sent some male captives to the West Indies, where they were sold, and kept women and children as servants and slaves in New England households. Others were given to Indigenous allies. The Treaty of Hartford of 1638 declared the Pequot dissolved as a people, forbade the use of the name, and assigned survivors to the Mohegan, Narragansett, and Niantic. The Pequot nevertheless survived, and descendants of those who remained in Connecticut or returned later helped sustain a community whose subsequent history is treated in later chapters.

Colonial law gave this practice legal justification. The Massachusetts Body of Liberties of 1641 prohibited slavery except in cases of lawful captives taken in just wars and of those who sold themselves or were sold to the colony. The just-war doctrine drew on European legal traditions that allowed the enslavement of enemies in wars deemed righteous, and colonial authorities were willing to define the wars they fought in those terms. This legal framework did not distinguish by race in the way later slave codes would, though the colonial application of just war depended on defining Indigenous opponents as enemies, and the principle could be extended to Africans purchased in the Atlantic trade.

King Philip's War, fought in 1675 and 1676, was far larger. Wampanoag leader Metacom, called Philip by the English, led a coalition that included Nipmuc, Narragansett, and other fighters against English settlements, which suffered heavy losses. Indigenous communities suffered even more, through combat, starvation, disease, and displacement. Colonial authorities treated surrendered fighters and captured noncombatants as prisoners who could be sold. Hundreds, and by some estimates over a thousand, Native people were sold into slavery, many shipped to the Caribbean, the Atlantic islands, and elsewhere. Other captives were retained in New England as servants or slaves. Meanwhile, Christian Indians, the "Praying Indians" who had accepted English missionary teaching and had in many cases supported the colonists, were removed from their towns and interned on islands in Boston Harbor, including Deer Island, where many died of exposure and hunger. The missionary John Eliot protested the sale of captives into slavery abroad, arguing against it on moral and legal grounds, though his objections failed to prevent the sales.

Section 53.8

Carolina and the Southeastern Indigenous Slave Trade, 1670–1717

The English colony of Carolina, founded in 1670 near the Atlantic coast, quickly became the center of a commercial trade in Indigenous captives that reshaped the Southeast. Alan Gallay's study, which remains the essential framework, demonstrates that this trade involved colonial merchants and officials, Indigenous partners who supplied captives, and Indigenous communities who were raided and sold. Alan Gallay estimated that approximately 24,000 to 51,000 Indigenous people were taken or sold into the English-directed southeastern slave trade between about 1670 and 1715; scholars often summarize this uncertain range as roughly thirty to fifty thousand. This is a regional estimate of people caught up in captive trading, not a count of individuals exported from Carolina. Some were retained within the colony or traded elsewhere in mainland North America, while many were shipped to Atlantic destinations. The underlying records are incomplete and different reconstructions use different assumptions, but even cautious estimates indicate devastating regional consequences.

The trade rested on several conditions. First, English traders brought firearms, ammunition, cloth, and other goods that Indigenous nations wanted for hunting, defense, and war. Second, merchants extended credit, creating debts that Indigenous traders repaid with deerskins and, increasingly, captives. Third, there was a market. Indigenous captives could be sold to Carolina planters and households, and large numbers were shipped north to other English colonies and to the Caribbean, where distance from home reduced the chance of escape. Fourth, warfare among Indigenous nations provided a supply. Raids and wars produced captives, and the demand for captives encouraged further raiding. These elements reinforced one another, and the system became a major engine of regional violence.

The early trade relied on the Westo, an Indigenous people established on the Savannah River who had obtained firearms through Virginia traders and conducted raids on neighboring peoples. Carolina colonists initially traded with them. In the early 1680s, however, Carolina turned against the Westo, supporting the Savannah, a Shawnee-related group, in attacks that destroyed the Westo as a power. The episode illustrates how commercial partnerships could quickly become hostile and how colonial authorities used Indigenous rivalries for their own ends. After the Westo, other nations took leading roles. The Yamasee, who had settled near the Carolina coast after leaving the Spanish Florida area, became major partners and raiders. The Creek, a confederation of towns of the Muscogee-speaking peoples, participated in the trade while also being targets of it. The Chickasaw, who had access to English traders, raided Choctaw and other communities of the Mississippi valley. Cherokee, Catawba, and other nations also engaged with the trade in various ways. These nations did not form a unified bloc. They had different interests, and the same community could be a supplier in one decade and a victim in another.

Spanish Florida and its Indigenous allies bore some of the heaviest losses. In 1704, a Carolina force under James Moore, accompanied by Indigenous allies, attacked the Apalachee missions of northern Florida, destroying villages, killing many people, and taking large numbers of captives, while others fled or were relocated. The attacks shattered the Spanish mission system in the region, a history related to Chapter 41. Timucua, Guale, and other peoples of the Florida frontier also suffered. Survivors dispersed, some resettling near Spanish St. Augustine, some near French Mobile, and some joining Creek communities.

Two wars ended the trade's expansion and marked turning points. The Tuscarora War, fought from 1711 to 1715 in the Carolina interior, began when Tuscarora communities, pressed by settler encroachment and by the kidnapping and enslavement of their people, attacked English settlements. Carolina forces, aided by Yamasee and other Indigenous allies, defeated the Tuscarora, and hundreds of captives were enslaved. Many survivors left the region and migrated north; by around 1722 they had been accepted into the Haudenosaunee Confederacy as its sixth nation, an example of a people whose capture and dispersal were followed by political incorporation elsewhere. The Yamasee War, beginning in April 1715, was a larger regional uprising. Yamasee, Creek, and other Indigenous peoples attacked Carolina traders and settlements after years of mounting debts, abuses by traders, and enslavement of their kin. The war nearly destroyed the colony, which survived in part by securing the support of Cherokee, and the conflict ended in 1717 with realignment. The Yamasee largely left the Carolina region for Spanish Florida, and the Creek and Cherokee adopted policies balancing among Carolina, Spanish, and French powers. Carolina authorities restricted some aspects of the Indigenous slave trade afterward and shifted toward deerskin commerce and African plantation slavery, though Native people continued to be enslaved in the colony.

Spanish Colonial Slavery, Forced Labor, and Imperial Law

Spanish colonization began in the Caribbean with the enslavement of Indigenous people, and by the 1540s the Crown had responded to criticism, notably from members of religious orders, with legislation that sought to limit it. The New Laws of 1542 prohibited new enslavement of Indigenous people and ended the inheritance of encomiendas, which were grants of tribute and labor from Indigenous communities to Spanish holders. Over the following decades, further decrees and the compilation of laws known as the Recopilación de Leyes de los Reynos de las Indias of 1680 restated the principle that Indigenous people were free vassals of the Crown. The formal doctrine was therefore one of Indigenous freedom paired with obligations: tribute, labor service, and conversion.

In practice, Spanish colonies depended on a range of coercive labor institutions that were not all slavery, and students should distinguish them. Encomienda, where it existed, entitled the holder to tribute and sometimes labor from a community, and was framed as a reward for service and a duty to protect and instruct. Repartimiento was a system of rotational labor drafts in which communities supplied a fraction of their workers for limited periods, often for agriculture, construction, or mining, in return for nominal wages. Debt peonage bound workers to employers through advances that could never be fully repaid. Mission labor, supervised by religious orders, required converts to work in fields and workshops under discipline. And chattel slavery persisted in forms that Spanish law either permitted by exception or tolerated through evasion. These institutions overlapped. A person might begin as a captive, be classified as a servant, be assigned to an encomienda or repartimiento, and end as a debtor, and the label attached to the person in a record might say little about the person's conditions.

The principal legal exceptions to the prohibition on Indigenous slavery allowed enslavement of those captured in "just war," especially in conflicts with Indigenous peoples who resisted Spanish rule or were considered hostile and unconverted. Spanish authorities applied this logic to many frontier peoples, notably in the Chichimeca War of the sixteenth century in north-central Mexico, where captives were enslaved despite royal restrictions. A related practice, called rescate, meant "ransom" or "rescue": Spaniards purchased captives held by Indigenous captors on the argument that the purchase saved them from death or heathenism, and the purchasers could then claim a term of service from the "rescued" person. In theory this created temporary servitude. In practice it created a market in captives, since Indigenous captors who received goods for their prisoners had incentives to take more, and it permitted the continuation of slavery under another name.

Section 53.10

New Mexico, Captive Exchange, and the Formation of Genízaro Communities

Colonial New Mexico, established by the Spanish in 1598 and reestablished after the Pueblo Revolt of 1680 (treated in Chapter 44), was a society in which Hispanic settlers, Pueblo communities, and mobile Indigenous peoples such as Apache, Navajo, Ute, and Comanche lived in tension and in exchange. Captives circulated through that society. Spanish military expeditions took prisoners, Indigenous raiders took captives from settlements and from other nations, and trading fairs at places such as Taos and Pecos brought together Plains peoples and New Mexicans. At these fairs, captives were exchanged along with horses, hides, and other goods. Spanish officials and settlers purchased them, sometimes in the name of ransom and rescate, and enrolled them in households as servants.

The captives who entered Spanish households came from many nations. Records identify Apache, Navajo, Ute, Comanche, Pawnee and other Plains peoples, among others, though the sources often use only general labels, and the person's own community may have been unrecorded. Most were children and young women. Priests baptized them, gave them Christian names, and frequently recorded them in parish registers as Indios or by terms that suggest their origin. They worked as domestic servants, herders, and agricultural laborers. Their children, born in Hispanic households, often continued in dependent roles.

Abiquiú offers a particularly instructive chronology because it was not founded all at once. A settlement called Santa Rosa de Abiquiú existed during the 1740s; raiding, insecurity, and successive attempts at resettlement interrupted occupation. On May 10, 1754, Governor Tomás Vélez Cachupín returned genízaro settlers to Abiquiú and surveyed a land grant for them. The legal history preserved in J. J. Bowden's compilation of southwestern land claims distinguishes the earlier settlement, its abandonment, and the grant of 1754. Those successive events mattered to residents who had to rebuild fields, houses, water access, and defenses more than once. A chronology that labels the community simply “founded in 1754” conceals both its earlier history and the instability of colonial frontier life.

Receiving a land grant did not make genízaro families politically or socially equivalent to settlers who possessed recognized Spanish lineage. Landholding was conditional upon residence and defense, and some colonial officials viewed such settlements as a protective screen for established towns and pueblos. The families themselves, however, sought workable arrangements that could provide farmland, collective protection, and the possibility of passing a place to their children. In this sense a community established under unequal colonial authority could become a setting for collective action rather than merely a garrison assembled by the state. Religious institutions, Spanish names, and changing occupational roles did not necessarily extinguish kinship knowledge or inherited connections to Indigenous peoples.

The documentary trail also imposes constraints on reconstruction. A grant describes boundaries and colonial authority, not what an individual former captive thought about living there; a baptismal record can identify ritual incorporation but not show whether a household provided affection, wages, or freedom of movement. Accounts of later generations sometimes preserve Indigenous identities erased by a clerk's generic category. Comparing land records with community histories and archaeology is therefore indispensable. The key historical distinction is between the official rationale for resettlement, which emphasized defense and Christianization, and the lives of families negotiating land, membership, religious obligations, and social standing. Abiquiú should be analyzed as a developing community with its own history, not as evidence that captivity had become benign.

Definition. In colonial New Mexico, a term applied to Indigenous people, or their descendants, who had been captured or purchased and raised in Hispanic households, and who were detached from their natal communities and not part of a Pueblo or other Indigenous polity.

Historical context. The word is commonly thought to be derived from a Spanish term, related to the Ottoman janissary, for soldiers of captive origin; the origin has been discussed by scholars and is generally treated as probable rather than certain. The term appears in New Mexican records during the eighteenth century.

Why it matters. The category recorded an intermediate status: genízaros were not enslaved in the same legal sense, nor did they hold the same standing as Spanish settlers or Pueblo members.

Potential misunderstanding. Genízaro does not name a single nation. It describes a social position produced by captivity and detribalization, and people with different Indigenous origins became genízaros. Many genízaro families developed distinct communities and identities that continue to be recognized today.

In the middle decades of the eighteenth century, colonial officials began to settle genízaro families on the frontiers of New Mexico. A grant at Belén in 1740 and the founding of Abiquiú in the 1750s are among the most studied examples. Spanish authorities viewed these communities as buffers that could defend the colony against raids by Comanche, Ute, and Navajo. The settlers received land grants and obligations to defend them. Residents of such settlements farmed, herded animals, and served in militia, and they gradually formed communities with their own leaders, churches, and shared experiences. These were not simply instruments of the colonial government. Genízaro communities developed religious confraternities, ceremonial practices, and political identities that blended Indigenous and Hispanic elements, and genízaro individuals used courts and petitions to defend their rights to land and water.

Incorporation into colonial society did not produce equality. Genízaros occupied a low position in the hierarchy of colonial New Mexico, as censuses and parish registers show. They were often described in condescending terms, and the clergy who ministered to them sometimes judged their religious practices harshly. The Franciscan Fray Atanasio Domínguez, who visited the province in 1776 and left a detailed report, described genízaro settlements in terms that reveal both Spanish dependence on them and Spanish disdain. The position of genízaros was thus ambiguous: they were formally free as Christian subjects of the Crown, yet their origins marked them, and they bore obligations of service and defense. Over generations, some were absorbed into broader Hispanic New Mexican society, while many retained a distinct identity that survived into the modern period. Recent New Mexico legislation and community scholarship have given genízaro history renewed public recognition.

Apache, Navajo, Pueblo, Ute, and Northern Mexican Borderland Captivity

The captive networks of New Mexico were part of a wider system of raiding, retaliation, trade, and diplomacy that stretched across the Southwest and northern Mexico. Its participants included Apache bands, Diné (Navajo) communities, Pueblo towns, Ute bands, Comanche, Spanish settlers and soldiers, and Indigenous auxiliaries who fought alongside Spanish forces. The history of the Pueblo Revolt and its aftermath is treated in Chapter 44, and the history of Apache communities in Chapter 51. This section examines how captivity operated within those wider relationships.

Captive-taking served several purposes. It could be retaliation for earlier raids or killings, in which a community sought to avenge losses and recover relatives. It could be a means of acquiring labor, especially the work of women and children in agriculture, herding, and household production. It could be linked to the competition for horses, trade goods, and territory. And it could be a diplomatic instrument, since the return of captives was a standard feature of peace negotiations. Those purposes were intertwined, and they changed with circumstances. A raid launched to take horses might also seize women and children; a campaign of retaliation might produce captives who were then traded.

Apache communities, which were diverse and politically decentralized, participated in captive-taking as both raiders and victims. Apache raiders took Spanish, Pueblo, and other Indigenous captives, and Spanish and allied forces took Apache captives, many of whom were sold or distributed into servitude, with some sent far from home. In the late eighteenth century, Spanish authorities deported some captured Apaches to central Mexico and the Caribbean, and also sought to settle Apaches near presidios as part of peace policies that provided rations and attempted to reduce raiding. Diné communities likewise captured and were captured. Navajo raiding of Spanish and Pueblo settlements, and Spanish and Ute raiding of Navajo communities, generated captives in both directions. Diné oral histories also describe the incorporation of outsiders, including Pueblo refugees and captives, into Navajo clans, a reminder that adoption and incorporation operated within Indigenous communities even as colonial markets sold other captives to Hispanic households.

Pueblo communities were involved in several ways. Pueblo auxiliaries accompanied Spanish expeditions and shared in the spoils, and Pueblo households sometimes acquired captives of their own, while Pueblo people were also targets of raids by mobile nations. The Ute, who allied for a time with the Comanche in the early eighteenth century, raided Spanish and Pueblo settlements and also sold captives, and later became participants in the New Mexican trade in Indigenous captives. In northern Mexico, presidios and settlements in Nueva Vizcaya and Sonora experienced raiding by Apache and other peoples, and responded with campaigns that took captives. Women and children moved between Indigenous communities and colonial households, learning new languages and adapting to new expectations. Some children raised in a new community considered it their own and did not wish to return, and others sought to rejoin relatives when given the chance.

Section 53.12

Comanche Captivity, Raiding, Ransom, and Incorporation on the Southern Plains

The political rise of the Comanche, examined in Chapter 50, rested on horses, trade, and military power. This section turns to the institution of captivity within Comanche society and to the experiences of the people it affected. The scholarship of Joaquín Rivaya-Martínez, who has studied Comanche captives and Hispanic-Comanche relations through Spanish records, provides a foundation for understanding these patterns, though the evidence is largely from Spanish sources and must be read with that in mind.

Comanche bands acquired captives through raiding and warfare against Apache, Spanish, Pueblo, Texan, Mexican, and other Indigenous communities. Raiding for horses was a major motive, and captives were often taken in the course of such raids. Men captured in conflict were frequently killed; women and children were more often kept. Captives taken as children could be incorporated into families, given new names, taught Comanche language and customs, and in time regarded as members of the band. Adult women could be taken as wives or put to work in household production, including the processing of buffalo hides, an activity of growing economic importance as the trade in robes expanded. Captives also tended horse herds. The labor of captive women and children thus formed part of the economy of Comanche households, though the extent to which it was decisive varied by band and period and cannot be precisely measured.

Not all captives shared the same experience. Apache captives, taken in a long-running rivalry, could be treated differently from Spanish and Mexican captives, whose families might ransom them or whose governments might negotiate for their return. Age was crucial: young children often integrated most thoroughly, whereas older captives might remain in an ambiguous status. Gender shaped roles. Individual circumstances mattered, including the status of the captor and the attitudes of the household. Some captives achieved recognized standing, married, raised children, and became part of the community. Others were sold or traded onward, returned through ransom, or died.

Ransom and exchange were integrated into Comanche relations with Spanish authorities and settlers. Comanche leaders traded captives to New Mexican communities for goods, and Spanish officials and families paid ransom to recover relatives. When Spanish and Comanche representatives negotiated peace in Texas in 1785 and in New Mexico in 1786, the treaties dealt with captives and trade. After the New Mexico settlement, in which Comanche leaders including Ecueracapa played an important role, a period of relative peace followed, and trading connections expanded. The settlement did not end captive-taking, and it did not eliminate the sale of captives in New Mexico, but it changed the relationships in which captivity operated. In later decades, as discussed in chapters on the nineteenth century, Comanche captive-taking continued within the changing politics of Mexico, Texas, and the United States.

The Comanche case illustrates why captivity cannot be reduced to a single model. In Comanche society, captive labor and incorporation coexisted with ransom and sale, and a captive's fate depended on identity, age, gender, and negotiation. It also illustrates that Indigenous power could shape the terms of the system, as Comanche leaders bargained with Spanish authorities from a position of strength, while the people captured within it paid the price of that power.

The status of a captive within a Comanche household could change during a lifetime, but a change of duties did not automatically confer full kinship membership. Children acquired in raids might learn Comanche speech and participate in work and travel with the people who raised them; other individuals remained dependent on a particular owner or were transferred in exchange. Skilled horse care, domestic labor, and knowledge of neighboring settlements might be valued for different reasons. Age and circumstances at capture strongly affected opportunities to form relationships, while gender shaped both labor expectations and vulnerability. The historian's task is to recover variation rather than place every Comanche-held person into one fixed category for the entire eighteenth century.

Joaquín Rivaya-Martínez has reconstructed patterns of captive incorporation, but an important part of his closely analyzed individual evidence extends into 1820–1875, after this chapter's principal cutoff. That later research is valuable for identifying possible mechanisms of incorporation, yet its statistics or descriptions cannot simply be applied as an eighteenth-century rate for all captives. For the earlier period, Spanish reports of raids, negotiations, and exchanges are more numerous than intimate accounts of household relationships. They often distinguish captives by origin or by a colonial label, while leaving the person's own understanding of belonging and dependence undocumented. Ransom petitions similarly capture the experiences of families able to seek official intervention rather than everyone who remained missing.

Captives and their relatives also influenced diplomacy. When an agreement promised an exchange, the authorities had to locate particular people who might have been moved between households or grown attached to new kin. Returning a captive could satisfy a treaty or restore a family, but coercive repatriation could also conflict with the wishes of someone who had lived for years in a different community. Conversely, the failure to recover a missing child might prolong hostility long after a raid. These difficulties reveal why captive exchange cannot be reduced to a simple price schedule. It was simultaneously a matter of emotional relationships, military leverage, claims of authority, and the uneven recordkeeping of Spanish officers and Indigenous negotiators.

Section 53.13

Slavery and Social Hierarchy among Northwest Coast Peoples

Along the Pacific coast from southern Alaska to the Columbia River and beyond, many societies maintained slavery as an established social institution. Leland Donald's comprehensive study, Aboriginal Slavery on the Northwest Coast of North America, remains the central scholarly work. It argues that slavery was economically and socially significant in particular societies and that it existed before sustained European contact. The coastal peoples discussed here include the Tlingit, Haida, Tsimshian, Kwakwaka'wakw, Nuu-chah-nulth, various Coast Salish communities, and Chinookan peoples of the lower Columbia. The societies of this large region differed from one another in language, social organization, and economy, and the historical record for slavery is stronger for some than for others, so generalization requires caution. The region is also treated in Chapters 10 and 33.

Slavery in these societies was closely tied to hereditary rank. Northwest Coast communities were organized around kin groups, houses, or lineages whose leaders held inherited names, ceremonial prerogatives, and rights to resources such as fishing sites, hunting territories, and crests. High-ranking families accumulated wealth, displayed it in feasts and potlatches, and used it to validate their status. Enslaved people were part of that system. They were captives or the descendants of captives, and they were treated as property: they could be given, sold, or inherited, and they were not members of the owner's kin group. Their status was usually hereditary, so that children born to enslaved mothers were themselves enslaved. Scholars debate how large a share of the population the enslaved constituted. Estimates for some societies, such as the Tlingit, Haida, and certain Chinookan communities, suggest that enslaved people could make up a significant minority, in some cases perhaps something on the order of one in ten or more, but the numbers are uncertain and varied by community and period.

The work of enslaved people supported the households of the rank-holding families. Reports from nineteenth-century observers, which must be read with caution, describe enslaved people engaged in fishing, gathering firewood and water, preparing food, paddling canoes, and performing other labor. Their labor contributed to the accumulation of surplus that rank-holders could convert into prestige. The relationship between enslaved labor and the potlatch economy has been a subject of discussion: enslaved people could be given away, and some nineteenth-century accounts describe enslaved persons being killed or freed at ceremonial occasions, though the frequency and meaning of such acts are debated and the sources are often outsider reports. The consistent finding across the evidence is that enslaved people were socially marginal and lacked the protections of kinship that others enjoyed.

The antiquity of the institution is a question on which evidence and interpretation intersect. Oral traditions, linguistic evidence, and the testimony of the earliest European visitors in the late eighteenth century indicate that slavery was already established when sustained contact began. Some archaeologists have argued, from patterns of burial, house size, and settlement hierarchy, that rank and slavery may have deep roots on the coast, though such inferences are contested because archaeological remains rarely show the legal status of persons directly. What can be said with confidence is that slavery in these societies was not introduced by Europeans, and that it was a recognizable form of slavery, involving property status, hereditary condition, and exclusion.

Section 53.14

Captive Raiding, Maritime Exchange, and Enslaved Labor on the Northwest Coast

Captives on the Northwest Coast were obtained through raiding and warfare, through purchase, and through exchange between communities. Large seagoing canoes allowed war parties to travel considerable distances along the coast. Haida and Tlingit raiders, for example, were known in the nineteenth century to travel far to the south, and communities of the Salish Sea and Puget Sound suffered from such expeditions, as did Nuu-chah-nulth and Kwakwaka'wakw communities from raids by neighbors. Raids sought not only captives but also property and prestige, and revenge for earlier losses. The captives, mostly women and children, were taken to the raiders' villages and became enslaved there, or were traded onward.

Exchange gave the system its reach. Captives were bought and sold along the coast and between coastal and interior communities. A center of trade was the lower Columbia and the Dalles area, where Chinookan peoples and their neighbors connected coastal and interior networks, and where captives were among the goods that circulated. Enslaved people could thus be moved far from their homelands, reducing the chance of escape and weakening their ties to kin. Distance and unfamiliar language were instruments of control. Ransom was also possible: relatives of a captive might attempt to buy back a person, and wealth and diplomacy could shape outcomes.

The arrival of European and American maritime traders from the 1770s onward altered economic relationships. The sea otter and later fur trades brought new goods, including metal tools, cloth, and firearms, and new sources of wealth to coastal leaders. Some historians have argued that increased wealth and trade intensified existing practices of potlatching and rank competition, and may have increased the demand for enslaved labor and the scale of raiding, though the evidence for such an effect varies by community and the interpretation remains debated. What the sources do not support is the claim that outsiders introduced slavery to the coast. Slavery predated the maritime trade, and European and American traders entered a world in which captives were already exchanged, though they sometimes purchased or witnessed such exchanges. In the following decades, trading posts, missionaries, and colonial authorities would pressure the institution in ways examined in later chapters.

The maritime geography of the Northwest Coast shaped both opportunities for captive-taking and the limits of what a single group could accomplish. Seagoing canoes, protected channels, seasonal winds, and knowledge of landing places made coastal travel possible, while narrow entrances and fortified villages could complicate an attack. Raiding parties needed information about rivals and relatives as well as the capacity to return with people under guard. The fact that nineteenth-century observers recorded long-distance expeditions does not prove identical patterns in 1700. Sources closer to the eighteenth century must be read alongside later accounts, and events recorded in one inlet cannot automatically stand for the practices of every Tlingit, Haida, Salish, or Nuu-chah-nulth community.

Enslaved labor belonged to a wider household economy than the word “servant” suggests. In some ranked coastal societies, the labor of enslaved people helped high-ranking households process and preserve salmon and other food, maintain possessions, and perform demanding tasks that enabled the hosts to organize feasts and exchanges. The household that controlled a person's labor also controlled the social claims attached to that person. But enslaved people's work varied across villages and seasons, and its economic importance cannot be reduced to a uniform share of production. Leland Donald's comparative study emphasizes both their value as laborers and their role as transferable wealth. The social boundary separating slave from free person could be maintained by hereditary status, public treatment, and restrictions on marriage and participation in rank privileges.

Transactions themselves require careful interpretation. An outsider's journal might record that a captive changed hands without explaining whether the transfer occurred as purchase, compensation, gift, or settlement of a dispute. Owners could inherit enslaved people from relatives, and some obligations were settled through payments involving captives. Such practices linked slavery to law and politics, not just to raiding. Equally, exchange routes made it possible to move people beyond the reach of their birth communities. The occasional possibility of ransom should not be mistaken for a dependable route to release: relatives needed knowledge, resources, and a willing intermediary, and those conditions could be absent. Recovering this social geography helps explain how a society could support intricate diplomacy while denying enslaved persons the protection enjoyed by recognized kin.

The late-eighteenth-century maritime fur trade introduced new material incentives, but it did not transform every coastal economy in the same direction or at the same time. Competition for European manufactured goods could enhance the ability of particular leaders to sponsor feasts, maintain alliances, or acquire dependents. In other circumstances, warfare and trade disruptions could undermine production and heighten insecurity. Because most detailed descriptions of coastal slavery were written after 1821, the first-edition chronology must distinguish what is securely documented by the early nineteenth century from practices reconstructed retrospectively. This is an argument for source criticism, not an excuse to deny the institution's precolonial existence.

Section 53.15

California Missions, Indigenous Labor, Confinement, and Resistance, 1769–1821

The Spanish colonization of Alta California began in 1769, when an expedition accompanied by Franciscan Junípero Serra founded a mission and presidio at San Diego. Over the following decades the Franciscans established a chain of missions along the coast, twenty of which had been founded by 1821; the twenty-first, San Francisco Solano, followed in 1823 under Mexican rule. Presidios and pueblos supported the mission system. The mission system is treated here because it exemplifies a form of coerced labor that was neither enslavement nor free work, and because its scale and consequences were severe. The wider history of California conquest after 1821 is the subject of Chapter 70.

Missions recruited Indigenous people, whom the Franciscans called neophytes after baptism. Some Native people joined missions for food, protection, or novelty, and some were brought by soldiers or relatives; once baptized, they were subject to mission authority. They were expected to work: farming, tending cattle and sheep, building adobe structures, weaving, and making tiles and other goods. Mission economies depended on this labor. The Franciscans and soldiers regulated movement, and neophytes who left without permission could be pursued by soldiers and returned. Unmarried women and girls were commonly locked in separate quarters at night. Punishments including flogging and confinement were used to enforce discipline. The system was justified in religious terms as instruction and protection, and in practical terms as a means of making missions productive.

It is important to describe the system precisely. Neophytes were not owned as chattel, were not sold, and in theory could expect to be released after a period of instruction, which in practice seldom happened. Nor were they free. Their labor was compelled, their movement was restricted, and their religious practices were suppressed. Scholars use various terms to capture this position, and Benjamin Madley has emphasized confinement and penal labor in California, while Steven Hackel has stressed the interplay of Indigenous agency and Spanish authority and the demographic collapse among mission populations. Both approaches are valuable. Hackel's work shows that deaths in mission communities regularly exceeded births, driven by epidemic disease, crowded living conditions, malnutrition, and the effects of disrupted family life, so that missions required continual recruitment to maintain their populations. The aggregate resident population of the missions reached roughly twenty thousand at its early-nineteenth-century peak, a measure of people living in the system at approximately one time rather than the much larger cumulative total of people baptized, confined, or buried across decades. Residents came from many distinct peoples, including Kumeyaay, Luiseño, Tongva, Chumash, Ohlone, and Miwok communities, whose languages and social relationships were disrupted.

The 1812 government questionnaire offers a way to make the documentary problem visible. Officials circulated questions about Native Californians, and Franciscan missionaries returned answers during approximately 1813–1815. A response from Mission Santa Barbara to question seventeen describes food distributed by the missionaries and acknowledges continuing Indigenous hunting and fishing preferences. The Smithsonian National Museum of the American Indian presents this response alongside an 1813 copy of the questionnaire. This is direct evidence of how a missionary chose to describe food practices to superiors; it is not, on its own, proof of the treatment of runaways, the frequency of whipping, or the population's mortality. Those matters require separate judicial, military, missionary, and demographic records.

For population history, the unit being counted matters. A baptism register enumerates ceremonies or named baptisms, a burial register records deaths, and a contemporaneous resident census estimates the people present at a mission at a particular time. Adding baptisms over many decades is not the same as the number of people simultaneously resident there; subtracting entries without considering flight, transfers, or incomplete coverage can also mislead. Steven Hackel's research on mission demography reconstructs these patterns from multiple kinds of records and emphasizes how high mortality could coexist with apparent institutional growth through additional recruitment. A credible estimate must state whether it concerns baptisms, residents, deaths, or the population of a wider Indigenous homeland.

The same separation of evidence applies to coercion. Records of soldiers retrieving people who left a mission, institutional rules restricting movement, and later historical analysis of disciplinary practices support an account of confinement and penal labor; they should not be put in the mouth of every missionary respondent to the 1812 questionnaire. Benjamin Madley's study distinguishes the expanding carceral features of the system across time, especially before and after 1790. Indigenous resistance appears in other sources, including the 1785 San Gabriel proceedings involving Nicolás José and Toypurina, which document objections to mission authority through a colonial court's translation and selection of testimony. Placing these records beside the missionaries' own descriptions allows students to identify disagreement and missing voices rather than merging everything into one invented “mission report.”

African-Indigenous Relationships, Racial Slavery, and Communities of Freedom

The histories of Indigenous and African-descended people were entangled. Enslaved Africans were present in the Southeast from the early colonial period, and Indigenous communities encountered them as slaves of Europeans, as fugitives, as traders, and as neighbors. The relationships that resulted varied by community, colonial setting, and time, and no single pattern describes them. This section focuses on the southeastern borderlands, including Cherokee, Muscogee (Creek), Choctaw, Chickasaw, and Seminole communities, and on the period before about 1821; the nineteenth-century plantation economies of Indian Territory belong to later chapters and should not be projected backward.

Colonial powers often drew Indigenous nations into the enforcement of racial slavery. English colonies in the Carolinas and Virginia made treaties and offered rewards that encouraged Indigenous nations to return runaway slaves, and some Indigenous people did capture fugitives for payment. At the same time, Indigenous communities sometimes sheltered fugitives, and the interests of Native nations did not always align with those of English planters. Spanish Florida created a different context. In 1693 the Spanish Crown offered freedom to fugitives from English colonies who converted to Catholicism, a policy that attracted people escaping Carolina. In 1738 Spanish officials established Fort Mose near St. Augustine as a settlement of free Black residents, who served as a militia and were an important part of Florida's defense. The Yamasee and other Indigenous peoples in the region interacted with such communities, and borderland politics shaped who could claim freedom.

Among Indigenous nations themselves, different paths emerged. Some communities took Black people into kinship networks or tolerated them as residents. Among the Muscogee and the peoples who became the Seminole, for example, some Black individuals and families lived in or near Indigenous towns under arrangements in which they owed tribute or loyalty but retained considerable independence, a relationship that developed more fully in the later eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries and is treated in Chapter 67. Elsewhere, Indigenous individuals and households acquired enslaved Africans. By the late eighteenth century, some Cherokee, Muscogee, Choctaw, and Chickasaw families had adopted forms of African chattel slavery, influenced by trade with Euro-American settlers, by marriages with traders, and by United States federal officials who promoted plantation-style agriculture among Southeastern nations in the 1790s as part of the so-called civilization program. The adoption was uneven. It was more common among wealthier households and among those with ties to traders, and many Indigenous families held no slaves. It also reflected choices within Indigenous societies, and was not simply imposed. The history of the Cherokee, examined further in Chapter 64, shows how political reform, market agriculture, and slaveholding were intertwined.

This history does not support either of two simple conclusions. It would be wrong to say that Indigenous nations were uniformly refuges for Black freedom, and equally wrong to say that Indigenous slaveholding made Native societies indistinguishable from white planters. Indigenous practices regarding enslaved people differed from those of colonial plantations in some places and converged with them in others over time. The presence of Indigenous slaveholding also coexisted with the presence of free and fugitive Black people within Native communities. Indigenous and African histories interwove through marriage, shared labor, shared flight, and shared resistance, and it is a mistake to treat them as separate narratives.

Section 53.17

Women, Children, Families, and the Everyday Experience of Captivity

The regional systems examined in the preceding sections shared a pattern: women and children were prominent among captives, though the reasons and outcomes varied. In the Eastern Woodlands, women and children were more likely to be adopted than adult men, who were more often killed. In the Great Lakes and New France trade, women and children made up a large share of the enslaved. In New Mexico, genízaro families originated from captive children and young women. On the Southern Plains, Comanche raiders kept women and children and often killed men. On the Northwest Coast, women and children were prominent among raided captives. This pattern reflects considerations that differed by society: the value placed on women's labor and reproduction, the greater adaptability attributed to children, and the lower risk that women and children would escape or seek revenge.

For the individual captive, the experience began with capture and separation, often violent and often involving the killing of relatives. Captives then traveled, sometimes for weeks or months, to unfamiliar places. They encountered unfamiliar languages, foods, and customs, and had to learn quickly what was expected of them. Language learning was a practical necessity and a means of survival and negotiation. Mary Rowlandson's narrative describes her struggle to understand her captors and to be understood, and the account of Mary Jemison, who was captured in the 1750s as a girl and adopted by two Seneca women who had lost a brother, describes how she learned Seneca and eventually became part of the community, married, and raised children. Jemison's narrative was shaped by its editor and its audience, and the words preserved as hers cannot be treated as an unmediated record, but the outline of her life is consistent with other evidence about adoption.

Labor expectations varied. In Indigenous households, captive women and girls typically performed the work expected of women in the community, including agricultural labor, processing food, making clothing, and, among Plains peoples, working hides. Captive boys might assist in herding or hunting, or, if adopted, be raised toward adult male roles. In colonial households, captives worked as domestic servants, cooks, and laborers. In mission communities, women and men worked in assigned tasks. The same labor could signify different things: in an adopting household, a young woman's work might mark her as a daughter, while in a colonial household it might mark her as a servant.

Family formation was among the most consequential and difficult aspects of captivity. Captive women might marry into the community that held them, either by choice within limits or by pressure, and bear children who belonged to the captor's community. Sexual violence was part of the experience of enslaved women in many settings; records of mission life, colonial households, and enslaved communities, though often indirect, show vulnerability to coercion by owners, soldiers, and priests. Motherhood complicated the possibility of return: a woman might be offered the chance to leave but be unwilling to abandon her children. The case of Eunice Williams, who stayed at Kahnawake, married, and raised a family, shows how adoption and marriage could produce lasting attachments. Historians caution that such attachments do not erase the violence of capture, and that distinguishing between becoming socially incorporated and exercising freedom is often impossible from the surviving evidence.

Section 53.18

Resistance, Escape, Ransom, Manumission, and the Recovery of Freedom

Captives and enslaved people acted to survive and to change their circumstances. Their actions took many forms, from open flight to quiet accommodation, and the evidence for them is uneven. Escape is the best documented form of resistance because it left traces in advertisements, court records, and correspondence. In Montreal, records show Panis individuals fleeing their owners, and French authorities and owners responded with pursuit and penalties. In New England, enslaved Native people and Africans ran away, sometimes together. In California, soldiers pursued neophytes who left the missions. In Indigenous communities, captives also fled, sometimes to French or English posts, sometimes toward their home communities. Escape was dangerous: captives could be killed if caught, and the distances and the lack of allies made success difficult.

Ransom and negotiated release provided another avenue. Relatives, communities, and governments could pay for captives to be returned, and the practice was embedded in diplomacy. Treaties such as the Montreal settlement of 1701 and the Spanish agreements with the Comanche in the 1780s dealt with prisoners. Such transactions required resources and connections, and they were more available to captives whose communities were organized to pursue them. A captive whose family had been destroyed or whose community was dispersed, as in the aftermath of the Pequot War or the Carolina raids, had far fewer prospects. The example of Rowlandson, whose release was negotiated and paid for, differs from that of Indigenous captives sold abroad with no chance of redemption.

Legal challenges became possible in some jurisdictions. In New England, as Newell has shown, Native people in the eighteenth century sometimes petitioned courts for release from servitude, with mixed success. In New Mexico, genízaros appealed to officials to defend land and status. Such petitions required knowledge of the system, access to officials, and sometimes allies. They indicate that captives and their descendants understood the legal terms of their condition and tried to use them. They also show that the legal avenues available differed by empire: French law recognized slavery by ordinance but permitted manumission by owners; Spanish law, with its doctrine of Indigenous freedom, gave grounds for claims in theory, though enforcement was weak; English colonial law, increasingly racialized, offered fewer openings.

Freedom itself meant different things. In many Indigenous societies, the opposite of captivity was kinship: to be a member of a family and community, with obligations and protections, was to be secure. A captive who was adopted might thus consider himself or herself free in a meaningful sense, even if the capture had been coercive. In French law, freedom meant release from ownership, which might leave a formerly enslaved person without family or support. In Spanish law, Indigenous vassals were free but bound by tribute, labor, and religious obligations. In English law, freedom was tied to legal status and, increasingly, to race. These differences mean that a person's "freedom" in the records may not correspond to what that person would have recognized as freedom.

The recovery of freedom often required rebuilding relationships. A person returned from captivity might face a community that had changed, a family that had mourned, and a language partly forgotten. Some returnees were welcomed; others found it difficult to reintegrate. Descendants of captives, such as genízaro families and Native families in New England, built communities that preserved their histories. Historians must also acknowledge that the surviving records capture only a fraction of such cases. For every person who gained freedom through a legal petition or ransom, many others lived and died in bondage without leaving a record, and their stories remain inaccessible.

Section 53.19

The Transformation of Captivity and Slavery in Colonial North America, 1500–1821

The preceding sections allow a comparison of the principal systems of captivity and slavery in colonial North America. In the Eastern Woodlands, captive-taking linked to mourning and warfare produced adoption on a large scale, especially among Haudenosaunee communities in the seventeenth century, alongside killing and ritual violence. In the Great Lakes and New France, Indigenous diplomatic customs and French demand produced a system of Indigenous slavery, formalized in 1709, in which the Panis category covered captives of many nations. In New England, English colonial warfare and just-war law produced enslavement of Pequot and other captives and the export of some to the Atlantic world. In the Southeast, the Carolina trade converted debt, firearms, and inter-Indigenous warfare into a captive trade on a very large scale until the Tuscarora and Yamasee Wars. In the Spanish borderlands, imperial law prohibiting Indigenous slavery coexisted with rescate, forced labor, and the household servitude out of which genízaro communities emerged. On the Northwest Coast, hereditary slavery was tied to rank and property and predated sustained European contact. In California, missions imposed confinement and compulsory labor distinct from chattel slavery. And in the Southeast, interactions with enslaved and free Africans led some Indigenous households to adopt racial slavery while other communities provided refuge.

These systems differed in several dimensions. In ownership, some treated captives as property that could be sold (Carolina, New France, the Northwest Coast), while others treated them as dependents or kin (Haudenosaunee adoption, Comanche incorporation). In heredity, some passed bondage to children (Northwest Coast, New France, and later racial slavery), while others did not, or aimed at the integration of descendants (adoption, genízaro settlement). In legal standing, empires varied from Spanish doctrines of Indigenous vassalage to French ordinance and English colonial statute. In commercial exchange, the Carolina and French systems were integrated into Atlantic markets, while Haudenosaunee adoption was not primarily commercial. In opportunities for freedom, kinship incorporation, ransom, manumission, legal petition, and flight offered different and often limited routes.

The central interpretive question is how colonial expansion transformed existing institutions. Evidence supports a middle position between continuity and rupture. Colonial markets and law reshaped older practices: demand for captives increased raiding, European legal categories fixed fluid statuses into formal ones, and ties between Indigenous and Atlantic systems created hybrids such as the Panis trade. The existence of precolonial slavery in some societies did not make colonial slavery a simple extension of it, because colonial systems introduced commodification at greater scale, new legal definitions, and long-distance export.

Historical Debate

Comparing Indigenous and Colonial Slaveries

The question: Was slavery in precolonial and Indigenous societies fundamentally different from colonial commercial slavery, or can both be understood as variants of a single institution? Scholars differ on whether the term "slavery" should be applied broadly to varied forms of captivity.

Interpretation A — Distinct institutions: Some historians emphasize that many Indigenous practices were based on kinship and incorporation, and that European markets commodified people at a different scale. On this view, treating them as one institution risks obscuring the transformation that colonial commerce brought.
Interpretation B — Related, interacting systems: Others stress that some Indigenous societies did hold people as property and transmit bondage by descent, and that Indigenous and colonial systems interacted so closely that separating them misrepresents the evidence, as in New France and the Southeast.

Evidence: Both draw on registers, court cases, travelers' accounts, archaeology, and oral history. Points of disagreement: terminology, scale, and interpretation, rather than the fact of captivity.

Why it matters: The answer shapes how historians describe Indigenous agency, responsibility, and the origins of racial slavery, and whether Indigenous societies are portrayed as victims, participants, or both.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined captivity, slavery, and adoption as distinct institutions in Indigenous and colonial North America between about 1500 and 1821. Its central argument is that Indigenous societies developed diverse institutions governing warfare, captivity, incorporation, servitude, and enslavement, that European empires introduced systems of commercial slavery, coercive labor, racial classification, and law that interacted with them, and that the outcomes cannot be explained by any single model.

Section 53.1 through 53.4 established the framework. Captivity describes being seized; slavery denotes a status of property and exclusion; adoption is incorporation into kinship; servitude is obligated labor. Evidence for precolonial practice is limited and unevenly interpretable, ranging from archaeological evidence of violence in the Eastern Woodlands and the Plains to stronger evidence for hereditary slavery on the Northwest Coast. Mourning wars and adoption among Eastern Woodlands peoples, ransom, and killing represented alternative outcomes. Because sources mostly come from captors, purchasers, and officials, and published narratives are unrepresentative, historians must read with care.

In eastern North America, epidemic loss and warfare in the seventeenth century drove Haudenosaunee captive-taking and adoption, in which clan women played important roles; the settlements of 1701 changed its diplomacy. In New France, Indigenous gift diplomacy and French demand produced the Panis slave system, legally recognized in 1709, whose label covered captives from many nations. In New England, the Pequot War and King Philip's War, together with just-war law, led to enslavement and export of Native captives. In the Southeast, the Carolina-centered trade, driven by firearms, credit, and inter-Indigenous warfare, captured and traded tens of thousands of people, many of whom were exported, until the Tuscarora and Yamasee Wars reoriented the region.

In the Spanish borderlands, imperial law restricted Indigenous slavery while allowing exceptions and tolerating evasion through rescate, forced labor, and debt. In New Mexico, captive exchange at trading fairs and baptism into households led to genízaro communities that were settled on the frontier and remained socially subordinate. Apache, Navajo, Pueblo, and Ute captive-taking was entangled with retaliation, labor, trade, and diplomacy, and Comanche captivity combined incorporation, labor, ransom, and sale within a society whose power shaped negotiation with Spanish authorities.

On the Northwest Coast, slavery was a hereditary institution tied to rank, property, and household production, and predated European contact; maritime trade altered but did not create it. In California, the mission system imposed confinement, discipline, and compulsory labor that was not chattel slavery but was coercive and accompanied by demographic collapse and resistance. In the Southeast, African-Indigenous relationships ranged from refuge and kinship to Indigenous adoption of racial chattel slavery, varying by community and period.

Across these regions, women and children were prominent among captives, and their experiences involved separation, language learning, labor, family formation, and ambiguity between incorporation and freedom. Captives resisted by flight, negotiation, litigation, and cultural persistence, and freedom was defined differently in Indigenous, French, English, and Spanish institutions. Surviving evidence captures only a fraction of these experiences.

Comparison reveals differences in ownership, heredity, kinship incorporation, legal standing, commercial exchange, and opportunities for freedom. Colonial expansion transformed existing institutions through markets, legal categories, and long-distance export, while many Indigenous societies preserved or adapted their own practices. The chapter has thus avoided both the claim of a universal Indigenous slavery and the claim that all Indigenous captivity was benign.

The consequences extended beyond 1821 through displaced and reconstituted communities, descendant populations such as genízaros, and the later expansion of Indigenous slaveholding in the Southeast. Those histories belong to later chapters, but their roots lie in the interacting systems described here.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it refuses a simple story. Indigenous history is neither a record of uniform victimhood nor a catalog of the same practices found elsewhere in the Atlantic world. It is a history of many societies making decisions about war, kinship, labor, and power under changing conditions, and of people whose lives were reshaped by those decisions and by forces beyond their control.

The chapter reveals how empires and Indigenous nations built interlocking systems. French alliances, English credit, and Spanish law each worked through Indigenous institutions, and Indigenous leaders used captives in diplomacy and trade. At the same time, individual captives and their families resisted, adapted, and built new communities, from Kahnawake to Abiquiú to the descendant peoples of New England.

Many later developments grew from these processes: the displacement of Southeastern peoples, the coalescence of new coalitions, the shape of New Mexican society, and the history of Indigenous slaveholding and African-Indigenous relationships that reappears in the era of removal. Chapter 55 turns to the diplomacy through which ransom and exchange operated, and later chapters follow these histories into the nineteenth century.

The chapter also leaves a methodological lesson: the quality of historical judgment depends on attention to who produced the evidence. Recovering the experiences of captives requires patience with silence and respect for descendants who preserve memory, and it is one of the tasks that continued historical work must pursue.
