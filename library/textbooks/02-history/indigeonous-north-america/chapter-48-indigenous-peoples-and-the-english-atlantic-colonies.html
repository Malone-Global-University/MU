Indigenous Peoples and the English Atlantic Colonies

Indigenous Peoples of North America: A Continental History

Chapter Opening

When the first English settlers stepped ashore on the James River in 1607, they did not enter an empty continent but a densely settled political landscape whose towns, cornfields, fishing weirs, and paths had been shaped by Indigenous people for many centuries. Over the following ninety-four years, English colonization transformed that landscape. It did so not through a single conquest but through migration, agriculture, law, missionary activity, commerce, and war, and Indigenous nations helped determine how each of those processes unfolded.

The two most famous English settlements of the early seventeenth century, Jamestown and Plymouth, are usually remembered through English eyes: a failing fort in a Virginia swamp, a small group of religious dissenters on a New England shore. Each was also a settlement planted within the territory of an Indigenous polity with its own leaders, alliances, grievances, and strategic calculations. Wahunsenacawh, the paramount leader of the Powhatan chiefdoms, and Ousamequin, the Pokanoket sachem whom English writers called Massasoit, both made deliberate decisions about how to treat the newcomers. They permitted settlement, supplied food, traded, and formed alliances, but they did so for reasons rooted in their own political circumstances rather than out of simple hospitality or helplessness.

This chapter examines the encounter between Indigenous societies and the expanding English Atlantic colonies from approximately 1607 to 1701. Its distinctive subject is a particular form of colonial expansion: permanent English settlement, together with its consequences for Indigenous political authority and territorial control. That distinguishes it from Chapter 46, which treats the commercial history of the fur trade, from Chapter 45, which treats New France and its Indigenous allies, and from Chapter 47, which follows the Haudenosaunee through the century's great intertribal wars. English colonies were not primarily trading posts. They were agricultural and commercial settlements designed to acquire land and to be occupied by growing numbers of colonists, and that design made the control of land the central issue between English colonists and Indigenous peoples.

The chapter's argument is that English colonization transformed Indigenous Atlantic North America through several overlapping processes rather than a single event. Settlement spread through the physical expansion of towns, tobacco plantations, fences, and livestock. Land passed to English hands through purchases, deeds, treaties, grants, and conquest, each carrying different meanings for the parties involved. Colonial courts claimed jurisdiction over Indigenous people. Missionaries, especially in Puritan New England, created Christian Indigenous communities whose members pursued their own purposes. Colonial merchants and officials drew Indigenous communities into debt, servitude, and, in the south, the beginnings of a commercial slave trade. And at intervals, these pressures produced wars: the Anglo-Powhatan wars in Virginia, the Pequot War of 1636–1637, Metacom's War of 1675–1678, and the Wabanaki wars on the northern frontier.

Indigenous nations were never merely recipients of these changes. They negotiated treaties on their own terms, exploited English rivalries, regulated access to their territories, sold land selectively, took up English law and literacy for their own ends, joined or refused coalitions, and sometimes fought. Their choices were constrained, often severely, by epidemic disease, by shifting balances of population and military technology, and by rivalries among Indigenous peoples themselves, but they were choices. The consequences also differed markedly among Virginia, New England, the Middle Atlantic, and Carolina, and this chapter pays sustained attention to those regional differences.

Students should be aware from the outset of the kinds of evidence involved. Much of the written record was produced by colonists: promotional tracts, captains' narratives, governors' letters, court records, sermons, and treaties transcribed in English. Indigenous perspectives must frequently be reconstructed from these sources read against the grain, supplemented by archaeology, by Indigenous oral histories preserved by tribal nations, and, increasingly, by the work of Indigenous scholars. A small but important body of Indigenous writing in Massachusett and other languages also survives from the later seventeenth century. Section 48.5 introduces these problems directly.

The chapter also resists a conclusion that earlier textbooks often drew: that these wars and settlements ended in the disappearance of the Powhatan, Wampanoag, Pequot, Narragansett, and other peoples. They endured. The Pamunkey and Mattaponi retain reservations in Virginia established in the seventeenth century. The Mashantucket Pequot, Mohegan, and Eastern Pequot communities of Connecticut, the Wampanoag nations of Massachusetts, and the Narragansett Indian Tribe of Rhode Island continue as political communities, and the Wampanoag language, once classed among the silenced, has been restored as a spoken language (Chapter 123). What changed in the seventeenth century was the balance of power, the extent of Indigenous territory, and the terms on which Indigenous people lived beside English neighbors, not the existence of Indigenous nations.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why English colonization is best understood as the establishment of permanent settlement within existing Indigenous political worlds rather than as the settlement of an empty landscape.
  2. Describe the principal Indigenous polities of the Atlantic coast from the Chesapeake to New England and the Carolina coast, and distinguish their forms of governance, subsistence, and diplomacy.
  3. Trace the course and significance of the three Anglo-Powhatan conflicts of 1609–1614, 1622–1632, and 1644–1646, and explain why they were not interchangeable.
  4. Analyze Wampanoag diplomacy with Plymouth in 1620–1621 as a strategic decision by Ousamequin and distinguish contemporary evidence from later Thanksgiving tradition.
  5. Reconstruct the causes, course, and consequences of the Pequot War, including the role of Native allies, the Mystic massacre, and the Treaty of Hartford.
  6. Compare English and Indigenous concepts of land, deeds, jurisdiction, and property, and evaluate how misunderstanding and structural pressure each contributed to conflict.
  7. Assess Puritan missionary work, Indigenous literacy, and the praying towns from the perspectives of missionaries and of Indigenous participants.
  8. Explain the multiple causes of Metacom's War and the diverse choices of Wampanoag, Nipmuc, Narragansett, Mohegan, Pequot, and Christian Indigenous communities.
  9. Evaluate Indigenous strategies in Virginia, Maryland, Pennsylvania, the Wabanaki homelands, and Carolina, including diplomacy, tributary status, migration, and alliance.
  10. Compare English colonial systems in Virginia, New England, the Middle Atlantic, and Carolina with respect to land acquisition, law, religion, labor, and diplomatic opportunity.
  11. Interpret the relationship among colonial archives, archaeology, Indigenous oral history, and public memory, and explain the evidence for Indigenous continuity after 1700.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did Indigenous leaders assess the English newcomers, and what strategic interests shaped their decisions to tolerate, ally with, or resist them?
  2. Why did English settlement produce recurring conflict even when formal treaties and peace agreements existed?
  3. In what ways did English and Indigenous understandings of land, authority, and obligation differ, and how much of the resulting conflict was misunderstanding as opposed to structural conflict of interest?
  4. How did divisions among Indigenous peoples, such as the rivalries of Pequot, Mohegan, and Narragansett, affect the course of colonization?
  5. Why did missionary Christianity and English literacy attract some Indigenous people, and what did conversion mean for those who accepted it?
  6. How should historians weigh colonial documents, archaeological evidence, and Indigenous oral history when they conflict or disagree in emphasis?
  7. Why did the consequences of English colonization differ among Virginia, New England, the Middle Atlantic, and Carolina?
  8. What is the difference between the military defeat of an Indigenous coalition and the disappearance of a people, and how does the evidence show Indigenous nations surviving?
  9. How have the stories of Pocahontas, Thanksgiving, the Pequot War, and Metacom's War been shaped by later political purposes?

Historical Context

By 1600 the Atlantic coast of North America had experienced more than a century of intermittent European contact, though very unevenly. Basque, Portuguese, French, and English fishermen worked the Newfoundland banks and the Gulf of Maine; Spanish missions and garrisons existed in Florida (Chapter 41); and French commerce was expanding along the St. Lawrence (Chapter 45). Between those zones lay a long stretch of coastline, from the Chesapeake to southern New England, where Indigenous nations had met occasional ships, traded furs and food for metal and cloth, and in some cases suffered kidnapping, violence, and introduced disease, but where no permanent European settlement existed. Indigenous people along this coast were therefore not uninformed about Europeans. Many had traded with them, and some, as in the Roanoke region and on Cape Cod, had already learned how dangerous they could be.

English interest in North America grew in the late sixteenth century for several overlapping reasons: rivalry with Spain, the hope of discovering precious metals or a passage to Asia, the promise of raw materials and new markets, anxiety about domestic poverty and unemployment, and, later, religious dissent. The English Crown did not itself plant most colonies. Instead it issued charters to private groups, including joint-stock companies and proprietors, who assumed the costs and risks in the hope of profit. This arrangement shaped colonial behavior. A company that needed to pay its investors was under pressure to find a profitable export, which in Virginia became tobacco; a religious community seeking to build a godly society needed land on which to farm and settle in family groups, which meant New England towns. Both models demanded land and both generated recurrent conflict with Indigenous inhabitants.

The Indigenous peoples of this coast spoke languages of the Algonquian family, with important exceptions such as the Iroquoian-speaking Susquehannock and the Siouan-speaking peoples of the Piedmont and Carolina interior. They lived by combining maize, bean, and squash agriculture with hunting, fishing, shellfish gathering, and the harvest of seasonal wild foods, and their settlements ranged from large, nearly permanent farming towns to smaller communities that moved between seasonal sites. Political organization was equally varied, extending from the paramount chiefdom of Tsenacommacah in Virginia to the sachemships of New England, where authority often rested on kinship, tribute, alliance, and personal reputation. These societies were already engaged in diplomacy, trade, and warfare with one another when the English arrived. The Haudenosaunee conflicts treated in Chapter 47 and the epidemic crisis summarized in Chapter 40 formed part of the regional setting.

Disease deserves particular emphasis, but it should be handled carefully. Epidemics in southern New England in about 1616–1619 sharply reduced the populations of many coastal communities, especially in the Massachusetts Bay region and the area of Patuxet, where Plymouth would be founded. This did not clear the land of people, and survivors reorganized their communities, but it altered the balance of power among Indigenous polities and affected how some leaders judged the usefulness of the English. Additional epidemics, notably smallpox in the early 1630s, struck again after English settlement began. Chapter 48 treats disease only where it altered specific relationships; the broader history belongs to Chapter 40.

Finally, students should note that the chapter's central actors had different time horizons. English colonists and investors expected permanence, growth, and the multiplication of settlements. Many Indigenous leaders initially expected to incorporate a small group of newcomers into an existing system of alliance and exchange. By the time that expectation gave way to alarm about the scale and permanence of English settlement, the English population had often grown large enough to make resistance far more costly. Understanding that gap between early expectations and later realities is essential to following the sequence of accommodation and war that structures this chapter.

Sections 48.1–48.5
Section 48.1

English Colonization as an Indigenous Historical Problem

From the standpoint of the Indigenous communities whose territories became English settlements, colonization was not an abstraction but a series of concrete questions. Who were these newcomers, and how many more would follow? Could they be incorporated into existing networks of exchange and alliance? What would they want in return for the metal tools, cloth, and firearms they offered? Did their leaders have the authority to bind their followers to agreements? And above all, would they leave? The answers varied by place and time, and they changed as English numbers grew.

It is useful to distinguish several kinds of English presence, because Indigenous responses depended on which kind they faced. Exploratory voyages, such as those along the New England coast in the early 1600s, produced brief encounters, trade, and sometimes kidnapping. Commercial contacts, including fishing and the exchange of furs for English goods, were familiar to many coastal communities and could be integrated into existing Indigenous exchange systems. Temporary residence, as at Roanoke, raised the question of food supply and relations with local leaders. Plantation settlement, meaning the permanent occupation of land by farming and commercial colonists, was different in kind: it claimed ground for exclusive use and drew in a steady stream of new arrivals. Finally, territorial dispossession, which developed over decades, involved the transfer of land to English hands through purchase, treaty, legal action, and conquest. The English Atlantic colonies moved from the first of these forms to the last at different speeds.

A second distinction concerns the kind of colony. This chapter will use the term settler colony to describe settlements whose purpose was to establish a permanent colonial population on Indigenous land, as opposed to colonies sustained mainly by commerce with Indigenous partners. English Atlantic colonies were overwhelmingly of the former kind. This helps explain why Indigenous leaders who were willing to supply food or trade furs often balked when English settlements spread, and why conflicts so often turned on fields, fences, and livestock rather than on isolated disputes over trade.

Indigenous leaders did not wait for English intentions to become clear before gathering information. Coastal communities exchanged news along rivers, paths, and kinship ties, and reports about ships, captives taken by earlier voyagers, and the behavior of particular captains moved between nations. When Powhatan, Wampanoag, or Narragansett leaders evaluated a new English settlement, they did so against what they already knew: that some visitors traded fairly, that others kidnapped or killed, and that guns, disease, and metal tools could each shift the balance among Indigenous rivals. This is why the first years of most English settlements were marked by close Indigenous scrutiny, selective generosity, and tests of English reliability rather than by simple welcome or simple hostility.

Historical Vocabulary
Settler Colony, Paramount Chiefdom, and Sovereignty

Settler colony refers to a colony whose principal aim is the permanent settlement of a colonizing population on land previously occupied by others. Settler colonies depend on the continuing acquisition of land and tend to generate pressure to displace, confine, or absorb Indigenous inhabitants. English Virginia and New England were settler colonies, whereas the commercial posts discussed in Chapter 46 usually depended on cooperation with Indigenous trading partners.

Paramount chiefdom is a scholarly term for a political system in which a leading chief holds authority over several subordinate chiefdoms, usually through tribute, kinship, and alliance, while local leaders retain considerable autonomy. It does not mean a modern monarchy, and English writers who called Wahunsenacawh an "emperor" or "king" distorted a more flexible structure.

Sovereignty, as in Chapter 1, refers to inherent political authority. Seventeenth-century Indigenous nations exercised it in practice by making war and peace, allocating land use, and regulating relations with foreigners. The English did not recognize this authority consistently. They sometimes dealt with Indigenous leaders as political equals, sometimes as subjects, and sometimes as obstacles.

Potential misunderstanding: Students should avoid assuming that colonists and Indigenous people shared a common vocabulary of political terms. Words such as king, queen, subject, submission, and treaty were English categories applied to Indigenous institutions that functioned differently.

Section 48.2

The Indigenous Political Geography of the Atlantic Coast

Between the Chesapeake Bay and the Gulf of Maine, English settlers entered several distinct Indigenous political worlds. In the tidewater of Virginia lay Tsenacommacah, a network of Algonquian-speaking chiefdoms associated with Wahunsenacawh and including the Pamunkey, Mattaponi, Chickahominy, Appamattuck, Nansemond, Rappahannock, and others, together with neighboring peoples such as the Patawomeck on the Potomac who stood in looser relation to him. Beyond the fall line to the west lived Siouan-speaking Monacan and Manahoac peoples, regarded by the Powhatan as rivals. To the north at the head of the Chesapeake lived the Susquehannock, Iroquoian speakers with extensive trading relationships and formidable military capacity. Farther south, along the Carolina coast, lay the Cusabo and Siouan- and Iroquoian-speaking peoples whose worlds are discussed in Division VIII.

In southern New England the political landscape was more fragmented. The Wampanoag, whose name means "people of the first light," comprised dozens of communities across southeastern Massachusetts, Cape Cod, and the islands, with the Pokanoket of Sowams (in present-day Rhode Island and southeastern Massachusetts) and the Patuxet, Nauset, Pocasset, and Sakonnet communities among them. To the north around Massachusetts Bay lived the Massachusett and the Pawtucket (including the Naumkeag), and inland the Nipmuc. West and south of the Wampanoag, the Narragansett occupied much of what is now Rhode Island and were among the most populous peoples of the region at the time of English settlement. In southeastern Connecticut lay the Pequot and, closely related by language and kinship, the Mohegan, whose separation from the Pequot formed part of the politics of the 1630s. Along the Connecticut River were Algonquian-speaking peoples including the Wangunk and Podunk, and on Long Island and along the Sound were the Montaukett and other communities.

The Middle Atlantic was home to the Lenape, speakers of Delaware languages, whose communities occupied the Delaware valley, northern New Jersey, and parts of the lower Hudson, with the Munsee dialect group in the north. North of the Gulf of Maine and into the Maritimes were the Wabanaki, a term that denotes the "people of the dawn land" and refers to a family of related peoples including the Mi'kmaq, Wolastoqiyik (Maliseet), Passamaquoddy, Penobscot, and western Abenaki and Pennacook.

Three features of this political geography deserve emphasis. First, scale and structure differed greatly. Tsenacommacah was a tribute-collecting paramount chiefdom, while many New England polities were smaller sachemships whose leaders balanced the claims of kin and community and could not issue binding orders. Second, these polities were already connected by alliance, tribute, intermarriage, and rivalry. The Narragansett received tribute from some neighbors, the Pequot exercised influence over groups in the Connecticut valley and Long Island, and the Wampanoag nations had paid deference to the Narragansett at times. Third, population levels, disease histories, and agricultural capacities varied. A map of Indigenous polities around 1600 therefore resembles a mosaic of connected but autonomous systems rather than a uniform field of "tribes."

Section 48.3

Land, Agriculture, Territorial Authority, and Indigenous Sovereignty

English colonists often described the land they found as underused, citing the absence of enclosed fields, European livestock, and fixed property lines. This description reflected English assumptions rather than Indigenous practice. Algonquian communities farmed extensively, growing maize, beans, and squash in cleared fields near their towns, and they managed the surrounding landscape through controlled burning, the cultivation and gathering of plant foods, the maintenance of fishing weirs, and the seasonal use of hunting territories. Many communities moved between summer planting grounds, fall hunting camps, and winter shelters, so that a single people might use several ecological zones across the year. Fields were often farmed for some years and then left fallow while the community cleared new ground. English observers who saw fallow land or regrowth concluded that the land was vacant and available.

Indigenous societies had well-developed ideas about rights and authority over land, but these differed from English concepts of exclusive, individually held, permanently alienable title. A family or lineage might hold rights to cultivate particular fields, a community to fish a particular weir, a sachem to receive tribute from those who lived within a territory, and several communities to share a hunting area. Political authority over a territory (the ability to admit or exclude outsiders, to negotiate access, and to collect tribute) was distinct from the use rights of those who worked the land. When an Indigenous leader permitted settlers to occupy a site, he might understand himself to be extending a conditional right of use and expecting reciprocal obligations of tribute, assistance, or alliance. English law, by contrast, understood purchase as transferring exclusive and perpetual ownership.

English colonial leaders added another layer of argument. They claimed that land which Indigenous people did not enclose or improve in English fashion was "vacant" and available to those who would cultivate it, a position sometimes expressed in Latin as vacuum domicilium, and they drew on royal charters that granted lands without consulting the Indigenous occupants. Yet colonists also recognized, at least in practice, that Indigenous leaders could not simply be ignored. Several colonies, particularly in New England, developed a practice of purchasing land from Indigenous sachems, partly for legal protection against rival English claims and partly because Indigenous goodwill or military strength made peaceful dealing prudent. The tension between theory, which denied Indigenous title, and practice, which recognized Indigenous authority when convenient, runs through this chapter.

Territorial authority also meant jurisdiction, the power to apply law and punish offenses. Indigenous sachems and councils resolved disputes within their communities and negotiated compensation for injuries between communities, often through gifts, wampum, and public ceremony. These procedures were not unlike diplomatic and legal mechanisms in other societies, and they were frequently adopted in early agreements with the English. But English colonies increasingly asserted that Indigenous people living within or near English settlements fell under English courts. The competing claims to jurisdiction, discussed in Section 48.25, generated some of the most persistent conflicts of the century.

Historical Debate

What Constituted an Indigenous Land Agreement?

The question: When an Indigenous leader and an English colonist signed or marked a deed, what was actually agreed? Did Indigenous participants knowingly sell land in a sense comparable to English law, or did they understand something different, such as a grant of shared use or a diplomatic gift?

Interpretation A — Different conceptions of land produced different understandings: Some historians emphasize that Algonquian peoples did not use a concept of exclusive, alienable private ownership and that deeds written in English legal language may have recorded transactions that Indigenous parties understood as conditional access, alliance, or gift exchange. On this view, English colonists exploited a gap in legal vocabulary, intentionally or not.
Interpretation B — Indigenous leaders understood the transactions and used them strategically: Other historians stress that many sachems were experienced diplomats who understood English intentions, negotiated the terms, reserved specific rights, and sold land to gain trade goods, military allies, and protection against rivals. On this view, characterizing Indigenous parties as naïve underestimates their political skill, and attention should fall on the structural pressures and coercion that increasingly limited their choices.

Evidence: Surviving deeds, colonial court records, petitions, and later Indigenous complaints, together with the careful reservations of fishing, hunting, and planting rights written into many agreements, inform both views. Deeds are English documents, and Indigenous understandings must be inferred from reservation clauses, later disputes, and tribal oral histories.

Points of disagreement: Historians differ about interpretation and degree rather than about the basic facts: how much to attribute to misunderstanding, how much to strategy, and how much to coercion by growing English numbers and military power.

Why it matters: If land transfers rested mainly on misunderstanding, conflict looks like a failure of communication. If they rested on strategic Indigenous choices under increasing pressure, conflict looks like the outcome of structural imbalance. Both readings have support, and the chapter returns to this problem in Section 48.23.

Student task: Choose one deed or treaty discussed later in the chapter and identify what evidence would let you decide whether the Indigenous signers understood it as a permanent sale.

Section 48.4

English Colonial Projects before and after Jamestown

English colonial ambitions in North America predated Jamestown by decades. In the 1580s, Sir Walter Raleigh sponsored expeditions to the coast of what is now North Carolina, a region the English called Virginia. An early reconnaissance in 1584 brought two Indigenous men, Manteo and Wanchese, to England; subsequent expeditions established a small military outpost on Roanoke Island in 1585 and, in 1587, a settlement that included women and children. The Roanoke colonists depended on local Algonquian communities, including the Secotan and Croatoan, for food, and relations deteriorated into violence. The Englishman Thomas Harriot, who learned some of the local language, produced a report in 1588 describing Indigenous farming, religion, and resources. The 1587 colony was found abandoned when a relief expedition returned in 1590. Its fate remains unresolved, and while hypotheses include dispersal among nearby Indigenous communities, the evidence does not permit certainty. For students of this chapter, Roanoke matters because it showed English promoters how heavily a small colony depended on Indigenous food and goodwill, a lesson Jamestown's investors had imperfectly absorbed.

Jamestown was founded in 1607 under a charter granted to the Virginia Company of London, a joint-stock company. The settlers numbered just over a hundred men and boys. They chose a low-lying peninsula on the James River that was easily defended against Spanish ships but poorly suited to health and farming, with brackish water and mosquito-borne disease. The company's directions urged the colonists to seek gold, a route to the Pacific, and survivors of Roanoke. They were also told to avoid offending Indigenous peoples, though the Company's leaders also expected to extract labor and tribute and held ambitions of conversion. Section 48.7 follows the early years in detail.

The Plymouth colony was settled in 1620 by a group of English separatist Protestants, sometimes called Pilgrims in later tradition, along with other settlers sometimes called Strangers, who arrived on the Mayflower. They had intended to settle near the Hudson River under a patent from the Virginia Company, but they landed instead at Cape Cod, outside the area of their patent, in November 1620, and in December established themselves at Patuxet, a site cleared of its Indigenous inhabitants by epidemic. Massachusetts Bay, founded in 1630, differed in scale and character. A large, well-capitalized body of Puritans, led by John Winthrop, migrated in family groups and within a decade had founded multiple towns. The Great Migration of the 1630s brought many thousands of English settlers to New England, a population surge that quickly made the English a major force in the region.

These colonial projects differed in motive, organization, and economic base, yet all were settler undertakings that depended on acquiring land. The Virginia Company sought returns for its shareholders. Plymouth and Massachusetts Bay sought land on which religiously motivated communities could live and prosper. Later proprietary colonies, such as Pennsylvania and Carolina, were granted by the Crown to individuals or groups who hoped to profit from land sales and trade. In each case, the question of how to acquire territory and manage Indigenous neighbors was fundamental, and the answers evolved through trial, conflict, and negotiation rather than from a single imperial plan.

Section 48.5

Evidence, Historical Names, and the Problem of Colonial Narratives

The principal contemporary narratives of the period were written by English colonists with purposes of their own. John Smith, a military adventurer who helped lead Jamestown in its early years, wrote several accounts, including A True Relation (1608) and the Generall Historie of Virginia (1624). The latter was composed years after the events it describes and includes episodes, such as his famous rescue by Pocahontas, that do not appear in his earlier writing, which leads many historians to treat it cautiously. William Bradford, long-time governor of Plymouth, wrote Of Plymouth Plantation decades after its founding, and Edward Winslow, a Plymouth leader, contributed to Mourt's Relation (1622), which narrates the colony's first year and is closer in time to the events. English officials and commentators such as John Underhill and John Mason, who fought in the Pequot War, wrote accounts that justified colonial conduct. Court records, treaties, deeds, missionary writings by John Eliot and others, and the writings of Roger Williams supplement these.

These sources illuminate Indigenous history, but they must be read critically. Their authors often wrote to attract investors, to justify wars, to demonstrate godly providence, or to defend their own reputations. They filtered Indigenous speech through interpreters and English assumptions and recorded Indigenous leaders' words in English, often long after the fact. They gave little attention to Indigenous reasoning. They also used loaded terms: "savages," "kings," "queens," "tribes," "submission," "rebellion." The labels imposed English political and moral categories. A Powhatan or Narragansett leader who negotiated with the English was not necessarily a "king" in the English sense, and an Indigenous community that resisted encroachment was not "rebelling" against an authority it had never accepted.

Other kinds of evidence help correct these distortions. Archaeology provides independent information about Indigenous settlement, diet, trade goods, and violence. Excavations at Pequot sites, for example, have documented Indigenous towns, trade, and the aftermath of the Mystic attack, and archaeology at Jamestown has revealed the extent of Powhatan-English interaction. Indigenous oral histories preserved by nations such as the Pamunkey, Mashantucket Pequot, Mohegan, Wampanoag, and Narragansett offer interpretations that sometimes differ sharply from colonial accounts. Indigenous scholars and tribal historians increasingly lead research projects, including language reclamation and community history. Finally, a body of writing by Indigenous authors survives from the later seventeenth century, including petitions, letters, and translations in Massachusett, which allow historians to hear Indigenous voices more directly.

Students should also attend to names. Many Indigenous leaders appear under several names, and the English forms often derive from imperfect transcriptions. Wahunsenacawh was called Powhatan by the English, after the name of his hometown and the term for his chiefdom; Ousamequin is also called Massasoit, which is better understood as a title meaning "great sachem"; Matoaka was known publicly as Pocahontas, a nickname, and after baptism as Rebecca; Pometacom is Metacom or Philip. This chapter uses Indigenous names where well established and identifies the familiar English names for clarity. It also uses "Pequot," "Narragansett," and similar terms to refer to Indigenous nations, and "tribe" only in the specialized legal sense introduced in Chapter 1.

Archaeology has transformed what can be said about the Indigenous side of these encounters. Excavations at Werowocomoco on the York River, the principal seat of Wahunsenacawh, undertaken with the participation of Virginia Indian nations, have documented a large, carefully planned settlement with ceremonial and residential areas, and they allow historians to compare John Smith's descriptions with the physical site. Work at Jamestown has recovered Indigenous pottery, copper, and tools within the fort's own deposits, evidence of daily exchange. In southern New England, survey and excavation of Pequot and Narragansett settlements and of the Mystic battlefield have recovered gun parts, projectile points, and structural remains that complement the English narratives. Archaeological evidence does not speak for itself, and it must be read alongside documents and tribal knowledge, but it is the one body of sources produced by Indigenous activity rather than filtered through an English author.

Section 48.6

Tsenacommacah and the Political Authority of Wahunsenacawh

The Powhatan name for their homeland, Tsenacommacah, is usually translated as "densely inhabited land." It extended across the tidewater peninsulas between the James, York, Rappahannock, and Potomac rivers and was home to Algonquian-speaking communities organized into roughly thirty chiefdoms, each with its own principal towns, leaders, and territories. The scale of the population is uncertain, since no census exists and estimates must rely on English observations and archaeology, but it clearly numbered in the many thousands, spread across many towns and farming hamlets along the rivers.

Wahunsenacawh, who inherited a handful of chiefdoms in the area of the upper James and York rivers, built the larger system through a combination of alliance, marriage, and military force over the late sixteenth century. He extended his authority over neighboring chiefdoms, sometimes by conquest and sometimes by installing relatives as leaders, and he collected tribute in corn, skins, copper, and other goods. English observers described the system as an "empire," and Wahunsenacawh as a "king" or "emperor," but scholars generally prefer the term paramount chiefdom. The system was real and effective in coordinating diplomacy and trade, yet individual chiefdoms retained significant autonomy and could pursue their own interests. The Chickahominy, for example, governed themselves through a council of elders and paid tribute but did not accept a ruler imposed by Wahunsenacawh, while the Patawomeck on the Potomac maintained their own relationship with the English.

The economy rested on agriculture, fishing, hunting, and exchange. Women did most of the farming, tending fields of maize, beans, and squash near the towns, while men hunted deer, fished, and engaged in diplomacy and war. The tributary system moved food and valuable goods toward leaders, who redistributed them to support allies, ceremonies, and military followers. Wahunsenacawh's principal residence at Werowocomoco on the York River was a center for these functions, and archaeological work there has revealed a large, carefully organized site. Trade extended inland to the Monacan and beyond for copper and other goods and, increasingly, to coastal European vessels.

These facts matter because they explain Indigenous decision-making. Wahunsenacawh was an experienced political leader who understood power and exchange. When the English arrived, he did not see isolated helpless strangers; he saw potential allies against the Monacan and Siouan-speaking rivals, a possible source of metal goods and weapons, and a possible threat. His decisions in 1607 and after therefore reflected calculations about both the English and other Indigenous powers, and they were also constrained by the autonomy of his subordinate chiefs, some of whom resisted English encroachment sooner than he did.

Political authority within the chiefdoms rested on leaders called werowances (and, for women, weroansquas), who held office through kinship and were supported by tribute from the towns under them. Smith described the principal communities he inherited or acquired as numbering about half a dozen, among them the Powhatan, Arrohateck, Appamattuck, Pamunkey, Mattaponi, and Youghtanund, and he added others by alliance or force. Religious specialists and councils of elders advised leaders, and succession often passed through the female line, so that brothers, nephews, and sisters appear among the later leaders. Women such as the weroansqua of the Appamattuck are recorded as exercising authority in their own right. English observers, who expected a male hereditary monarchy, struggled to understand these arrangements and often misreported them.

Powhatan oral traditions, preserved by the Pamunkey, Mattaponi, and other nations, offer a different view of this world from the one found in English texts. They emphasize that Tsenacommacah was a home, a network of towns and families, and not merely an obstacle to English expansion, and they preserve interpretations of events such as the capture of Pocahontas that diverge from colonial accounts. These traditions are not interchangeable with documents, but historians increasingly treat them as evidence about how descendant communities understand their past and as a check on the assumptions embedded in colonial narratives.

Section 48.7

Jamestown, 1607: Settlement, Exchange, and Indigenous Decisions

The English expedition of three ships reached the Chesapeake in April 1607, and the settlers established Jamestown in May on a marshy peninsula within the territory of the Paspahegh people, a community within Tsenacommacah. The colonists had little experience of farming in the region and many were gentlemen unfamiliar with manual labor. The site was unhealthy, the summer drought severe, and within a few months many died of disease and malnutrition. From the beginning they depended on Indigenous communities for corn, which Powhatan towns supplied through trade and, increasingly, demands.

Why did the Powhatan allow the English to remain? The evidence permits several inferences. English firearms and metal goods were attractive and potentially useful. The small, weak colony did not initially threaten Powhatan territory. Wahunsenacawh may have hoped to incorporate the newcomers into his system as a dependent community and to use them against enemies. English leaders, for their part, gave gifts and offered trade but were reluctant to subordinate themselves, and the early exchanges involved mutual misreading. The English believed they were negotiating with a "king" who could bind his people, while the Powhatan expected reciprocal tribute and respect.

John Smith emerged as the colony's most active leader, exploring rivers and trading for corn, using intimidation at times. In December 1607, while on an expedition, he was captured by men associated with Opechancanough and brought to Wahunsenacawh. The meaning of this episode has been much discussed. Smith's own early account described it as an encounter with ceremonies and negotiation, and he was returned to Jamestown. Some scholars interpret the sequence as a ritual of adoption designed to incorporate Smith and the English into the Powhatan system, while others emphasize uncertainty. In any case, the encounter established that Wahunsenacawh sought a relationship in which the English would depend on him for food and, in return, provide metal tools and goods.

Relations in 1608–1609 oscillated between trade, tension, and coercion. Wahunsenacawh sent corn and Indigenous leaders exchanged visits with Smith, while the English attempted to crown Wahunsenacawh in a ceremony in 1608, an act he reportedly resisted, since it implied subordination to the English king. Smith in turn used force to extract corn. These events show a contest over the terms of the relationship, and both sides recognized that the other might become hostile. When Smith was injured and returned to England in autumn 1609, the Powhatan lost the one English leader with whom they had a working, if tense, relationship.

The relationship between the colony and its neighbors was never limited to the exchanges between Smith and Wahunsenacawh. The Paspahegh, on whose land Jamestown stood, were from the beginning the community most directly affected, and their leader Wowinchopunk had reason to resent a settlement placed on his people's fields. Other communities traded corn for copper and beads in exchange for English survival, while Pocahontas and other young people moved between Jamestown and Werowocomoco as messengers. In the autumn of 1608 the English staged a coronation of Wahunsenacawh with a crown and scarlet cloak sent from London, an event that English writers presented as an honor and that Wahunsenacawh reportedly treated warily, since accepting a crown from another king implied an inferior position. The episode shows how differently the two sides understood ceremonies of friendship.

The colony's dependence on Indigenous food gave Powhatan towns a real if unstable bargaining position. Smith's expeditions for corn alternated between trade and intimidation, and Powhatan communities responded by withholding supplies, hiding stores, or demanding firearms and metal goods in exchange. Individual English colonists also deserted to Powhatan towns, where some were sheltered and used as sources of English knowledge. These relationships of mutual need and suspicion explain why violence could break out suddenly even when both sides still had reasons to preserve the peace.

Section 48.8

The First Anglo-Powhatan War, 1609–1614

In 1609 the Virginia Company reorganized under a new charter and sent several hundred additional settlers, many unprepared for conditions. A hurricane scattered the fleet and delayed the new governor, and the colony entered the autumn short of food and without strong leadership. At about the same time, relations with Powhatan communities deteriorated. Several factors were at work. A drought was straining food supplies for both peoples, English demands for corn had become more insistent, and settlers had begun to establish outposts on Indigenous lands. For the Powhatan, the colony no longer looked like a small trading partner but a growing and aggressive presence.

During the winter of 1609–1610, remembered by the English as the "starving time," Powhatan forces restricted access to the settlement and attacked colonists who ventured out. The English, confined within Jamestown, ate whatever food they could find, and many died. Commonly cited estimates suggest that of several hundred colonists, only a small fraction survived into spring, though exact figures are uncertain. The arrival of Sir Thomas Gates and Lord De La Warr in 1610, along with fresh supplies, prevented abandonment of the colony. The Company then adopted a harsher approach under military discipline, notably under Sir Thomas Dale, who arrived in 1611.

English military expeditions in 1610 and afterward attacked Indigenous towns, burned houses and crops, and killed civilians. An expedition against the Paspahegh in 1610 destroyed their town and killed the leader's wife and children, an act that English writers recorded without apology. The Powhatan responded with raids and by harassing outlying settlements and blocking trade. Wahunsenacawh's strategy appears to have been to contain the English, to deny them corn, and to isolate settlers, rather than to attempt their immediate destruction. The Powhatan had advantages of numbers, familiarity with the terrain, and mobility. The English had firearms, armor, fortified positions, and the ability to destroy crops and towns, which hurt agricultural communities severely.

By 1613 neither side had defeated the other. The English had established a more secure position with new settlements farther upriver, such as Henrico, but the colony still did not turn a profit. Powhatan chiefdoms remained autonomous and capable. The conflict can be understood less as a clean war with sharp boundaries than as a series of raids, reprisals, and attempted blockades against the background of a shifting struggle over food and territory. It ended through a diplomatic arrangement whose circumstances involved a young woman who has become one of the most mythologized figures in American history.

The English response to the crisis of 1609–1610 combined relief, discipline, and terror. Gates and, after him, Dale imposed a military code on the colonists and extended the settlement upriver, founding Henrico in 1611 on lands that had belonged to Indigenous communities. Raids against Indigenous towns, including the destruction of Paspahegh, aimed to cut the Powhatan off from their fields as well as their fighters. The Powhatan did not meet these raids with pitched battles. They ambushed stragglers, killed settlers who strayed from the forts, and made it dangerous to gather wood or work fields, a form of warfare that suited their numbers and local knowledge. Neither side could end the war on favorable terms, and by 1613 both had reasons to look for a diplomatic way out.

Section 48.9

Pocahontas, Captivity, Diplomacy, and the Peace of 1614

Matoaka, called Pocahontas as a childhood nickname, was a daughter of Wahunsenacawh, born in the mid-1590s. She appears in English sources as a young girl who visited Jamestown and associated with colonists. Nothing in contemporary evidence supports the later romantic story that she and Smith were lovers; she was a child in 1607, and Smith left Virginia in 1609. In 1613, while she was visiting the Patawomeck people on the Potomac, Captain Samuel Argall, who was trading for corn, arranged her capture with the cooperation of local leaders, using her as a hostage to compel Wahunsenacawh to return English captives and weapons and to restore trade.

Pocahontas was held at Jamestown and Henrico, where English clergy instructed her in Christianity. She was baptized and given the name Rebecca, and she developed a relationship with the tobacco planter John Rolfe, whom she married in April 1614 with the consent of Wahunsenacawh, who sent family representatives to the ceremony. Rolfe wrote a letter justifying the marriage in religious and political terms, which survives. Whether she consented freely under conditions of captivity is difficult to know. Some Powhatan oral traditions, preserved among the Mattaponi and others, hold that she was mistreated during her captivity and that the colonial narrative of voluntary conversion and love is a distortion. Historians should treat these accounts as important historical evidence of how Powhatan descendants understand the past, even though they cannot be verified from contemporary documents.

The marriage helped produce a peace between the Powhatan and the English that lasted for roughly eight years. The peace did not reflect English friendship. Wahunsenacawh, facing English military pressure and the difficulty of maintaining a blockade, likely judged peace advantageous, and the marriage gave a kinship basis for relations. Certain Powhatan chiefdoms, including the Chickahominy, negotiated their own agreements with the English at this time. The English, for their part, valued peace as an opportunity to focus on developing the colony.

In 1616 Pocahontas traveled to England with Rolfe and their young son Thomas, as a display of the colony's potential for the Virginia Company, which sought investors. She was received at court and met Smith again. While preparing to return, she died in March 1617 at Gravesend and was buried there. Wahunsenacawh died in 1618. Thomas Rolfe later returned to Virginia, and descendants trace their lineage through him. Pocahontas's story has been reshaped repeatedly, often in ways that obscure her political role as a young woman who was a hostage, a diplomat, and a symbol, and that turn a complex story of captivity and negotiation into a romantic narrative that suggested Indigenous acceptance of English colonization.

Section 48.10

Tobacco Expansion, Land Pressure, and the Breakdown of Peace

The key economic development of the peace years was the success of tobacco. John Rolfe began experimenting with a West Indian variety of tobacco that was milder than the native Virginia plant, and by about 1617 the colony was exporting it to England. The crop transformed Virginia. It offered the first reliable source of profit, drew new settlers and investors, and shaped the colony's patterns of land use. Tobacco was labor-intensive and exhausted soils within a few years, which meant that planters constantly sought fresh ground, especially along navigable rivers where ships could load directly from plantations.

The Virginia Company also reorganized its system for attracting settlers. In 1618 it introduced the headright system, which granted fifty acres to anyone who paid for the passage of an immigrant, including themselves. This encouraged settlers to import servants and led to a rapid increase in population and land claims. A representative assembly met in 1619, and the same year saw the first recorded arrival of Africans in the colony. By 1622 the English population had grown to over a thousand, and plantations were scattered along both banks of the James River and beyond, far from the original fort.

For the Powhatan, the consequences were profound. English plantations occupied fields and river sites, often without any agreement, and displaced Indigenous communities from the best agricultural land. Settlers moved onto lands that Indigenous leaders understood as their own, often near towns. Colonial leaders also began to attempt to convert and educate Indigenous children, projects that Powhatan leaders viewed with suspicion. Wahunsenacawh's death removed a leader who had managed the relationship for years, and Opechancanough, who had long been skeptical of the English, assumed greater prominence.

English plans for Indigenous education illustrate the other side of expansion. The Virginia Company raised funds in England in 1619 for a college at Henrico intended to educate Indigenous children, and colonial leaders hoped to obtain Powhatan youths as pupils. Powhatan leaders understood that children placed in English care could become hostages or be converted away from their communities, and they did not entrust their sons to the project. The plan, like the land clearing around Henrico, reveals how English expectations of permanent settlement and cultural transformation were already outpacing the limited, reciprocal relationship that Wahunsenacawh had tried to establish, and the college did not survive the events of 1622.

The Virginia Company's need to produce profit for investors; English demand for tobacco; a settler colony premised on acquiring land; and the autonomy of Powhatan chiefdoms that could not be easily controlled.

Short-term causes

Immediate effects

Rising English population and land claims, displacement of Indigenous farming communities, growing Powhatan alarm, and increasing tension over crops, livestock, and the killing of Indigenous leaders such as Nemattanew in 1622.

The war of 1622, and ultimately a Virginia economy and legal system built on territorial expansion that treated Indigenous land as available for plantation settlement.

The crop created economic pressure for land, but the outcome depended on company policy, individual decisions by colonists and Indigenous leaders, the vulnerability of Indigenous farming communities to displacement, and Opechancanough's own strategic judgment. Other outcomes, such as sustained negotiated limits to settlement, were possible but would have required restraint on a scale that neither company policy nor colonial incentives favored.

Section 48.11

Opechancanough and the Second Anglo-Powhatan War, 1622–1632

On March 22, 1622, Powhatan warriors struck English settlements along the James River in a coordinated surprise attack. The English companies listed 347 colonists killed, out of a population of perhaps 1,200, and several outlying plantations were destroyed or abandoned. Jamestown itself was not attacked, partly because of a warning. The coordination across many communities suggests careful planning, which historians usually attribute to Opechancanough. The immediate occasion may have been the killing, earlier that year, of Nemattanew, a Powhatan war leader respected by his people, by English settlers.

What did Opechancanough hope to achieve? The question is difficult because no Powhatan source explains his aims, but his actions suggest a strategy of limiting or halting English expansion rather than annihilating the colony. English dependence on Indigenous corn, he may have reasoned, could be used to enforce a retreat. He might also have expected the surprise attack to destroy a large share of English plantations and strike hard enough that survivors would leave or accept limits. He underestimated the English capacity to regroup, rely on reinforcements, and retaliate by destroying Powhatan crops, which proved more destructive than the initial attack.

The Virginia Company's reaction, expressed in pamphlets and correspondence, was to call for a war of extermination or at least of expulsion. Edward Waterhouse's Declaration of 1622 argued that the attack removed any obligation to treat Indigenous people with restraint, because it supposedly justified seizing their lands. Over the following years the English burned cornfields, attacked towns, and killed noncombatants. In 1623, at a peace parley, English leaders offered poisoned drink to Indigenous negotiators, reportedly killing many. The Company, in financial crisis, lost its charter in 1624, and Virginia became a royal colony. A peace was reached in 1632, but the underlying issues of land and settlement had not been resolved.

The Second Anglo-Powhatan War established patterns that persisted. The English adopted a policy of annual campaigns against Indigenous crops, a method that made it difficult for the Powhatan to sustain a prolonged defense. English commentators increasingly described Indigenous people as treacherous and unfit for coexistence. The Powhatan, meanwhile, held on to their core territories, and the war did not lead to their defeat. By the late 1620s, English settlement continued to expand, and the colony's population was growing again.

The attack of 1622 is better described as a coordinated rising than as a series of isolated raids. Powhatan warriors entered English plantations on the morning of March 22, often as guests or traders, which allowed them to take settlers by surprise. English accounts stressed that the assault came after friendly contacts, and they used the fact to justify a policy of unrestrained retaliation. For the Powhatan, the use of ordinary social visits as cover reflected the problem of striking dispersed plantations that could not otherwise be approached in force.

After the initial blow, the war settled into a pattern of English offensives against Indigenous crops and towns, and Indigenous raids against exposed plantations. The colonists abandoned many outlying settlements and concentrated their population in a few defensible sites, so that the dispersed pattern of 1621 was sharply reduced. Yet English reinforcements and the company's determination meant that the Powhatan could not keep the colonists from returning to the river. The war dragged on in sporadic fighting until a peace in 1632, by which time the English presence along the lower James and York rivers was larger, not smaller, than before the rising, and the Powhatan had suffered heavy losses from hunger and destroyed fields.

Section 48.12

The War of 1644, the Treaty of 1646, and Powhatan Survival

By the 1640s the English population of Virginia had grown to several thousand, a far larger and more entrenched presence than in 1622. Settlement had spread across the York River and up several rivers. Opechancanough, by then very old, again organized a coordinated attack in April 1644 on outlying settlements. Contemporary estimates of English deaths are on the order of several hundred, often given as about four hundred, though exact figures are uncertain; the proportion of the colonial population killed was far smaller than in 1622. English forces under Governor William Berkeley responded with campaigns that destroyed towns and crops and captured Opechancanough in 1646, who was shot by a guard while a prisoner at Jamestown.

The treaty of 1646, negotiated with Necotowance, who succeeded Opechancanough as principal leader, marked a turning point. It established that Powhatan chiefdoms were tributary to the English Crown, required annual tribute, and attempted to separate English and Indigenous territories by a boundary line, with the lands north of the York River designated for Indigenous peoples and English settlement forbidden there. It also controlled movement, restricting Indigenous entry into English areas. The line did not hold. English patentees began claiming land north of the York in the late 1640s, and the colonial government repeatedly legislated and failed to prevent encroachment.

The treaty did not end the Powhatan peoples. Communities such as the Pamunkey and Mattaponi, along with the Chickahominy, Nansemond, Rappahannock, and others, continued to occupy their towns and farm, though under shrinking territorial control and with a tributary status that exposed them to legal restrictions. They retained leadership, language, ceremonies, and diplomatic traditions. Later chapters and the treaty of 1677 (Section 48.39) show how the Pamunkey in particular used English law to protect their land. Today the Pamunkey and Mattaponi retain reservations whose origin lies in this period, and several Virginia Indigenous nations hold state and federal recognition, a story of continuity examined in Section 48.47.

The three Anglo-Powhatan conflicts were therefore not interchangeable. In 1609–1614 the English were weak and dependent on Indigenous food, and the war was largely a contest of blockade, raiding, and coerced exchange that ended in negotiated peace. In 1622–1632 the English were more numerous, tobacco plantations were expanding, and the attack was a calculated Indigenous attempt to halt expansion that provoked a destructive English campaign. In 1644–1646 the English population and military capacity had so increased that even a major surprise attack could not alter the balance, and the outcome was a treaty that imposed tributary status. The balance of power shifted from English dependence toward English dominance, even as Powhatan communities survived.

Between 1632 and 1644 Virginia's English population grew and moved north and west. Settlers occupied the lands along the York River, and in 1634 the colony authorized a palisade across the peninsula from the York to the James, with settlement at Middle Plantation, a line that was meant to protect colonists but also asserted English claims to the territory below it. Land patents multiplied, and newcomers pressed Indigenous towns to give up fields and fishing places. Opechancanough, who must have been very old by 1644, judged that a second blow might still compel the English to retreat, a calculation that some historians connect to the political crisis in England, though the evidence for such a link is thin.

The treaty of 1646 and its aftermath shaped Powhatan life for generations. Necotowance agreed that his people would hold their lands from the Crown and pay tribute, and the English promised that colonists would not settle north of the York River without permission, a promise soon broken. In the 1650s Virginia's assembly passed laws on the purchase of Indigenous land and the treatment of Indigenous people in English settlements, though enforcement was weak. In 1656 Totopotomoy, the Pamunkey leader, died fighting alongside the English against a hostile Indigenous force at the Battle of Bloody Run, a reminder that Powhatan nations continued to pursue their own alliances and obligations under the new order. His widow Cockacoeske, who later emerged as the leading Pamunkey figure, is central to the events of 1676–1677.

Wampanoag Diplomacy, Plymouth, and the Expansion of New England

Sections 48.13–48.17 · 1610s–1630s
Section 48.13

The Wampanoag World before Plymouth

The Wampanoag peoples occupied a long, interconnected territory across southeastern Massachusetts, eastern Rhode Island, Cape Cod, and the islands of Martha's Vineyard and Nantucket. They were not a single political unit but a set of communities, each led by a sachem, linked by language, kinship, and alliance. The Pokanoket, whose principal seat was at Sowams near Narragansett Bay, were among the most powerful, and Ousamequin was their leading sachem. Other communities, such as the Nauset on outer Cape Cod, the Patuxet on Plymouth Bay, the Pocasset, and the Sakonnet, retained their own leaders and, at times, divergent interests.

Wampanoag life followed a seasonal rhythm. In spring and summer, communities planted maize, beans, and squash in fields near the coast, fished for herring, alewives, and other species, and gathered shellfish. In fall and winter many moved inland to hunting camps and sheltered river valleys. Shell beads, especially wampum, were made along the coast and used in diplomacy, gift exchange, and ceremonial display. Women were central agricultural producers and held significant authority in some communities, and women sachems, called "sunksquaws," are recorded in several Wampanoag and neighboring polities.

The region had been in contact with Europeans for a decade or more before Plymouth. Fishing vessels, traders, and explorers visited, and some Wampanoag people had been kidnapped. In 1614 Thomas Hunt, an English captain, seized a number of Indigenous men from Patuxet and Nauset and carried them to Spain to sell; among them was Tisquantum, who eventually reached England and later returned to New England in 1619. The experience taught some Wampanoag leaders to be wary of English ships, and it also meant that a few Indigenous people understood English and the English world.

The most important change was demographic. Between about 1616 and 1619, an epidemic of uncertain identity struck coastal communities of southern New England with devastating effect, especially in the Massachusetts Bay region and Patuxet. English observers reported villages abandoned and bodies unburied. Historians continue to debate the disease, its exact mortality, and its geographic reach, but there is agreement that the loss was severe in some communities and that it altered the regional balance of power. The Pokanoket, hit by the epidemic and facing pressure from the Narragansett, who were less affected, were in a vulnerable position as Plymouth was established. Ousamequin's decisions about the English must be understood in this context.

Wampanoag familiarity with Europeans was greater than later legend suggests. In 1611 Epenow, a man from Martha's Vineyard, was seized by an English captain and taken to England, where he reportedly told of gold in his homeland to win a voyage back; he escaped when the ship returned in 1614 and rejoined his community. Thomas Dermer, who traveled the coast in 1619 with Tisquantum as interpreter, found Patuxet empty and the shore marked by abandoned dwellings. Each of these episodes taught coastal leaders that English visitors could be dangerous, and each left Wampanoag communities with an informal body of knowledge about English language, ships, and weapons. The Pokanoket also stood in a tributary or deferential relationship to the Narragansett in some matters, a relationship that the epidemic had made harder to resist and that the English alliance would later help Ousamequin renegotiate.

Section 48.14

Plymouth, 1620–1621: Indigenous Diplomacy and the Treaty of Mutual Protection

The English settlers who arrived at Cape Cod in November 1620 explored the coast, taking corn from Indigenous storage pits and disturbing graves, acts that alarmed Nauset communities and led to a brief skirmish. They settled in December at Patuxet, which they renamed Plymouth, on a cleared site whose residents had died or dispersed during the epidemic. During the first winter, roughly half of the colonists died, and the survivors remained fragile, with no surplus and no ally.

In March 1621 a man named Samoset, an Abenaki from the Pemaquid area in present-day Maine who had learned some English from fishermen, walked into the settlement and spoke to the colonists. He soon returned with Tisquantum, who spoke English and who, as a Patuxet survivor, knew both the land and the situation of the surviving Wampanoag communities. Tisquantum, together with Hobbamock, a Pokanoket man who served as a counselor and guide, became the English settlers' principal intermediaries. Their precise motives are difficult to establish, and both men pursued their own interests: Tisquantum seems to have sought to enhance his own standing among the English and Indigenous communities, while Hobbamock seems to have served Ousamequin's interests in observing the newcomers.

On or about March 22, 1621, Ousamequin came to Plymouth with a large party of men and negotiated an agreement with the English governor, John Carver. The terms, as recorded in Mourt's Relation (1622), provided that neither party would injure the other, that those who committed offenses would be handed over for punishment, that each would assist the other if attacked by a third party, and that visitors would come unarmed. The agreement thus created a mutual-defense alliance. For the English, it provided security, access to food and knowledge, and relations with the people of the region. For Ousamequin, it offered an ally against the Narragansett, who threatened Wampanoag independence, and a source of metal goods and weapons.

The treaty should be understood as a strategic decision rather than an uncomplicated expression of friendship. Ousamequin's authority extended over some Wampanoag communities but not all. Some leaders, such as Corbitant of the Pocasset, resisted the alliance. In the summer of 1621 Corbitant threatened Tisquantum, and the English responded by sending a force to intimidate him, an episode that demonstrated both the alliance's value to Ousamequin and its risks, since it drew the English into Indigenous disputes. Early in 1622, the Narragansett sachem Canonicus sent a bundle of arrows in a snakeskin to Plymouth, which the English interpreted as a challenge; they sent it back filled with powder and shot. The alliance with Plymouth thus placed the Pokanoket and the English on the same side of a regional rivalry.

The agreement of March 1621 was followed by visits that gave each side a closer look at the other. In July 1621 Edward Winslow and Stephen Hopkins traveled with Tisquantum to Sowams to carry gifts to Ousamequin, and Winslow's account describes the hospitality they received and the reserved, careful style of Wampanoag diplomacy. In 1623 Winslow returned when Ousamequin was gravely ill, and the Plymouth men treated him; Ousamequin, in gratitude, passed on information about a plot against the English, the information that led Plymouth's Myles Standish to the killings at Wessagusset. Tisquantum himself died of illness in late 1622 while traveling with Governor Bradford, and Hobbamock remained the English colony's principal Wampanoag intermediary. These relationships were practical, personal, and political at once, and they illustrate why the alliance endured even as the underlying imbalance of population grew.

Section 48.15

The 1621 Harvest Gathering and the Politics of Historical Memory

In the autumn of 1621, after the colonists' first harvest, the English held a celebration of the harvest. A brief description appears in a letter from Edward Winslow, written in December 1621 and included in Mourt's Relation. Winslow says that the colonists sent men to hunt fowl and that Ousamequin came with about ninety men, who stayed for three days, and that the Wampanoag brought deer. He also describes shooting and military exercises. Plymouth had only about fifty surviving settlers at the time, so the Wampanoag guests far outnumbered the English hosts. Bradford's later account mentions the harvest but not in detail.

The documentary evidence is thin, and several features of the later story are absent from it. Neither Winslow nor Bradford called the event a "thanksgiving," a term the English reserved for solemn religious observances of gratitude, often involving fasting and prayer rather than feasting. The assumption that the gathering was a feast of friendship between two peoples has been largely a later construction. The meeting may have been diplomatic and military in character, a demonstration of the alliance in which the Wampanoag brought food and the English displayed arms. The size of the Wampanoag party may reflect Ousamequin's intention to assess the English or to display Pokanoket strength.

The idea of a "First Thanksgiving" emerged much later. During the nineteenth century, and particularly after President Abraham Lincoln's proclamation of a national day of thanksgiving in 1863, the Plymouth gathering was drawn into a national story of founding and harmony. Illustrations, school lessons, and holiday traditions created an image of Pilgrims and Indians sharing a meal. The story left out the events that followed: the growth of English settlement, the disruption of Wampanoag communities, and the conflicts of later decades. Many Wampanoag and other Indigenous people regard the later holiday with ambivalence. Since 1970, the United American Indians of New England have held a National Day of Mourning on Thanksgiving Day in Plymouth, a protest with roots in the invitation to Wamsutta (Frank) James, a Wampanoag speaker, whose address critical of the Pilgrim narrative was suppressed that year.

For students, the episode illustrates how a small, ambiguous event can be turned into a national myth, and how Indigenous perspectives were excluded from that myth. It also underlines the value of reading the primary evidence closely: what the sources actually say differs substantially from what generations of Americans have been taught.

Wampanoag communities today preserve their own accounts of this history. The Mashpee Wampanoag Tribe and the Wampanoag Tribe of Gay Head (Aquinnah) hold knowledge of their ancestors' diplomacy, of the epidemic, and of the generations that followed, and they have worked with museums and schools to present the 1621 treaty as a decision by Wampanoag leaders rather than as a story about English survival. That work, and the language revival discussed in Chapter 123, has helped shift public understanding from the mythic harmony of the nineteenth-century holiday toward a history in which Indigenous political judgment is visible.

The Wampanoag–Plymouth alliance lasted for about fifty years, though not without strain. Wampanoag leaders cultivated the alliance, but they also watched as English numbers grew and as Plymouth purchased and claimed land on terms that gradually narrowed their independence.

Section 48.16

Massachusetts Bay and the Transformation of Indigenous Coastal Territory

The founding of Massachusetts Bay in 1630 transformed southern New England. John Winthrop and several hundred settlers arrived that year, and over the following decade thousands more followed, founding towns such as Boston, Salem, Charlestown, Dorchester, Roxbury, Watertown, and others. Unlike the early Plymouth and Virginia communities, these settlers migrated largely in family groups, with livestock and tools, and they intended to create permanent, self-sustaining towns. Within a few years the English population in the Bay region exceeded the surviving Indigenous population in the same area.

The Indigenous communities of the Massachusetts Bay region, among them the Massachusett, the Pawtucket (including the Naumkeag of Salem), and the Nipmuc of the interior, had suffered severely from the earlier epidemic. Sachems such as Chickatawbut of the Massachusett, Montowampate and Wonohaquaham of the Pawtucket, and Cutshamekin of the Neponset area sought relationships with the newcomers. Some invited English settlement as a source of protection against Narragansett or Mohawk pressure and as a source of trade goods. Another epidemic, of smallpox in 1633–1634, caused further mortality, including the death of several sachems, and weakened Indigenous capacity to resist. English leaders interpreted the disease as divine providence clearing the land for settlement, a view that shaped their claims to territory.

The colonists justified their occupation in several ways. Their charter, granted by the Crown, authorized settlement, and they argued that land not actively cultivated by Indigenous people was open to them. At the same time, Massachusetts Bay developed a practice of recognizing Indigenous use rights where land was actually planted and of acquiring land by purchase, partly in response to the critique by Roger Williams and others, partly to avoid conflict. Indigenous sachems sold or granted land, sometimes with the intent of gaining allies and protection, sometimes under pressure. Colonial courts recorded these transactions, and over time the colonial government exercised control over the process by requiring that purchases be authorized.

Competition over resources intensified. English towns needed meadow for hay, timber for building, and fishing grounds, and they relied on livestock that foraged freely and destroyed Indigenous cornfields. Trade became more organized, with wampum used by the English as currency in transactions with Indigenous suppliers of furs, and the supply of beaver in the coastal region declined. The relationships between English towns and Indigenous neighbors varied, and many Indigenous people continued to live within or beside the English towns, working as laborers, hunters, and guides, and trading baskets, game, and fish. But the pattern of expansion, and the growing numbers of colonists, steadily reduced the space in which Indigenous communities could maintain their independent way of life.

The sachems of the Bay region responded to the new settlement in different ways. Chickatawbut of the Massachusett sought a modus vivendi with Winthrop's colonists, and his brother and other leaders died in the smallpox epidemic of 1633–1634, leaving the Massachusett and Pawtucket with weakened leadership at the moment English numbers soared. In 1644, after the Pequot War and as the United Colonies took shape, several sachems of the Bay region signed articles of submission to Massachusetts, acknowledging English authority and agreeing to accept instruction in Christianity. These documents were drafted by the colonists, and they may have been regarded by the Indigenous signers as protective alliances, but they gave Massachusetts a legal basis for later claims of jurisdiction. The colony also began to appoint magistrates and officials charged with Indigenous affairs, among them Daniel Gookin, whose later writings are among the best sources on Indigenous communities in Massachusetts.

Section 48.17

Narragansett Diplomacy, Roger Williams, and the Founding of Rhode Island

The Narragansett were among the most populous and politically influential peoples of southern New England. Occupying lands around Narragansett Bay, they had been spared the worst of the 1616–1619 epidemic, and their leaders, the elderly sachem Canonicus and his nephew Miantonomo, held considerable regional influence, receiving tribute from neighboring communities. They viewed the English as potential trading partners and as a force to be balanced against rivals such as the Pequot and Wampanoag. The Narragansett were also skilled traders in wampum.

Roger Williams, a minister who had come to Massachusetts Bay in 1631, quarreled with colonial leaders over religious toleration and over the legitimacy of land taken without Indigenous consent. He argued that a royal charter alone could not confer title to land occupied by Indigenous people, a position that contributed to his banishment from Massachusetts in 1635. Williams went to Narragansett country, where he had already established relations with Canonicus and Miantonomo, learned the language, and in 1636 received land at the head of Narragansett Bay, where he founded Providence. His writing, notably A Key into the Language of America (1643), records Narragansett vocabulary and customs with respect and has become an important source on Narragansett life.

Williams's relationship with the Narragansett illustrates the complexity of colonial land arrangements. The Narragansett leaders granted him access for reasons of their own: the establishment of a friendly English settlement nearby could serve as a buffer, a source of goods, and a diplomatic link. Williams later described the transaction as a gift motivated by friendship, though he also provided gifts in return, and later documents treat the land as sold. The ambiguity illustrates the difficulty of separating negotiated land access from the gradual assertion of colonial territorial authority, a problem returned to in Section 48.23. Williams also served as a diplomatic intermediary, and in 1636 he is reported to have helped persuade the Narragansett to refrain from joining the Pequot against the English, an intervention with major consequences, discussed in Division IV.

Rhode Island grew as a refuge for dissenters, and several settlements were founded on land purchased from Narragansett leaders. Over time, however, settlers from these towns and from neighboring colonies pressed the Narragansett for land, and the colony's own political fragmentation left the Narragansett vulnerable to claims by Massachusetts, Connecticut, and Plymouth. The Narragansett leaders' alliance with Williams and with the English, which at first provided protection, eventually could not shield them from the pressures that would culminate in Metacom's War.

Narragansett leaders pursued a deliberate strategy of securing English recognition. In 1644, with Williams's help and facing pressure from Massachusetts and Connecticut, Narragansett sachems placed themselves under the protection of King Charles I, a step that English writers described as a submission and that Narragansett leaders may have regarded as a way to gain a powerful patron against the colonial confederation. Williams traveled to England in 1643–1644 to obtain a parliamentary patent for Providence Plantations, and that charter helped Rhode Island resist claims by its neighbors. Other Rhode Island towns, such as Portsmouth and Newport on Aquidneck Island, were founded on land purchased from Narragansett leaders in 1638, and Samuel Gorton's Shawomet purchase in 1643 led to a bitter dispute with Massachusetts that further complicated the legal status of Narragansett land. The fragmentation of English authority around Narragansett Bay gave the Narragansett room to maneuver, but it also created multiple English claimants to the same territory.

Sections 48.18–48.22 · 1630s–1640s
Section 48.18

The Pequot, Mohegan, and Narragansett Political Worlds

The Pequot occupied the lands of southeastern Connecticut along the Thames and Mystic rivers and exercised influence over neighboring communities, including peoples in the lower Connecticut valley and on Long Island. Their paramount sachem, Sassacus, led a polity that was strong in the 1620s and early 1630s, benefiting from its location at the center of the wampum trade and from tribute exacted from neighbors. Archaeological work has documented substantial Pequot settlements and evidence of extensive trade.

The Mohegan, who spoke a closely related language and had long been linked to the Pequot through kinship, followed a separate leader, Uncas, who was related to Sassacus by marriage and had at times been part of the Pequot polity. Uncas and Sassacus quarreled, and in the early 1630s Uncas broke from Sassacus, leading a following that became the Mohegan as a distinct political group. The Narragansett, to the east, had their own rivalries with the Pequot over tribute and trade. The three polities thus shared language and kinship but competed intensely.

Into this situation came the Dutch, who established a trading post, the House of Hope, on the Connecticut River in 1633, and the English, who in 1633–1636 began founding settlements in the Connecticut valley, including Windsor, Wethersfield, and Hartford, and at the river's mouth at Saybrook. The Pequot, who had traded with the Dutch and had at times sought English trade, now saw English settlements on the Connecticut River intruding into their sphere of influence. In some cases they had invited English trade, in part to counter Narragansett power, and in 1634 they sought an agreement with Massachusetts. They were therefore not simply opponents of the English but a power trying to manage a rapidly changing diplomatic environment.

Population and disease shaped the setting. The epidemic of 1633–1634 killed many people in the Connecticut valley, and the Pequot, like other peoples, were weakened. Competition for control of the wampum trade and tribute networks, combined with the English advance, put Pequot leaders in a difficult position. Their political system depended on maintaining tribute relationships and trade monopolies, and the intrusion of English settlers and traders threatened both.

The Pequot polity was supported by trade as well as tribute. Wampum, made from the shells of the Sound, was in strong demand among both Indigenous nations and the Dutch and English, and the Pequot were well placed to supply it. Their control of routes along the Connecticut River and Long Island Sound allowed them to exchange wampum for furs from the interior and for European goods. The Mashantucket Pequot Museum and the Pequot War archaeological project have documented fortified towns and extensive evidence of this trade. The political structure of the Pequot, like that of other southern New England peoples, depended on the sachem's ability to hold the loyalty of subordinate leaders. The break between Sassacus and Uncas, and the pressure on Pequot tributaries from the Narragansett and the English, were therefore as much threats to the foundation of Pequot power as the colonists' military strength.

Section 48.19

The Road to the Pequot War, 1634–1636

The proximate causes of the Pequot War lay in a series of killings and diplomatic failures. In 1634 a trading party under Captain John Stone, an English trader with a poor reputation, was killed on the Connecticut River by a group of Indigenous people associated with the Western Niantic and perhaps the Pequot. Massachusetts demanded that the Pequot surrender the killers. Pequot leaders offered a treaty in late 1634, in which they reportedly agreed to hand over some offenders, to pay compensation in wampum and furs, and to allow English settlement and trade. The terms favored the English, and the Pequot did not fully carry them out, partly because the killers were not within Pequot authority and partly because Pequot leaders were unwilling to appear subordinate.

In July 1636 John Oldham, a trader, was killed near Block Island, an act associated with Indigenous people connected to the Narragansett. Massachusetts responded by sending an expedition under John Endecott to Block Island, where it burned wigwams and cornfields, and then on to the Pequot country, where it demanded the surrender of the murderers of Stone and Oldham, and attacked Pequot villages and crops. The Pequot, who had not been responsible for Oldham's death, responded by besieging the English fort at Saybrook and by killing English settlers who ventured outside.

Both sides courted allies. The Pequot sought alliance with the Narragansett, appealing to a shared interest in resisting English expansion. The English, with the assistance of Roger Williams, worked to prevent such an alliance, and Miantonomo agreed to ally with Massachusetts against the Pequot. The Narragansett's decision reflected their own long-term rivalry with the Pequot as well as their desire to maintain good relations with the English and to weaken a competitor. Uncas and the Mohegan also chose the English side. These decisions illustrate the nature of Indigenous diplomacy: leaders acted in light of their own interests, and the conflicts among Indigenous polities were as significant as the English threat.

In April 1637 Pequot warriors attacked Wethersfield, killing about nine settlers and capturing two young women. The attack appeared to confirm English fears and persuaded Connecticut to declare an offensive war. By this point, the events had moved from a series of disputed killings to a regional war whose outcome would depend on Indigenous alliances and English willingness to use extreme violence.

The negotiations of 1634 are an important example of Pequot diplomacy. Winthrop recorded that Pequot envoys came to Boston and offered a settlement that included compensation in wampum and furs, the surrender of those responsible for Stone's death, and access to land for English settlement, in return for peace and trade. Whether the Pequot leaders believed they could control all the killers, or whether they were seeking a way to avoid a quarrel with a rising power, the terms strained their position at home. The Dutch, who had their own trade at the House of Hope, added further complications. In 1633 they had seized the Pequot grand sachem Tatobem, Sassacus's father, and he was killed after a ransom was paid, an event that left deep Pequot resentment and that English accounts of the crisis often leave out.

Winter 1636–1637 saw the Pequot besiege Fort Saybrook, where Lion Gardiner, the English commander, warned that Endecott's attack would provoke a war the colonists were not prepared to fight. His warnings were borne out when Pequot warriors killed English who left the fort and kept the garrison under siege for months. English colonists in Connecticut, who had been established for only a few years, felt exposed, and their leaders concluded that they could not remain safe unless the Pequot were broken. In this way a series of killings, a punitive expedition, and the dread of a general rising combined to produce a decision for offensive war.

Section 48.20

The Pequot War, 1636–1637, and the Mystic Massacre

In May 1637 a force of about ninety Connecticut colonists under Captain John Mason, together with allied Mohegan, Narragansett, and Niantic warriors, whose numbers are often given as several dozen to several hundred in different accounts, sailed to the Pequot homeland. Rather than attack Sassacus's principal fortified town, Mason decided to attack a smaller palisaded village on the Mystic River, which held many noncombatants, including women, children, and the elderly, since many warriors were away. On the morning of May 26, 1637, English and allied forces approached the village, and Mason and his men entered, then set fire to the wigwams. As the fire spread, the English and their allies surrounded the palisade and killed those who attempted to flee.

The scale of the killing is uncertain. English accounts, including those of Mason and John Underhill, gave figures ranging from roughly four hundred to seven hundred dead. Modern historians and archaeologists continue to refine these estimates, taking into account the likely population of the village, the English accounts, and the archaeological record at the Mystic site; they agree that hundreds of people were killed, the great majority of them noncombatants. Mason and Underhill treated the act as a victory and a providential triumph, and the Plymouth governor William Bradford later described the burning in terms that celebrated God's judgment. Underhill reports that some Indigenous allies protested the scale of killing, but his narrative cannot establish a uniform response among Mohegan, Narragansett, and Niantic participants. Their military aims and experiences differed, and his testimony should not be made into a universal rule about Indigenous warfare.

After Mystic, the war was effectively decided. Pequot survivors dispersed, some seeking refuge among neighboring peoples, and English forces pursued them. A fight in a swamp near present-day Fairfield in July 1637 destroyed much of the remaining Pequot resistance. Sassacus fled westward with a few followers and was killed by Mohawk, who sent his scalp to the English, according to contemporary accounts. Captives were divided; some men were executed, and many women and children were enslaved, distributed among the colonists and allies, or shipped to the West Indies, where some were sold.

The war introduced an English way of warfare that Indigenous observers found extreme. It involved targeting villages, burning homes and food supplies, and killing noncombatants, as well as enslaving survivors. Scholars have debated whether the Mystic attack should be characterized as a massacre, a deliberate act of terror, or a military operation within the norms of its time. Most historians use the term massacre for the killing of noncombatants at Mystic, and the Mashantucket Pequot Tribal Nation emphasizes the event in its history. The attack's impact extended beyond the Pequot; it demonstrated to all Indigenous peoples of the region what the English were willing to do.

The composition of the force that reached Mystic reflected the alliance politics described above. Mason led Connecticut volunteers, and Underhill commanded a Massachusetts contingent that had joined them; Uncas's Mohegan contributed warriors who knew the country, and Narragansett and Niantic fighters, whose numbers the English accounts describe in the hundreds, joined or followed. The Indigenous allies acted from their own motives, including a long-standing enmity with Sassacus and the hope of acquiring Pequot tribute and captives. They also provided scouting, which allowed the English to approach the village unseen. Underhill's account suggests that some allies objected to the scale of the killing, a view not shared in the colonial narratives, and that others pursued survivors with the English; the sources do not allow all of the allies to be treated alike.

Archaeologists who surveyed the battlefield and the Mystic fort site have recovered evidence of the palisade and of the fire, supporting the broad outline of the English narratives while adding information about Pequot material life. They have also helped to identify the positions of the English and allied forces. The Mashantucket Pequot Tribal Nation, whose research and museum work are central to this scholarship, has emphasized that the village sheltered noncombatants and that the slaughter was an attack on a community. After Mystic, the English and allies pursued the remaining Pequot, and a fight near present-day Fairfield in July 1637 at a swamp in which many refugees had gathered effectively ended organized resistance.

Section 48.21

The Treaty of Hartford, 1638, and Pequot Survival

In September 1638 representatives of Connecticut, Massachusetts, the Narragansett, and the Mohegan met at Hartford to settle the aftermath of the war. The resulting agreement, often called the Treaty of Hartford, declared the Pequot dissolved as a political entity. Its provisions included a prohibition on the use of the name "Pequot," a prohibition on living in their former territory, and the distribution of surviving Pequot captives among the Mohegan, the Narragansett, and the Niantic, who were to receive them as dependents and pay tribute to the English. In effect, the English attempted to eliminate the Pequot as a distinct political community by absorbing its survivors into others and prohibiting the name.

The effort failed. Pequot survivors resisted, and many continued to identify as Pequot despite the prohibition. Some lived with the Mohegan and Narragansett under conditions that varied from kinship to subordination, and others remained in the region, living quietly. Within a few decades the colony of Connecticut recognized separate Pequot communities and set aside land for them. In 1666 Connecticut set aside land at Mashantucket for Pequot people, which later became the basis for the Mashantucket Pequot reservation. Pequot communities persisted through the colonial period, the American Revolution, and the nineteenth century, and the Mashantucket Pequot received federal recognition in 1983, and the Eastern Pequot and others continue their own paths to recognition. The Mohegan Tribe received federal recognition in 1994.

This section illustrates the central distinction of Division IV: the defeat of a political-military coalition is not the same as the disappearance of a people. The Treaty of Hartford attempted a kind of political erasure by law, an important example of the English use of legal measures to restructure Indigenous societies. Its failure shows the resilience of Indigenous identity and kinship ties, as well as the limits of colonial power over communities that maintained their own social networks and memory.

The fate of Pequot captives illustrates the commercial side of the war. Some were distributed to English households as servants, and others were shipped to the West Indies. Winthrop's journal records that the ship carrying Pequot captives to Providence Island returned with goods and enslaved Africans, one of the earliest documented links between the enslavement of Indigenous people in New England and the Atlantic slave trade. The treaty's provisions for distributing captives among the Mohegan, Narragansett, and Niantic appear to have set figures of roughly eighty, eighty, and twenty individuals, according to later accounts, though the allocation was never fully carried out.

In the following decades Pequot survivors reasserted themselves. By the early 1650s a Pequot leader, Robin Cassacinamon, had led a group out from under Mohegan control to settle at Nameag near the Thames River, and in 1655 the United Colonies' commissioners ordered that Pequot people be allowed to live under their own leaders, with land set aside for them. Connecticut later established the Mashantucket reservation, in 1666. These steps were gained by Pequot persistence in the face of the treaty's ban on their name, and they show that the Hartford settlement could not survive contact with Pequot kinship and political determination.

Section 48.22

Mohegan–Narragansett Rivalry and Miantonomo's Death, 1643

After the Pequot defeat, Uncas's Mohegan and the Narragansett emerged as the dominant Indigenous powers of southern New England, and their rivalry intensified. Both competed for Pequot survivors, tribute, and influence, and each sought to use the English as a counterweight. English colonists, who had promised to protect their allies, found themselves repeatedly drawn into disputes. Miantonomo, who had cooperated with the English during the Pequot War, grew uneasy about their expansion. In 1642, according to an English account, he tried to persuade other Indigenous leaders on Long Island to unite against the English, arguing that failure to do so would result in the loss of their lands. English officials seized on reports of this plan as evidence of conspiracy.

In 1643 the Narragansett and Mohegan went to war. Miantonomo was captured by Uncas in an engagement and brought to Hartford, where the commissioners of the newly formed United Colonies of New England, a confederation of Massachusetts, Plymouth, Connecticut, and New Haven formed that year, judged the matter. They declined to decide the case themselves but concluded that Miantonomo should be put to death, and they authorized Uncas to carry out the killing outside English jurisdiction. Uncas executed him, and the English observed. The decision reflected English calculations that Miantonomo was dangerous and that Uncas was a reliable ally.

The episode requires care. It is incorrect to describe the Mohegan or Narragansett leaders as mere instruments of English interests. Uncas pursued his own ambitions and used English backing to consolidate Mohegan power, and Miantonomo had pursued his own strategy of cooperation and then caution. At the same time, English colonial intervention changed the balance: the United Colonies' endorsement of Miantonomo's death established a precedent of English participation in Indigenous succession and diplomacy. The Narragansett did not forget the killing, and the resentment contributed to distrust of the English in the decades that followed.

Miantonomo's appeal to Long Island leaders in 1642, as reported by English writers, urged unity against the colonists on the ground that the English would consume Indigenous land unless Indigenous nations acted together. Whether or not the report preserves his words, it records an idea that would recur, that a pan-Indigenous resistance was the only effective answer to English numbers. English authorities treated the report as proof of conspiracy and were prepared to believe the worst of the Narragansett leader. When Miantonomo was captured in 1643 at the battle of the Great Plain, Uncas turned him over to Connecticut, and the commissioners of the new confederation agreed that he should die; Uncas executed him near present-day Norwich. English officials had made use of a Mohegan ally to remove a rival, and the Narragansett, led thereafter by Canonicus's heirs and, later, by Pessicus and Canonchet, remembered the killing for generations. Meanwhile Ninigret, sachem of the Niantic, maintained an independent line and a strong position on the coast, an indication that Indigenous politics in the region did not reduce to a simple English versus Indigenous divide.

Section 48.23

Land Purchases, Colonial Deeds, and Conflicting Understandings of Territory

The principal legal instrument by which English colonists acquired Indigenous land was the deed. Deeds recorded the transfer of a described tract from named Indigenous grantors to English grantees in return for a stated payment, typically goods such as cloth, tools, kettles, wampum, or coats. Colonial governments soon regulated these transactions. Massachusetts, Plymouth, and Connecticut required, with varying consistency, that purchases receive official approval, partly to prevent fraud and disputes, and partly to ensure that the colony rather than private individuals controlled the process. In Virginia, by contrast, expansion proceeded largely by patents issued by the governor and council, with Indigenous consent occasionally sought but not required.

English officials could regard a deed as a permanent and complete alienation of land: the grantors' rights were extinguished, and the grantee could exclude anyone, including the former owners. Many Indigenous participants understood the transaction differently. In their legal traditions, a grant could convey rights to settle, plant, or build while preserving the grantors' rights to hunt, fish, gather, and travel. Where deeds contained reservation clauses, such as the retention of fishing weirs or planting grounds, they recorded these expectations. Disputes arose when colonists later denied the reservations or treated the whole land as theirs. The question was not only one of vocabulary. Indigenous leaders also sold land because they needed trade goods, protection, or allies, because rival sachems claimed overlapping authority, or because pressure from settlers made refusal costly.

Indigenous sellers could also be divided among themselves. Several people might claim the right to sell the same land, and English buyers sometimes obtained multiple deeds for a single tract to guard against challenges. Sachems who sold too freely could lose standing among their people, while communities who lost land often protested. Over time, Indigenous leaders began to bring complaints to colonial courts and governors, using English legal channels to challenge sales and encroachments, a strategy that appears in records from Massachusetts, Connecticut, Rhode Island, and Virginia. These petitions are important evidence that Indigenous communities understood and used English law for their own protection, even when the outcomes were rarely favorable.

The practice of reserving rights appears clearly in deeds from Long Island, where Montaukett and other Algonquian leaders in the mid-seventeenth century sold lands to English towns while keeping rights to fish, whale, and gather on the shore. Such clauses suggest that Indigenous parties thought of a sale as a shared arrangement in which they kept important uses. Colonial legislatures responded by tightening the rules: Plymouth and Massachusetts by the 1640s required that purchases be licensed by the general court, a rule intended to keep private buyers from creating disputes that might lead to war. The rule also gave colonial governments control over which land passed to English hands and on what terms, converting what had been a negotiation between communities into a matter of colonial law. Indigenous sellers who protested later that they had not understood the terms found that courts were reluctant to disturb recorded deeds, even when signatories claimed that they had not authorized the sale or that the boundaries described were not those they had shown.

Historical Vocabulary
Deed, Title, Reservation, and Jurisdiction

Deed is a written legal instrument recording the transfer of land. In colonial New England, deeds with Indigenous grantors were typically written by English scribes and signed with marks or pictographs by the Indigenous parties.

Title refers to legally recognized ownership. English law treated title as exclusive and, in principle, permanent, which differed from Algonquian systems in which rights to land could be layered and conditional.

Reservation has two related meanings in this chapter. It can refer to rights reserved by Indigenous sellers within a deed, such as continued fishing or planting, and to land set aside by a colony for a particular Indigenous community, as in the 1646 Virginia treaty, the 1677 Articles of Peace, and the Connecticut lands set aside for Pequot and Mohegan people. A reservation in the second sense was a legal recognition of Indigenous rights, but also a limitation on the territory that colonial authorities were willing to leave to Indigenous communities.

Jurisdiction is the authority to make and enforce law. Conflicts over jurisdiction concerned who could judge disputes involving Indigenous people, and who had authority over land and communities.

Potential misunderstanding: Students should avoid assuming that the presence of a deed means that the Indigenous signers understood and accepted permanent transfer. They should equally avoid assuming that Indigenous signers were deceived. Evidence of understanding, strategy, and coercion varies from case to case.

Section 48.24

Fences, Livestock, Fields, and the Material Expansion of Settlement

While deeds and treaties set the legal terms of land transfer, the daily expansion of English settlement was carried out by farmers, herdsmen, and woodcutters. English agriculture depended on livestock: cattle, pigs, sheep, and horses, which provided meat, milk, hides, and labor. Indigenous communities of the Atlantic coast had no comparable domesticated grazing animals, and their agricultural system, centered on unfenced fields of maize, beans, and squash, was highly vulnerable to livestock that wandered into crops. Pigs, which often roamed freely in woods and shorelines, also dug up shellfish beds and ate acorns and nuts important to Indigenous diets.

Colonial law placed the burden of fencing on those who planted crops, so that English farmers were obliged to fence their cornfields to keep out cattle, while Indigenous farmers, who did not fence, bore the damage when livestock invaded. Indigenous communities protested repeatedly, and in some cases English officials responded by requiring settlers to pay damages or to fence Indigenous fields, but enforcement was inconsistent. Narragansett and Wampanoag leaders complained about trespassing animals, and by the 1660s and 1670s, complaints about livestock were among the most common grievances in colonial records. These complaints were not trivial. A destroyed cornfield could mean hunger, and a lost shellfish bed meant the loss of a seasonal staple.

Other aspects of English settlement transformed the landscape. Towns required timber for houses, fences, and fuel, and clearing forests removed hunting habitats and plant resources. Milldams on rivers blocked fish runs, reducing the supply of herring, alewives, and salmon that Indigenous communities depended on. Expanding fields and meadows replaced coastal and riverside planting grounds that Indigenous communities had used for generations. Trade also changed, as the decline of beaver in coastal areas shifted Indigenous economies toward wampum production, wage labor, and sale of crafts, and as English debt arrangements drew Indigenous people into obligations.

These disputes should not be interpreted merely as misunderstandings of property law. The expansion of English settlement was a structural process driven by population growth, agricultural methods, and economic incentives. Even where individual English leaders sought fairness, the material demands of their communities ensured continued pressure. This is the central point of Division V: many conflicts between Indigenous peoples and the English emerged from the continuing structural expansion of settlement, even when formal peace treaties existed.

The damage caused by livestock produced a continuing flow of complaints and, at times, retaliation. Indigenous farmers killed pigs and cattle that destroyed their corn, and English courts treated such killings as theft or property destruction, fining or punishing the Indigenous offenders. Colonial governments occasionally ordered settlers to fence Indigenous fields or to compensate for damage, as Massachusetts did in some cases, but the enforcement rested on local officials who often sided with their neighbors. Some Indigenous communities adapted by acquiring livestock themselves or by moving their fields to places that settlers did not want, and in the praying towns residents built fences and kept animals on the English model. Others left, abandoning hunting grounds and planting grounds that settlers had made unusable. By the 1670s, in towns such as Rehoboth, Swansea, and Dartmouth, where Wampanoag fields lay beside English meadows, the daily friction between cattle and crops had become one of the most irritating features of life on the frontier, and Metacom's complaints to Plymouth officials included the damage done by English livestock.

Everyday Life and Social History

Indigenous Farming and Colonial Livestock

In an Algonquian town, the agricultural year followed the work of women, who planted maize in hills with beans growing along the stalks and squash covering the ground, and who weeded, harvested, dried, and stored the crop. Fields near the town were tended daily, and a family's winter food supply might depend on a few acres of well-tended corn. When a colonist's pigs rooted through a cornfield, or a cow trampled a field before harvest, the loss fell on women who had managed the planting and on the households that depended on the crop.

English farmers, for their part, were establishing their own households and meadows, and they worried about fencing costs, grazing land, and winter fodder. Their households depended on livestock, and they regarded Indigenous complaints as burdensome. The records, produced by English clerks, tend to preserve the English accounting of damages and costs, while Indigenous experiences must often be inferred from petitions, complaints brought by sachems, and later oral histories. These ordinary conflicts, repeated across hundreds of farms and fields, helped build the tension that surfaced in wars.

Section 48.25

Colonial Courts, Legal Jurisdiction, and Indigenous Political Authority

English colonies claimed legal authority over the people living within their charter limits, and they gradually extended that claim to Indigenous communities. In New England, colonial governments made agreements with sachems in which the Indigenous leaders acknowledged English jurisdiction in certain matters, commonly in cases involving offenses against English persons or property. Massachusetts, for example, obtained submissions from several Indigenous leaders in the 1640s, in which sachems agreed to follow English law and to accept Christian instruction, in exchange for English protection. English authorities regarded these as acts of submission, while Indigenous leaders may have understood them as diplomatic arrangements, alliances, or acknowledgments of a protective relationship.

Colonial courts heard cases involving Indigenous people, including criminal charges, debt, land disputes, and civil suits. Indigenous litigants appeared as plaintiffs as well as defendants, and some used the courts effectively to recover debts, to protest land fraud, and to seek redress. At the same time, colonial law imposed punishments that Indigenous communities often found alien or unjust, including fines, corporal punishment, and sale into servitude for debts. Disputes arose over who should judge crimes committed by an Indigenous person against an Englishman, and vice versa. Indigenous leaders insisted on handling internal disputes themselves and sometimes refused to surrender their people to English courts.

Virginia and New England followed different paths. In Virginia, after 1646 Indigenous communities were treated as tributary peoples under the Crown, and colonial legislation controlled where they could live and travel, while the Pamunkey and other nations retained internal governance. Governors and assemblies negotiated with Indigenous leaders and sometimes protected them, though the assembly also legislated against them. In New England, the colonies exercised jurisdiction through their courts and, after the 1640s, through the structure of praying towns, which brought Indigenous communities under English-style civil governance. The United Colonies of New England served as a forum for settling disputes with Indigenous leaders. These differences should not be exaggerated, and in practice both regions relied on a mixture of negotiated agreements and assertive claims of English sovereignty.

Indigenous resistance to imposed courts took many forms: negotiated exceptions, appeals to the Crown or to colonial governors, refusal to appear, withdrawal, and, at times, violence. The tension over jurisdiction was an important element in the origin of Metacom's War, in which a dispute over the justice applied to Wampanoag men became a focal point (Section 48.30).

Indigenous leaders learned to use English legal procedure for their own ends. Records from Plymouth, Massachusetts, Connecticut, and Rhode Island show sachems and ordinary Indigenous people appearing as plaintiffs to recover debts, to contest land sales, and to seek compensation for crop damage. In Massachusetts, the praying towns had their own officials who heard minor cases under English-style rules, an arrangement that gave Indigenous communities a measure of self-government within a framework imposed by the colony. These procedures were limited and subject to appeal to colonial courts, and Indigenous defendants often faced harsher punishment than English offenders. Still, the willingness of Indigenous people to take disputes into English courts indicates that they regarded the law as one of several tools, alongside diplomacy and force, that might protect them.

Section 48.26

Missionary Expansion, John Eliot, and the Praying Towns

Puritan leaders in Massachusetts had stated in their charter and seal that they intended to bring Christianity to Indigenous people, but systematic missionary work began only in the 1640s. John Eliot, minister at Roxbury, learned the Massachusett language with the help of Indigenous interpreters, including Cockenoe and a young man called John Sassamon, and began preaching to Indigenous people in October 1646 at Nonantum, near present-day Newton, where the sachem Waban and his community listened. Eliot's approach was to preach in the Indigenous language, to answer questions about Christianity, and to encourage Indigenous people to adopt English-style settled towns and civil government.

The result was a system of "praying towns," communities of Indigenous people who professed Christianity and followed codes of conduct designed by Eliot and colonial authorities. Natick, founded in 1651, was the first and became a model: Indigenous residents built houses, laid out streets, farmed fenced fields, and adopted a form of local government with rulers and judges chosen from within the community. By 1674, Daniel Gookin, the colony's superintendent of Indians, described fourteen praying towns in Massachusetts, with over a thousand residents, though exact numbers vary. Missionary efforts in other places, notably Martha's Vineyard under Thomas Mayhew Jr. and later his family, produced Indigenous Christian communities that retained greater autonomy. Funding came from the Society for the Propagation of the Gospel in New England, established by Parliament in 1649.

Why did Indigenous people take part? Motives varied, and sources often reflect the missionaries' accounts more than those of the converts. Some were drawn by the religious message, particularly after epidemics that left them seeking new sources of spiritual power. Others sought protection of land, political support, access to English goods, education, and a refuge from colonial pressures. In the weakened communities of the Massachusetts Bay region, the praying towns offered a means of preserving land and community in a landscape of English settlement. The Indigenous Christians who joined the towns were not simply passive; many became teachers, preachers, and leaders, and they adapted Christianity to their own concerns.

The costs were substantial. Eliot and colonial officials expected converts to abandon customary practices, including some religious ceremonies, hairstyles, dress, and marriage and kinship arrangements. The towns were intended to bring Indigenous people into English-style agriculture and civil order, and they were located near English settlements, subject to colonial oversight. Many Indigenous people refused to join, and traditional leaders often resisted missionary pressure. During Metacom's War, as Section 48.33 shows, praying Indians faced suspicion from colonists and were subjected to removal and confinement, exposing the limits of English acceptance of Indigenous Christians. Chapter 54 returns to Native Christianity across the continent.

Missionary work on Martha's Vineyard followed a different course from Eliot's and illustrates the range of Indigenous responses. Thomas Mayhew Jr. began preaching to Wampanoag communities in the 1640s, and his first convert, Hiacoomes, became a teacher and preacher who helped spread Christianity among his neighbors. Mayhew worked with local sachems rather than against them, and the island's Indigenous Christians developed their own congregations and leaders. On the mainland, Richard Bourne's mission to Mashpee on Cape Cod in the 1660s established another Wampanoag Christian community that retained land and identity into the modern era. The Harvard Indian College, built in the 1650s, trained a small number of Indigenous students, among them Caleb Cheeshahteaumuck, a Wampanoag from Martha's Vineyard who graduated in 1665. These cases show that Christianity became a resource for Indigenous communities that chose it, and not only an instrument of colonial control.

Section 48.27

Indigenous Literacy, Translation, and the Massachusett Bible of 1663

Literacy was central to the mission, and it became one of the most remarkable cultural developments of the century. Eliot and his Indigenous collaborators devised a written form of the Massachusett language, a dialect of Algonquian also called Natick, using the Roman alphabet. Indigenous speakers taught Eliot the language, and many Indigenous people learned to read and write in it. Eliot produced catechisms, primers, and sermons, and he translated the Bible. In 1661 the New Testament was printed at Cambridge, Massachusetts, and in 1663 the complete Bible, called Mamusse Wunneetupanatamwe Up-Biblum God, appeared. It was the first complete Bible printed in British North America, and it was printed in an Indigenous language.

The project depended on Indigenous participation. Cockenoe, who had worked as an interpreter, helped Eliot at an early stage. John Sassamon, a Massachusett man educated among the English, and, according to some accounts, briefly at Harvard, served as a translator and later as a court counselor to Metacom. James Printer (Wowaus), a Nipmuc man who had learned the printing trade, worked on the press at Cambridge and helped print the Bible; he later experienced the war from both sides. Job Nesutan, another Indigenous scholar, also contributed. The printing was done by Samuel Green and Marmaduke Johnson, English printers. The Bible's publication thus represents a collaboration in which Indigenous translators, printers, and teachers played essential roles.

Christianity and literacy meant different things to different people. Missionaries understood the Bible as a means of conversion and civilization. For Indigenous readers and writers, literacy provided a tool for administration, record-keeping, correspondence, and legal action, and Indigenous Christians produced deeds, petitions, wills, and church records in Massachusett over the next century. Indigenous churches, such as those on Martha's Vineyard, developed their own leaders and practices. Literacy allowed Indigenous communities to represent themselves in English legal proceedings and to preserve their language and history in written form. Modern descendants have used these documents in language reclamation, as in the Wôpanâak Language Reclamation Project, discussed in Chapter 123.

The written Massachusett that emerged from the Bible project continued in use long after Eliot's death. Indigenous scribes wrote deeds, wills, petitions, and church records in the language, and Eliot's grammar of Massachusett, published in 1666, assisted students of the language, though it was based on Indigenous speakers' knowledge. James Printer, who returned to printing after the war, helped produce a second edition of the Bible in the 1680s. The corpus of Indigenous-authored documents, which scholars have increasingly studied, provides evidence of Indigenous thought and administration that is largely absent for other nations of the period. Modern language revitalization draws directly on this record, which is among the most remarkable results of the encounter.

Section 48.28

Labor, Servitude, Enslavement, and Colonial Economic Power

Indigenous people in the English Atlantic colonies were drawn into labor relationships in several forms. Many worked voluntarily for wages, hunting, fishing, harvesting, building, or serving as guides and interpreters. Others were drawn into debt, a powerful instrument because English traders extended goods on credit and colonial courts enforced repayment, sometimes by selling the debtor's labor. In New England, laws permitted Indigenous people convicted of crimes or unable to pay fines or debts to be bound to service for a term of years. Children were sometimes placed in English households as servants, an arrangement some Indigenous parents accepted and others resisted.

Enslavement also occurred. After the Pequot War, English authorities distributed Pequot captives as servants or slaves and shipped some to the West Indies, among them a group sent from Salem in 1637. The practice was repeated after Metacom's War, when hundreds of Indigenous captives were sold abroad or bound into service. In Virginia, laws of the 1640s and later regulated Indigenous servants and, by the later seventeenth century, treated some Indigenous captives as enslaved. These practices coexisted with the growth of African slavery, which was becoming entrenched in Virginia and the West Indies, and Indigenous and African captives were sometimes held in similar conditions. Chapter 53 offers the comparative history of Indigenous captivity and slavery across the continent, and the present chapter documents the specific colonial institutions.

Labor relations gave colonial economic power a personal dimension. English households relied on Indigenous workers, and Indigenous communities near English towns depended on employment, selling goods, and credit. At the same time, labor relations were a means of control, a way to bind Indigenous people to the colonial economy and to reduce their capacity to resist. Indigenous people responded with a range of strategies: negotiating wages, leaving service, running away, petitioning courts, and, in some cases, using labor as a bridge to community life.

The sale of captives after the wars made enslavement a visible feature of colonial commerce. Plymouth, Massachusetts, and Rhode Island sold Indigenous prisoners to merchants who shipped them to Caribbean islands, to Atlantic islands such as Bermuda, and to other markets, and surviving shipping and court records show the process, though not the precise numbers. Indigenous children were bound as servants for long terms, and Indigenous women and men were distributed among colonial households. In Virginia, laws of the later seventeenth century addressed the status of Indigenous people taken in war and the sale of Indigenous captives. These practices were widely known at the time and were criticized by some colonists, among them Gookin and Eliot, who protested the sale of Christian Indigenous captives, though without altering the policy. They remind students that the labor system of the English colonies drew on several sources of unfree labor, including coerced Indigenous people, whose enslavement was neither marginal nor accidental.

Metacom's War and the Crisis of New England, 1675–1678

Sections 48.29–48.35
Section 48.29

From Ousamequin to Wamsutta and Metacom: The Breakdown of Accommodation

By the 1660s the Wampanoag–Plymouth alliance had lasted for four decades, but the circumstances that had produced it had changed. Plymouth's population had grown, new towns such as Swansea and Dartmouth were being founded on or near Wampanoag lands, and English settlers and colonial officials increasingly acted as though the Wampanoag were subjects rather than allies. Ousamequin died around 1661, and leadership passed to his sons, Wamsutta, whom the English called Alexander, and then Metacom (also Pometacom or Philip).

In 1662 Plymouth officials summoned Wamsutta to explain rumors that he was plotting with the Narragansett and selling land to other buyers without Plymouth's permission. Wamsutta was taken to Duxbury, fell ill, and died shortly after being released. Wampanoag tradition and some contemporary reports attributed his death to ill treatment by the English, a belief that embittered Metacom, though the English asserted natural causes. Metacom thus came to leadership in a climate of suspicion. He renewed treaties with Plymouth, but he also sought to limit land sales and to resist English claims to authority over his people.

Tension rose through the 1660s and early 1670s. In 1671, Plymouth summoned Metacom and compelled him at Taunton to acknowledge English authority, to surrender firearms, and to agree to pay a fine. The disarmament demand was both a sign of English distrust and a means of weakening Wampanoag capacity. Metacom complied formally but retained many weapons and sought alliances with other Indigenous leaders. Plymouth leaders, in turn, regarded him as a threat, while Wampanoag people saw English power growing. Disputes over land, fences, livestock, and legal jurisdiction multiplied. The accommodation that had begun in 1621 had broken down in practice well before 1675.

Plymouth's expansion in the 1660s had made Wampanoag lands increasingly insecure. Towns such as Swansea and Dartmouth were founded on or near the territory that Metacom and other Wampanoag leaders regarded as their own, and Plymouth purchased land from Metacom and other sachems with increasing frequency, sometimes on terms that left the sellers bitter. Wamsutta had married Weetamoo, the leader of the Pocasset, uniting the Pokanoket and Pocasset in a way that added to Plymouth's suspicions. After Wamsutta's death, Metacom resisted summons to Plymouth, sought the mediation of Rhode Island, and tried to preserve the Wampanoag's independence from Plymouth's courts. In 1671 he was brought to Taunton, where Plymouth and Massachusetts representatives forced him to acknowledge that his people were subject to English authority and to surrender arms, and the Wampanoag leader was made to pay a fine. For the Wampanoag, the Taunton agreement was a demonstration that the old alliance had become a relationship of dependence, and the hardening of terms contributed to the climate in which a violent crisis could erupt.

Section 48.30

John Sassamon, Colonial Justice, and the Outbreak of War in 1675

The immediate sequence of events began with John Sassamon, an educated Christian Indigenous man who had worked as a translator and secretary for Metacom and who also served as a preacher at Namasket. In early 1675, Sassamon reportedly warned Plymouth authorities that Metacom was planning an attack. Shortly after, in January, Sassamon's body was found under the ice of Assawompset Pond. English authorities accused three Wampanoag men, one the son of a counselor to Metacom, of murder. A jury that included Indigenous members, a notable concession, convicted them in June 1675, and they were hanged. One of the three reportedly confessed before his execution, though the reliability of the trial and the evidence remains debated.

Wampanoag leaders regarded the trial as an assertion of English jurisdiction over Wampanoag people in a matter that, by their own law, should have been handled by their sachem. Within days of the executions, Wampanoag warriors began to raid English farms near Swansea, taking livestock and burning houses. By late June, violence had escalated, and Wampanoag fighters killed English settlers at Swansea. Plymouth and Massachusetts mobilized militia, and the conflict began.

John Sassamon's role requires careful treatment. He had been educated among the English, assisted Eliot as a translator, taught at Natick, and served Metacom as an interpreter and secretary, so that he occupied a position between two worlds that neither fully trusted. According to Plymouth's account, he warned the governor in early 1675 that Metacom was preparing for war, and he was found dead soon afterward. The Plymouth court tried three Wampanoag men, including a counselor of Metacom, before a jury that included Indigenous members, and convicted them largely on the testimony of a single Indigenous witness. They were hanged in June 1675, and a violent response from Wampanoag warriors followed within days. Wampanoag leaders regarded the trial as an exercise of jurisdiction that Plymouth had no right to assert over their people, and the executions brought the dispute over authority to a head.

The first weeks of the war, in late June 1675, saw Wampanoag attacks on Swansea and the surrounding farms, and the response of Plymouth, Massachusetts, and Connecticut forces. English leaders sent troops to Mount Hope, and the war spread as groups that had their own grievances joined the fighting. The speed with which a local legal dispute escalated into a regional war shows how little room for negotiation remained. Rhode Island, whose leaders had tried to mediate, chose to remain outside the colonial alliance, though its towns suffered heavily in 1676.

Historical Debate

Interpreting the Origins of Metacom's War

The question: Was Metacom's War the result of a planned Wampanoag conspiracy, a spontaneous outbreak provoked by a legal crisis, or the culmination of long-term structural pressures?

Interpretation A — A premeditated Wampanoag plan: Contemporary English writers, drawing on Sassamon's warning, described the war as the result of a conspiracy by Metacom. Few modern historians accept this account in full, because evidence for a coordinated pan-Indigenous plan is thin and the war's course suggests improvisation.
Interpretation B — A crisis precipitated by jurisdictional conflict: Some historians stress the Sassamon trial and the assertion of English authority as the trigger, which pushed Wampanoag communities already angry about land and law into war, even if Metacom had not planned it.
Interpretation C — Long-term structural pressures: Others emphasize that a century of settlement, land loss, livestock damage, debt, and political subordination had created conditions in which a small spark could produce a wide conflagration, and that many Indigenous nations had their own reasons to join.

Evidence: English narratives, Plymouth court records, deeds, petitions, and later accounts are the principal sources. Indigenous voices survive in recorded speeches and complaints filtered through English transcription, and in tribal oral histories.

Points of disagreement: The debate concerns causation and responsibility, and the weight of the Sassamon episode relative to larger structures. These interpretations are not mutually exclusive.

Why it matters: Attributing the war to Metacom's conspiracy places blame on an individual, while attention to structure highlights the colonial context. How historians frame the war also affects how its victims, Indigenous and English, are remembered.

Section 48.31

The Expansion of War among Wampanoag, Nipmuc, and Other Communities

Early operations went poorly for the English. Plymouth forces besieged Metacom and his followers in a swamp near Mount Hope, but the Wampanoag slipped away in July 1675 and moved north and west, joining Nipmuc communities of central Massachusetts, who had their own grievances and had been placed under pressure by English towns. Weetamoo, the sunksquaw (female sachem) of the Pocasset, who had a complex relationship with Metacom, also joined the conflict, as did many Sakonnet and other Wampanoag groups, although some communities, including those led by Awashonks, negotiated with the English before deciding.

In the late summer of 1675, fighting spread to the Connecticut River valley and central Massachusetts. Indigenous forces attacked English towns, including Brookfield in August, Deerfield and Northfield in September, and Springfield in October, burning houses and killing settlers. The battle of Bloody Brook, in September, killed dozens of English soldiers escorting a wagon train. Indigenous fighters used ambush, mobility, and detailed knowledge of the landscape, and English forces struggled to find and engage them. Many Indigenous participants fought for their own communities' reasons, including defense of land and retaliation for English offenses, and no single leader coordinated the whole.

The war changed the character of English settlement. Frontier towns were abandoned, and refugees crowded into larger towns. English forces responded with campaigns to destroy Indigenous food supplies and settlements, and the Indigenous side, deprived of planting seasons, faced hunger. The war's costs were severe on both sides. The English population, around fifty thousand in New England in 1675, suffered heavy losses of life and property, while Indigenous communities lost many more people in proportion to their numbers.

The summer and autumn of 1675 saw a rapid widening of the war. In July the English trapped Metacom's followers at Pocasset swamp, but they escaped northward, taking refuge among the Nipmuc. In early August, Nipmuc attacks on Brookfield (Quaboag), after a failed diplomatic effort by Massachusetts, led to a siege of the town. In September, Indigenous forces attacked Deerfield and Northfield and ambushed a detachment of English soldiers and wagons near Bloody Brook, killing several dozen. In October they burned much of Springfield and attacked Hatfield, and the English evacuated outlying towns. Leaders such as Monoco, Matoonas, Muttaump, and Shoshanim, among the Nipmuc and allied peoples of the Connecticut valley, including the Pocumtuc and Sokoki, directed many of these attacks, acting in concert at times but largely in command of their own communities. Their motives included longstanding grievances over land and English treatment, the opportunity of an alliance with Metacom, and fear of English reprisals against those who stayed neutral.

English forces struggled. Colonial militia were unfamiliar with Indigenous methods and were tied down defending towns, and the authorities hesitated to arm Indigenous allies. Governors and generals debated whether to attack Indigenous communities suspected of sympathy, and measures such as the seizure of Indigenous people for sale or confinement were adopted early. The war also extended to the Hudson valley, where New York's governor Edmund Andros viewed the conflict with concern and the Mohawk, in 1676, attacked refugees from the New England war, an episode that showed how the war reached into the wider diplomacy discussed in Chapter 47.

Section 48.32

Narragansett Neutrality and the Great Swamp Massacre, December 1675

The Narragansett, led by Canonchet, son of Miantonomo, and the elder sachem Pessicus, attempted to remain neutral. In July 1675, the Narragansett had signed a treaty with the United Colonies that obliged them to hand over any Wampanoag refugees who entered their territory. The Narragansett did shelter some Wampanoag, including women and children, and they did not hand them over, reasoning that doing so would violate traditions of hospitality and compromise their own security. English officials, suspecting that the Narragansett would join Metacom, decided to launch a preemptive strike.

On December 19, 1675, a colonial army of around a thousand men from Massachusetts, Plymouth, and Connecticut, assisted by Mohegan and Pequot allies, attacked the Narragansett's fortified winter settlement in a swamp in present-day South Kingstown, Rhode Island. After a fierce fight, the English set fire to the wigwams, burning many people alive, including women, children, and the elderly. English accounts give numbers of the dead ranging from several hundred to a thousand or more, and the toll included many noncombatants, though exact figures are impossible to establish. English casualties were also heavy. The Narragansett's winter stores of food were destroyed.

The attack turned a neutral or hesitant nation into an enemy. Canonchet and surviving Narragansett warriors joined the war and fought through the winter and spring of 1676. The assault also reinforced a pattern: English forces were willing to attack villages, to burn food supplies, and to kill noncombatants, and they treated Indigenous neutrality with suspicion. The Great Swamp attack ranks, with Mystic, among the most destructive episodes of Indigenous–English warfare in seventeenth-century New England.

The decision to attack the Narragansett was taken in the colonial council in the fall of 1675. The United Colonies declared war in November, and an army of roughly a thousand men under Josiah Winslow of Plymouth marched in December through snow to the Narragansett fort. A captured Narragansett man guided them to the one point at which the palisaded refuge, built on high ground in a swamp, could be entered. The assault of December 19 was costly: English company commanders were killed and the first assault was repulsed, and only after a fight did the attackers break in and set fire to the wigwams. The fire killed or drove out many Narragansett, among whom were elders, women, and children. English losses were heavy, with dozens killed and many more wounded, and the Narragansett, who had lost both people and winter food, scattered. Estimates of the number of Narragansett killed and of people in the fort vary widely and cannot be settled. Canonchet and many warriors survived and carried the war into the spring.

Narragansett tradition and later Rhode Island historians have stressed that the attack fell on a people who had tried to avoid the war, and that the fort sheltered refugees from other nations as well as Narragansett. The attack also affected the Narragansett's relations with other Indigenous nations: Mohegan and Pequot who fought alongside the English were seen by Narragansett as agents of their enemy, while the Narragansett's own refugees fled to neighboring nations.

Section 48.33

Indigenous Alliances, Christian Communities, and the Divided Colonial Frontier

Metacom's War was not a war between all Indigenous people and all English colonists. Indigenous nations made a range of decisions according to their circumstances. The Mohegan under Uncas, and later his son Oweneco, and many Pequot, remained allied with Connecticut, providing scouts and warriors who knew the land and enemy tactics. Their cooperation reflected old rivalries with the Narragansett and their own interests in maintaining standing within the colonial system, though participation also carried costs and risks. Some Indigenous communities, such as portions of the Wampanoag on Cape Cod and the islands, remained peaceful, while other communities attempted neutrality.

The praying Indians faced some of the most difficult choices. Most Christian Indigenous communities of Massachusetts wanted no part of the war, and some, including Natick residents, offered assistance to the English as scouts and soldiers. Despite this, colonists regarded them with suspicion, and in October 1675, the Massachusetts General Court ordered the removal of Christian Indigenous people to Deer Island in Boston Harbor, where hundreds were confined through the winter with inadequate shelter and food. Many died of exposure and hunger. Some Christian Indigenous men served with English forces, while others were attacked by English civilians. The experience illustrated the limits of English acceptance of Indigenous Christians, and the dangers faced by those who tried to avoid the war.

The experiences of Christian Indigenous communities show the war's costs for those who tried to stay out of it. In October 1675 the Massachusetts General Court ordered that Indigenous Christians be gathered at Natick and similar places and then moved to Deer Island, in Boston Harbor, where they were held through the winter with little shelter, fuel, or food. Daniel Gookin and John Eliot protested and tried to supply the camp, and Gookin's account describes the confinement and the suffering of the people held there. Christian Indigenous men served the colonists as scouts and soldiers in 1676 and provided information that proved valuable, yet some English civilians attacked Indigenous Christians and continued to regard them with suspicion. Many returned to their towns after the war to find fields and houses destroyed.

Other communities negotiated their way through the war. Awashonks, the female sachem of the Sakonnet, weighed alliance with Metacom against an English offer and, in 1676, made peace with Plymouth, and some of her people then fought with Benjamin Church. Wampanoag communities on Martha's Vineyard and Nantucket remained at peace, protected by distance, by their own Christian leaders, and by agreements with the island proprietors. On Cape Cod the Nauset and Mashpee Wampanoag, too, avoided the fighting, though they were affected by the colony's measures. The Mohegan, under Uncas and his son Oweneco, served Connecticut as scouts and warriors and received land and standing as a result. These choices were made in light of local circumstances and not of a single Indigenous cause.

Section 48.34

The Campaigns of 1676 and the Death of Metacom

In the winter and spring of 1676, Indigenous forces achieved their greatest successes. In February, a force of Nipmuc, Narragansett, and Wampanoag attacked Lancaster, Massachusetts, killing and capturing many inhabitants. Among the captives was Mary Rowlandson, the minister's wife, whose narrative of captivity, published in 1682, is a valuable though partial source on Indigenous life during the war. Attacks continued on towns including Medfield, Weymouth, and Providence, and by March the English colony of Rhode Island saw Providence burned. Canonchet won a victory in March against a Plymouth force near Pawtucket, but in April he was captured by a force that included Pequot and Mohegan allies and executed.

By late spring, the tide began to turn. Indigenous forces suffered increasingly from food shortages, disease, and the loss of planting seasons, as English raids destroyed crops. Meanwhile, the English created mixed companies that included Indigenous scouts, learned to fight in smaller units, and offered amnesty to those who surrendered. Attacks on Indigenous fishing camps, such as the English attack at Peskeompskut (Turners Falls) in May, killed many people, mostly noncombatants, though a counterattack inflicted heavy losses on the English. Indigenous coalitions began to fragment, and some communities negotiated surrender or withdrew.

In August 1676, Weetamoo drowned while trying to cross a river, and her body was discovered and beheaded by English soldiers. Metacom, who had returned to Mount Hope with a reduced following, was tracked by a force under Captain Benjamin Church, which included Indigenous scouts. On August 12 he was shot and killed by an Indigenous man in English service. His body was quartered and his head displayed on a pike at Plymouth for many years. His wife and young son were captured and sold into servitude. It is important not to center the war entirely on Metacom, whose role was significant but not dominant. Weetamoo, Canonchet, Pessicus, Awashonks, Matoonas, Monoco, and other leaders directed actions of their own communities and shaped the war's course.

The sequence of 1676 shows the war's turning. The attack on Lancaster on February 10 was followed by raids on Medfield, Weymouth, and Groton and by the burning of Warwick and Providence in March, during which Indigenous forces also defeated a colonial detachment under Captain Pierce near Pawtucket. In April Canonchet was captured near the Blackstone River and taken to Stonington, where he was executed by Pequot and Mohegan allies of the English; accounts report that he refused the English offer of life in return for surrender. In May, Captain William Turner led an attack on the fishing encampment at Peskeompskut, killing many people, most of them noncombatants, and Indigenous forces then killed Turner and a significant number of his men on the retreat. By summer, Indigenous communities faced hunger and disease, and the English offered amnesty. Many Nipmuc, Narragansett, and Wampanoag people surrendered or fled. In July Church, with Indigenous scouts, extracted submissions from Awashonks and others, and in August he pursued Metacom to Mount Hope. After Metacom's death, his follower Annawon was captured in late August and executed, which effectively ended the fighting in southern New England.

Section 48.35

Defeat, Enslavement, Displacement, and Indigenous Survival

The consequences of the war were devastating. Estimates of casualties vary and are uncertain. English losses are often placed in the range of several hundred to roughly a thousand killed, with many towns damaged or destroyed, and Indigenous deaths, including combat, hunger, disease, and exile, are generally thought to have been several thousand, a far higher proportion of the Indigenous population. Some historians regard the war as among the deadliest in proportion to population in American history. Many surviving Indigenous people were captured, executed, or enslaved. Colonial authorities sold hundreds of captives, including women and children, to slave traders who shipped them to the West Indies and elsewhere. Some prominent leaders were executed, including Quinnapin, Weetamoo's husband, in Newport.

Others fled. Some Wampanoag, Nipmuc, and Narragansett people moved west into the Hudson valley and beyond, or north to the Abenaki, joining existing communities, while others migrated to the Mohawk and to French-allied communities in Canada. Many remained in New England, living in marginal circumstances or in small Indigenous communities such as those on Cape Cod, Martha's Vineyard, and Nantucket, where Indigenous Christians retained some autonomy. The praying towns shrank, with several destroyed or abandoned, though Natick and a few others survived. Indigenous families worked as laborers and servants on English farms, and Indigenous communities lost land through sales, debts, and colonial seizure.

Yet Indigenous nations survived. The Narragansett, whose leaders retained a land base in Rhode Island, maintained their political identity, and the Wampanoag communities of Mashpee, Aquinnah (Gay Head), Herring Pond, and other places continued to exist. The Mashpee Wampanoag Tribe and the Wampanoag Tribe of Gay Head (Aquinnah) are federally recognized today, and the Narragansett Indian Tribe gained federal recognition in 1983. The war ended large-scale organized Indigenous military resistance in southern New England, but not the presence or identity of Indigenous peoples.

The postwar settlement shaped Indigenous New England for generations. Hundreds of prisoners were sold abroad or distributed as servants, and others, among them Quinnapin, Weetamoo's husband, were executed. Metacom's wife and son were captured and sold. In September 1676 at Dover, New Hampshire, Major Richard Waldron seized several hundred Indigenous people who had come in under truce, and sent those thought to have fought to Boston for sale or execution, an act that Wabanaki would remember. Massachusetts and Plymouth annexed or claimed the lands of Indigenous communities that had been at war, among them Mount Hope and Pocasset. Rhode Island and other colonies claimed Narragansett land, relying on the 1659 Atherton purchase. Of the fourteen praying towns that had been established, only a few were reestablished after the war, among them Natick, Punkapoag, Hassanamesit, and Wamesit, and many Christian Indigenous people died or lived in marginal conditions.

The war also created new routes of survival. Governor Andros of New York invited refugees from the New England war to settle at Schaghticoke on the Hudson, and several hundred Wampanoag, Narragansett, Pennacook, and others did so. Other survivors joined Abenaki communities to the north or moved to Canada. Ninigret's Niantic, who had stayed out of the war, retained their lands in what is now Charlestown, Rhode Island, and Narragansett survivors regrouped around them. On the islands and on Cape Cod, Wampanoag communities such as Mashpee and Aquinnah preserved their lands and ceremonies. English losses were severe as well, with several hundred colonists killed, towns burned, and the colonial economy damaged. Casualty figures are uncertain and vary among historians, but the war's consequences for Indigenous people, in death, enslavement, and loss of land, were far heavier in proportion.

Cause and Effect

War, Displacement, Enslavement, and Survival

Long-term causes

Land loss and jurisdictional subordination across decades of English expansion, livestock damage, debt, and the weakening of Indigenous power after epidemics and the Pequot War.

Short-term causes

Plymouth's 1671 disarmament demand, the Sassamon trial and executions, and the spread of fighting to Nipmuc and Narragansett communities.

Effects of the war

Large Indigenous population loss, destruction of towns and food stores, executions, enslavement and sale abroad, and heavy English losses and property damage.

Long-term consequences

Reduced Indigenous territory and political autonomy in southern New England, migration of some communities, and the persistence of others in reorganized forms.

Historical complexity: No single cause or actor produced the war. Indigenous nations had different grievances and strategies, English leaders made decisions under fear and pressure, and structural conditions made conflict likely without making any particular outcome inevitable.

In the north the fighting continued after southern New England had quieted. Wabanaki communities fought English settlers in Maine through 1676 and 1677, and negotiations in 1678 brought a temporary peace, discussed in Section 48.42. The northern war was linked to Metacom's War by timing and by some shared grievances, but it had its own causes and course.

Section 48.36

The Lenape, Munsee, and the English Conquest of New Netherland, 1664

The Lenape (also called Delaware) lived along the Delaware River, in New Jersey, and in the lower Hudson valley, with Munsee speakers in the north around present-day New York City, the lower Hudson, and the Esopus region. They lived in dispersed communities, farming maize, beans, and squash, fishing, hunting, and gathering shellfish, and they traded furs and wampum with Dutch, Swedish, and later English colonists. Leadership rested with local sachems and councils, and no paramount authority united all Lenape communities, so that diplomacy proceeded village by village.

Relations with the Dutch of New Netherland had been marked by trade but also by violence. Kieft's War (1643–1645), triggered by Governor Willem Kieft's attacks on Indigenous communities near Manhattan, including the Pavonia massacre of 1643, and the Esopus wars (1659–1660 and 1663–1664) involving the Munsee in the Hudson valley, killed many people and drove communities from their lands. These conflicts form necessary background, but Dutch colonial history lies mostly beyond this chapter. They taught Munsee and Lenape leaders that European settlement could provoke sudden violence and that their best protection lay in diplomacy and mobility.

In 1664 an English fleet under Richard Nicolls seized New Netherland, which became the colony of New York, and the territory south of the Hudson was granted to proprietors who created East and West New Jersey. The change in colonial administration did not erase Indigenous communities. Lenape and Munsee people continued to live, trade, and negotiate, and English governors generally maintained the Dutch practice of purchasing land by deed and holding councils. The English also assumed the diplomatic relationships that the Dutch had developed with the Haudenosaunee and the Covenant Chain tradition discussed in Chapter 47. Over time, English settlement increased, and Lenape communities sold land, moved, and consolidated, some migrating westward to the Susquehanna and Ohio country. This chapter treats those movements briefly, since later chapters, especially those on eighteenth-century diplomacy, take up the Lenape story at greater length.

The Dutch period had left a legacy of treaties and mistrust. During Kieft's War the Hackensack sachem Oratam emerged as a mediator who sought to protect his people and to keep open channels with the Dutch, and the settlements negotiated in 1645 and later involved the exchange of wampum and formal councils. After the English conquest, Governor Nicolls required that Indigenous land purchases be licensed by the colony's government, and the articles of surrender for New Netherland protected Dutch property but said nothing of Indigenous claims. Lenape and Munsee communities went on selling land in New Jersey, Staten Island, and the Hudson valley, often reserving rights to hunt and fish. The area's smallpox epidemics and the pressure of settlement reduced Indigenous numbers, and English governors, concerned with the colony's finances and with relations with the Haudenosaunee, treated Lenape communities as subordinate partners within a Covenant Chain framework whose Haudenosaunee dimensions are discussed in Chapter 47. The Delaware River takes its English name from the colony's early governor, Lord De La Warr, and the Lenape are therefore also called Delaware, a naming that reflects colonial priorities and not Indigenous self-description.

Section 48.37

The Susquehannock, Doeg, Piscataway, and the Maryland–Virginia Frontier

The Susquehannock, Iroquoian speakers who lived at the head of Chesapeake Bay and along the lower Susquehanna River, were a formidable power in the early seventeenth century. They traded furs with the English, Dutch, and Swedes, and they fought wars against Algonquian neighbors and Maryland colonists in the 1630s–1650s. In the 1660s and 1670s they came under pressure from Haudenosaunee attacks, discussed in Chapter 47, and from English expansion, and many moved south toward the Maryland frontier. Maryland, founded in 1634, had established relations with the Piscataway, an Algonquian-speaking people on the Potomac, whose leaders had at times sought English protection against Susquehannock raids.

The Chesapeake frontier of the 1670s was crowded with refugees, settlers, and rival Indigenous groups. In July 1675 a dispute between a Virginia planter, Thomas Mathew, and Doeg (Dogue) people over unpaid goods produced a violent exchange across the Potomac, in which colonists killed Doeg, and Virginia and Maryland militia pursued the matter into Susquehannock territory. The militia besieged a Susquehannock fort, and in September, during negotiations, Maryland officers killed several Susquehannock leaders, a violation of the diplomatic conventions the Susquehannock understood. The Susquehannock escaped and in early 1676 raided settlements in Virginia and Maryland, killing dozens of colonists.

These events show the volatility of a frontier in which colonial governments exercised uncertain control over settlers and in which diplomacy could be violated by local commanders. They also show that Indigenous people responded to treachery by retaliation. The raids ignited tension within Virginia between colonial leaders, who favored a defensive policy, and frontier settlers who demanded offensive war against Indigenous people generally, whether or not they had been involved in the raids. This division opened the way for Bacon's Rebellion.

The Susquehannock had met English explorers early in the century, and John Smith described them in 1608 as a tall, powerful people whose fortified towns lay at the head of the bay. They traded with several European nations and were at war with Maryland for much of the 1640s, until a treaty in the 1650s opened a long period of trade. Pressure from the Haudenosaunee, who attacked Susquehannock towns in the 1660s and 1670s, weakened them and drove them south to the Potomac region, where they occupied a fort near Piscataway Creek. The Piscataway, whose leader had accepted Catholic missionaries in the 1630s, lived on the Maryland side of the river under treaty relations with the colony, and many of them were also displaced by the movement of refugees and settlers. Maryland and Virginia authorities thus faced a crowded Potomac frontier in which several Indigenous peoples, each with its own grievances, lived near English plantations that were expanding quickly. The killing of the Susquehannock leaders in 1675 and the Susquehannock's retaliation in early 1676 transformed that crowded frontier into the spark for a political crisis.

Governor Berkeley's response to the raids was to propose a defensive strategy of forts and a limited military action, partly because he distrusted the settlers' demand for general war and partly because the colony's trade with friendly Indigenous nations depended on a policy of discrimination. Frontier planters, who had lost kin, servants, and property, rejected this approach, and the division widened. That the dispute soon turned on the colonial government's relations with its tributary Indigenous allies indicates how closely Indigenous policy and English politics were tied.

Section 48.38

Bacon's Rebellion, 1676, and Indigenous Resistance in Virginia

Nathaniel Bacon was a recently arrived planter with connections to leading families, who gathered frontier settlers and demanded a commission to attack Indigenous people. Governor William Berkeley, who favored a policy of defending the frontier with forts and who maintained trade and tribute relationships with friendly Indigenous nations, refused. Bacon proceeded without authority. In May 1676 his followers attacked the Occaneechi, a people on the Roanoke River who had been trading with Virginians and who had just defeated Susquehannock enemies, killing many of them. Bacon then turned against the Pamunkey and other tributary nations, whom the colonial government considered allies.

The Pamunkey were led by Cockacoeske, widow of Totopotomoy, who had died in 1656 fighting for the English against other Indigenous enemies. Cockacoeske had asserted authority over several Powhatan-descended peoples, and she opposed Bacon's attacks. When Bacon's men approached, the Pamunkey withdrew into Dragon Swamp, using the terrain to evade pursuit, an example of how Indigenous communities used geography to survive. Bacon's forces plundered Indigenous goods and killed some people, while Cockacoeske and her followers attempted to reach safety. Bacon's attacks were directed at Indigenous communities that had not harmed the colonists, and they illustrate the vulnerability of tributary peoples whose treaty status did not prevent frontier violence.

The rebellion became a broader crisis within English Virginia. Bacon's followers, including many poor freemen, indentured servants, and some enslaved Africans, challenged Berkeley's government on issues of taxation, land policy, and Indigenous relations. In September 1676 Bacon's forces burned Jamestown. Bacon died of disease in October, and the rebellion collapsed as royal forces arrived. The rebellion's Indigenous dimension has often been overshadowed by its political significance for English colonial history, but for Indigenous Virginians it was a campaign of plunder and killing that exposed their precarious position. The events also raised alarm in London about colonial governance and prompted the Crown to define the legal status of Indigenous nations more explicitly, producing the 1677 treaty.

Bacon's attacks reveal the fragility of Indigenous security under English law. The Occaneechi, who had controlled an important trading island on the Roanoke River and who had recently defeated a Susquehannock group, welcomed Bacon's men at first, and the colonists then attacked them, killing their leader and many others. Bacon's followers in the June 1676 assembly secured laws authorizing war against Indigenous people and permitting the enslavement of captives, measures that endangered all Indigenous communities in the colony regardless of their relation to the Crown. When Berkeley condemned Bacon as a rebel, Bacon turned on the Pamunkey, in part because they had land and goods and in part because he denied that any Indigenous community was truly friendly.

Cockacoeske faced a hard choice. Her people had served the English before and had suffered in the colony's wars, and her son John West served with colonial forces. She also had to preserve the community from men who regarded all Indigenous people as enemies. Pamunkey refugees took cover in Dragon Swamp and other wetlands, where Bacon's men could not easily follow, and the community's survival depended on its knowledge of the landscape. After Bacon's death and the end of the rebellion, Cockacoeske sought compensation and recognition from the colonial authorities, and her efforts, along with the work of the royal commissioners sent to investigate the crisis, led to the treaty of 1677. The Pamunkey's experience is a reminder that Indigenous history in Virginia during the rebellion was not a footnote to English politics but a record of survival and negotiation.

Section 48.39

The Treaty of Middle Plantation, 1677, and Tributary Nations

In 1677 representatives of the Crown and the Virginia government negotiated the Articles of Peace at Middle Plantation, an interior settlement near the present-day Williamsburg, with leaders of several Indigenous nations. Cockacoeske, who participated as the leader of the Pamunkey and as a representative of other tributary peoples, played a major role. The treaty placed the Indigenous signatories under the protection of the Crown, declared that they would hold their lands as the Crown's tributaries, and provided that the English would not settle within a defined distance of their towns, a protection that colonial officials repeatedly failed to enforce.

The legal structure was one of tributary relations. The Indigenous signatories acknowledged the English king's authority in the treaty's language and undertook two distinct annual obligations: a quit-rent of three Indigenous arrows acknowledging landholding, and the customary tribute of twenty beaver skins tendered to the governor. In return, the Crown promised protection, recognition of their landholdings, and the right to complain to the governor about injuries. The treaty also prohibited colonists from taking Indigenous land, harming their crops, or allowing livestock to damage their fields, a recognition of the grievances discussed earlier in this chapter. It set out a procedure for settling disputes through the Crown's representatives. But it also embedded Indigenous nations within English law as subordinate peoples, limiting their freedom of action, and it did not prevent later settlers from encroaching.

Cockacoeske's diplomatic position was important. English officials treated her as a "queen," and she exercised authority over multiple peoples, an arrangement that reflected the older structure of the Powhatan paramount chiefdom. She pressed the English to honor the agreement, and she continued to petition colonial authorities for protection until her death in the mid-1680s. The treaty remained a foundation for the legal claims of Virginia's Indigenous peoples. The Pamunkey and Mattaponi still pay annual tribute to the Governor of Virginia, in the form of game, a tradition that continues as a symbolic recognition of treaty obligations dating to the seventeenth century.

The treaty also assigned Cockacoeske a position of leadership over several tributary peoples, including the Nansemond, the Appomattuck, and others, a recognition of the paramount structure of the Powhatan chiefdom and an acknowledgment that the English preferred to deal with a single leader. Not all peoples accepted her authority, and some, such as the Nansemond, pursued their own claims. Within a year, Cockacoeske petitioned the commissioners about English encroachment, stating that settlers were violating the treaty's protections. The treaty was further tied to the system of alliances later called the Covenant Chain, as Virginia, New York, and the Haudenosaunee reorganized their relations in the late 1670s, a development discussed in Chapter 56. It left a legal record that Virginia's Indigenous nations still invoke in claiming reserved lands and rights.

Section 48.40

William Penn, the Lenape, and the Foundations of Pennsylvania

King Charles II granted William Penn the charter for Pennsylvania in 1681, in settlement of a debt owed to Penn's father. Penn, a Quaker, planned a colony that would offer religious freedom and profit, and he wrote to Indigenous leaders to assure them of fair dealing. He arrived in 1682 and began negotiating with Lenape sachems for land along the Delaware River. Deeds survive from 1682 and 1683 that record purchases of tracts from Lenape leaders, including purchases near present-day Philadelphia and Bucks County, in exchange for goods such as cloth, tools, and wampum.

The Lenape leaders who negotiated with Penn had experience with Dutch, Swedish, and English settlers, and they approached the negotiations with their own goals. They hoped to secure peace, trade, and a stable boundary, to protect their communities from other colonists and from enemies, and to obtain goods. Penn's policy sought to prevent settlers from occupying land before purchase, to regulate trade, to hold joint courts with Indigenous juries for disputes, and to prohibit sale of alcohol to Indigenous people. These policies set Pennsylvania apart from some neighbors, and for several decades the colony enjoyed relatively peaceful relations with the Lenape.

Tradition holds that Penn made a great treaty with the Lenape at Shackamaxon under an elm tree, a story that was repeated in art and literature, most famously in Benjamin West's painting of 1771–1772. No surviving treaty document corresponds to this story, and historians treat it as an idealized later construction, though Penn did meet with Lenape leaders, and his 1683 letter to the Free Society of Traders describes the Lenape and his dealings with them. The surviving deeds are the more reliable evidence. Pennsylvania's policy was not without self-interest, since peaceful purchase was cheaper than war and attracted settlers. After Penn's time, the colony's relations with the Lenape deteriorated as the settler population grew and as proprietors and officials pursued land through contested agreements, a story that belongs to later chapters.

Penn's own writings reveal his intentions. In 1681, even before he left England, he addressed a letter to the Indigenous inhabitants of his grant, assuring them of his desire to live in peace and to deal justly. In his 1683 letter to the Free Society of Traders, he described the Lenape's languages, customs, and political organization with attention, and he took care to record their dealings with the colonists. His government's instructions required that colonists buy land from Indigenous owners only with the proprietor's permission, and Pennsylvania's laws provided for juries of both English and Indigenous members in cases involving the two peoples. The Lenape sachems who dealt with Penn, among them Tamanend, expected gifts, regular councils, and respect for their land boundaries, and they used treaties to secure promises that they could invoke later. They were also positioned between the Haudenosaunee, who claimed authority over the Susquehanna valley, and other Indigenous nations, so that Pennsylvania's Indigenous diplomacy was part of a larger network. The later Walking Purchase of 1737 and the erosion of Lenape land in the eighteenth century belong to subsequent chapters, but the early deeds show that the colony's founding reflected both a commitment to negotiated purchase and an interest in land that would, over time, change the terms of that relationship.

Section 48.41

Wabanaki Peoples and English Settlement in Northern New England

The Wabanaki homelands stretched across present-day Maine, New Brunswick, Nova Scotia, and neighboring areas. The Mi'kmaq lived in the Maritimes, the Wolastoqiyik (Maliseet) along the Saint John River, the Passamaquoddy on the Saint Croix and Passamaquoddy Bay, the Penobscot on the Penobscot River, and the western Abenaki and Pennacook in the Kennebec, Merrimack, and upper Connecticut valleys. These peoples were not primarily agricultural in the north, where short growing seasons limited farming, but they combined hunting, fishing, gathering, and in the south some maize cultivation, moving seasonally between coastal and interior sites.

English contact began with fishermen and explorers in the early seventeenth century. A short-lived English colony was established at the mouth of the Kennebec in 1607 (the Popham colony), and English fishing stations such as Monhegan and settlements such as Pemaquid dotted the coast. Plymouth and Massachusetts traders established posts on the Kennebec and Penobscot. At the same time, the French established a presence in Acadia and made alliances with Wabanaki peoples, supported by missionaries and traders, a subject treated in Chapter 45. The Wabanaki thus lived between two empires, and they adapted by trading with both, accepting alliances based on their own interests, and sometimes playing one against the other.

Epidemics in the 1610s and later reduced Wabanaki populations, and English settlement along the coast brought disputes over land, fishing rights, and treatment. Colonial settlers sometimes cheated Indigenous traders, kidnapped them, or damaged their resources, and Indigenous communities sought redress through their own means, through diplomacy, or through French support. The legal claims of the English colonies in Maine were also complicated, as Massachusetts acquired control of Maine settlements in the 1650s and again in 1677, which gave Indigenous communities one more authority to deal with. Settlers in Maine tended to be dispersed fishermen, farmers, and sawmill operators rather than compact towns, a pattern that produced numerous small-scale encounters.

Wabanaki communities were bound together by language, kinship, and shared resource areas, and they maintained their own diplomatic traditions. The Pennacook, led in the early seventeenth century by Passaconaway, held a position between the Merrimack valley and the coast and dealt with both Massachusetts and the Abenaki farther north; his son Wonalancet tried to keep neutrality in 1675. The Penobscot, the Kennebec Abenaki, and the Passamaquoddy lived within reach of both English fishing stations and French posts at Pentagoet and elsewhere, and they exploited that location. In 1628 Plymouth established a trading post at Cushnoc on the Kennebec to obtain furs, and it drew Indigenous traders into dependence on English goods. The English fishermen and settlers of the Maine coast, who worked sawmills and fishing stations, often treated Indigenous communities casually or roughly. A story told in New England accounts, which may preserve a historical event, held that English sailors upset a canoe carrying the wife and infant child of the Saco leader Squando in 1675, and that the child died, an incident said to have contributed to his hostility. Whether or not the details are accurate, the story reflects the kind of everyday friction that produced violence on a dispersed frontier.

Section 48.42

Anglo-Wabanaki Warfare and Diplomacy, 1675–1701

Fighting between Wabanaki and English settlers began in the northern theater in the late summer of 1675, as tensions that had been building for years combined with the news of the war in southern New England. Settlers were killed and farms burned, and English militia responded by attacking Indigenous towns. Wabanaki leaders such as Squando of the Saco and Madockawando of the Penobscot led raids and negotiations. The war was not an extension of Metacom's War, though some refugees from southern New England reached Wabanaki communities and may have influenced events. It had its own local causes: disputes over trade, fishing rights, and treatment, and fears about English power.

The northern war continued through 1676 and 1677 with raids and counter-raids and ended in a settlement at Casco in April 1678. The terms, known through later reports rather than an extant original treaty, included the return of captives and permission for English settlers to return to their holdings on Wabanaki land. Crucially, English families—not Wabanaki communities—were expected to pay an annual acknowledgment, conventionally described as a peck of corn per family, to Wabanaki leaders. This payment recognized continuing Indigenous claims and jurisdiction; it was not Wabanaki tribute to the English. The Wabanaki understood the arrangement as a peace in which they retained their lands and independence, and they did not consider themselves conquered. English leaders, for their part, tended to treat the agreement as a submission, a misinterpretation that recurred in later conflicts.

Warfare resumed in 1688–1689, when the imperial conflict between England and France, known in the colonies as King William's War, drew Indigenous nations on both sides. French officials and missionaries encouraged Wabanaki attacks on English settlements, and English governors, including Massachusetts's, attempted to contain the conflict by forts and campaigns. Wabanaki warriors, sometimes with French and Indigenous allies from Canada, attacked settlements such as Dover (1689) and York (1692), while English forces raided Wabanaki villages. Diplomatic negotiations at Pemaquid in 1693 produced a temporary peace. The war formally ended with the Treaty of Ryswick in 1697 between England and France, but local conflicts continued, and Wabanaki leaders maintained their ties with the French while negotiating separately with the English.

Wabanaki strategy in this period combined military action, diplomacy, and the preservation of political independence. They were skilled in using the borderland between empires, exchanging captives, accepting gifts, and negotiating treaties. Individual leaders, such as Madockawando, whose daughter married the French baron Jean-Vincent d'Abbadie de Saint-Castin, developed ties with the French that gave them leverage. The wars of the late seventeenth century did not destroy the Wabanaki, who in the early eighteenth century developed the alliances later associated with the Wabanaki Confederacy and continued to negotiate with both English and French. Chapters 55 through 57 return to this diplomacy.

The northern war's course turned on events that Wabanaki communities remembered long afterward. In 1676 Wabanaki fighters attacked English settlements at Casco Bay, on the Kennebec, and at Pemaquid, and many Maine settlers fled southward. In the autumn of 1676, Richard Waldron's seizure of several hundred Indigenous people at Dover, many of them Pennacook who had come in under a promise of safety, created lasting bitterness, since many were sold or executed. The Treaty of Casco in 1678 ended the first war, and the English then built a fort at Pemaquid under Governor Andros. For the next decade the English population of Maine grew, and disputes over land, trade, and justice accumulated.

When war resumed in 1688 and 1689, Wabanaki leaders linked their action to grievances against the English and to alliances with the French. Andros's raid on the Penobscot region in 1688 and the Dover (Cocheco) attack of June 1689, in which Wabanaki and Pennacook fighters killed Waldron and others, were in part acts of vengeance for 1676. The Candlemas attack on York in January 1692, the English capture and destruction of Wabanaki towns, the Wabanaki-French capture of Pemaquid in 1696, and many smaller raids constituted a long war of attrition. After the Treaty of Ryswick, Wabanaki delegations negotiated with Massachusetts, including at Casco Bay in 1699, and with the French, and some Abenaki and Wabanaki representatives took part in the diplomacy that culminated in the Great Peace of Montreal of 1701, discussed in Chapters 45 and 47. These negotiations demonstrate that Wabanaki leaders pursued their own strategy of peace with both powers while preserving their lands, a strategy that would be tested again in the eighteenth century.

Everyday Life and Social History

Indigenous Communities on the Northern and Southern Frontiers

In the Wabanaki homelands, a family's year might begin with spring fishing for alewives and sturgeon on the rivers, move to summer coastal sites for shellfish, sea fishing, and the gathering of berries and plants, and then to fall and winter hunting for moose, deer, and beaver in the interior. Women processed fish and game, prepared hides, made birchbark containers, and tended gardens where climate allowed, while men hunted, fished, and managed diplomacy. Warfare disrupted this cycle by interrupting planting and fishing seasons, forcing families to flee, and causing hunger.

On the southern frontier, as Section 48.43 shows, Cusabo families lived in coastal and river communities that fished, farmed, and hunted, and who now faced a new town and, with it, new trade goods, new debts, and the danger of captivity. In both frontiers, ordinary life was a series of choices about where to settle, with whom to trade, whom to trust, and how to protect children and elders. The records, mostly produced by colonists, tell this history imperfectly, and historians combine them with archaeology and Indigenous oral history to understand daily life.

Section 48.43

Carolina, 1670, and Indigenous Relations on the Southeastern Coast

The English colony of Carolina was granted by Charles II in 1663 to eight proprietors, and in 1670 the first settlers, many from Barbados, arrived in the Charleston area and established a settlement on the Ashley River, which moved to the present site of Charleston (Charles Town) in about 1680. The region was inhabited by the Cusabo, a group of coastal peoples including the Kiawah, Etiwan, Stono, Wando, and Edisto, whose communities lived along the rivers and coast. The Cusabo had experience of Europeans through Spanish and French contacts, and they received the English with caution and with interest in trade, some even welcoming them as potential allies against more powerful neighbors.

The English colony was established on the margins of Spanish Florida. Spain still maintained St. Augustine and mission networks extending into the Guale coast, but its earlier fortified town of Santa Elena near Port Royal had been abandoned in 1587; it was not an operating Spanish garrison when Charles Town was founded in 1670. Spanish authorities regarded the English settlement as an intrusion, and the Carolinians in turn saw Florida as a threat. Indigenous communities on the border between the two colonies were pulled between them, with some aligned with the Spanish missions and others open to English trade. The Carolina colonists depended on Indigenous trade for deerskins, food, and information, and a rapidly developing trade in deerskins and Indigenous captives tied Indigenous communities to English markets.

The conditions of the southern frontier differed from those in Virginia and New England. Settlement was initially sparse and concentrated on the coast, the colony's economy depended on exports of provisions and trade rather than on tobacco, and the colony's population was small. Indigenous peoples of the interior held significant power, and English traders needed their goodwill. Carolina's Indigenous diplomacy therefore relied heavily on trade and alliance, though the same trade created opportunities for coercion and violence. This section does not anticipate the Tuscarora and Yamasee wars of the early eighteenth century, which belong to later chapters.

Relations between Cusabo communities and the first Carolina settlers were shaped by diplomacy at the outset. The Kiawah, whose cacique received the English at Albemarle Point in 1670, saw potential benefits in trade and protection from Spanish raids and from Indigenous enemies to the north and west. The proprietors instructed colonists to treat Indigenous people fairly and to buy land, and early agreements with Cusabo leaders set the terms under which settlers could occupy the coastal lands. Henry Woodward, who had been captured by the Spanish before 1670, learned Indigenous languages, and he used his knowledge to establish trade with interior peoples, including the Westo, in 1674. Spanish authorities challenged the English colonial intrusion and sought to defend their northern mission frontier; the best-documented Spanish destruction of an English settlement in this region came at Stuart Town near Port Royal in 1686. During the 1680s Yamasee and other Indigenous groups from the Spanish mission zone moved north to settle near Port Royal, responding to pressures and opportunities created by the two colonial systems. Cusabo communities, reduced by disease and by the pressure of settlement, found their lands and political independence gradually eroded in the decades after 1670.

Section 48.44

Carolina's Indigenous Slave Trade and the Transformation of Southern Alliances

Carolina merchants quickly discovered that Indigenous captives could be sold for profit, to buyers in the Caribbean, in other mainland colonies, and in Carolina itself. In the 1670s the colonial traders, notably Henry Woodward, developed a trade with the Westo, an Iroquoian-speaking people on the Savannah River who, equipped with English firearms, raided neighboring peoples and supplied captives and deerskins. The Westo became powerful intermediaries, and their raids terrorized Indigenous communities of the Southeast, including peoples of the Spanish mission zone.

The partnership collapsed. In 1680 Carolina colonists, including some who wanted a free hand in the slave trade without a Westo monopoly, supported a war against the Westo conducted by the Savannah (Shawnee) people, who had also entered the English trade and who destroyed the Westo as a power. The Savannah thereafter became Carolina's principal trading partners and raiders. This sequence shows that Indigenous communities participated in the slave trade as intermediaries and as victims, and that Carolina traders manipulated rivalries for profit. It also shows that Indigenous alliances were fluid and that no Indigenous group could rely on a colonial partner.

Enslaved Indigenous people were exported to other colonies and used in Carolina for domestic and agricultural labor. The trade grew in the 1680s and 1690s, and its effects spread across the Southeast, prompting migration, the formation of new alliances, and heightened violence. Spanish Florida and its Indigenous allies were targeted by Carolina-supported raids, and Scottish settlers at Stuart Town on Port Royal Sound, founded in 1684, were attacked by Spanish forces in 1686. This chapter limits itself to the documented developments before 1701. The full magnitude of the trade, and estimates of the number of enslaved Indigenous people across the entire period to 1715, belong to the broader treatments in Chapter 53 and in later chapters, and should not be projected backward onto the seventeenth century.

The Westo war of 1680 shows how Carolina's traders pursued profit independently of the proprietors. Carolina traders associated with the Goose Creek faction, among whom Maurice Mathews became prominent, supported the Savannah campaign against the Westo in 1680, contrary to proprietary preferences for maintaining regulated trade. The campaign broke the Westo position as a dominant regional trading power; the surviving record does not justify assigning every military decision to Mathews personally or implying that all Westo people disappeared. The result was a new system in which the Savannah, who settled along the Savannah River, and later other allied communities such as Creek towns, acted as slave raiders and suppliers to Charles Town merchants. Captives were sold into the Caribbean, to other mainland colonies, and in Carolina. Spanish Florida and its mission towns, whose Indigenous inhabitants were targeted, suffered from the raids, and the Spanish retaliated, as in the destruction of the Scottish settlement at Stuart Town in 1686. In response, many Indigenous communities of the interior and the coast moved, merged, or armed themselves, and the social and political geography of the Southeast was reorganized. This reorganization, which accelerated in the eighteenth century, is outside this chapter's limits, and its full scale should not be projected onto the years before 1701.

Section 48.45

Indigenous Strategies at the Close of the Seventeenth Century

By 1700, Indigenous peoples along the English Atlantic seaboard had developed a range of strategies. Diplomacy was the most common: treaties, councils, gift exchanges, and alliances with the English, French, or other Indigenous powers. The Wabanaki balanced the English and French, the Lenape negotiated with Pennsylvania, the Pamunkey petitioned Virginia, and the Narragansett and Wampanoag sought protection in colonial courts. Indigenous leaders used the language of English law when it served them, framing grievances as violations of treaties and legal rights.

Migration was another strategy. Survivors of Metacom's War moved to the Hudson valley, to Abenaki and Canadian communities, and to other places, and Lenape people began to migrate west. Some Indigenous communities merged with others to preserve their identity. Military resistance, though less common after the defeats of 1646, 1637, and 1676, remained a possibility, as the Wabanaki wars showed. Religious adaptation offered a different path: Christian communities like Natick, Martha's Vineyard, and others preserved language and land while adopting new forms. Political reorganization, including the formation of reservations, tributary agreements, and the recognition of leaders such as Cockacoeske, enabled some communities to maintain a land base under colonial law.

These strategies were not mutually exclusive, and Indigenous communities often combined them. A community might petition a governor, trade with neighbors, send young men to fight as allies of the English, and maintain traditional ceremonies. The strategies reflected both the pressures of colonization and the creativity of Indigenous leaders and communities, who adapted to circumstances while preserving core values and identities.

Seen from a comparative view, the strategies pursued in different regions reflected differences in Indigenous political structure as well as in colonial policy. Where Indigenous nations had a strong central leadership, as among the Powhatan, resistance could be coordinated but also decapitated, as the capture of Opechancanough showed. Where authority was dispersed, as in southern New England, Indigenous communities negotiated separately and could be set against one another, but they were also harder to subdue in a single campaign. Where Indigenous nations lived between two empires, as in the north, they could exploit the competition. Where trade was the main link to the colony, as in Carolina, Indigenous leaders could gain advantage by controlling access to goods, though those who did so risked becoming agents of enslavement. These patterns help explain why generalizations about Indigenous reaction to English colonization are usually misleading.

Section 48.46

Comparing English Colonial Systems: Virginia, New England, the Middle Atlantic, and Carolina

The English Atlantic colonies shared a common premise: settlement required land, and land required dealing with Indigenous inhabitants. Yet their histories diverged. Virginia was an export-oriented tobacco colony whose expansion depended on plantation agriculture and unfree labor, and its wars were fought to open the tidewater to planters. New England was a society of family farms, towns, and churches, whose expansion was driven by population growth and religious purpose and whose Indigenous policy combined purchase, missionary activity, and, in war, extreme force. The Middle Atlantic colonies, which inherited Dutch and Swedish patterns, relied on negotiated purchases and councils, and Pennsylvania followed a policy of purchase and regulated trade. Carolina's economy rested on trade with Indigenous peoples, on provisions, and on the slave trade, which tied Indigenous communities to colonial markets in particularly destructive ways.

The scale of English settlement is one of the most important variables. By 1700 the English population of the mainland colonies is generally estimated at a quarter of a million or more, with large populations in the Chesapeake and New England and only a few thousand in Carolina. The Chesapeake's growth was driven by tobacco and the importation of indentured servants and, increasingly, enslaved Africans, while New England's growth came mainly from natural increase after the Great Migration. In both regions, settlers outnumbered Indigenous people by 1675 or earlier, and this demographic shift limited Indigenous options. Carolina's small population gave interior Indigenous nations greater leverage, but the colony's economy tied them to the slave trade. The Middle Atlantic's Quaker proprietors benefited from a period in which Indigenous nations were still strong and settlers still few.

The role of religion and law also set the regions apart. New England's colonies justified their wars as providential and combined missionary activity with strict control of Indigenous towns, so that conversion and territorial control were linked. Virginia pursued conversion half-heartedly and relied on treaties that defined Indigenous nations as tributaries. Pennsylvania's Quaker policy of purchase and mixed juries produced a more peaceable early record, though it too served the colony's land interests. Carolina's proprietors were chiefly concerned with land sales and trade. Law, in all these regions, was used to define Indigenous status, to regulate land, and to limit Indigenous independence, but its content varied.

Comparison with other European empires clarifies what was distinctive. French colonies in the St. Lawrence valley, discussed in Chapter 45, depended on Indigenous trading partners and allies and kept their settler population small, while Spanish colonies, treated in Chapters 41 and 42, relied on missions, tribute, and labor drafts. English colonies, because they sought land for large numbers of settlers, entered into more direct conflict over territory, a feature that explains the repeated wars. But English colonies were also weak in military terms for much of the century and depended on Indigenous allies, as the Pequot War and Metacom's War both show. English dominance was not inevitable in 1607, and the Indigenous nations of the Atlantic coast shaped the colonies' development by choosing among alliance, trade, resistance, and withdrawal.

Section 48.47

Colonial Memory, Historical Evidence, and the Survival of Indigenous Nations

The stories of Pocahontas, Thanksgiving, the Pequot War, and Metacom's War have been told and retold in ways that reflect the needs of later generations. Pocahontas was transformed in the nineteenth century into a symbol of Indigenous acceptance of English settlement and later into a romantic heroine. Thanksgiving became a national holiday that celebrates harmony. The Pequot War was long remembered in English tradition as a providential victory, and Metacom, called King Philip, was depicted alternately as a villain and, in the nineteenth century, as a noble vanishing warrior. Each of these narratives served purposes in American memory and often obscured Indigenous political agency and survival.

Historians now work with several kinds of evidence to correct these narratives. Colonial archives remain essential but must be read critically for their authors' purposes. Archaeology, including work at Mystic, at Jamestown, and at Wampanoag and Narragansett sites, supplies independent evidence about Indigenous life and violence. Indigenous oral histories, kept by nations such as the Pamunkey, Mattaponi, Mashantucket Pequot, Mohegan, Wampanoag, and Narragansett, carry knowledge and interpretations that colonial documents omit, and tribal scholars and historians lead research projects and museums. Public memory is changing as a result, with new exhibits, school curricula, memorials, and commemorations that include Indigenous voices, an issue taken up in Chapter 132.

Above all, the evidence shows continuity. The Pamunkey and Mattaponi retain reservations in Virginia, and the Pamunkey received a final federal acknowledgment determination in 2015, effective January 28, 2016, followed by six other Virginia nations recognized by Congress in 2018. The Mashantucket Pequot Tribal Nation, federally recognized in 1983, and the Mohegan Tribe, recognized in 1994, operate governments, museums, and economic enterprises in Connecticut. The Mashpee Wampanoag, the Aquinnah Wampanoag, and the Narragansett Indian Tribe continue as recognized nations, and the Wampanoag language has been revived through the Wôpanâak Language Reclamation Project, a testament to the survival of Indigenous knowledge. Other communities, including the Nipmuc and several Pequot and Wampanoag groups, continue to pursue or hold state recognition or federal acknowledgment, issues discussed in later chapters on tribal sovereignty. The seventeenth-century wars did not end Indigenous history in the Atlantic Northeast and Southeast; they changed its conditions.

Public memory has begun to change as Indigenous nations have taken a larger role in telling their own history. The Mashantucket Pequot Museum and Research Center presents the Pequot War and Pequot survival from the nation's perspective, and Wampanoag communities have worked with Plimoth Patuxet Museums and others to interpret 1620–1621 in Wampanoag terms. In Virginia, the Virginia Indian nations have taken part in the commemoration of Jamestown and in archaeological work at Werowocomoco, and the Pamunkey Indian Museum presents the nation's history. The Wôpanâak Language Reclamation Project, founded in 1993 by Jessie Little Doe Baird and others, has returned Wampanoag to use as a spoken language, relying on the seventeenth-century texts discussed in this chapter. Such work shows that colonial documents, which were created to serve colonial purposes, can also serve Indigenous purposes when read by their descendants.

The record of recognition is part of the evidence of continuity. The Narragansett Indian Tribe and the Mashantucket Pequot received federal recognition in 1983, the Mohegan Tribe in 1994, the Wampanoag Tribe of Gay Head (Aquinnah) in 1987, and the Mashpee Wampanoag in 2007. The Pamunkey received a final federal acknowledgment determination in 2015 that took effect January 28, 2016, and Congress extended recognition to six more Virginia nations in 2018. Recognition is a legal status granted by the United States, not the source of Indigenous nationhood, and the Pamunkey and Mattaponi held reserved lands under colonial treaties for centuries before it was granted. The history of these nations since the seventeenth century, including their engagement with later colonial and national governments, is traced in later chapters.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined English colonization of the Atlantic coast between 1607 and 1701 as a process that unfolded within existing Indigenous political worlds. English settlers arrived in territories governed by the Powhatan paramount chiefdom, the Wampanoag sachemships, the Pequot, Mohegan, and Narragansett, the Lenape, the Susquehannock, the Wabanaki, and the Cusabo, each with its own history, alliances, and strategies. Colonization was not the settlement of an empty land but the establishment of settler colonies whose growth required land, and whose expansion progressively limited Indigenous political and territorial independence.

In Virginia, the English depended initially on Powhatan food and goodwill. Wahunsenacawh sought to incorporate the newcomers into his system, and the first war ended in a negotiated peace sealed by the captivity and marriage of Pocahontas. The tobacco economy changed the balance by generating relentless demand for land. Opechancanough's attacks of 1622 and 1644 attempted to halt expansion but provoked destructive retaliation, and the treaty of 1646 placed the Powhatan peoples in a tributary status. The three conflicts were not interchangeable: they reflected a shift from English dependence to English dominance.

In New England, Ousamequin allied with Plymouth in 1621 as a strategic response to epidemic and Narragansett pressure, an alliance that provided mutual benefit for decades. The later Thanksgiving tradition obscures this political context. Massachusetts Bay's mass migration brought a different scale of settlement, and Roger Williams's dealings with the Narragansett illustrated both the possibility of negotiated land access and the difficulty of separating it from colonial territorial authority.

The Pequot War of 1636–1637 resulted from a convergence of English expansion, Pequot, Mohegan, and Narragansett rivalries, and a series of killings. Mystic, where hundreds of mostly noncombatant Pequot died, exemplified a style of warfare that targeted communities, and the Treaty of Hartford attempted to erase Pequot political identity. The attempt failed, and Pequot nations survive. The distinction between the defeat of a coalition and the disappearance of a people is central.

Division V analyzed the structural mechanisms of colonial expansion across regions. Deeds, purchases, and treaties transferred land under conflicting understandings and growing pressure. Livestock, fences, milldams, and towns altered the landscape and Indigenous economies. Colonial courts extended jurisdiction. Puritan missionary work produced praying towns and a major body of Indigenous-language literature, including the 1663 Bible, in which Indigenous translators and printers played essential roles. Indigenous people were also drawn into servitude, debt, and enslavement.

Metacom's War of 1675–1678 was the culmination of these pressures. Its immediate triggers included the Sassamon trial, but its deeper causes lay in decades of dispossession and subordination. The war involved many Indigenous nations making different choices: the Mohegan and Pequot allied with the English, the Narragansett sought neutrality before being attacked at the Great Swamp, Christian Indigenous communities were confined, and Wampanoag, Nipmuc, and others fought. Its consequences included mass death, enslavement, and displacement, yet Indigenous communities endured.

In the Middle Atlantic, English conquest of New Netherland extended English authority over Lenape and Munsee peoples, and the Susquehannock, Doeg, and Piscataway were drawn into Chesapeake frontier violence that fed Bacon's Rebellion. Cockacoeske and the Pamunkey survived the rebellion by using the terrain and diplomacy, and the Treaty of Middle Plantation defined a legal framework that still matters. William Penn's Pennsylvania offered a more negotiated approach, though its idealization in later tradition exceeds the documentary evidence.

On the northern and southern frontiers, the Wabanaki balanced English and French interests through warfare and diplomacy between 1675 and 1701, and Carolina's founding in 1670 opened a southern frontier in which trade, alliance, and an emerging Indigenous slave trade reshaped relations among Indigenous peoples and with the English. By around 1700, Indigenous peoples had adopted diplomacy, migration, military resistance, legal petition, religious adaptation, and political reorganization, often in combination.

The comparative analysis showed that English colonial systems differed in settlement, land acquisition, missionary activity, labor, and law, while sharing a dependence on land. It also showed that Indigenous choices, rivalries, and institutions shaped each region's course. The chapter closed by examining how colonial narratives have been reshaped by public memory, and how colonial archives, archaeology, Indigenous oral history, and tribal scholarship together support a more accurate account.

The central lesson is that English colonization transformed Indigenous Atlantic North America through overlapping processes of settlement, law, diplomacy, religion, land acquisition, and warfare rather than through a single conquest. Indigenous nations responded in different ways, and those responses shaped the English colonies. The Pamunkey, Mattaponi, Wampanoag, Pequot, Mohegan, Narragansett, and many others continue as political and cultural communities.

Key Terms

Settler colony
A colony established to settle a permanent colonizing population on land occupied by others, dependent on continuing land acquisition.
Tsenacommacah
The Powhatan name for the territory of the paramount chiefdom of the Virginia tidewater, often translated as "densely inhabited land."
Paramount chiefdom
A political system in which a leading chief holds authority over subordinate chiefdoms through tribute and alliance while local leaders retain autonomy.
Sachem
A leader of a New England Algonquian community whose authority rested on kinship, tribute, alliance, and reputation; a woman leader was a sunksquaw.
Tribute
Goods or services owed by one community or leader to another, or by Indigenous nations to the English, as an acknowledgment of relationship or subordination.
Tributary nation
In Virginia after 1646 and 1677, an Indigenous nation placed under the protection of the Crown and obliged to pay annual tribute.
Wampum
Shell beads used in diplomacy, gift exchange, and ceremony, and in the seventeenth century as currency in colonial trade.
Charter
A royal grant authorizing a company or proprietor to establish a colony, issued without consulting Indigenous inhabitants.
Headright
A grant of land, introduced in Virginia in 1618, given for each immigrant whose passage was paid.
Starving time
The winter of 1609–1610 at Jamestown, when colonists confined by Powhatan hostility suffered severe hunger and death.
Treaty of mutual protection
The 1621 agreement between Ousamequin and Plymouth to refrain from injury and to assist one another against attack.
Mystic massacre
The May 26, 1637, attack on a Pequot village on the Mystic River by Connecticut and allied forces, in which hundreds of people, mostly noncombatants, were killed.
Treaty of Hartford
The 1638 agreement dissolving the Pequot as a political entity and prohibiting the use of the name.
United Colonies of New England
A confederation of Massachusetts, Plymouth, Connecticut, and New Haven formed in 1643 for common defense and diplomacy with Indigenous nations.
Deed
A written instrument recording the transfer of land, understood by English parties as permanent alienation and sometimes understood differently by Indigenous parties.
Jurisdiction
The authority to make and enforce law, contested between colonial courts and Indigenous leaders.
Reservation
Either a right reserved in a deed or land set aside by a colony for an Indigenous community.
Praying town
A community of Indigenous Christians in Massachusetts organized under missionary and colonial guidance, beginning with Natick (1651).
Massachusett Bible
The 1663 translation of the Bible into Massachusett, the first complete Bible printed in British North America.
Metacom's War
The 1675–1678 war in New England, also called King Philip's War, involving Wampanoag, Nipmuc, Narragansett, and other nations, and the English colonies and their Indigenous allies.
Great Swamp Fight
The December 1675 colonial attack on the Narragansett winter settlement in present-day Rhode Island.
Deer Island
An island in Boston Harbor where Massachusetts confined Christian Indigenous people during Metacom's War.
Bacon's Rebellion
The 1676 uprising of Virginia colonists against Governor Berkeley, which included attacks on Indigenous nations.
Articles of Peace (1677)
The treaty of Middle Plantation defining the status of Virginia's tributary Indigenous nations.
Shackamaxon tradition
The later story of a great treaty between William Penn and the Lenape, not supported by a surviving treaty document.
Wabanaki
The peoples of the northeastern coast and interior, including Mi'kmaq, Wolastoqiyik, Passamaquoddy, Penobscot, and western Abenaki.
Cusabo
The coastal peoples of the South Carolina lowcountry who met the first Carolina settlers.
Westo
An Iroquoian-speaking people of the Savannah River who raided for captives in the Carolina trade until their regional power was broken in the war of 1680.
Indigenous oral history
Knowledge of the past transmitted by Indigenous communities, treated here as evidence requiring the same careful interpretation as other sources.
Historical memory
The ways communities and nations remember and represent the past, often shaped by later political purposes.

Chapter Reflection

Chapter 48 matters because it shows that English colonization of the Atlantic coast was neither inevitable nor uniform. It resulted from decisions made by English investors, governors, ministers, and settlers, and by Indigenous leaders who weighed alliance, accommodation, resistance, and migration. Its outcomes depended on epidemics, rivalries among Indigenous nations, the economics of tobacco and trade, and the sheer growth of settler populations.

The chapter also reveals processes that reach far beyond 1701. Deeds, treaties, tributary arrangements, and reservations established legal forms through which land would continue to be transferred and Indigenous status defined. Missionary and literacy projects left written records that descendants use today. Wars and enslavement scattered communities and created diasporas. And colonial stories about Pocahontas, Thanksgiving, and the Pequot and Metacom's wars became part of national mythology, challenged in recent decades by Indigenous scholars and communities.

Within the textbook, this chapter links the commercial and diplomatic histories of Chapters 45 through 47 to the eighteenth-century struggles for imperial and Indigenous power taken up in Chapters 55 through 60, and to the removal and sovereignty histories of later volumes. Students should carry forward the habits of reading colonial records critically, attending to Indigenous strategies and institutions, and remembering that the survival of Indigenous nations, with their languages, governments, and historical memory, is as much a part of the story as the conflicts that tested them.
