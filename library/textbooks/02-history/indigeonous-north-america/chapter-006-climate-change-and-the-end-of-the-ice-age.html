Climate Change and the End of the Ice Age

Indigenous Peoples of North America: A Continental History

Chapter Opening

A reconstructed composite scene. Somewhere along a shrinking Great Basin lake, sometime after roughly thirteen thousand years ago, the shoreline a family had camped beside the previous generation was no longer where they remembered it. The water had receded, the marsh grasses that once supported waterfowl had thinned, and the large animals that had once come to drink at the lake's edge were becoming harder to find. Nothing about this change was announced. No single event marked its arrival. It simply became, over years and decades, a different world than the one their grandparents had known.

The preceding chapter reconstructed the diversity of Paleoindian lifeways: the varied technologies, subsistence strategies, and social arrangements through which small, mobile communities across North America met the demands of a still-glaciated continent. This chapter turns to a different but closely related question. It asks not what Paleoindian communities were like, but what was happening to the world around them, and how that world's transformation set the stage for the increasingly regionalized histories that occupy the remainder of this volume and the chapters that follow it in Part II.

Between roughly nineteen thousand and eight thousand years ago, North America underwent one of the most significant environmental transformations in its geological history. The great ice sheets that had covered much of Canada and the northern United States for tens of thousands of years, having reached their maximum extent at different times in different sectors, entered a long and unevenly paced final retreat. Global temperatures rose, though unevenly and with at least one significant reversal. Sea levels climbed as glacial meltwater returned to the oceans, drowning coastal landscapes that earlier peoples had once walked, hunted, and possibly settled. Plant communities reorganized across the continent, and animal populations, including many of the large-bodied species collectively known as megafauna, entered a period of sustained decline that culminated, for most species, in extinction.

It is tempting to describe this transformation as a single continental event: an "Ice Age" that ended on a particular date, followed by a "post-glacial" world that followed predictable, uniform rules. The evidence does not support so tidy a story. Deglaciation proceeded at different rates in different regions. A sharp cooling interval known as the Younger Dryas interrupted the general warming trend for more than a thousand years, and its effects were felt unevenly, sometimes barely at all, depending on location. Lakes in the Great Basin shrank dramatically while, at roughly the same time, forests were expanding across parts of the Eastern Woodlands. Some regions lost their large mammals in what appears, from the available evidence, to have been a rapid decline; other populations persisted for centuries or millennia longer, in some cases on isolated islands far from the mainland extinctions.

Indigenous communities did not experience this transformation passively. They tracked changing plant and animal distributions, adjusted the size and shape of their territories, experimented with new tools and techniques, and, over many generations, developed increasingly regionally specific ways of living that anticipated the more clearly differentiated cultural traditions historians and archaeologists describe using terms such as "Archaic." This chapter treats that process of adaptation and regional differentiation as evidence of continued historical agency, not as a story of communities helplessly buffeted by forces beyond their control.

This chapter also asks students to think carefully about one of the most persistent debates in North American archaeology: why did dozens of large-bodied mammal species disappear from the continent within a geologically narrow window of time? Was human hunting responsible? Was environmental and ecological change the primary driver? Or did these factors interact in ways that neither alone can fully explain? This textbook does not resolve that debate on the reader's behalf. It presents the evidence, the major interpretive positions, and the reasoning historians and archaeologists use to evaluate them, and it asks students to reach their own carefully reasoned conclusions.

Finally, this chapter examines the archaeological convention of dividing early North American history into a "Paleoindian" period and an "Archaic" period that follows it. That boundary, this chapter argues, describes a real and important set of changes in technology, subsistence, and settlement, but it should not be mistaken for a single moment of cultural transformation. The transition unfolded gradually, unevenly, and at different times in different regions, a pattern this chapter traces directly into the regionally organized chapters of Part II.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why the end of the last Ice Age is better understood as a regionally uneven process than as a single continental event.
  2. Distinguish between the geological transition from the Pleistocene to the Holocene epoch and the archaeological transition from the "Paleoindian" to the "Archaic" period.
  3. Describe the general pattern of deglaciation across North America, including the retreat of the Laurentide and Cordilleran ice sheets.
  4. Analyze the Younger Dryas cooling interval, its uncertain and debated causes, and the regional variability of its effects.
  5. Explain how glacial meltwater, proglacial lakes, changing drainage systems, and rising sea levels reorganized North American landscapes.
  6. Describe how plant communities and ecological zones reorganized across the continent rather than shifting northward as intact units.
  7. Identify the major megafaunal species that became extinct at the end of the Pleistocene and describe the general chronology and regional variability of their decline.
  8. Evaluate competing scholarly interpretations of megafaunal extinction, including overkill hypotheses, climatic and ecological explanations, and multicausal models.
  9. Explain why increasing archaeological visibility of diverse, locally available foods after the terminal Pleistocene does not mean such foods were unknown earlier.
  10. Describe changes in human mobility, settlement patterns, and technology associated with the Holocene transition.
  11. Compare regional responses to environmental change across North America during this period.
  12. Critique the "Paleoindian to Archaic" transition as a periodization label, explaining why it varies by region and does not describe a single cultural replacement.

  2. What kinds of evidence allow scientists to reconstruct climate, vegetation, and animal populations from thousands of years before any written record?

Historical Context

Chapter 5 described the material culture, subsistence strategies, mobility patterns, and social organization of Paleoindian communities across a still-glaciated North America, emphasizing that these communities were diverse and regionally varied rather than uniform "big-game hunters" following a single way of life. That diversity is essential background for this chapter, because the environmental transformation examined here did not act upon a single, uniform Paleoindian population; it acted upon dozens of distinct communities already pursuing different combinations of large-game hunting, small-game hunting, fishing, and plant gathering suited to their particular regional settings.

Chapters 2 through 4 established the deeper chronological and evidentiary background for human presence in North America, including the debates over pre-Clovis evidence, the Clovis tradition, and the broader question of how and when the continent was first peopled. This chapter does not revisit those debates. It assumes a human population already present and already regionally diverse by the time the most significant environmental changes examined here began to unfold.

Understanding this chapter also requires a basic distinction between two different kinds of periodization that are easy to conflate. The first is geological: the boundary between the Pleistocene epoch, commonly associated with repeated glacial cycles, and the Holocene epoch, the current geological epoch, conventionally placed at approximately 11,700 years before present based on ice-core evidence from Greenland. The second is archaeological: the boundary between the "Paleoindian" period, discussed in Chapter 5, and the "Archaic" period, discussed beginning in Chapter 7. These two boundaries do not occur at the same moment everywhere, and they measure different things. The geological boundary describes global climatic conditions. The archaeological boundary describes changes in human technology, subsistence, and settlement, changes that occurred at different times in different regions and that did not necessarily track the geological boundary precisely. This chapter treats the geological transition as context for, rather than as a direct cause of, the archaeological transition, while also showing where the two plainly intersected.

Finally, students should understand that reconstructing environmental change over such a long and remote period depends on specialized scientific evidence rather than direct observation or written record. Sediment cores from lakes and bogs, pollen preserved in those sediments, ice cores from glaciers and ice sheets, radiocarbon-dated plant and animal remains, isotopic analysis of bones and shells, and the geological record of former shorelines, riverbeds, and glacial features all contribute pieces of a larger reconstruction. No single line of evidence tells the whole story, and scientists continue to refine and sometimes revise this reconstruction as new evidence and new dating techniques become available.

Section 6.1

A World in Transition

By roughly nineteen thousand years ago, the North America described in Chapters 3 through 5, a continent still substantially shaped by glacial ice, cold-adapted ecosystems, and a distinctive assemblage of large mammals, had already begun to change. Over the following eleven thousand years, that change accelerated and broadened, eventually producing a continent whose climate, vegetation, animal populations, coastlines, and drainage systems bore only partial resemblance to the world Paleoindian communities had first encountered. This chapter examines that transformation directly, treating it as a central subject of Indigenous North American history rather than as background scenery for a human story told elsewhere.

Two distinctions are essential from the outset. The first distinguishes global or continental climatic change from local and regional environmental change. Warming after the Last Glacial Maximum was a broad hemispheric trend, but its effects on any particular landscape, a specific lake, forest, or grassland, depended on local topography, latitude, elevation, proximity to retreating ice, and other factors that produced substantial regional variation. The second distinction, introduced in the Historical Context section above, separates the geological Pleistocene-Holocene boundary from the archaeological Paleoindian-Archaic boundary. This chapter uses both frameworks, but keeps them conceptually separate throughout.

Historical Vocabulary
Pleistocene, Holocene, and Period Labels

Pleistocene is the geological epoch, marked by repeated glacial cycles including the Last Glacial Maximum discussed in Chapter 3, that preceded the current epoch. Holocene is the current geological epoch, conventionally beginning approximately 11,700 years ago based primarily on Greenland ice-core evidence, and generally, though not perfectly, associated with warmer and more climatically stable conditions than the Pleistocene. These are formal geological terms defined by global climatic and stratigraphic criteria, and they apply everywhere on Earth at the same time.

Paleoindian and Archaic, by contrast, are archaeological period labels, discussed in Chapter 5 and Section 6.11 respectively, that describe particular configurations of technology, subsistence, and settlement documented in the North American archaeological record. Unlike the Pleistocene-Holocene boundary, the Paleoindian-Archaic boundary is not a single global date; archaeologists place it at different times in different regions, depending on when locally documented technological and subsistence changes occurred.

Why it matters: Students frequently, and understandably, conflate these two sets of terms, assuming that "the Holocene" and "the Archaic" began at the same moment everywhere. This chapter uses the geological terms to describe global and hemispheric climatic conditions, and the archaeological terms to describe regionally specific cultural developments, keeping the two frameworks conceptually distinct throughout, even where they overlap in time for a given region.

Framing the chapter this way allows a more historically accurate account than a simple story in which "the Ice Age ended" on a single date, mammoths conveniently disappeared, and "Archaic" peoples then invented new tools to cope with a suddenly different world. The actual evidence describes a slower, uneven, and geographically variable set of processes, in which environmental change and human adaptation unfolded together over thousands of years rather than as a single before-and-after transition.

Section 6.2

The End of the Pleistocene

The global Last Glacial Maximum, discussed in Chapter 3, extended roughly from 26,500 to 19,000–20,000 years ago, a period during which most of the world's ice sheets, including the Laurentide Ice Sheet covering most of Canada and parts of the northern United States and the smaller Cordilleran Ice Sheet covering much of the mountainous Pacific Northwest and western Canada, stood at or near their maximum positions. Northern Hemisphere deglacial warming was underway by about 19,000 to 20,000 years ago, though individual sectors of these ice sheets reached local maximum positions, began retreating, or even temporarily readvanced at different times; portions of the Cordilleran Ice Sheet in the Puget Lowland, for example, did not reach their own local maximum extent until after roughly 16,000 years ago. Beginning broadly across this window, global temperatures entered a general, though not perfectly steady, warming trend that continued, with the significant interruption discussed in Section 6.3, into the early Holocene.

This warming was driven primarily by long-term changes in Earth's orbital geometry, which altered the distribution of solar radiation received at different latitudes and seasons, in combination with feedback processes involving atmospheric greenhouse gases, ice-sheet albedo, and ocean circulation. As temperatures rose, the great ice sheets began to lose mass, both through surface melting and through the calving of ice into meltwater lakes and, eventually, the ocean. Ice retreat was not uniform. Some sectors of the Laurentide Ice Sheet retreated relatively quickly, while others, particularly in areas where ice remained thick or where cold air continued to pool, retreated more slowly or even readvanced temporarily.

As the ice sheets thinned and retreated, they exposed new land, some of it heavily reshaped by glacial erosion and deposition, including moraines, eskers, and outwash plains, and some of it depressed by the sheer weight of ice that had rested upon it for millennia. This depressed land began a slow process of isostatic rebound, rising over thousands of years as the weight of ice was removed, a process that continues in parts of northern Canada and the Arctic to the present day and that affected relative sea levels in complex, regionally specific ways.

Scientists reconstruct this broad deglacial sequence using multiple independent lines of evidence: oxygen isotope ratios preserved in ice cores from Greenland and Antarctica, which record past temperatures; sediment records from the ocean floor and from lakes, which preserve evidence of changing glacial meltwater input and vegetation; and the geological record of glacial landforms and former ice margins, which allows researchers to map the retreat of specific ice lobes over time. These independent lines of evidence generally agree on the broad outline of deglaciation, even as researchers continue to refine specific dates and rates for particular regions.

Section 6.3

The Younger Dryas

The broad deglacial warming trend was interrupted by one of the most studied and debated climatic events in the Quaternary record: the Younger Dryas, a cooling interval beginning around 12,900 years before present and lasting approximately a thousand years, before a comparatively abrupt return to warming near the start of the Holocene. The event takes its name from a small Arctic and alpine wildflower, Dryas octopetala, whose pollen appears in European lake sediments from this period, indicating a return to cold, tundra-like conditions in regions that had begun to warm.

The causes of the Younger Dryas remain actively debated among Quaternary scientists. One influential hypothesis proposes that a large pulse of glacial meltwater, potentially released through the drainage of a proglacial lake such as Glacial Lake Agassiz, discussed further in Section 6.4, disrupted North Atlantic Ocean circulation patterns responsible for transporting heat northward, triggering cooling across much of the Northern Hemisphere. Other researchers have proposed alternative or contributing mechanisms, including changes in solar activity, volcanic activity, or internal variability within the climate system. A more controversial hypothesis, proposing that a cosmic impact event triggered the Younger Dryas, has been extensively debated and has not achieved broad scientific acceptance, with much of the supporting evidence contested by other researchers. Students should understand that the meltwater-disruption hypothesis currently commands substantial though not universal support, and that this remains an area of active research rather than settled consensus.

Whatever its ultimate cause, the Younger Dryas did not affect all of North America uniformly. Evidence from ice cores, pollen records, and other proxies indicates that cooling was most pronounced in the North Atlantic region and adjacent areas, including parts of eastern North America, while effects in other regions, including much of the western interior, appear to have been more muted or to have taken different forms, such as changes in precipitation rather than simply temperature. This regional variability is itself an important lesson: even a well-documented, relatively short climatic event did not produce a single, uniform experience across the continent.

It is equally important to avoid overstating the Younger Dryas's role in human history. Popular accounts have sometimes linked the Younger Dryas directly to the disappearance of the Clovis toolmaking tradition discussed in Chapter 4, or have proposed that a sudden climatic shock alone explains the decline of Pleistocene megafauna discussed in Section 6.6. The archaeological and paleontological evidence does not support treating the Younger Dryas as a single decisive cause of either development. Clovis-style fluted points had already begun giving way to a variety of regionally distinct point styles before and during this period, a pattern more consistent with ongoing regional diversification, discussed further in Section 6.9 and in Chapter 7, than with an abrupt cultural collapse.

Section 6.4

Retreating Ice, Rising Seas, and Changing Waters

As the Laurentide and Cordilleran ice sheets retreated, enormous volumes of meltwater accumulated, often trapped between the retreating ice front and higher terrain to the south, forming vast proglacial lakes. The largest of these, Glacial Lake Agassiz, covered portions of present-day Manitoba, Saskatchewan, Ontario, Minnesota, and North Dakota at various stages of its existence, and its periodic drainage events, sometimes catastrophic, released enormous volumes of fresh water into the ocean, reorganizing drainage patterns across the northern Plains and Great Lakes region and potentially influencing ocean circulation and climate, as discussed in Section 6.3. Other proglacial lakes formed in the Great Lakes basin itself as ice retreated northward, with lake levels, outlets, and drainage directions changing repeatedly as the ice margin shifted and land rebounded isostatically.

At the same time, the meltwater from retreating ice sheets worldwide raised global sea levels substantially, a process that continued through the early Holocene as remaining ice sheets, including the Laurentide Ice Sheet's final remnants, continued to melt. This global sea-level rise, combined with regional variations caused by isostatic rebound and subsidence, permanently altered coastlines across North America. Continental shelves that had been dry land during the height of the Last Glacial Maximum, when so much of the world's water was locked in ice that global sea levels stood many tens of meters lower than today, were gradually submerged.

This submergence has a direct and important consequence for the archaeological record discussed throughout Chapters 2 through 5: any sites that once existed on these now-submerged coastal plains, including potential sites associated with early coastal migration routes, are largely inaccessible to conventional archaeological survey and have been investigated only through specialized underwater archaeology in a small number of locations. Historians and archaeologists must therefore reckon with the likelihood that a portion of the terminal Pleistocene archaeological record, potentially including evidence relevant to coastal migration routes discussed in Chapter 3, lies underwater and remains largely unstudied.

Inland, retreating ice and changing precipitation patterns reorganized river systems, wetlands, and lake basins across the continent. Some regions saw the formation of extensive wetlands and marshes as meltwater and changing drainage created new low-lying, water-rich environments, particularly in areas recently vacated by ice. Other regions, especially in the interior West, experienced the opposite trend during parts of the early Holocene, discussed further in Section 6.5, as large Pleistocene lakes began to shrink under warming and drying conditions.

Section 6.5

Forests, Grasslands, Deserts, and Moving Ecological Zones

One of the most persistent misconceptions about post-glacial environmental change treats plant communities as though they migrated northward as intact, unchanging units, with an entire forest simply relocating in step as the climate warmed. Pollen records recovered from lake sediments and bogs across North America tell a more complicated story. Individual plant species responded independently to changing temperature, precipitation, and soil conditions, moving at different rates and in different directions, so that plant communities of the early Holocene were frequently novel combinations of species that had not previously grown together, rather than simple relocations of Pleistocene-era plant communities.

In eastern North America, pollen evidence indicates a general, though gradual and uneven, expansion of temperate deciduous forest northward and eastward as spruce-dominated boreal forest and, in some areas, more open parkland retreated toward what are now more northern latitudes. This process unfolded over thousands of years rather than as a rapid transition, and it produced intermediate plant communities, including mixed conifer-hardwood forests, that persisted for extended periods before giving way to the more familiar temperate forest associations of the mid-Holocene.

In the interior West, the pattern differed substantially. Many parts of the Great Basin had supported extensive Pleistocene lakes, including Lake Bonneville and Lake Lahontan, fed by cooler temperatures and different precipitation patterns than prevail today. As the climate warmed through the terminal Pleistocene and into the early Holocene, many of these lakes began to shrink, a process examined further in this chapter's Geography Study feature, exposing former lakebeds and reducing the wetland and marsh habitats that had supported waterfowl, fish, and the plant and animal communities dependent on them. This drying trend did not proceed at a constant rate, and some intervals saw partial reversals, but the overall trajectory across much of the Great Basin and Southwest was toward increasing aridity through the early and middle Holocene.

Northern environments underwent their own transformations as ice retreated and exposed new land for plant colonization. Tundra and, later, boreal forest expanded into areas the ice sheets had only recently vacated, though the pace of this ecological succession depended on soil development, seed dispersal, and local climate, meaning that some recently deglaciated areas remained sparsely vegetated for centuries after the ice itself had retreated. Along the Pacific Coast and in the Pacific Northwest, changing sea levels, glacial retreat in coastal mountain ranges, and shifting precipitation patterns produced their own distinctive sequence of vegetation change, laying groundwork for the maritime forest environments examined in Chapter 10.

The overall lesson of this section is that "the environment" did not change as a single, uniform entity. It reorganized differently in different regions, sometimes moving toward increased moisture and forest expansion, sometimes toward increased aridity and lake desiccation, and always through the individual responses of particular plant and animal species rather than through the wholesale relocation of intact ecosystems.

Geography Study

A Continent Reorganizing

Using a map of North America that shows major terminal Pleistocene and early Holocene features, such as the maximum extent of the Laurentide and Cordilleran ice sheets, the boundaries of Glacial Lake Agassiz, the former extent of Lake Bonneville and Lake Lahontan in the Great Basin, and the approximate terminal Pleistocene coastline reflecting lower sea levels, students should consider the following:

  • The dramatic difference in scale between the former Great Basin lakes and their much smaller present-day remnants, including the Great Salt Lake as a remnant of Lake Bonneville
  • The area of continental shelf exposed during the Last Glacial Maximum along the Atlantic, Pacific, and Gulf coasts, now submerged and largely inaccessible to archaeological survey
  • The shifting boundary of the Laurentide Ice Sheet at several points in its retreat, and the proglacial lakes that formed along its southern margin
  • The approximate boundary between expanding eastern deciduous forest and retreating boreal forest and parkland at different points in the early Holocene
  • The relationship between isostatic rebound in formerly glaciated areas and relative sea-level change in those same regions, as compared with regions farther from the former ice margin

  1. Why does comparing the former extent of Lake Bonneville with the modern Great Salt Lake give students a clearer sense of Holocene environmental change than verbal description alone?

Section 6.6

The Megafaunal Extinctions

Among the most striking features of the terminal Pleistocene record is the disappearance, within a geologically narrow window of time, of dozens of genera of large-bodied mammals from the North American mainland. These species, collectively termed megafauna, included Columbian and woolly mammoths, American mastodons, several species of native horses and camelids, giant ground sloths, glyptodonts, the short-faced bear, the American lion, the saber-toothed cat known as Smilodon, and numerous other large herbivores and their predators. Taken together, this represents one of the most significant faunal turnovers in the continent's geological history.

The chronology of this extinction event is better understood in outline than in precise detail for every species. For the subset of North American megafaunal genera with the most securely dated last appearances, roughly sixteen genera possess radiocarbon chronologies precise enough to support confident conclusions, the best-dated extinctions cluster approximately between 13,800 and 11,400 calendar years ago, a span that converts from the classic radiocarbon-year estimates of roughly twelve thousand to ten thousand years ago long used in older literature. This interval overlaps substantially with both the Younger Dryas and the period of most intensive Clovis-era archaeological visibility discussed in Chapter 4. For the many additional extinct genera whose fossil record is more sparsely dated, the timing of final disappearance remains considerably less secure. Students should resist treating this as a single synchronized event even within the best-dated cluster. Different species appear to have declined at different rates, in different regions, and some evidence suggests that certain populations persisted, in reduced numbers, somewhat later than the bulk of the extinction record would suggest.

The clearest illustration of extinction's staggered character comes from isolated island populations that survived long after their mainland relatives disappeared. A population of woolly mammoths on St. Paul Island, off the coast of Alaska, persisted until approximately 5,600 years ago, thousands of years after mainland North American mammoths had disappeared. An even longer-surviving population on Wrangel Island, off the Siberian coast, persisted until approximately 4,000 years ago. These island populations were geographically isolated from the ecological and, in some interpretations, human pressures that affected mainland populations, and their survival provides valuable evidence for evaluating competing explanations of extinction discussed in Section 6.7. Genomic research on the Wrangel Island mammoths has documented a severe population bottleneck, subsequent inbreeding, and the accumulation of some mildly deleterious genetic variants; however, the same research indicates that the population nonetheless remained demographically stable for millennia after this bottleneck, and it does not show that genetic deterioration caused the population's final disappearance. The proximate cause of the Wrangel population's extinction therefore remains uncertain, and researchers continue to investigate possible contributing factors, including environmental change, disease, or human contact, none of which is yet firmly established.

It is important to note that megafaunal extinction was not confined to North America; broadly similar, though not identical, patterns of large-mammal extinction occurred across much of the world during the late Pleistocene and early Holocene, including in South America, Australia, and Eurasia, though with different timing, different affected species, and, in some cases, evidence suggesting different combinations of causes. This global pattern is itself an important piece of evidence in the extinction debate, since any fully satisfying explanation must account for both the broad global pattern and the specific regional and chronological variations within it.

Section 6.7

What Role Did Humans Play?

The question of why North American megafauna became extinct at the end of the Pleistocene has generated one of the longest-running debates in North American archaeology and paleontology. This section presents the major interpretive positions and the kinds of evidence each draws upon, without declaring a single position definitively settled, because the scholarly debate itself remains genuinely unresolved.

Historical Debate

What Caused the Megafaunal Extinctions?

The question: Did human hunting, environmental and ecological change, or some combination of factors cause the extinction of most North American megafaunal genera at the end of the Pleistocene?

Interpretation A — The overkill hypothesis: Some researchers argue that the arrival and subsequent population growth of human hunters, associated with the Clovis tradition and its contemporaries discussed in Chapter 4, placed unsustainable predation pressure on megafaunal populations that had not evolved defenses against a novel, technologically sophisticated predator. Proponents point to the rough chronological overlap between increased archaeological visibility of human hunting technology and the onset of megafaunal decline, to a small number of Clovis–proboscidean association and processing localities, many traditionally interpreted as kill sites, and to comparable, though not identical, patterns of extinction associated with human arrival in other regions of the world, including Australia and various Pacific islands.
Interpretation B — Climatic and ecological explanations: Other researchers emphasize that the terminal Pleistocene was a period of profound climatic instability, including the abrupt warming and cooling episodes discussed in Sections 6.2 and 6.3, that reorganized vegetation, disrupted established ecological relationships, and may have reduced the carrying capacity of the landscape for large-bodied herbivores accustomed to Pleistocene conditions. Proponents note that megafaunal populations had survived numerous earlier glacial-interglacial cycles, and argue that something about the particular combination of rapid, non-analog climatic and vegetational change at the end of this cycle, rather than human hunting alone, better explains the extinction pattern, particularly since human populations before this period appear to have coexisted with megafauna for millennia without triggering comparable extinctions.
Interpretation C — Multicausal and synergistic models: A growing number of researchers argue that neither human hunting nor climatic change alone provides a fully satisfying explanation, and that the extinctions more likely resulted from the interaction of several factors: climatic and ecological stress that reduced megafaunal population resilience, combined with even modest additional predation pressure from human hunters, possibly compounded by habitat fragmentation, changing fire regimes, and, in some hypotheses, disease. Under this model, populations already under ecological stress may have been unable to withstand additional pressures that they might otherwise have survived.

Evidence: All three interpretations draw on overlapping evidence, including radiocarbon-dated extinction chronologies, association/processing localities and a smaller number of securely identified kill sites, paleoclimatic reconstructions, and comparative evidence from other continents, but differ in how they weigh and combine this evidence.

Points of disagreement: Disagreement concerns causation and the relative weight of contributing factors, but also the exact timing and degree of synchronicity for more sparsely dated genera. The broad scale of the terminal-Pleistocene faunal turnover is well established, while taxon-by-taxon chronology remains uneven, as Section 6.6 explained.

Why it matters: How historians and scientists resolve this debate affects broader questions about the role of human activity in shaping ecosystems, the resilience of large mammal populations to environmental stress, and the historical relationship between early Indigenous communities and the animal populations they depended upon and interacted with.

Student task: Using the evidence summarized above, evaluate each interpretation's strengths and weaknesses. Which combination of evidence would most effectively distinguish among these interpretations? What kind of new evidence might change the current balance of scholarly opinion?

Regardless of which interpretation, or combination of interpretations, a given researcher favors, several points command broad agreement among specialists. First, direct archaeological evidence connecting humans to now-extinct megafaunal species, while real, is limited to a relatively small number of documented association and processing localities, and at most of these localities current archaeological evidence generally cannot reliably distinguish active hunting from scavenging of animals that died of other causes. This does not establish that Clovis-era communities did not hunt megafauna; some researchers argue on independent grounds, including the ballistic effectiveness of Clovis-style points, that hunting was likely. It does mean that any argument for large-scale human predation pressure rests partly on inference rather than direct, comprehensive documentation of kills. Second, the timing of extinction, while broadly overlapping with the period of increased human archaeological visibility for the best-dated genera, is not precise enough in every case to establish a strict cause-and-effect sequence for every affected species. Third, purely climatic explanations must account for the fact that comparable climatic transitions at the end of earlier glacial cycles did not produce extinctions of similar scale, which some researchers take as evidence that the presence of human hunters made this particular transition different from its predecessors.

This textbook treats the megafaunal extinction debate as a genuinely open scholarly question rather than a settled matter, and it encourages students to approach popular accounts that present either "humans did it" or "climate did it" as obvious and uncomplicated conclusions with appropriate skepticism.

Section 6.8

Changing Foodways

As megafaunal populations declined and environments reorganized, the archaeological record shows an increasing visibility, across many regions of North America, of subsistence strategies drawing on a wide range of smaller, more locally available resources: small mammals, birds, fish, shellfish, and a growing variety of plant foods, including seeds, nuts, roots, and tubers, along with, in some regions, early evidence of intensified processing technologies such as grinding stones. Archaeologists commonly refer to this pattern as broad-spectrum subsistence.

Historical Vocabulary
Broad-Spectrum Subsistence

Definition: A subsistence strategy that draws on a wide range of food resources, often including small game, fish, shellfish, and diverse plant foods, rather than concentrating primarily on a narrow range of large-bodied prey.

Historical context: Archaeologists observe increasing evidence of broad-spectrum subsistence practices across many regions of North America beginning in the terminal Pleistocene and continuing through the early Holocene, coinciding with the environmental changes and megafaunal decline discussed elsewhere in this chapter.

Why it matters: This pattern is central to understanding how Indigenous communities responded to environmental change, since diversifying the resource base can reduce vulnerability to the decline of any single food source.

Potential misunderstanding: Increasing archaeological visibility of broad-spectrum subsistence should not be mistaken for its invention at this time. Chapter 5 documented that many Paleoindian communities already relied on diverse combinations of large game, small game, fish, and plant foods well before the terminal Pleistocene, with the specific balance depending heavily on regional environment. What changes in this period is less the existence of broad-spectrum practices than their frequency, intensity, and archaeological visibility in many, though not all, regions, alongside the disappearance of many formerly exploited megafaunal taxa, especially proboscideans such as mammoths and mastodons, as a significant dietary component in areas where they had previously been available.

This distinction matters because an older generation of archaeological interpretation sometimes described the shift from "Paleoindian" to "Archaic" lifeways as a story of technological and dietary progress, in which supposedly primitive big-game specialists were forced, by the disappearance of their preferred prey, to adopt a more sophisticated and varied diet. Current evidence does not support this narrative. Many Paleoindian communities, particularly outside the Great Plains, had already relied substantially on smaller game, aquatic resources, and plant foods, as Chapter 5 demonstrated. What changed after the terminal Pleistocene was primarily the disappearance of mammoths, mastodons, and other extinct megafaunal prey from many regional economies, a loss that was not accompanied by the disappearance of all large-bodied game, since the American bison, discussed further in Section 6.10, survived the extinctions and remained a major prey species in some regions. Alongside this taxon-specific loss, communities continued the refinement, regional specialization, and intensification of subsistence strategies that were, in most fundamental respects, already present.

Regional variation remained substantial. Communities near productive rivers, lakes, and coastlines increasingly exploited fish and shellfish, leaving behind, in some regions, the earliest substantial shell midden deposits, which begin to appear in the archaeological record of several coastal and riverine regions during the early Holocene. Communities in increasingly arid interior regions, including parts of the Great Basin discussed further in Chapter 8, developed subsistence strategies centered on seasonally available plant foods, including a range of seeds and roots, combined with hunting of smaller and medium-sized game such as deer, pronghorn, and rabbits, as the large lakes and their associated waterfowl populations discussed in Section 6.5 diminished.

Section 6.9

Mobility, Settlement, and Technology in a Changing Landscape

Changing resource distributions altered patterns of human mobility and settlement across the continent, though not in a single, uniform direction. In some regions, the disappearance of large, migratory megafauna, whose seasonal movements may have structured earlier patterns of long-distance human mobility, coincided with evidence for somewhat more geographically restricted, though still often seasonally mobile, patterns of land use, organized around the more predictable and spatially concentrated distribution of smaller game, fish, and plant resources. Archaeologists sometimes describe this shift as increasing "territorial circumscription," though the degree and pace of this change varied enormously by region and should not be treated as universal.

Repeated, sometimes intensive, use of particular locations, springs, streams, plant-gathering areas, and productive hunting locations, becomes increasingly visible in the archaeological record of many regions during this period, sometimes indicated by accumulating layers of debris and tool manufacture at a single site over long periods of use. This pattern does not necessarily indicate permanent settlement, which remained rare during this period in most regions, but it does suggest more structured, predictable patterns of seasonal return to particular places than some earlier interpretations of highly mobile Paleoindian lifeways had assumed.

Technological change accompanied these shifts, though, as with subsistence, more through gradual regional diversification than through sudden replacement. The distinctive, widely distributed fluted-point traditions discussed in Chapter 4 gave way, over the course of the terminal Pleistocene and early Holocene, to an increasing variety of regionally distinct projectile-point styles, reflecting both a general trend and considerable local experimentation. In some regions, tools associated with intensive plant processing, particularly grinding stones used to process seeds and other plant foods, become more common in the archaeological record during this period, though such tools were not entirely absent earlier, again cautioning against treating this as a sudden invention rather than an intensification of existing practices.

None of these technological and settlement changes should be understood as automatic responses dictated by the environment. Communities made choices about which resources to prioritize, which tools to develop or adapt, and how to organize their mobility, choices shaped by particular local circumstances, available materials, accumulated knowledge, and, quite plausibly, social and cultural preferences that archaeological evidence alone cannot fully recover. This chapter treats these adaptations as evidence of continued historical agency operating within a changing set of environmental constraints and opportunities, not as evidence of communities being mechanically reshaped by their surroundings.

Evidence Window

Reading a Lake Bed: Reconstructing Environmental Change from Sediment Cores

What the evidence is: Long cylindrical samples of sediment, called cores, extracted from the bottom of lakes, bogs, and wetlands, in which layers of accumulated material, including pollen grains, charcoal fragments, and mineral sediment, are preserved in roughly chronological order, oldest at the bottom and youngest near the top. Long pollen records of this kind have been recovered from numerous lake and bog basins across eastern North America, including well-studied sites in the southern Appalachians, and have been used by paleoecologists to document the regional forest expansion discussed in Section 6.5; comparable long cores from lake basins across the Great Basin have documented the shrinking of Pleistocene-age lakes over the same general period.

How researchers use it: By counting and identifying pollen grains preserved at different depths within a core, researchers can reconstruct changing vegetation around a lake or wetland over long periods of time, since different plant species produce pollen with distinctive, identifiable shapes. Radiocarbon dating of organic material within the core, combined in some cases with other dating methods, allows researchers to assign approximate ages to different layers, producing a chronological sequence of vegetation change. Charcoal fragments preserved within the same layers can provide evidence of past fire activity, while sediment composition can indicate changing lake levels, erosion rates, and water chemistry.

What it can establish: Sediment cores of this kind from sites across North America have allowed researchers to reconstruct the general sequence of vegetation change discussed in Section 6.5, including the timing and character of forest expansion in the East and lake desiccation in the Great Basin, with reasonable confidence for the broad regional patterns, even though specific dates and rates continue to be refined as additional cores are analyzed and dating methods improve.

What it cannot establish: A sediment core cannot, by itself, indicate human presence, activity, language, or identity. It also cannot resolve extremely local variation with precision, since pollen and charcoal in a core may derive from a mix of very local sources and materials transported from farther away, and different plant species produce and disperse pollen in different quantities, complicating direct comparison between species. Sediment cores from a single lake also cannot, on their own, establish broader regional or continental patterns; conclusions about continental-scale change depend on comparing many cores from many locations.

Questions for students:

  1. Why is it necessary to compare sediment cores from many different locations before drawing conclusions about continental-scale vegetation change?
  2. Why can a pollen record indicate changing vegetation without directly indicating human presence or absence at a site?
  3. What kinds of additional evidence would researchers need to connect a documented change in vegetation to a specific change in human subsistence or settlement at a nearby archaeological site?
  4. Why does dating uncertainty in sediment cores complicate efforts to establish precise cause-and-effect relationships between climatic events and human behavior?
Section 6.10

Regional Responses Across North America

The environmental transformations examined in this chapter produced no single continental response. This section surveys, in brief, how several major regions experienced the transition, previewing the more detailed regional treatments that follow in Chapters 7 through 12.

In the Eastern Woodlands, examined in detail in Chapter 7, the expansion of temperate deciduous forest through the terminal Pleistocene and early Holocene gradually replaced the more open, mixed environments that had supported some Paleoindian large-game hunting, and communities increasingly developed subsistence strategies suited to forest, riverine, and wetland environments, including nut collection, deer hunting, and, in many areas, fishing.

In the interior West, including the Great Basin examined in Chapter 8, the shrinking of Pleistocene lakes discussed in Section 6.5 reduced wetland habitats even as it exposed new terrain, and communities developed increasingly specialized strategies for exploiting seasonally available plant foods, smaller game, and remaining aquatic resources around the margins of shrinking lakes and marshes.

In California, examined in Chapter 9, the region's already substantial environmental diversity, from coastline to interior valleys to mountains, supported a variety of regional adaptations during this period, with increasing use of plant-processing technology and, in some areas, early evidence of intensified use of aquatic and coastal resources.

Along the Northwest Coast, examined in Chapter 10, changing sea levels and the gradual stabilization of coastal and estuarine environments during the early to middle Holocene laid the ecological groundwork for the highly productive maritime economies, based especially on salmon and other marine resources, that would develop more fully in later millennia.

In the Arctic and Subarctic, examined in Chapter 11, continued ice retreat and the gradual amelioration of climate opened new terrain to human use, though the pace and character of this process varied enormously between the rapidly changing southern margins of the retreating ice and the more slowly changing high Arctic environments that would not see substantial human settlement until later periods, examined further in Chapter 11.

On the Great Plains, the decline of megafauna, particularly mammoths, shifted hunting focus toward the American bison, a species that survived the terminal Pleistocene extinctions and would come to dominate Plains subsistence strategies for thousands of years, a development previewed here and examined further in Chapter 29's treatment of the Plains before the horse.

In the Southwest and Mexico, examined further in Chapter 13 and Chapter 15, communities responded to the drying trend affecting much of the interior West with increasingly intensive use of seasonally available plant resources, and, in parts of Mexico, this period saw the beginning of plant cultivation well ahead of similar developments elsewhere on the continent. The domesticated squash Cucurbita pepo is documented in highland Mexico by approximately ten thousand years ago, representing early, small-scale cultivation rather than a full agricultural economy, which would not develop widely until the much later period examined in Chapter 13. This early Mexican evidence is a useful reminder that this chapter's continental scope, extending through Mexico as established in the textbook's editorial framework, includes regions where food production began earlier than the hunting-gathering-fishing pattern that remained foundational across most of North America during this period.

This regional survey illustrates the chapter's central argument in concrete terms: communities across the continent were responding to genuinely different environmental circumstances, using different combinations of available resources and technologies, producing an increasingly regionally differentiated set of Indigenous societies rather than a single, uniform "post-glacial" way of life.

Section 6.11

From "Paleoindian" to "Archaic"?

Archaeologists conventionally divide the earliest periods of North American human history into a "Paleoindian" period, discussed in Chapter 5, and an "Archaic" period, examined beginning in Chapter 7. This chapter has already shown why the environmental changes underlying this transition were gradual and regionally variable. The same caution applies to the cultural periodization itself.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Paleoindian to Archaic Periodization

What Changed

  • The loss of many Pleistocene megafaunal prey taxa, especially proboscideans, from regional diets, while large-bodied survivors such as bison remained important prey in some regions, including the Great Plains
  • Increasing regional diversification of projectile-point styles and other tool traditions, replacing the wide geographic distribution of earlier fluted-point technologies
  • Increasing archaeological visibility of intensive plant-processing technology in many, though not all, regions
  • More geographically structured, though still often seasonally mobile, patterns of land use in many regions

What Stayed the Same

  • Hunting, gathering, and fishing remained foundational across most regions, although early plant cultivation and domestication had begun in parts of Mexico during the early Holocene, examined in Section 6.10; agricultural economies were not yet widespread across the continent, a development examined much later in Chapter 13
  • Substantial regional diversity in subsistence and technology, a pattern already well established in the Paleoindian period documented in Chapter 5
  • Small-scale, kin-based social organization, so far as the limited evidence allows historians to judge
  • Continued reliance on stone tool technology, oral transmission of knowledge, and mobile or semi-mobile settlement in most regions

Why the pattern matters: Treating "Paleoindian" and "Archaic" as two sharply distinct cultures, separated by a single transformative event, misrepresents a transition that was gradual, regionally staggered by as much as several thousand years, and built upon subsistence and technological practices that were substantially already present. The labels remain useful for organizing archaeological discussion, provided students understand them as convenient analytical categories rather than as natural, sharply bounded historical stages.

Because the transition depended on regionally specific environmental and cultural developments, archaeologists date the beginning of the "Archaic" differently in different regions, sometimes placing it as early as roughly 11,500 years ago in some eastern locations and considerably later in other regions where environmental change proceeded more slowly or where distinctive Paleoindian technological traditions persisted longer. This variability is not a flaw in the periodization scheme; it is an accurate reflection of a genuinely uneven historical process, and this textbook uses "Paleoindian" and "Archaic" throughout with this caution in mind, specifying regional and chronological context wherever precision matters.

Cause and Effect

Megafaunal Decline and Human Adaptation

Long-term environmental pressures

Orbital-driven climatic warming after the Last Glacial Maximum; long-term ecological adjustment of plant and animal communities to changing temperature and precipitation regimes across the deglacial sequence described in Sections 6.2 and 6.5.

Debated contributing pressures

The Younger Dryas cooling reversal and its regionally variable effects; possible human hunting pressure, whose scale and significance remain genuinely contested, examined in the unresolved debate in Section 6.7; rapid, non-analog reorganization of vegetation communities.

Mainland extinction pattern

Best-dated last appearances of numerous mainland North American megafaunal genera cluster within the interval of roughly 13,800 to 11,400 years ago, though timing remains less secure for many other extinct genera, accompanied by broad ecological and vegetational reorganization.

Associated human changes

Diversification of subsistence strategies toward smaller game, fish, and plant foods; increasing regional technological differentiation; more structured, though still generally mobile, settlement patterns; gradual emergence of regionally distinct Archaic-period traditions.

Historical complexity: The developments listed as "Associated human changes" coincided with, and interacted with, the environmental and faunal changes traced in this chapter, but they should not be read as effects demonstrably caused by megafaunal extinction alone. As Section 6.8 emphasizes, broad-spectrum subsistence, regional technological diversity, and structured settlement patterns were already present in many Paleoindian communities before megafaunal decline, as Chapter 5 established, meaning that later intensification built upon existing knowledge and practice rather than representing a new set of survival strategies invented in direct response to extinction.

Section 6.12

A New Holocene Continent

By roughly eight thousand years ago, North America had become, in most fundamental respects, an environmentally different continent than the one Paleoindian communities had first encountered, although the transformation was not yet complete. The Laurentide Ice Sheet had fragmented dramatically and was undergoing rapid final retreat, particularly after Hudson Bay became seasonally ice-free around 8,200 years ago, but substantial remnant ice still survived in northern Canada at this date; complete Laurentide deglaciation followed later, around 6,700 years ago. Sea levels stood closer to their modern positions than they had at the height of the Ice Age, though continued adjustment, including ongoing isostatic rebound in formerly glaciated regions, would proceed for thousands of years more. Most of the continent's largest Pleistocene mammals were gone from the mainland, surviving in only a few isolated, geographically restricted island populations, some of which, as Section 6.6 showed, would persist for thousands of years yet. Plant communities had reorganized into associations that, in most regions, would be broadly recognizable to later observers, even as further changes continued to unfold over subsequent millennia.

Indigenous communities met this transformation not with a single, uniform response, but with an increasingly diverse array of regionally specific adaptations, building upon subsistence and technological knowledge that had already been diverse during the Paleoindian period documented in Chapter 5. The disappearance of many megafaunal prey taxa, the reorganization of plant and smaller animal resources, and the changing shape of coastlines, lakes, and river systems together created new constraints and, in many cases, new opportunities. As Section 6.9 emphasized, these environmental circumstances did not mechanically dictate the responses communities made; inherited knowledge, social relationships, technological traditions, and historically contingent choices shaped how particular communities addressed these changing constraints and opportunities, producing the gradual development of increasingly regionally distinct ways of life.

This process of regional differentiation, only briefly previewed in Section 6.10, becomes the central subject of Part II of this volume. Chapter 7 examines in detail the Archaic-period societies of eastern North America, tracing how the forest expansion and riverine adaptation discussed in this chapter developed into increasingly organizationally diverse regional economies and social patterns. Subsequent chapters extend this regional treatment to the desert West, California, the Northwest Coast, and the Arctic and Subarctic, before Chapter 12 draws these regional threads together to examine the exchange networks and ceremonial developments that increasingly connected these regionally distinct worlds.

The environmental transformation examined in this chapter therefore functions as a hinge in this textbook's larger narrative. It closes the story, begun in Chapter 2 and developed through Chapters 3 through 5, of a continent's initial peopling and the emergence of widespread technological signals, such as the Clovis tradition, within already regionally diverse Paleoindian worlds. It opens the much longer and more regionally varied story, extending through the remainder of Volume I and into Volume II, of increasingly differentiated Indigenous societies developing on their own terms across an environmentally transformed continent.

Multiple Perspectives

Living Through Environmental Change: Reconstructed Perspectives

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns archaeologists have inferred from evidence. They are not quotations from any named individual, since no written record survives from this period.

From the perspective of a community relying on Great Basin wetland resources: Generations of accumulated knowledge about where waterfowl nested, where marsh plants could be gathered, and where fish concentrated would have required steady revision as favored lakes and marshes gradually shrank, a change likely more perceptible across a lifetime than within any single season.
From the perspective of a hunting community on the Great Plains: The disappearance of mammoths and other large prey over the course of some generations, while the American bison remained available in substantial numbers, likely encouraged an increasing focus on bison hunting strategies, a pattern of specialization archaeologists trace forward across thousands of subsequent years, examined further in Chapter 29.
From the perspective of a community in the expanding eastern forests: As deciduous forest replaced more open, mixed environments across many generations, new resources, including nuts, deer, and forest-adapted small game, would have become increasingly available, requiring corresponding adjustments in tool use, seasonal movement, and food storage or processing.
From the perspective of a modern archaeologist or paleoclimatologist: Reconstructing this transition requires synthesizing multiple independent and sometimes imperfectly aligned lines of evidence, sediment cores, radiocarbon dates, archaeological assemblages, and faunal remains, each with its own margin of uncertainty, meaning that confident, precise, region-by-region reconstruction remains an ongoing and incomplete scientific project.
Everyday Life and Social History

Experiencing Gradual Change Across Generations

Environmental change of the kind examined in this chapter unfolded too slowly to have been experienced by any single individual as a sudden crisis in most cases, though localized episodes, such as the failure of a particular water source or the sudden reduction of a previously reliable resource, may well have registered as urgent within a single lifetime. For most communities, adaptation to a changing world was less a single dramatic response than an accumulated, intergenerational process: children learning slightly different combinations of plant knowledge, hunting technique, and seasonal movement than their parents and grandparents had known, transmitted through observation, practice, and oral instruction rather than through any single innovative moment.

This intergenerational character of environmental adaptation is difficult to recover directly from archaeological evidence, which more readily documents changes in tool types, food remains, and settlement patterns than the social and educational processes through which such changes were transmitted and normalized within communities. Historians and archaeologists must therefore infer this dimension of everyday life carefully, drawing on ethnographic analogy with later, better-documented hunting and gathering societies, while remaining cautious about projecting later cultural patterns uncritically onto communities separated from those later societies by many thousands of years.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined one of the most significant environmental transformations in North American history: the transition from the glaciated, Pleistocene world described in Chapters 3 through 5 to the increasingly regionally differentiated Holocene world that leads into Part II of this volume. It has emphasized throughout that this transformation was not a single continental event but a gradual, asynchronous, and regionally variable set of processes unfolding across several thousand years.

The chapter began by distinguishing the geological Pleistocene-Holocene boundary from the archaeological Paleoindian-Archaic boundary, a distinction that recurs throughout the chapter's treatment of both environmental and cultural change. It then traced the broad deglacial sequence, from the retreat of the Laurentide and Cordilleran ice sheets through the Younger Dryas cooling interval, emphasizing that this interval's causes remain debated and its effects were felt unevenly across the continent.

The chapter examined how retreating ice, proglacial lakes such as Glacial Lake Agassiz, and rising global sea levels reorganized drainage systems and coastlines, submerging substantial portions of the terminal Pleistocene landscape and, with it, a portion of the archaeological record relevant to earlier chapters' discussion of coastal migration. It then showed how plant communities reorganized independently rather than migrating as intact units, producing divergent regional trajectories, expanding forest in much of the East, and shrinking lakes across much of the Great Basin and interior West.

Turning to the megafaunal extinctions, the chapter established the general chronology and scale of this faunal turnover while emphasizing, through the example of the St. Paul Island mammoths, that extinction was staggered across thousands of years rather than instantaneous. The chapter then presented, without declaring a single winner, the major scholarly interpretations of what caused these extinctions: human hunting, climatic and ecological change, and multicausal models combining both factors, along with the specific evidence and points of disagreement associated with each.

The chapter examined how subsistence strategies diversified in this period, cautioning against the common misconception that broad-spectrum subsistence was invented only after megafauna disappeared, since Chapter 5 had already documented its earlier presence in many regions. It then traced corresponding changes in mobility, settlement, and technology, again emphasizing gradual regional diversification over sudden replacement, before surveying, in brief, how several major regions of North America experienced this transition differently, previewing the detailed regional chapters that follow.

Finally, the chapter examined the archaeological convention of dividing this history into "Paleoindian" and "Archaic" periods, arguing that this periodization, while useful, describes a gradual and regionally staggered transition rather than a single moment of cultural transformation, and closed by explicitly identifying this chapter's role as the hinge between the peopling and Paleoindian narrative of Chapters 1 through 5 and the increasingly regionalized Indigenous histories that begin with Chapter 7.

Students completing this chapter should understand not only what environmental changes occurred at the end of the Ice Age, but also why those changes proceeded unevenly across the continent, what kinds of evidence allow scientists to reconstruct them, why the megafaunal extinction debate remains open rather than settled, and why the transition from Paleoindian to Archaic lifeways should be understood as a gradual process of regional adaptation rather than a single cultural replacement.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Pleistocene
The geological epoch, characterized by repeated glacial cycles, that preceded the Holocene, conventionally ending approximately 11,700 years ago.
Holocene
The current geological epoch, beginning approximately 11,700 years ago, marked by generally warmer and more climatically stable conditions than the preceding Pleistocene.
Deglaciation
The process by which glacial ice sheets retreat and melt as climatic conditions warm.
Younger Dryas
A roughly thousand-year cooling interval beginning around 12,900 years before present, whose causes remain debated and whose regional effects varied considerably.
Last Glacial Maximum
The period, extending roughly from 26,500 to 19,000–20,000 years ago, when most Pleistocene ice sheets stood at or near their greatest extent, though individual sectors reached local maxima at different times, discussed in Chapter 3.
Proglacial lake
A lake formed by glacial meltwater trapped between a retreating ice sheet and higher terrain, such as Glacial Lake Agassiz.
Meltwater pulse
A large, sometimes rapid release of glacial meltwater into the ocean, potentially affecting sea level and ocean circulation.
Isostatic rebound
The gradual rise of land that had been depressed by the weight of glacial ice, occurring over thousands of years after deglaciation.
Megafauna
Large-bodied animal species, such as mammoths, mastodons, and giant ground sloths, most of which became extinct in North America at the end of the Pleistocene.
Overkill hypothesis
The interpretive position that human hunting was the primary cause of late Pleistocene megafaunal extinction.
Multicausal model
An explanation for megafaunal extinction that combines human hunting pressure with climatic and ecological stress rather than attributing extinction to a single cause.
Broad-spectrum subsistence
A subsistence strategy drawing on a wide range of food resources, including small game, fish, shellfish, and diverse plant foods.
Faunal turnover
A significant change in the composition of animal species present in a region or period, such as the disappearance of Pleistocene megafauna.
Paleoindian
The archaeological period, discussed in Chapter 5, describing the earliest well-documented North American cultural traditions, generally preceding the Archaic period.
Archaic
An archaeological period label describing technological, subsistence, and settlement patterns that developed at different times in different regions following the Paleoindian period.
Sediment core
A cylindrical sample extracted from lake, bog, or ocean sediment, used to reconstruct past environmental conditions through layered evidence.
Pollen record
Evidence of past vegetation derived from the identification and dating of pollen grains preserved in sediment.
Ice core
A cylindrical sample of ice extracted from a glacier or ice sheet, used to reconstruct past temperature and atmospheric composition.
Radiocarbon dating
A dating method based on the decay of radioactive carbon-14, used to estimate the age of organic material.
Sea-level rise
The increase in global sea level caused by the melting of glacial ice, which submerged portions of the terminal Pleistocene coastline.
Ecological zone
A region characterized by a particular, broadly consistent combination of climate, vegetation, and animal life.
Regional differentiation
The historical process by which increasingly distinct regional cultural and technological traditions developed across North America following the terminal Pleistocene.
Glacial Lake Agassiz
The largest of the proglacial lakes formed by Laurentide Ice Sheet meltwater, whose periodic drainage reorganized drainage systems across the northern Plains and Great Lakes region.
Lake Bonneville
A large Pleistocene lake in the Great Basin, whose much smaller remnant is the modern Great Salt Lake.
Grinding stone
A stone tool used to process plant foods such as seeds and nuts, increasingly visible in the archaeological record of the terminal Pleistocene and early Holocene in many regions.

Major People, Sites, and Concepts

Identification & Significance

The Younger Dryas
A roughly thousand-year cooling interval that interrupted deglacial warming, examined in Section 6.3 as evidence of the non-linear character of Ice Age termination.
Glacial Lake Agassiz
The largest North American proglacial lake, whose drainage events reorganized regional drainage systems and may have influenced climate, discussed in Section 6.4.
Lake Bonneville and Lake Lahontan
Major Pleistocene lakes of the Great Basin whose desiccation through the early Holocene reshaped regional subsistence strategies, examined in Section 6.5 and further in Chapter 8.
St. Paul Island mammoths
An isolated woolly mammoth population that survived until approximately 5,600 years ago, providing direct evidence of staggered megafaunal extinction, discussed in Section 6.6.
The overkill hypothesis
A major interpretive position in the megafaunal extinction debate, attributing extinction primarily to human hunting pressure, examined in Section 6.7.
Broad-spectrum subsistence
An increasingly visible subsistence pattern involving diverse small game, fish, and plant foods, examined in Section 6.8.
The Archaic period
The archaeological period label applied to the regionally variable technological and subsistence patterns that followed the Paleoindian period, examined critically in Section 6.11 and treated in detail in Chapters 7 through 12.

This project asks you to investigate a specific environmental record, extinct species, or regional case discussed in this chapter, using library and reference resources to examine how scientists reconstruct evidence from this period and how interpretations have developed or changed over time.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has served as a hinge between two large sections of this textbook's early narrative. It closes the story, developed across Chapters 2 through 5, of a continent's initial peopling and the emergence of widespread technological signals, including the Clovis tradition, within Paleoindian worlds that Chapter 5 showed were already regionally diverse in subsistence, technology, and social organization. It opens the much longer and more regionally varied story that occupies the remainder of Volume I and continues through Volume II, in which increasingly differentiated regional societies developed their own technologies, economies, and, eventually, political and ceremonial systems across an environmentally transformed continent.

The habits of mind this chapter has modeled, distinguishing geological from archaeological periodization, resisting simple single-cause explanations for complex historical and environmental developments, recognizing regional variation within seemingly continental-scale processes, and treating scientific uncertainty honestly rather than resolving it artificially, are not incidental to this chapter alone. They are essential tools for engaging responsibly with the deep, indirectly evidenced past that much of this textbook's early material addresses, and they recur throughout the chapters that follow.

Understanding that the end of the Ice Age was neither instantaneous nor uniform helps explain why the Indigenous societies examined in the remainder of this volume differed so substantially from one region to another, but environmental circumstance alone does not tell the whole story. Communities in the Eastern Woodlands, the Great Basin, California, the Northwest Coast, and the Arctic and Subarctic faced genuinely different environmental circumstances, traced in this chapter from their origins in the retreat of Pleistocene ice sheets and the reorganization of the continent's plant and animal communities, but, as Section 6.9 emphasized, those circumstances supplied changing constraints and opportunities rather than dictating particular outcomes. Inherited knowledge, social relationships, technological traditions, and historically contingent choices shaped how each community responded to its changing surroundings, a form of continued historical agency this textbook treats as central rather than incidental.

The chapters that follow, beginning with Chapter 7's treatment of the Eastern Archaic, take up the regional threads this chapter has only briefly previewed, tracing how the environmental foundations established here developed into the increasingly differentiated, organizationally diverse Indigenous societies that occupied the remainder of the pre-agricultural and, eventually, agricultural history examined throughout Volumes I and II of this textbook.
