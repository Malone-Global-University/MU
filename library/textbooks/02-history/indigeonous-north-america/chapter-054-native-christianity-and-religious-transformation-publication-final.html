Native Christianity and Religious Transformation

Indigenous Peoples of North America: A Continental History

Chapter Opening

Christianity reached Indigenous North America through conquest, trade, family ties, famine, epidemic, diplomacy, and conviction, and it arrived in languages and institutions that Native peoples did not control. What they did with it, over generations and across a continent, became part of Indigenous history.

In 1663 a Bible appeared from a printing press in Cambridge, Massachusetts. It was not in English. It was a complete translation of the Old and New Testaments into the Massachusett language, an Algonquian language spoken by communities of southern New England, and its pages were set in type with the assistance of Native men, including a Nipmuc printer later known as James Printer. Few books of the seventeenth century illustrate so compactly the problem this chapter takes up. The translation was organized by an English minister, John Eliot, and it served the aims of an English colonial missionary enterprise. It was also, unavoidably, a work of Indigenous language, produced with Indigenous collaborators, and read by Native congregations who had their own reasons for engaging with its contents. Whose book was it?

That question can be asked in many places. In central Mexico, Nahua painters and scribes helped Franciscan friars produce doctrinal texts in Nahuatl in the generations after the Spanish conquest. In the Florida panhandle, Apalachee leaders hosted missionaries in their towns. In Pueblo country, priests and kiva societies pursued competing claims on the same communities until 1680. At Kahnawake on the St. Lawrence, Mohawk and other Haudenosaunee Catholics built a community whose members were both devout and unmistakably Indigenous. In Labrador, Inuit families came to the Moravian settlements at Nain, Okak, and Hopedale, and in Alaska, Alutiiq (Sugpiaq) communities met Orthodox monks while living under the pressures of the Russian fur trade. Each setting produced a different relationship between Native people and Christianity, because each was shaped by different imperial powers, different missionary organizations, different Indigenous societies, and different circumstances of power.

This chapter, which concludes Part IX, studies that diversity. It asks how Indigenous peoples across the continent encountered, interpreted, resisted, adopted, and transformed Christianity between roughly 1520 and 1821. It does not treat missionary history as the history of Native people's religion, and it does not assume that conversion meant the replacement of one belief system by another. Conversion could be compelled, negotiated, strategic, devotional, or all of these at once. Baptism recorded in a mission register might reflect a sincere commitment, a family decision, a response to epidemic crisis, or institutional pressure, and historians cannot always tell which. Communities that resisted a missionary might remain committed to Christian practice; communities that accepted baptism might protect ceremonies the missionary never saw.

The chapter's central argument is therefore historical rather than theological. Colonial coercion and missionary authority shaped the conditions under which Native peoples encountered Christianity, but neither determined what Indigenous communities believed, how they practiced religion, or how they understood their identities. Native Christian traditions, including Nahua and Maya Catholicism, Pueblo and Yaqui ritual life, Wendat and Mohawk devotion, Algonquian Protestant congregations, the writing and ministry of Samson Occom, Moravian Lenape and Inuit communities, and Alaskan Orthodoxy, became part of Indigenous history and not merely extensions of European churches.

The evidence for this history is unusual and demanding. Much of it was written by missionaries, whose purposes were evangelical and institutional. Alongside it survive Indigenous-language catechisms and sermons, confraternity account books, town council records, letters, petitions, and, in some cases, texts written by Native authors. Archaeology, oral tradition, and community histories supply additional perspectives. Because each source type is shaped by its creator's purposes, Section 54.4 gives special attention to how historians read these materials, and the chapter's source windows model that work.

Placement within the textbook matters. Earlier chapters examined the Spanish conquest (Chapter 39), epidemic catastrophe (Chapter 40), the Spanish Southeast and northern frontier (Chapters 41 and 42), Pueblo colonization and revolt (Chapters 43 and 44), New France and the Great Lakes (Chapter 45), English Atlantic colonization (Chapter 48), and coerced labor and captivity (Chapter 53). This chapter does not retell those histories. It asks a different question of them: what happened to Indigenous religious life, and what did Native people themselves make of the religion that colonial agents brought? Chapter 55 will then turn to Indigenous diplomacy and the balance of power in the age of imperial rivalry, and Chapter 62 will return to religious revitalization in the early nineteenth century.

None of this is meant to soften the violence that accompanied many missions. Corporal punishment, forced relocation, destruction of ceremonial objects, trials for idolatry, and massacres of Christian Native people all appear in the pages ahead. But a history that recorded only coercion would also miss what Indigenous people built: congregations, schools, confraternities, hymn traditions, translations, networks of kin and belief, and arguments within the churches themselves about justice and authority. Both parts of the story belong to Indigenous history.

  7. Trace the development of Christian communities in Spanish Florida, the Pueblo world, California, and New France, and explain how political context shaped each.

Historical Context

By the early sixteenth century, the Indigenous societies of North America possessed long-established religious systems. These included the state cults and calendrical ritual cycles of central Mexico and the Maya region, the clan and society-based ceremonial organization of the Pueblo world, the mound-center religion and town ceremonial life of the Southeast, the clan and longhouse ceremonies of the Northeast, the shamanic and ancestral practices of Arctic communities, and the potlatch-centered ceremonial economies of the Northwest Coast. Earlier chapters examined the economic, political, and social worlds in which these systems operated. What matters here is that religion was not a separate department of life. It organized agriculture, healing, diplomacy, kinship, and law, and so a missionary's demands rarely touched belief alone. They touched marriage, political authority, ceremonial labor, and the calendar.

Christianity itself was not a single thing. Roman Catholic missionary orders differed from one another, and the Protestant and Orthodox traditions that reached Indigenous people differed again. Catholicism in the Spanish empire operated under the patronato real, an arrangement in which the Crown held broad responsibilities for the church's expansion in the Americas and the Spanish state treated the conversion of Native peoples as a legal justification for its presence. French Catholic missions operated in a different political environment, in which the Crown and Jesuits depended heavily on Indigenous alliances and trading relationships. English Protestant mission efforts were more diffuse, often organized through voluntary societies, colonial governments, and individual ministers. Moravian, Lutheran, and Orthodox missions each had their own institutional structures and their own relationships to commerce and state power.

Historians of this subject also have to contend with the changing character of the scholarship. Older missionary-centered histories celebrated or criticized the missionaries. Mid-twentieth-century approaches often analyzed Native religion through the lens of cultural loss and acculturation. More recent work, drawing on Indigenous-language sources, community records, archaeology, and Indigenous scholarship, has emphasized Native interpretation, local institutions, and continuity. This chapter follows that more recent direction while retaining the older literature's attention to colonial power. It is attentive, too, to a difficulty: some Indigenous people today are Christian and some are not, and communities hold differing and sometimes sharply divided views of the missionary past. A responsible textbook does not resolve that debate and should not treat it as settled.

Finally, the chronology of this chapter is deliberately broad. Missions began in central Mexico in the 1520s and in Alaska in 1794; some communities experienced Christianity within a single generation of contact while others encountered it centuries after first European arrivals, and others, as Chapter 62 will show, developed Indigenous religious movements partly in response to it. The framework of approximately 1520 to 1821 is a planning boundary and not a claim that Christianity's meaning for Native peoples stabilized then.

Indigenous Religious Worlds, Christian Encounters, and Historical Interpretation

How can historians distinguish Indigenous religious agency from the institutional and political pressures surrounding Christian conversion?

Section 54.1

Native Christianity as an Indigenous Historical Problem

Histories of Christianity in North America were long written as histories of missionaries: their journeys, hardships, theology, and institutions. Native people appeared as audiences, converts, or obstacles. This chapter reverses the emphasis without denying the missionaries' importance. Missionaries did bring Christianity, and mission institutions often had enormous power over the lives of Indigenous people. But the history of a religion in a community is also the history of what that community's members did with it: which parts they took up, which they left aside, which they reinterpreted, and how they used church institutions for purposes the missionaries had not planned. Seen this way, Native Christianity is an Indigenous historical problem as well as a missionary one.

Several terms need careful handling. Missionization refers to the organized effort to bring a population into a church, usually involving the establishment of missions, instruction, and sacraments. Conversion is far harder to define. For a missionary, it meant acceptance of Christian doctrine and entry into the church through baptism. For historians, it names a range of experiences that can differ sharply, from sincere religious transformation to outward compliance. Baptism was the ritual of entry, and mission registers recording baptisms are among the most abundant sources on Indigenous Christianity, but a baptism cannot by itself establish what the baptized person believed. Some baptisms were deathbed rites for the sick; some were of infants whose parents had made the decision; some followed years of instruction; some were administered in large numbers under conditions that gave Native people few alternatives.

Historians therefore distinguish several levels of religious change. Formal affiliation means inclusion in church records. Participation means taking part in services, feast days, or confraternity life. Personal belief concerns what individuals understood and held to be true, which sources reveal only indirectly. Community-level transformation concerns whether a community's institutions, calendar, leadership, and moral vocabulary came to be organized partly around Christian forms. These levels can diverge. A town might hold elaborate saints' festivals while its members regarded the saints within a long-established framework of reciprocal obligation; an individual might be a devoted Christian while the community around her remained largely committed to older ceremonies.

The distinction has practical consequences for interpreting mission statistics. A missionary might count baptisms to demonstrate progress to a patron in Mexico City, Paris, London, or St. Petersburg. A Native town council might instead ask whether a resident priest could help defend its land, whether a Christian teacher could educate its children, or whether access to the sacraments would strengthen ties with another community. Neither record necessarily describes the private commitments of every participant. In a crisis, those calculations could change abruptly: a settlement might request religious instruction, reject an overbearing missionary, and later sustain its own congregation. Historians therefore need to follow institutions over time rather than treating an initial baptism as the end of a story. A church building, translated prayer, elected religious officer, or dispute over a feast may reveal successive decisions by people who did not agree with one another. This is especially important when colonial archives preserve the voices of officials more readily than those of Indigenous families.

Historical Vocabulary
Conversion, Missionization, and Indigenous Christianity

Definition. Conversion is the adoption of Christian belief and practice. Missionization is the institutional process by which churches and colonial authorities organized instruction, baptism, and settlement. Indigenous Christianity (or Native Christianity) refers to Christian belief and practice as held, interpreted, and organized by Indigenous people and communities, in their own languages and institutions.

Historical context. Missionaries often used the language of "conversion" as though it were a single event. In practice, Native peoples came to Christianity gradually, selectively, and for varied reasons: spiritual conviction, family ties, protection from enemies or epidemics, access to food and trade goods, political alliance, or legal compulsion. Missionization could be voluntary, negotiated, or imposed, and these categories often overlapped within a single community.

Why it matters. Treating conversion as a single outcome obscures the ways Native people shaped religious life. Using "Indigenous Christianity" recognizes that Christian practice in Native communities developed within Indigenous histories.

Potential misunderstanding. "Indigenous Christianity" does not imply that Native Christians were less Christian, or less Indigenous, than anyone else. Retaining a Native language, kinship system, or ceremonial responsibility alongside Christian practice does not make that practice inauthentic.

Historical Vocabulary
Syncretism

Definition. In religious studies, syncretism describes the combination of elements from different religious traditions.

Historical context. Scholars of colonial Mexico and elsewhere have used the term to describe forms of religious practice in which Indigenous and Christian elements appear together. Critics note that it can imply that one tradition is "pure" and the combination is a contamination, even though every religious tradition, including European Christianity, has developed through borrowing and reinterpretation. The term can also treat blended practices as though they were the work of passive recipients, when communities often deliberately selected and reshaped elements.

Why it matters. The word can describe particular patterns, such as the treatment of a saint's image within local reciprocal obligations, but historians increasingly prefer descriptions of the specific beliefs, practices, and decisions involved. Section 54.19 returns to the debate.

Potential misunderstanding. Syncretism does not mean a fixed "mixture" of two stable religions. It describes ongoing processes of interpretation by people who typically understood their own practice as coherent.

Section 54.2

Indigenous Spiritual Traditions and the Religious Worlds of Early Contact

No single "Native religion" existed for Christianity to encounter. Indigenous spiritual life varied as widely as language, environment, and political structure, and understanding that variety is a precondition for understanding how Native people responded to missionaries. The examples below are illustrative, not comprehensive.

In central Mexico, Nahua religion was organized around a complex ritual calendar, temple institutions, specialized priesthoods, and the veneration of deities associated with agriculture, rain, war, and the sun. Religious obligations were collective and tied to the altepetl, the local ethnic polity, whose patron deity and ceremonies were integral to political identity. Maya communities in Yucatán and neighboring regions also maintained calendrical ritual systems, ancestor veneration, and ceremonial specialists, and Maya written and painted traditions recorded cosmological and calendrical knowledge. In both regions, religious practice was entangled with political authority, and the Spanish conquest disrupted state-sponsored cults even where household and community practice continued.

Farther north, the Pueblo peoples of the Rio Grande region practiced religion through societies and kiva-based ceremonies that structured the agricultural year, with ritual specialists responsible for rain, fertility, healing, and community balance. Ceremonial obligations were distributed across lineages, clans, and societies, and religious knowledge was often restricted to initiated members. In the Southeast, the Timucua, Guale, and Apalachee lived in chiefdoms whose leaders held ceremonial as well as political roles, and the heritage of mound-center religion shaped ritual life. In the Northeast, Wendat and Haudenosaunee communities maintained ceremonial cycles connected to agriculture and the seasons, rituals of healing and dream interpretation, and obligations of mourning, as discussed in earlier chapters. Algonquian peoples of southern New England held diverse beliefs concerning spiritual power, healing, and the role of religious specialists (often called powwows in English sources). In the Arctic, Inuit spiritual specialists, known in Greenlandic as angakkut (singular angakkoq), mediated relationships with spirit beings associated with animals and the sea, and ritual observances governed hunting and relationships with game. On the Northwest Coast and in southern Alaska, ceremonial life was bound to kinship, rank, and hereditary privileges.

Three features of these traditions shaped the encounters with Christianity. First, religion and community life were fused, so a missionary's message about a new god or a new moral code was never only a spiritual claim. It was a claim about authority, kinship, and ritual obligation, and it could challenge or reinforce existing leaders. Second, many Indigenous traditions emphasized spiritual power as something demonstrated through effects, including healing, success in hunting or war, and survival. Native audiences often evaluated Christian claims by those standards, which helps explain why epidemics, famines, and the failure of older ritual protections sometimes increased interest in Christian rites and sometimes produced fierce rejection of them. Third, many Indigenous religious systems were accommodating of additional sources of power. A community might add a new ritual or being to its repertoire without seeing a contradiction, while missionaries demanded exclusive commitment, a demand that Native people often found unfamiliar and sometimes unacceptable.

None of this means that Indigenous people were unable to understand Christianity as its missionaries intended. Native listeners could grasp the exclusivity of a missionary's claims and weighed them against their own experience. Many did commit exclusively; others did not; still others accepted Christian elements within a framework the missionary would not have recognized. The point is that Native responses were intelligent interpretations from within existing systems of knowledge.

Religious specialists did not everywhere possess the same political authority, and practices summarized by colonists under terms such as 'sorcery' or 'idolatry' often served different purposes. A Pueblo ceremonial society was not equivalent to a Nahua temple priesthood, and neither can be treated as a direct counterpart of an Inuit angakkoq. The surviving accounts also vary in reliability: a missionary trying to condemn a ceremony might emphasize its supposed hostility to Christianity, whereas a Native explanation could center on healing, responsibility to ancestors, or the maintenance of relations with the land. Historians must therefore avoid both romanticizing an unchanged precontact spirituality and assuming that Europeans described Indigenous concepts accurately. Contacts took place amid epidemics, migration, war, and disruptions of subsistence, so religious decisions were made in changing communities. A practice preserved through those circumstances was not simply a remnant. It might be defended, relocated, modified, or taught anew, sometimes alongside Christian observance and sometimes in open opposition to it.

Section 54.3

Catholic Missions, Protestant Evangelization, and the Colonial Uses of Christianity

Missionary Christianity came in several institutional forms, and the differences among them mattered to Native communities. In the Spanish empire, the principal Catholic orders were the Franciscans, Dominicans, Augustinians, and, after 1572 in New Spain, the Jesuits. The Franciscans, who began organized work in central Mexico in 1524, generally sought to establish parish-like instruction in or near existing Indigenous towns, and they emphasized learning Indigenous languages and teaching through catechisms and pictorial aids. Dominicans were prominent in Oaxaca and southern Mexico, where they worked among Mixtec and Zapotec communities. Jesuits, a newer order with a strong educational orientation, established mission pueblos in northwestern New Spain and in Sonora, Sinaloa, and neighboring regions, and they also played a major role in New France.

Spanish missions were embedded in imperial policy. Under the patronato real, the Crown shouldered responsibility for Christianizing Indigenous populations and in return exercised extensive control over ecclesiastical appointments and mission funding. On frontiers, the mission often served as an institution of settlement as well as religion: a mission pueblo concentrated Native populations, introduced agriculture and livestock, and could function as a base for further colonial expansion, with military escorts (presidios) nearby. The practice of congregating dispersed populations into compact towns, known as reducción or congregación, was a major element of Spanish policy in many regions, and it is one reason Native communities were often as concerned about the political and economic effects of missions as about their doctrine.

French Catholic missions in the St. Lawrence and Great Lakes region were shaped by a different environment. French colonists were few, and the Crown and the missionaries depended on Indigenous diplomatic and commercial partners. Jesuits therefore lived among Native communities, learned their languages, and often worked within Indigenous political systems, while the Crown's policies and the fur trade shaped their opportunities. Chapter 45 treats the diplomacy and trade of this world; this chapter focuses on religious life within it.

English Protestant evangelization was more fragmentary. The Puritan colonies of New England saw some ministers undertake missions to nearby Algonquian communities in the mid-seventeenth century, supported partly by a corporation established in London for the purpose. In the eighteenth century, voluntary religious societies, evangelical revivals, and Anglican organizations supported missions in various colonies. Protestant missions emphasized scripture, literacy, and preaching in Native languages, and they often expected converts to adopt English-style social patterns as evidence of piety. The Moravians, a Protestant renewal movement centered on communal congregations, established mission communities in the eighteenth century among Mahican, Lenape, and other Native peoples and, with Lutheran missionaries, among Inuit in the Arctic. Russian Orthodox missions came later, from 1794, in Alaska.

It is essential to distinguish individual missionaries' religious motives from the political and economic purposes of colonial governments. Many missionaries sincerely believed they were saving souls and often endured hardship, illness, and death. Some defended Native people against colonial abuses; others participated in or justified coercion. Colonial officials, meanwhile, frequently treated mission institutions as instruments of control, settlement, and labor mobilization, and invoked the goal of Christianization to legitimize conquest. Native peoples recognized these distinctions. They sometimes sought missionaries precisely because they hoped a mission would provide spiritual resources, protection, trade goods, or an intermediary with colonial authorities, while remaining wary of the demands that came with it.

Institutional arrangements also affected what Christians could ask of Native communities. A mendicant friar attached to an Indigenous town, a French Jesuit dependent on the hospitality of a trading partner, and a Moravian minister overseeing a congregation's daily routines did not wield identical kinds of power. The availability of soldiers, the reach of colonial courts, the funding of schools, and the proximity of settlers all mattered. Royal permission to evangelize did not necessarily mean local officials could enforce every demand; conversely, a mission promising protection could become a site of confinement or labor extraction. The categories 'Catholic,' 'Protestant,' and 'Orthodox' identify theological traditions but do not explain daily conditions by themselves. For a family considering baptism, the decisive issues might be whether it could remain near relatives, continue hunting and farming, dispose of its own produce, or select community leaders. This is why comparisons throughout the chapter consider land, labor, and political jurisdiction together with worship and doctrine.

Cause and Effect

Missionization, Colonial Power, and Conversion

Long-term causes

European religious and imperial ideologies that treated Christianization as a justification for expansion; the institutional development of missionary orders; Native communities' longstanding practices of seeking spiritual power and diplomatic alliance.

Short-term causes

Crown policies supporting missions; epidemics and famines that unsettled Indigenous communities; competition among imperial powers; Native leaders' invitations or negotiations with missionaries.

Immediate effects

Baptism, instruction, relocation into mission settlements, new labor and ceremonial obligations, conflict with traditional religious leaders, and, in some places, Indigenous revolt.

Long-term consequences

Formation of Native Christian communities, Indigenous-language Christian texts, new local institutions, persistent religious pluralism, and continued disputes over authority, memory, and belonging.

Historical complexity: Conversion cannot be explained by missionary effort alone, nor by colonial coercion alone. Native decisions, community politics, demographic crisis, and the particular institutional setting each affected the outcome, and the mixture differed from one community to the next.

Section 54.4

Indigenous Interpreters, Catechists, Religious Knowledge, and the Problem of Sources

Christianity could not spread through Native communities without translation, and translation was not a neutral process. Missionaries rarely mastered Indigenous languages fully, and many depended on Native interpreters, assistants, and students. These individuals decided which Indigenous words would carry Christian concepts such as God, sin, soul, grace, and salvation. Their choices determined what listeners heard. A word chosen for "god" might carry connotations of an existing deity; a word for "sin" might imply ritual impurity, social offense, or something else. Franciscans in central Mexico, for instance, debated whether to translate key terms or retain Spanish and Latin words, and the decisions affected how Native audiences understood the message.

Indigenous catechists, interpreters, and assistants performed work that was both indispensable and, in the formal church hierarchy, subordinate. Catholic canon and colonial practice generally barred Native men from ordination as priests in much of New Spain, a restriction that left them to serve as teachers, sacristans, choir leaders, and local officials. Their influence was nevertheless considerable. They taught prayers and doctrine, led communal recitations, and in many villages they were the principal day-to-day religious authorities, particularly where priests visited infrequently. In Protestant settings, Native teachers and, increasingly, ministers played comparable roles, and in the eighteenth century a few, such as Samson Occom (Section 54.14), became ordained and widely known.

Historians must therefore read their sources with great care. Missionary letters, chronicles, and reports were written to inform superiors, solicit funding, and demonstrate success; they often exaggerated conversions and condemned Native practices as idolatry. Colonial legal records of idolatry trials preserve testimony shaped by interrogation, and sometimes torture. Baptismal and burial registers record who entered and left the church but not why. By contrast, Indigenous-language texts, including catechisms, sermons, and devotional works, were often composed by Native collaborators and may reveal how concepts were translated and interpreted, though many were reviewed and controlled by missionaries. Records of confraternities, town councils, and wills, many written in Native languages by Native officials, offer additional views of community religious life. Archaeological evidence from mission sites and burial grounds provides material evidence of daily life and practice. Oral histories and community traditions, handled with appropriate care, preserve Native memories of the missionary era that written sources omit or distort. Finally, a small but significant body of writing by Native Christians, such as Occom's letters and sermons, offers first-person interpretation.

No single source type is sufficient. Missionary writings alone risk repeating missionary assumptions; Indigenous-language texts alone may give a misleadingly smooth picture of official teachings; community records reveal local organization but little of private belief. The best historical reconstruction compares sources and asks, for each, who made it, for whom, and what it leaves out.

The difference between a translated text and a transcript of religious practice deserves particular attention. A printed catechism shows what an author or sponsoring institution wanted readers to learn; it does not prove that every sentence was understood or obeyed. A confessional manual can be equally revealing about the missionary's fears, because its questions identify the practices clergy expected to encounter. Conversely, an Indigenous petition may record a community's strategic language before a colonial court rather than a complete statement of collective belief. The task is to compare these genres. An interpretation based on a catechism gains strength if town records show the book was used by Native teachers, if wills reveal the same devotional vocabulary, or if community histories preserve the institution concerned. Where such connections cannot be made, uncertainty should remain visible. Indigenous collaborators may also be unnamed even when their linguistic labor made an entire manuscript possible; absence of a name is not absence of agency, but it prevents a responsible historian from inventing individual authorship.

Indigenous Catholicism in Mexico and Northern New Spain

Indigenous Christianity in Mexico emerged through multiple locally rooted historical processes, not through a single program of cultural replacement.

Section 54.5

Nahua Christianity after the Spanish Conquest

The Nahua communities of central Mexico were the first large Indigenous population in North America to face sustained Christian missionization, and the evidence they left is unusually rich. The conquest of Tenochtitlan in 1521, treated in Chapter 39, destroyed the Mexica state, though not Nahua society. In 1524 a group of Franciscans, remembered as "the Twelve," arrived to begin organized evangelization, followed by Dominicans and Augustinians. The friars worked through the altepetl, the Nahua local polity, because the Spanish Crown had left the day-to-day administration of Native towns largely in the hands of Indigenous noble officeholders. Missionaries needed those leaders' cooperation, and Native elites needed accommodation with the new church to protect their positions.

Early evangelization depended on language and on spectacle. Friars learned Nahuatl, preached in public squares, and organized large-scale baptisms that were sometimes reported in vast numbers. They made extensive use of pictorial catechisms, theater, music, and the construction of churches, often on the sites of former temples, whose stones were reused. Many children of the nobility were brought into mission schools. The Colegio de Santa Cruz de Tlatelolco, which opened in 1536, taught Native students Latin, rhetoric, and Christian doctrine, and several of its graduates became collaborators in the production of Nahuatl texts. Bernardino de Sahagún, a Franciscan, worked with Native scholars at Tlatelolco on an extensive documentation of Nahua life in the mid-sixteenth century, the work now known as the Florentine Codex, and with them produced Christian texts in Nahuatl as well. Scholars have documented the contribution of Native writers, including Antonio Valeriano, and the collaborative nature of these projects.

Nahua Christianity took shape in the vocabulary of Nahuatl itself. Louise Burkhart's study of sixteenth-century Nahuatl religious texts shows that friars and their Native collaborators sought words that could carry Christian ideas while the meanings in the minds of Nahua listeners shifted. For example, Nahuatl concepts of moral failing and ritual impurity were drawn into the discussion of sin; Nahua notions of reciprocal obligation influenced how saints and prayer were understood. Burkhart emphasizes that dialogue occurred in both directions: the friars' ideas were reinterpreted, while Nahua authors also reshaped their own moral vocabulary in response. A sixteenth-century Dominican chronicler recorded an account of a Nahua man who, asked why he still practiced older customs after baptism, described his community as being "in the middle," a Nahuatl term often rendered nepantla. Historians treat the anecdote with caution, since it is filtered through a Spanish observer, but it has become a widely discussed image of the experience of living between two religious systems.

The relationship between Christianity and traditional practice involved coercion as well as instruction. In the 1530s some friars and bishops, including Juan de Zumárraga, pursued the destruction of temples, images, and ceremonial books. In 1539, the Inquisitorial trial and execution of don Carlos Ometochtzin, a Texcocan noble accused of idolatry, produced a controversy; after the formal establishment of the Inquisition in New Spain in 1571, Indigenous people were excluded from its jurisdiction, and concerns about idolatry were handled instead through episcopal and other channels. This did not end suppression, but it shifted its form.

Nahua communities also incorporated Christianity into civic life. Churches became symbols of town identity, and patron saints replaced or paralleled earlier patron deities as focal points of community pride. Local officials supervised church building, organized festivals, and managed sacred images. Over the following century, these local arrangements and confraternities (Section 54.7) gave towns substantial influence over their religious life. The devotion that developed around the Virgin of Guadalupe, associated with the hill of Tepeyac north of Mexico City, later became one of the most important Christian devotions in Mexico; historians debate the early development of that devotion, and the best-known Nahuatl account of the apparitions was published in the seventeenth century, long after the events it describes, so it cannot be treated as a sixteenth-century source.

A useful comparison is between prescriptions for confession and records of how people prepared for death. Printed Nahuatl manuals instructed priests to ask about particular acts, relationships, and beliefs. Surviving Indigenous wills, by contrast, show testators requesting masses, naming guardians for children, distributing goods, or making arrangements for burial. These documents speak to different moments in religious life: one attempts to regulate conduct, while the other records decisions about family, property, and the fate of the soul. Mark Z. Christensen's comparison of Nahuatl and Yucatec Maya texts illustrates why even familiar prayers and commandments could acquire different emphases when translated and explained locally. The religious vocabulary of a will cannot disclose the writer's entire inner life, but repeated patterns across many documents demonstrate that Catholic institutions had become usable resources in Indigenous households and communities.

The written record also contains disagreements within the church itself. A friar who feared that a Nahua expression preserved an older sacred meaning might try to substitute a loanword or a more elaborate explanation. Native assistants who had to make the lesson intelligible could prefer existing vocabulary and familiar rhetorical forms. Printed editions were intended partly to standardize doctrine, yet manuscript sermons and instructional texts continued to circulate beyond complete ecclesiastical supervision. This difference between official prescriptions and local communication matters more than a simple division between 'Spanish' and 'Indigenous' Christianity. Native authors worked within Christian genres while choosing words and examples; friars sometimes depended on those choices while disputing their implications. The resulting literature demonstrates cooperation under inequality, not the disappearance of either side's intellectual commitments.

Section 54.6

Maya Christianity in Yucatán: Conversion, Language, and Religious Persistence

The experience of Maya communities in the Yucatán Peninsula overlapped with, but differed from, that of the Nahua. Spanish conquest of Yucatán was slower and more fragmentary, extending through the 1540s and leaving some Maya polities outside Spanish control for generations. Franciscan friars arrived in the 1540s and, over the following decades, built a network of mission centers and visiting stations in Maya towns. Their work depended heavily on the Yucatec Maya language, and they produced grammars, doctrinal texts, and sermons. The Maya, like the Nahua, had a tradition of written and painted records, and in the colonial period Maya scribes learned to write their own language in Latin script, producing a substantial body of local documents including town records, wills, and religious and historical books.

The most notorious episode of the early mission period came in 1562. Franciscan friars at Maní, led by Diego de Landa, discovered Maya ceremonial objects and images in caves and hidden locations, and investigated what they treated as continuing idolatry and human sacrifice. They employed torture to extract confessions, and Landa organized a public auto-da-fé at which many Maya books and images were burned. Spanish officials and other clergy criticized Landa's methods, and he was recalled to Spain to answer for them; he was eventually exonerated and returned to Yucatán as bishop. The episode destroyed an irreplaceable body of Maya written knowledge, killed or injured many Maya people, and demonstrated that Christianization in Yucatán proceeded through violence as well as persuasion. Landa also wrote a work describing Maya life, which survives and is widely used by historians, though it is not an unbiased account.

After the 1560s, Maya religious life developed along multiple paths. Maya nobles and town officials, many of whom had been educated by the friars, took leading roles in Christian organization. Maya cantores (choir leaders and teachers) taught prayers and doctrine, maintained church music, and in many towns served as the principal day-to-day religious authorities; Nancy Farriss has emphasized their importance in her work on language and instruction. In Maya communities, Christian practice was often organized through local institutions, and confraternities held livestock and property that funded festivals and provided community resources. Meanwhile, ceremonial practices connected to agriculture, ancestors, and the calendar continued in many places, often on a smaller scale or in more private settings. The Books of Chilam Balam, compilations written in Yucatec Maya in the colonial period, combine calendrical and historical material of Maya origin with Christian elements, and offer evidence of how Maya intellectuals understood their position. Their content spans a long period and their composition and copying history is complex, so they require careful interpretation.

Maya communities that remained outside Spanish control, including the Itzá of the Petén region, whose capital Nojpetén fell to Spanish forces in 1697, followed different trajectories, and missionaries who approached them in earlier decades met with limited success. The Maya story is therefore not one of uniform submission. It includes enthusiastic participation, strategic accommodation, concealed practice, violent suppression, and independence.

One way to see Maya initiative is to follow the people who maintained records after initial evangelization. Town scribes working in alphabetic Yucatec Maya could copy documents, record land and inheritance arrangements, and preserve accounts that combined Christian dating with older frameworks of history. Local officers and church musicians kept collective practices operating even when a resident missionary was absent. Their writing should not be mistaken for a complete record of village religion: it represents particular literate circles and often served legal or political ends. Nevertheless, it demonstrates that written Christianity in Yucatán became more than a sequence of directives issued by friars. The same communities that faced investigations into ceremony could use the institutions and vocabulary of Catholicism to organize obligations and defend local interests.

The comparison with Nahua towns also warns against treating a language as a single religious culture. Yucatán contained communities linked to different political histories and local saints, while access to clergy and schooling varied from town to town. The severity of the Maní investigations cannot be made into a uniform account of all Maya responses, just as the continued copying of ritual books cannot prove that every household practiced the same rites. Some Christian devotional forms acquired deep local significance; other demands, particularly those concerning ceremonies, labor, and family practices, remained objects of conflict. Historians reconstruct this variation from manuscripts, ecclesiastical complaints, litigation, material remains, and community knowledge. Each source illuminates a different social position.

Section 54.7

Indigenous Confraternities, Saints, Festivals, Women, and Community Catholicism

Beyond the sermon and the catechism, Christianity became part of community life through institutions that Native people helped to organize. Among the most important were confraternities, known in Spanish as cofradías, which were lay associations dedicated to a saint or devotion, charged with maintaining an image, paying for masses and festivals, and often providing burial support for members. Confraternities were widespread in Spanish Catholicism, and the friars encouraged their establishment among Native communities. Indigenous communities took them up and developed them in ways that served local needs.

A confraternity typically had officers who were chosen from among community members, a collective fund built from contributions, and sometimes land or livestock whose income supported its activities. Because Native officers managed funds and kept accounts, often in their own language, confraternity records are valuable sources of evidence on leadership and local autonomy. Studies of Indigenous confraternities in colonial Mexico have emphasized how they allowed communities to exercise influence over church spending, to protect community assets, and to maintain relationships of obligation among members. In some places, confraternity property protected resources that might otherwise have been claimed by outsiders or taxed by the church. They also supported mutual aid, helping with funerals and the care of the sick.

Saints' festivals were the visible expression of this local Catholicism. Processions, music, dance, feasting, and the decoration of images and churches brought entire communities together, and in many Native towns they replaced or reshaped earlier ceremonial cycles. A saint's image could be treated as a patron and protector of the town, and relationships with it could be understood in terms of reciprocal obligation, with offerings and care expected to bring protection. Some church authorities complained that Native communities treated the saints as the equivalents of earlier deities; Native participants, as far as sources allow us to say, often understood their devotions as properly Christian. Historians caution against assuming that either view captures what everyone believed.

Women's roles in these institutions are harder to document but clearly significant. Women prepared food, made offerings, cared for household altars, taught children prayers, and took part in processions and festival labor. In some regions women held positions in confraternities or participated as members and benefactors, and wills left by Native women sometimes bequeathed property to saints and churches. Household religion, including images kept in homes and prayers recited within families, often escaped missionary scrutiny and persisted regardless of the actions of priests. The household was therefore an essential site of religious transmission, and historians increasingly attempt to reconstruct it from wills, inventories, and inquisitorial or ecclesiastical records.

An account book is especially valuable because it documents religion as administration. The purchase of candles or cloth, payments for musicians, care for a saint's image, or expenses for a funeral reveals how a community supported repeated observance. It may also show who controlled resources and who owed labor or contributions. Such entries cannot by themselves establish whether participants believed what the priest taught, but they make visible a durable organization that was neither purely ceremonial nor simply an instrument of colonial rule. Laura Dierksmeier's research on early Mexican confraternities emphasizes their varied patterns of collective governance. The comparison is instructive: institutions inspired by Catholic charity could be managed locally and could reflect existing social distinctions rather than erase them.

Authority within a confraternity was negotiated, not necessarily egalitarian. Officeholders could gain prestige and influence; poor members might struggle to meet expected contributions; priests could contest control of income; and women might perform much of the work while appearing less often among the named officers. Festivals also required coordination with agriculture, markets, and the availability of workers. A town's patron saint was therefore connected to its capacity to allocate time, money, and public space. When community members defended a festival against an outside official, they might be protecting local government as much as religious sentiment. The historical question is not whether the festival was wholly Indigenous or wholly Catholic but how the town sustained its obligations and decided who had authority over them.

Everyday Life and Social History

Indigenous Confraternities and Religious Festivals

For many Native people in colonial Mexico, religion was lived through service and obligation more than doctrine. A household might contribute food or money to a confraternity; a member might serve a year as an officer, responsible for the saint's image and the costs of its festival; a family might rely on the confraternity to pay for a funeral. Festivals required weeks of preparation, with cooks, musicians, dancers, and carpenters each doing their part. These activities distributed wealth and status within the community, created occasions for reaffirming kinship and neighborly ties, and gave participants a role in the community's relationship with the saint.

The records of such organizations, including account books, lists of members, and wills, show what ordinary people spent, owned, and valued. They also show conflict: disputes over who controlled funds, accusations of mismanagement, and quarrels with priests over how money was used. Such records remind students that Native Catholicism was an arena of local politics as well as devotion.

Section 54.8

Jesuit Missions and Indigenous Religious Adaptation in Northern Mexico

North and west of the Mesoamerican heartland, the mission frontier took a different form. Jesuit missionaries began work in Sinaloa in the 1590s and gradually extended their activity northward through Sonora and into the highlands and canyons of the Sierra Madre. The peoples they met, including the Yaqui (Hiaki), Mayo (Yoreme), Rarámuri (Tarahumara), Tepehuan, Pima, and O'odham, were generally organized in smaller communities than the Nahua, living in river valleys or highlands, farming where water allowed and drawing on hunting and gathering. Jesuit missions aimed to gather such populations into mission pueblos, teach them Christian doctrine and European agricultural and craft techniques, and form them into stable communities under mission supervision.

The Yaqui case shows how complex these relationships could be. The Yaqui had resisted Spanish military expeditions in the early seventeenth century and negotiated a peace around 1610. In 1617, according to Jesuit accounts, Yaqui leaders invited missionaries to their territory. The invitation is often cited as an example of Indigenous choice, although historians are mindful that the Jesuit sources describing it served missionary purposes, and that the Yaqui faced the continuing threat of Spanish force and slaving. The missions that followed concentrated the Yaqui population in eight principal pueblos along the Yaqui River. These pueblos developed durable community identities and institutions, including local governance structures and ceremonial societies, which later became central to Yaqui identity. Yaqui ritual traditions documented in later periods draw on both Catholic and older practice, and the Yaqui have often defended them as their own.

Other missions met more resistance. In 1616 the Tepehuan of the Sierra Madre rose against Spanish and mission institutions, killing several Jesuits among others; the revolt was suppressed after years of violence. Among the Rarámuri, missions began in the early seventeenth century, and the pressures of Spanish mining and ranching, forced labor, and epidemic disease produced recurring conflict, including revolts in the 1690s. Some Rarámuri communities withdrew to more remote canyons rather than live under mission control, a strategy that preserved autonomy at the cost of access to the resources missions offered. Mission institutions were thus both attractive and threatening, depending on context.

The Jesuit Eusebio Francisco Kino entered the Pimería Alta, the homeland of the O'odham, in 1687, founding the mission at Dolores and developing a network of missions and farms in the region now divided by the United States and Mexico border. His work illustrates themes that matter for Indigenous history: O'odham leaders invited some missionaries and negotiated terms; mission farms introduced livestock and new crops; and Spanish settlement and mining interests pressed on the region. The O'odham also resisted, and a major uprising occurred in 1695. A larger revolt in 1751, led by Luis Oacpicagigua, a Native officeholder, demonstrated continued discontent with Spanish demands. Kino's own writings and maps are important documents, but they are missionary sources, and O'odham perspectives must be reconstructed from other evidence.

The Jesuit era ended abruptly. In 1767 the Spanish Crown expelled the Society of Jesus from its dominions, and Jesuit missions were turned over to other clergy. In the Pimería Alta, Franciscans took over in 1768, and the mission at San Xavier del Bac, among others, continued under Franciscan direction. For Native communities, the change meant new priests, new administrative arrangements, and in some cases new losses of land and autonomy. It also shows that mission institutions depended on imperial policy rather than on local Native decision.

Jesuit correspondence frequently framed village life through the achievements or disappointments of the mission, but the permanence of a mission pueblo also depended on Native decisions. Yaqui towns brought political histories, agricultural knowledge, and relationships among communities into the negotiations. The organization of fields, access to river water, and claims over leaders were material questions, not secondary consequences of a spiritual encounter. A family could be willing to attend Mass yet resist restrictions on movement or demands for labor. Mission officials sometimes reported substantial baptism and attendance while also describing continued ceremonial practices or departures from settlements; these different observations should not be collapsed into a single verdict on conversion.

The wider region further complicates the pattern. Rarámuri communities of the Sierra Madre lived in environments and settlement arrangements unlike the Yaqui river valleys. O'odham populations crossed what would only much later become a boundary between Mexico and the United States. Epidemic disease, mining, livestock, and Spanish military operations changed the choices available in each place. Later Yaqui and O'odham Christian ritual traditions are important evidence of continuity, but scholars should not project every modern practice directly back to a seventeenth-century mission. The defensible conclusion is that Native peoples did not simply receive a completed institution: they repeatedly reworked its personnel, calendars, and obligations under changing political conditions.

Spanish Borderland Missions and Indigenous Christianity in New France

Christianity developed in very different ways among Indigenous communities in Spanish and French colonial environments.

Section 54.9

Timucua, Guale, Apalachee, and Christianity in Spanish Florida

The Franciscan missions of La Florida grew out of an earlier failure. Spain founded St. Augustine in 1565, and Jesuits who worked on the Atlantic coast and in the Chesapeake region in the late 1560s and early 1570s withdrew after meeting resistance. Franciscans began work in 1573 and, after a period of reorganization, established a more durable mission system in the late 1580s and 1590s. The mission chain eventually ran along the Atlantic coast among the Guale, across northern Florida among the Timucua, and, from 1633, into the territory of the Apalachee west of the Aucilla River. The Chapter 41 narrative treats Spanish expansion and military conflict; here the focus is on the Indigenous communities that received, negotiated with, and sometimes rejected the friars.

Missions in La Florida were not planted on an empty frontier. They were located in or beside the towns of Native chiefs, called caciques in Spanish sources, whose authority rested on kinship, ceremonial leadership, and control of labor and food. Friars could not remain in a town without a chief's consent, and chiefs saw advantages in accepting them: access to Spanish goods and military support, recognition from the colonial government, and a means of strengthening their own positions against rivals. At the same time, missionaries demanded changes that challenged a chief's authority and customs, including restrictions on polygyny and on ceremonial practices such as ritual ballgames and dances. The relationship was therefore one of negotiation, with the balance varying among towns and over time.

Conflicts sometimes erupted over these demands. In 1597 a revolt broke out among the Guale, associated in Spanish accounts with a young heir to a chiefdom named Juanillo, who had resented a friar's rebuke concerning marriage practices. The uprising killed several Franciscans, and Spanish forces responded with punitive campaigns that devastated Guale towns. The episode is often cited to illustrate that missionary demands on marriage and household organization could threaten Native political structures, though the Spanish sources reflect a missionary view, and Native motives were probably broader. In 1656 a Timucua rebellion arose in response to labor demands and Spanish interference with chiefly authority, a reminder that political and economic pressures accompanied religious ones.

Translation was a significant aspect of Florida missions. The Franciscan Francisco Pareja, who arrived in 1595 and worked for decades among the Timucua, composed grammars and devotional works in Timucua, including catechisms and a manual for confession, printed in Mexico City in the early seventeenth century. These are the principal surviving sources for the Timucua language, which has no living speakers today, and they were produced with Timucua assistance, though the Native contributors are poorly documented. A small number of letters in Timucua from Native leaders survive and are rare examples of Indigenous writing from the mission period.

Daily life in mission towns changed. Communities were expected to attend Mass and instruction, observe the Christian calendar, and build churches. Native labor was demanded for food production and for the mission and colonial economy, and the Apalachee in particular produced maize on a scale that supplied St. Augustine. Epidemic diseases, discussed in Chapter 40, devastated Native populations through the seventeenth century, and the loss of leaders and elders strained the transmission of ceremonial knowledge. The mission system ended not through Native Christian decision but through conquest. In 1704, forces led by Carolina colonist James Moore, together with Native allies, destroyed the Apalachee missions, killing and enslaving many and driving others into exile, as Chapter 53 discusses. Surviving communities relocated, some of them continuing Christian practice in new locations such as the vicinity of Pensacola and Mobile, a reminder that Christian identity could outlast the institutions that introduced it.

The Guale uprising exposes the problem of reconstructing motives from a documentary record written largely by survivors and colonial officials. The familiar story centers on Juanillo, an heir supposedly angered by a friar's criticism of multiple marriages. Marriage policy was certainly one source of friction, but J. Michael Francis and Kathleen M. Kole's examination of the surviving evidence cautions against claiming that the exact leadership and causes of the attacks are fully established. Succession disputes, demands placed on chiefs, and the larger loss of autonomy all belong to the historical setting. The deaths of five Franciscans do not erase the fact that other Indigenous communities continued to bargain with the mission system. A revolt against particular priests should not be read as a referendum on one unified Guale position.

The Timucua books make a different kind of Native participation visible. Pareja's doctrinal works could not have been produced without people capable of explaining the language and evaluating written forms, but the surviving imprints foreground the friar rather than those collaborators. Their publication in Mexico City also shows how a distant colonial printing network could turn locally acquired linguistic knowledge into portable instructions for other clergy. A confession manual's treatment of marriage, ritual, and misconduct shows Catholic disciplinary expectations; surviving correspondence and letters associated with Timucua communities indicate that alphabetic writing also had political uses. These bodies of evidence must be kept separate: translated questions reveal clerical categories, whereas a Native letter may disclose a specific diplomatic or administrative demand.

Section 54.10

Pueblo Christianity, Religious Suppression, and the Revolt of 1680

The Franciscans arrived in the Rio Grande region with the colonizing expedition of Juan de Oñate in 1598 and expanded their presence through the following decades. Missions were built at or beside Pueblo villages, and friars sought to teach Christian doctrine, baptize, and end ceremonial practices they regarded as idolatrous. Pueblo communities were compelled to provide labor and goods for the church and the colonial government, a burden examined in Chapters 43 and 44. The religious dimension of the colonial relationship was nonetheless central, and it shaped the Pueblo response.

The Pueblos' own ceremonial system was organized around kivas, ritual societies, and ceremonies, including those associated with the kachinas, which were bound to rain, agriculture, and community welfare. Franciscans attacked these institutions. They destroyed kiva ritual objects and masks, prohibited ceremonies, and punished those who continued them. Pueblo religious leaders were thus placed in direct confrontation with the friars. Some Pueblo people accepted baptism and took part in church life, whether from conviction, strategy, or coercion, while others maintained ceremonies in secret. In many communities these approaches coexisted, sometimes within a single family. In 1675 Spanish authorities arrested dozens of Pueblo religious leaders on charges of sorcery and publicly punished them, an episode that deepened resentment. Among those arrested was Popé of San Juan Pueblo, who later became a leader of the revolt.

The Pueblo Revolt of August 1680 expelled the Spanish from New Mexico for over a decade. Chapter 44 examines its planning, leadership, and military course. For this chapter the key point is its religious dimension. Pueblo forces killed many missionaries (twenty-one Franciscans died, according to commonly cited figures) and destroyed churches and religious objects. Accounts from the period, which are mostly Spanish and thus filtered, report that Pueblo people washed away baptism and resumed ceremonial practices, and they emphasize Popé's call to return to ancestral ways. Historians recognize that the revolt had multiple causes, including labor, tribute, drought, epidemic, and political abuses, and that religious suppression was one among several. They also note that the movement's unity involved many separate communities with their own leaders, and that after the revolt Pueblo communities did not all return to the same religious practice. Some retained Christian elements even in the years of Spanish absence.

The Spanish returned under Diego de Vargas in 1692, and the reconquest was complete by the mid-1690s after renewed resistance, including a revolt in 1696. The postreconquest relationship differed in important ways. Historians generally find that the colonial church and government showed greater tolerance for Pueblo ceremonial life than before, with the Franciscans' power over daily practice reduced. Kiva ceremonies continued, often in more guarded forms, while Pueblo communities honored patron saints and participated in the church calendar. The Smithsonian's interpretation of Pueblo history recognizes both the suppression of Pueblo religion before 1680 and the greater accommodation of certain Native worship practices afterward. The result in many villages was a durable pattern in which Catholic feast days and Pueblo ceremonies coexisted, a pattern whose character varies by village and is sometimes kept private by communities today. Students should avoid treating this coexistence as an uncomplicated blend; Pueblo communities have their own understandings of what is shared and what is restricted.

Pueblo responses were never uniform. Villages had distinct languages, political relationships, and ceremonial organizations, and the reach of an individual friar depended on local circumstances. Mission buildings stood in a landscape already marked by kivas, plazas, fields, and places of ancestral significance. The attempt to suppress dances or ceremonial responsibilities therefore competed with institutions that organized agriculture and community life. Native Christians could be members of those same households and villages. Treating 'Christian' and 'traditional' as fixed opposing populations risks missing the daily negotiations through which people took part in baptisms, marriages, and ceremonies while disagreeing about authority.

After the return of Spanish rule in the 1690s, the persistence of Pueblo ceremonial practice did not mean the destruction of earlier conflict or a simple return to conditions before 1680. The colonial government needed to govern with limited resources, and Pueblo communities retained capacities for local organization that shaped accommodation. Some churches were rebuilt; some Christian rites persisted; kiva societies continued to anchor religious life. Historians describe the resulting arrangements with care because they varied by pueblo and changed over time. Written Spanish reports are necessary evidence of the negotiations but cannot alone recover the experience of ceremonial members or women whose responsibilities were less visible to officials.

Section 54.11

Christianity and Indigenous Life in the California Missions, 1769–1821

The Franciscan missions of Alta California began with the founding of Mission San Diego de Alcalá in 1769 and expanded under Junípero Serra and his successors along the coast, eventually numbering twenty-one by 1823. Chapter 53 treats the labor, confinement, and punishment central to the mission system and Chapter 70 follows the later history of dispossession, so this section concentrates on religious change.

The peoples of coastal California, including the Kumeyaay, Chumash, Tongva, Ohlone, and many others, spoke dozens of languages and lived in autonomous villages with varied ceremonial systems. The mission program sought to bring individuals and families into the missions, baptize them, and organize their lives around Christian instruction, work, and worship. Newly baptized people were called neophytes, and received Christian names. The friars generally expected a period of instruction before baptism, but practice varied, and the mission registers recording baptisms, marriages, and deaths are among the principal sources, together with missionary correspondence and reports.

Why did Native Californians enter the missions? Steven Hackel's research emphasizes that their reasons differed from the Franciscans' goals. Some joined for food, particularly during periods of scarcity when mission agriculture and livestock disrupted local gathering and hunting; some sought protection from enemies or from Spanish soldiers; some followed kin who had entered; some were attracted by trade goods and new skills; and some were pressured or forced. The decision was often made by families or villages and not only by individuals. Once in the missions, residents experienced constraints, including limits on leaving, enforced attendance at services, and corporal punishment, and they suffered extraordinarily high mortality from epidemic disease, so that mission populations were sustained only by continued recruitment from surrounding villages.

Within these conditions, Native people interpreted Christianity in their own ways. Some embraced it sincerely, finding in the church a source of community, ritual, and meaning; others participated outwardly while maintaining older practices, including mourning ceremonies, healing, and dance, some of which missionaries tolerated and others suppressed. Historians must distinguish sincerely held faith from baptism or ritual participation under institutional pressure, a distinction difficult to draw from the records. Children raised in the missions, and the generations that followed, developed a Christianity increasingly their own, and some descendants of mission Indians remain Catholic and consider the mission church part of their heritage, even as they remember its harms.

Resistance took forms from flight to armed revolt. In November 1775 Kumeyaay people attacked Mission San Diego, killing a missionary, an episode that demonstrated hostility to the new institution. In 1781 the Quechan (Yuma) destroyed Spanish settlements and missions along the Colorado River, and in 1785 Toypurina, a Tongva woman recognized as a spiritual leader, helped organize a plot against Mission San Gabriel, for which she was tried. Many others resisted by running away or by refusing to enter. Such resistance was not necessarily rejection of Christianity itself; it often targeted the conditions of the mission regime.

The mission register is indispensable but insufficient evidence for this world. Baptisms, marriages, and burials allow historians to examine population movement and mortality, yet those entries were created by clergy and rarely record the full circumstances in which individuals entered a mission. A person listed as a neophyte might have been born in a nearby village, separated from kin, attached to a mission household, or later attempted to leave. Comparing these registers with population reconstructions and military reports shows why numerical growth in baptisms cannot be equated with the flourishing of Indigenous communities. The spread of livestock and mission agriculture also altered access to food resources on which independent villages depended, making entry into missions a choice increasingly constrained by colonization.

Religious experience within the mission system remained diverse. Music, processions, and Christian feasts could acquire meaning for converts; Indigenous interpreters and skilled workers contributed to the operation of church and settlement life. At the same time, soldiers and friars exercised coercive authority, and the regulation of marriage, punishment, and residence could provoke resistance. Children's and women's lives were especially exposed to institutional controls that sought to remake family organization. Accounts by missionaries must therefore be compared with archaeological evidence, material culture, and the testimony of Native descendants rather than taken as an exhaustive description of belief. The contrast between missionary reports of 'progress' and evidence of illness, flight, and resistance is not a contradiction to smooth away; it reflects competing measures of what the mission accomplished.

Section 54.12

Wendat, Haudenosaunee, and Indigenous Catholic Communities in New France

French Catholic missions began in the early seventeenth century with Recollet friars and, from 1632, a strengthened Jesuit presence. Missionaries traveled to Native communities, since the French colony itself was small. The Jesuit mission to the Wendat (Huron) began in 1634 and was concentrated in Wendake, south of Georgian Bay. Jesuits learned the Wendat language, produced grammars and dictionaries, and recorded their experiences in annual reports, the Jesuit Relations, which are major but strongly missionary-slanted sources. Epidemics of the 1630s struck Wendat communities severely, as Chapter 40 discusses, and Wendat responses to the missionaries divided: some came to Christianity, while others blamed the French and their religion for the deaths. Community divisions between Christians and traditionalists became a source of political tension, and then the confederacy's collapse in the wars of 1649–1650, treated in Chapter 47, scattered survivors. Wendat refugees, some of them Christian, settled near Quebec, and a Wendat community at Lorette (Wendake) endured as an Indigenous Catholic community.

Missionaries also worked among the Haudenosaunee. Jesuits established missions in Mohawk, Onondaga, and other Haudenosaunee villages in the mid-seventeenth century, with mixed results and at times considerable danger. Over time, a number of Haudenosaunee Catholics moved to mission settlements along the St. Lawrence, motivated by religious commitment, family ties, conflict with traditionalist relatives, or the attraction of living in a Catholic community. The settlement of Sault Saint-Louis, now Kahnawake, near Montreal, became the most prominent of these. Founded in the 1660s and moved to its present site in 1716, it developed as a predominantly Mohawk Catholic community with its own church, leadership, and trading relationships. Its members maintained Haudenosaunee identity and political ties even while participating in the French colonial world, and the community's history is treated further in later chapters.

Kateri Tekakwitha (1656–1680) is the best-known Indigenous Catholic of this period. Born in a Mohawk village to a Mohawk father and an Algonquin Christian mother, she lost her parents to smallpox in childhood, and was left with scarring and impaired vision. Raised by relatives, she was baptized in 1676 by a Jesuit, and, facing hostility from some in her community, moved in 1677 to the mission community at Sault Saint-Louis. There she took part in the Christian community's life, practiced intense devotion, and in 1679 made a vow of virginity. She died in 1680 and was later canonized by the Catholic Church in 2012. Sources about her life, including biographies by the Jesuits Pierre Cholenec and Claude Chauchetière, were written after her death by men with devotional purposes, and historians such as Allan Greer have analyzed how these accounts reflect missionary priorities. What can reasonably be said is that she was a Mohawk woman whose commitment to Christianity was recognized in her community, that she faced family tension, and that her decision to move to Kahnawake belonged to a broader pattern of Haudenosaunee Catholic migration. Her interior experience can be reached only through mediated sources.

The movement of Christian Haudenosaunee to settlements along the St. Lawrence also had political implications. Families who entered mission villages maintained kin relationships with communities beyond them and could participate in diplomacy, travel, and trade. Kahnawake should not be portrayed as a French community merely inhabited by Native converts: its residents made collective choices and sustained forms of leadership associated with their own histories. Nor was it a representative sample of all Haudenosaunee religious practice. Some people remained in communities where Christianity had much less influence, and disagreement about alliances, residence, and ceremonial life could cross family lines.

Missionary accounts of Kateri Tekakwitha are indispensable for dates and for the vocabulary in which Jesuits interpreted her devotion, but hagiography is not a neutral biography. An author wishing to establish sanctity would select episodes that showed humility, suffering, or exemplary commitment. That does not mean Kateri's Christianity was insincere; it means scholars must distinguish her actions and circumstances from the theological framework through which they were narrated. Later maps of Sault Saint-Louis likewise help students understand the settlement's spatial arrangement, but a plan made in 1752 cannot be treated as a direct depiction of the village during Kateri's lifetime in the 1670s.

Section 54.13

Praying Towns, John Eliot, and Native Christianity in Colonial New England

In the mid-seventeenth century, southern New England was the setting of the first sustained Protestant mission effort among Indigenous peoples in North America. The Massachusett, Nipmuc, Wampanoag, and neighboring Algonquian communities had been devastated by epidemics in the decades before and after English settlement, as Chapter 48 recounts, and they faced growing English pressure on their land. Within this setting, John Eliot, minister at Roxbury, began preaching to Native audiences in the Massachusett language in 1646, working with Native teachers and translators, and the practice of establishing Christian Indigenous towns, known in English as praying towns, followed. The first, Natick, was founded in 1651. By the early 1670s there were fourteen praying towns in Massachusetts, with a population that contemporary English observers placed at roughly a thousand people.

The praying towns were intended to be places where Native people would live in a Christian, English-influenced manner, with structured settlements, courts, and congregations. Their residents were expected to adopt regular worship, cut their hair, and wear English-style clothing in some cases, and to give up practices the English condemned, such as polygyny and the work of powwows. They also adopted writing. Literacy in Massachusett spread, and some Native residents became teachers, translators, and printers. Eliot's translation of the Bible (1663), discussed in Section 54.4, symbolized this relationship, and Native students attended a Native school at Harvard in the 1650s, one of whom, Caleb Cheeshahteaumauck, graduated in 1665.

Why did Native people join? The motives were diverse. Some were drawn by religious conviction; some by the prospect of political protection and legal standing within the colony; some by the need for land that could be defended from English encroachment, since praying towns were granted land formally; and some by the loss of kin and the dislocation caused by epidemic disease. Eliot and his supporters recognized that Native residents also asserted their own interests, including the authority of their own leaders. Congregations developed Native-led institutions, including Native elders and ruling officials, and in the following decades Native pastors served some congregations, including at Natick. Praying-town residents also kept aspects of their own kinship and community organization, and in some cases pursued their own goals within the colonial framework.

On Martha's Vineyard, Christianity took a distinctive form. Wampanoag people on the island accepted missions begun by Thomas Mayhew Jr. and his father in the 1640s, and the Native minister Hiacoomes became an early and respected leader. David Silverman's study of the island's Wampanoag shows that Native Christians there built their own churches, led their own congregations for generations, and maintained a Wampanoag Christian identity that was integral to community life, including during periods of severe colonial pressure. Their experience shows that Native Christianity could be sustained as a community's own institution.

King Philip's War (1675–1676), treated in Chapter 48, was catastrophic for Native Christians. Although some praying Indians sided with the English and served as scouts and soldiers, colonists distrusted them. In the autumn of 1675 the Massachusetts authorities ordered praying Indians removed to Deer Island in Boston Harbor, where hundreds suffered from exposure, hunger, and disease through the winter. Some praying Indians were attacked by colonists, and others fled or were killed or enslaved. After the war, only a few praying towns survived, including Natick, though Native Christian communities continued in New England and the Vineyard, and the Massachusett language lived on in writing. The war showed that Christian affiliation and loyalty to the colony did not shield Native people from the suspicion and violence of settlers.

The political position of a praying town was unstable because English authorities often regarded Native residents as subject peoples rather than partners possessing equal territorial rights. Christian instruction might provide access to literacy and an avenue to petition the government, yet it did not prevent land claims from being contested. Native ministers and teachers also carried much of the ongoing work, especially where a European missionary could not remain continuously present. Their congregations were not necessarily copies of nearby English churches: patterns of leadership, kinship, and local decision-making shaped the community from within.

The persecution of Christian Native people during King Philip's War demonstrates the limited protection that conversion conferred. The forced removal of many Native Christians to Deer Island in Boston Harbor was a measure imposed by colonial authorities amid fear and suspicion. The broader military emergency also divided Indigenous communities, so it is misleading to assume that every Christian Native person supported the English or that every non-Christian Native person opposed them. On Martha's Vineyard, Wampanoag congregations experienced a different balance of local leadership and colonial power from towns on the mainland. David Silverman's research on the island emphasizes how Christian affiliation and community boundaries changed across generations rather than following a single trajectory toward cultural disappearance.

Section 54.14

Samson Occom, Indigenous Ministers, and the Struggle for Christian Autonomy

The eighteenth century brought a different Protestant context. A wave of evangelical revivals beginning in the 1730s and 1740s, often called the Great Awakening, reached some Native communities of southern New England, Long Island, and elsewhere. Linford Fisher has argued that the revivals constituted an "Indian Great Awakening," in which Native people took up evangelical Christianity partly on their own terms, forming Native-led congregations and sometimes separating from English churches. In this setting emerged Samson Occom (1723–1792), a Mohegan from Connecticut whose life illustrates the possibilities and limits of Native Christian leadership.

Occom was born into a Mohegan community under pressure from colonial land encroachment. He experienced a religious awakening around 1740 and studied for several years with the minister Eleazar Wheelock, who later founded a school for Native students. Occom taught and preached among Native communities on Long Island, particularly the Montaukett, where he also married, and he was ordained by a Presbyterian body in 1759. He served as a minister, teacher, and community adviser, and also earned his living partly by labor, since the sums paid to him were far less than those paid to white missionaries, a disparity he protested.

In the 1760s Occom traveled to Britain with the minister Nathaniel Whitaker to raise funds for Wheelock's school. The tour, which lasted from 1765 to 1768, was a great success, and Occom drew large audiences as one of the first Native Protestant preachers to be heard in Britain. The money raised, on the order of ten thousand pounds or more, however, was used by Wheelock to found Dartmouth College in New Hampshire, which educated chiefly English students. Occom felt betrayed, and in the early 1770s he broke with Wheelock, criticizing the school's shift away from Native education. His criticism of the misuse of funds, and of the broader hypocrisy of Christians who professed charity while dispossessing Native people, is among the most important pieces of early Native Christian political writing.

Occom also published. His sermon preached at the execution of Moses Paul, a Native man convicted of murder, was printed in 1772 and widely reprinted, and is generally regarded as one of the first Native-authored works published in English in North America. In 1774 he published a collection of hymns for Native and other congregations. He wrote a brief autobiographical account in 1768, which survives in manuscript, and many letters. These writings, preserved in collections such as Dartmouth's Occom Circle, offer rare first-person evidence of an Indigenous Christian's thought.

Occom's influence extended to community-building. In the 1770s he helped organize Brothertown, a settlement of Christian Native people from several southern New England communities, including Mohegan, Pequot, Niantic, Montaukett, and others, who sought to move to land in central New York offered by the Oneida. The project, interrupted by the American Revolution, reflected a desire for a Native Christian community independent of colonial supervision and land pressure. Brothertown settlers later relocated to Wisconsin in the nineteenth century, and the community endures today. Occom's story shows that Native Christians could challenge colonial institutions while remaining committed to Christianity, and that their religious identity was entwined with their political aims.

Occom's correspondence provides unusually direct evidence of the consequences of unequal treatment. His criticism of Eleazar Wheelock was not an outsider's dismissal of Christianity but an argument that a Christian educational enterprise had failed to honor its stated obligations to Native students and to those who raised money for it. The school that helped establish Dartmouth College was increasingly directed toward a different institutional future from the one Occom had promoted. When students analyze his letters, they should distinguish the financial arrangements, the promises he believed had been broken, and the religious and ethical standards by which he judged them. These concerns were connected to broader Mohegan struggles over land and political authority.

Brothertown carried a related argument into community-building. Native families from several southern New England peoples sought a setting where Christian worship, landholding, and collective governance could support an intertribal future. That endeavor required negotiations with the Oneida and with colonial and later state authorities; it was not a simple escape from dispossession. Occom's life thus connects preaching and hymnody to decisions about territory, schooling, migration, and political survival. It also prevents an easy equation of an Indigenous Christian leader with either complete colonial accommodation or complete opposition to European institutions.

Section 54.15

Moravian Missions, Christian Lenape Communities, and Gnadenhutten

The Moravians, members of a renewal movement associated with the Unitas Fratrum, established mission communities in the eighteenth century with a distinctive approach. They organized Native converts in settlements where religious life, work, and community organization were closely integrated, emphasized hymn singing and personal devotion, and generally avoided the cultural demands of English-style dress and law that characterized other Protestants, though they imposed their own disciplines. Their first mission in the northeast was at Shekomeko, a Mahican village in New York, in the early 1740s, where colonial hostility forced its closure within a few years. Moravians then built missions in Pennsylvania, including Gnadenhütten on the Lehigh River, which was destroyed in 1755 during frontier warfare.

Moravian Native communities faced repeated danger as warfare shaped the Pennsylvania frontier. During Pontiac's War, treated in Chapter 58, Christian Native people sheltered in Philadelphia and elsewhere for their safety, after colonists, among them the Paxton Boys, had massacred Conestoga people in 1763. In the early 1770s the missionary David Zeisberger and Lenape and Mahican Christians established new communities on the Tuscarawas River in present-day Ohio, including Schoenbrunn and Gnadenhutten, created partly with the permission of Lenape leaders in the region. These towns held several hundred Native Christians, who farmed, worshipped, and developed a community life centered on the church, while maintaining relationships with neighboring Lenape and other Native nations, some of whom were not Christian and some of whom viewed the settlements with suspicion.

The American Revolution placed these towns between competing forces. Moravian Native Christians tried to remain neutral, but both Americans and British-allied Native warriors suspected them. In 1781, British and Native allies forced the Christian Lenape from their towns and moved them to the Sandusky region. Some, facing hunger, returned in early 1782 to harvest the crops they had left behind. On March 8, 1782, Pennsylvania militiamen under David Williamson captured them at Gnadenhutten and killed about ninety-six men, women, and children, according to widely cited counts; the National Park Service describes more than ninety victims. Accounts, including those of survivors and later Moravian writers, say that the victims were held for a night and prayed and sang before being killed, though details vary among sources. The massacre showed that Christian affiliation did not protect Native people from settler violence, and it deepened frontier hostilities.

The Moravian Lenape who survived did not abandon their community. Later, some relocated to Ontario, where a Moravian Native town was established in the 1790s, and where it endured until its destruction in the War of 1812. Descendants of these communities remain in Canada and the United States and maintain historical memory of Gnadenhutten.

The contrast between the communities' declared preference for peace and the risks surrounding them requires careful attention to Lenape political diversity. Lenape people did not all make the same choices about alliances or residence, and missionaries could misunderstand the calculations of neighbors engaged in war. The Moravian settlements were also not outside politics: their lands, crops, routes, and correspondence mattered to military forces on each side. When one party demanded information or supplies, a refusal could be read as hostility; when another group entered a town, its residents could be suspected of complicity. Native Christians were forced to make decisions inside a conflict they could not control.

At Gnadenhutten in March 1782, Pennsylvania militiamen killed more than ninety Christian Lenape, including children. The victims had returned to gather food and were not responsible for the killings used to justify the attack. The atrocity did not prove that Lenape Christianity had failed, nor does it erase the political and spiritual choices of survivors who rebuilt communities elsewhere. It exposes how settler violence could ignore distinctions that missionaries and Native congregations considered central. A full interpretation should consult the National Park Service's chronology alongside Moravian records and the history preserved by descendant communities, including the Delaware Nation at Moraviantown.

Section 54.16

Inuit Christianity in Greenland and Labrador: Lutheran and Moravian Missions

In the Arctic, Christianity arrived through the Danish-Norwegian crown and the Moravian Church. Hans Egede, a Lutheran pastor, sailed to Greenland in 1721, initially hoping to find descendants of the Norse colonists who had vanished centuries earlier, and he established a settlement on the southwest coast that became Godthåb (now Nuuk) in 1728. The Kalaallit, the Greenlandic Inuit, met him and the Danish traders who followed with a mixture of curiosity, cooperation, and wariness. A smallpox epidemic in 1733–1734 devastated Kalaallit communities near the settlement, an event that, as in other regions, tested Native interpretations of spiritual power.

Moravian missionaries arrived in 1733 and established the community of New Herrnhut. They preached to Kalaallit households in their winter dwellings, using Greenlandic and emphasizing the suffering of Christ in terms that missionaries felt resonated with listeners. In 1739 the first baptisms of Greenlanders at the Moravian mission took place. Missionaries translated hymns and religious texts into Greenlandic. Hans Egede's son Poul published a Greenlandic dictionary in 1750 and a complete Greenlandic New Testament in 1766, drawing on collaboration with Kalaallit language specialists and teachers, including the woman translator Arnarsaq. The existence of such contributors corrects the impression created by title pages naming only European clergy. Native spiritual specialists, the angakkut, were opposed by missionaries as sources of rival authority, yet the Kalaallit negotiated the balance between older ritual obligations and Christian teaching in their own ways. Danish trade policy also shaped the mission setting: a royal monopoly on Greenland trade was established in the 1770s, and commerce and mission were intertwined.

In Labrador, Moravian efforts began with a failed attempt in 1752, in which a missionary, Johann Erhard, and several companions were killed in an encounter with Inuit. The Moravians returned under Jens Haven, who learned some Inuktitut in Greenland and secured British support. After receiving a land grant in 1769, they founded Nain in 1771, followed by Okak in 1776 and Hopedale in 1782. These stations became centers of Inuit Christian life, with churches, schools, and trade stores. Inuit families chose how closely to associate with them: many lived near the stations for part of the year and traveled for seasonal hunting, and some resisted or maintained distance. Leaders also engaged in independent trade with European ships, and the Moravians' commercial role created both opportunities and tensions.

The Labrador and Greenland experiences were not identical. Greenland was under Danish colonial administration, with Lutheran and Moravian missions operating within a state-run trading system; Labrador was a Moravian enterprise on land granted by the British Crown, dependent on its own trade, with a larger measure of mission control over daily life at the stations. In both regions, Inuit literacy in their own language developed through the church, and religious life increasingly drew on Inuit leaders. In Labrador, a religious revival beginning in the first years of the nineteenth century drew many Inuit to the stations, which shows that Inuit participation was not simply the result of missionary pressure. Disease, including epidemics at the stations, and changes in subsistence and settlement patterns accompanied the missions, with consequences considered later in this textbook.

The geography of Arctic missions gave Inuit households forms of leverage that a missionary's reports may understate. Travel and subsistence required knowledge of sea ice, animal movement, weather, and coastal routes; newcomers needed Indigenous expertise to survive and communicate. Families might approach a settlement to exchange goods, obtain instruction, or stay near relatives without understanding those choices as an abandonment of every older responsibility. Mission stations could bring new access to imported goods, literacy, and worship, while also encouraging new settlement patterns and dependence on mission-controlled trade. These effects differed between Danish-governed Greenland and Labrador, where Moravian stations developed through separate commercial and political arrangements.

The documentary chronology of Labrador is unusually useful: Nain began in 1771, Okak in 1776, and Hopedale in 1782. Later accounts of Inuktitut printing and education show the long-term significance of the Moravian language program, but they should not be read back wholesale into the first decades of these stations. Similarly, a Greenlandic translation could provide tools for Christian learning while embedding missionaries' choices about vocabulary. Because hunting, travel, and social life remained organized through Inuit knowledge, the new religious texts entered an existing intellectual world. A responsible history asks who taught whom and what forms of authority followed from literacy, without assuming that written material displaced oral teaching.

Alaska, Indigenous Religious Agency, and the Transformation of Native Christianity

Christianity became part of Indigenous North American history through the choices, struggles, interpretations, and institutions of Indigenous peoples.

Section 54.17

Russian Orthodoxy and Indigenous Religious Encounters in Alaska, 1794–1821

The Russian presence in Alaska began with the fur trade. From the 1740s, Russian traders called promyshlenniki sailed from Siberia to the Aleutian Islands and the Gulf of Alaska in search of sea otter and other pelts, and the Unangan (Aleut) and Alutiiq (Sugpiaq) peoples of the region were drawn into, and coerced into, hunting for them. The relationship was marked by violence, hostage-taking, and severe population decline, themes treated in other chapters. Some Russians had Native wives and baptized Native people informally, and godparent relationships created ties between Russian traders and Native families, but no organized church mission existed until the end of the century.

That changed in 1794, when a group of Orthodox monks, headed by Archimandrite Ioasaf (Joasaph), arrived at Kodiak. The Library of Congress identifies this mission as the beginning of the formal Russian Orthodox missionary presence in Alaska. The mission was supported by the merchant Grigorii Shelikhov, whose company had established a settlement on Kodiak and whose forces, according to accounts, had killed many Alutiiq people at a refuge rock in 1784. The mission's relationship with the company was thus uneasy from the start. The monks baptized large numbers of Alutiiq people and began instruction, and they also criticized the company's treatment of Native people. Among the missionaries was Herman, a monk later revered in the Orthodox Church as a saint, who lived on a nearby island and, in the following decades, wrote of Native mistreatment by company officials. The relationship between missionaries and commercial officials was therefore marked by conflict as well as cooperation. Ioasaf was consecrated a bishop but died at sea in 1799 while returning to Alaska.

The effect on Native communities was mixed. Baptism could offer a measure of recognition within the colony, and Native people sometimes used the church to seek protection or intervention against company abuse. At the same time, the mission coexisted with a coercive labor system, and its early success was limited by the small number of clergy, language barriers, and the company's priorities. The Russian-American Company, chartered in 1799, controlled much of Alaska's commerce and often regarded missionaries as unwelcome. Many Native people, particularly in remote communities, practiced Christianity at a distance from clergy, with laypeople and local leaders taking on religious roles, a pattern that foreshadowed later developments.

The more extensively documented work of translation and religious education in Alaska came later. Ivan Veniaminov, a priest who arrived in the Aleutians in 1824, learned the Unangan language, and with Unangan collaborators, including Ivan Pan'kov, developed a written form of the language and produced religious texts. This work falls just beyond the chapter's chronology, and students should distinguish the early period of 1794–1821, in which the mission was small and conflicted, from the later era in which Orthodoxy became deeply rooted in many Alaska Native communities. Orthodox Christianity remains an important part of Alaska Native life today.

The distinction between traders and clergy mattered on the ground but did not remove the inequalities of Russian colonialism. Some monks criticized mistreatment and attempted to advocate for Alaska Native people; company officials could regard such intervention as a threat to commercial authority. The Library of Congress documents the troubled relationship between the Orthodox mission and the fur-trade system, while National Park Service historical studies record episodes in which clergy carried complaints about abuses. Such evidence complicates any simple view of the mission as either a benevolent refuge or an identical branch of the company. The same Native household could face compulsory fur procurement, participate in baptism, and seek support from a priest against a trader.

The years before 1821 were also a period of institutional fragility. The death of Ioasaf at sea and difficulties maintaining clergy meant that lay instruction and relationships with Indigenous families mattered greatly. Orthodox forms of prayer and visual devotion did not spread only through formal sermons; they could be learned through household ties, sponsors at baptism, or contact with people already connected to Russian settlements. Yet the fuller story of Native-language liturgical books and Native clergy belongs chiefly to the nineteenth century after the period emphasized here. In 1821 the Russian-American Company's renewed charter required support for church activity, marking a shift in institutional expectations rather than the completion of Indigenous conversion.

Language and Cultural Study
Translation in the Arctic and Alaska

Missions in Greenland, Labrador, and Alaska all depended on translation, but they differed in institutions and methods, and students should not treat them as a single Arctic pattern.

Greenland (Lutheran and Moravian). Missionaries learned Greenlandic, produced dictionaries, hymns, and scripture translations, and worked within Danish colonial administration. Writing used the Roman alphabet.

Labrador (Moravian). Missionaries used Inuktitut, drawing on Greenlandic experience, and produced hymns, school materials, and religious texts at stations that were also trading posts. The Moravian emphasis on communal worship and hymn singing shaped Inuit religious practice.

Alaska (Russian Orthodox). Early missionaries worked with limited language knowledge in a setting dominated by the fur trade. Systematic translation, with Unangan collaborators, followed from the 1820s. The Orthodox tradition's liturgical use of local languages and the use of the Cyrillic script give it a distinct character.

Common features. In each region, translation depended on Native speakers whose names often go unrecorded; written literacy in Native languages grew through the church; and translation choices affected how Christian terms were understood.

Section 54.18

Indigenous Women, Households, Religious Knowledge, and Everyday Christianity

Religious transformation did not take place only in churches and missions. It occurred in households, in conversations between parents and children, in marriage negotiations, at funerals, and in the daily decisions of ordinary people. Because most sources were produced by male clergy and officials, historians must read them against the grain, and rely on wills, court records, inventories, archaeological evidence, and oral histories, to recover women's and families' experiences.

In New Spain, the household was a site where Christian images were kept, prayers recited, and children taught. Women had central roles in food preparation for festivals, care of saints' images, and the teaching of prayers, and Native wills show women bequeathing property to churches and saints. In New France, Native women were prominent in mission communities such as Kahnawake, and the Ursuline sisters at Quebec, founded in 1639, ran a school that taught Native girls, though many Native families found the discipline objectionable and the results limited. Women's roles in Haudenosaunee society, which included considerable influence over clan and household decisions, shaped how Catholic practice was adopted in these settings. In California, unmarried women and girls were housed separately in the missions, and Franciscans' attention to marriage and sexuality produced sharp conflicts with Native customs.

In Greenland, Arnarsaq's documented work with Poul Egede shows that Indigenous women could contribute directly to the creation and interpretation of Christian texts, even when colonial publication conventions centered the missionary's name. In New England, women appear in praying-town and Vineyard congregations as members, and in the revivals of the eighteenth century Native women, as well as men, participated as exhorters and church members. Occom's marriage to Mary Fowler, a Montaukett woman, and the role of Native women in maintaining Brothertown households illustrate the importance of family ties in Native Protestant communities. In Moravian settlements, residents were organized into groups, known as choirs, by age, sex, and marital status, which gave religious life a structure that touched families directly. In Labrador and Greenland, Inuit families negotiated the rhythms of seasonal movement and mission attendance, and children learned to read in mission schools. In Russian America, godparent relationships and marriages between Russians and Native women created households in which Orthodox practice and Native culture mingled, though under conditions of inequality and sometimes coercion.

Death rituals show particularly clearly how older and newer practices interacted. Christian burial replaced or modified earlier mortuary customs in many places, but mourning practices, remembrance of ancestors, and ideas about the afterlife often persisted in modified forms. Naming also mattered. Baptismal names were sometimes used alongside Native names; in other cases Native names went underground; and some communities incorporated saints' names into their own naming systems. Marriage practice was another point of tension, because missionaries insisted on monogamy and church ceremonies, while many Native societies recognized other forms of marriage and divorce.

Across these regions, Christian instruction rarely replaced Native knowledge but interacted with it. Parents and grandparents continued to teach stories, ecological knowledge, and moral values, while children learned church prayers and sometimes literacy. Conflicts occurred when missionaries sought to take children from families for mission schooling, a pattern that anticipates later boarding-school policies examined in Chapter 87. Historians emphasize that religious transformation was a family matter, with generations interpreting the changes differently, and that differences within communities, between converts and traditionalists, elders and youth, or men and women, often shaped outcomes as much as missionary policy.

Household religion can be approached through objects and practices as well as statements of belief. A prayer learned in a Native language, a cross placed beside family possessions, a baptismal name used in a marriage record, or contributions to a funeral could give Christianity an enduring place in everyday life. Yet the meaning of these practices was not necessarily uniform across generations. Parents could welcome a school while disputing a missionary's disciplinary methods; grandparents could teach Christian prayers and community ceremonies to the same children. Tracing these choices requires looking beyond missionary praise of exemplary wives or mothers and asking whose labor kept worship and mutual aid functioning.

Questions of marriage expose a particularly sharp meeting of spiritual and political authority. Clergy treated Christian marriage and sexual conduct as matters of doctrine, while Indigenous communities could understand marriage in relation to kin obligations, residence, exchange, and the autonomy of spouses. A dispute over a marriage might therefore become a dispute over which government had power to recognize a household. Evidence of conflict must not be interpreted to mean that all Indigenous women or men rejected Christianity; some found in the church resources for making their own moral claims. The historical task is to reconstruct the range of choices while recognizing the coercion involved when missionaries or colonial courts tried to define acceptable family life.

Section 54.19

Conversion, Resistance, Syncretism, and the Emergence of Indigenous Christian Traditions

The evidence surveyed in this chapter allows a comparison of patterns of religious change. Several can be distinguished, though they often overlapped and none describes a whole region. Coercive conversion occurred where missionaries and colonial authorities used force, confinement, or the threat of punishment to compel baptism and attendance, as in parts of California and in the early campaigns of the 1530s and 1560s in Mexico; even there, outward conformity did not determine inward belief. Gradual community adaptation characterized Nahua and Maya towns, where local officials and catechists integrated Christian forms into town life over generations. Indigenous-led Christian movements appear in the Native-led congregations of Martha's Vineyard, in the evangelical revivals that Fisher describes, in Occom's ministry, and in Brothertown. Preservation of traditional ceremonies beside Christian affiliation is clearest in the Pueblo world after 1692, in Yaqui ritual life, and in Maya communities. Religious coexistence describes arrangements in which both systems were maintained, sometimes in separate spheres. Explicit resistance took the form of revolt, flight, concealment, or withdrawal.

It is important to distinguish resistance to colonial religious institutions from resistance to Christian belief itself. A community might oppose a missionary's authority, labor demands, or land seizures while holding Christian convictions, as Occom's criticism of Wheelock shows. Conversely, some communities rejected Christianity itself, associating it with epidemics, conquest, or loss of ceremonial order. The Pueblo Revolt, the 1597 Guale revolt, and the 1775 attack on Mission San Diego each combined religious and political grievances, and historians disagree on how to weigh them. What is clear is that Native people used a range of strategies, and that a single person's choices could change over a lifetime.

Native Christian identity should not be treated as a contradiction of Indigenous belonging. Many communities today, including Nahua, Maya, Yaqui, Mohawk, Lenape, Inuit, and Alaska Native communities, include Christians whose faith is part of their Indigenous identity, and many descendant communities also remember the harm done by missions. Both are historically grounded. Historians therefore resist both the older view that Christian Natives were simply assimilated and the opposite view that any Christian practice among Native people was merely a mask for older belief. The record supports neither generalization.

By about 1821, Native Christianity was neither complete nor uniform. In central and southern Mexico, Native communities had maintained Catholic institutions for nearly three centuries, with local autonomy over many aspects of religious life, while northern missions had been disrupted by the Jesuit expulsion and by the wars of independence that began in 1810. The Florida missions had been destroyed a century earlier. Pueblo communities lived with a settled but negotiated religious pluralism. California's missions were at their height, with consequences that would be harsh and are discussed later. In the Northeast, Native Protestant communities such as Brothertown and Mohegan congregations survived under heavy pressure. Moravian and Lutheran missions held positions in the Arctic, and Orthodoxy was beginning to spread in Alaska. In the St. Lawrence valley, Kahnawake and other Indigenous Catholic communities endured. Elsewhere across the continent, most Native peoples had not been affected by missions directly, and in some regions, such as the Great Lakes and the Plains, Native religious movements were beginning to respond to the pressures of the period.

The next chapters take the story in different directions. Religious revitalization movements, including the teachings of the Seneca leader Handsome Lake after 1799, and, as Chapter 62 will show, those of the Shawnee prophet Tenskwatawa, drew on Indigenous traditions and, in some cases, selectively on Christian ideas, and became central to Native political responses. In the early nineteenth century Native Christian communities such as the Cherokee also took part in the political reconstruction described in Chapter 64. Chapter 55 turns to Indigenous diplomacy and the balance of power in the age of imperial rivalry, the political world within which many of these religious communities lived.

A final evidentiary distinction helps prevent a false continental ranking of 'successful' conversions. The dense parish and notarial records of central Mexico cannot be compared straightforwardly with fragmentary accounts from the Great Basin, the Arctic, or eighteenth-century Alaska. A region with many recorded baptisms may have been more thoroughly administered, not necessarily more uniformly Christian. A region with few surviving texts may have had vigorous oral teaching and local practice that colonial archives failed to preserve. Likewise, violent resistance in one decade does not establish that all religious change ceased afterward. A defensible comparison uses common questions—authority, language, ritual, family, land, coercion, and community continuity—while allowing that the evidence answers them unevenly.

Historical Debate

Syncretism or Indigenous Christianity?

The question: When Native communities held Christian practices alongside older ones, or reinterpreted Christian ideas, should historians describe the result as syncretism, as concealed continuity of Indigenous religion, or as Indigenous Christianity in its own right?

Interpretation A — Syncretism remains analytically useful: Some scholars hold that the term captures real patterns of combination, such as saints treated within frameworks of reciprocal obligation or Christian and Native ceremonies joined within a single calendar, and that avoiding the term risks denying that traditions interacted.
Interpretation B — The term should be used cautiously or replaced: Other scholars argue that it implies a pure original and a corrupted product, ignores that all religions blend, and underestimates deliberate Native interpretation. They favor describing specific beliefs and practices, or speaking of Indigenous Christianities.

Evidence: Both draw on Indigenous-language texts, confraternity records, ritual descriptions, and missionary reports, as well as the work of scholars such as Burkhart, Christensen, and Farriss.

Points of disagreement: Mainly terminology and interpretation, rather than the underlying evidence of blended practice, though scholars also differ over how much concealed traditional belief persisted in a given community.

Why it matters: The terms shape whether Native Christians are seen as inauthentic, as covert traditionalists, or as full participants in the creation of Christian traditions.

Section 54.20

Indigenous Christian Institutions and Religious Sovereignty by 1821

By the early nineteenth century Indigenous Christians across North America had created religious institutions that differed in language, leadership, and political standing. The history presented in this chapter cannot be reduced to the geographical advance of European denominations. Nahua and Maya town councils, catechists, and confraternities maintained Catholic worship within structures of local government; Christian Mohawk communities took part in the diplomatic world of the St. Lawrence; Wampanoag and other Algonquian congregations developed Native teachers and ministers; and Inuit and Alaska Native households interpreted imported religious forms amid maritime trade and colonial expansion. These experiences were connected by the demands of missionaries and empires, but they were not one movement and did not produce one outcome.

Language was among the most consequential instruments of transformation. Religious instruction depended on translators whose decisions shaped what a sermon, catechism, confession, or prayer could mean. Some collaborated with European clergy; others later wrote petitions, sermons, letters, and records on behalf of their own communities. The Massachusett Bible, Nahuatl and Maya devotional works, Timucua texts printed in Mexico City, Occom's manuscripts, and early Arctic translations belong to different histories of literacy, and none can stand alone as a transparent record of belief. Together they demonstrate that Indigenous people worked on the language of Christianity and that colonial churches were more dependent on Native knowledge than their administrative hierarchies often admitted.

The growth of community institutions also changed what religion meant in public life. A confraternity collected funds for burials and festivals; a local catechist carried instruction into households; a mission town coordinated agricultural labor and worship; a Christian minister might advocate for land or education. Such activities made churches places where authority and resources could be contested. A Native leader could seek a priest as an ally and later oppose that priest's demands. A Christian congregation could remain loyal to its religion while rejecting a colonial official. These possibilities explain why it is insufficient to place every Indigenous person on a single scale running from 'traditional' to 'converted.' The categories obscure how people defended local autonomy through institutions that had originated under colonial power.

Coercion remained fundamental. Mission settlement could restrict residence and labor, and attacks on ceremonial life could challenge the obligations that held communities together. In Spanish Florida, the Guale uprising showed the instability of a system that depended on chiefs while threatening aspects of their authority. In the Pueblo world, religious repression helped produce revolt, yet postreconquest history involved new accommodations rather than a permanent division into Christians and non-Christians. Alta California's missions expanded through institutions that subjected many Indigenous people to discipline and vulnerability to disease. In the Ohio country, the killing of Christian Lenape at Gnadenhutten showed that a community could maintain a Christian identity and still be treated by settler attackers as an enemy. No account of Indigenous Christianity is adequate if it ignores these asymmetries of force.

At the same time, coercion does not authorize historians to declare the convictions of converts unreal. Some Indigenous believers deliberately adopted Christian teachings, defended their devotion, and built relationships with other Christians. Others accepted particular rites while continuing older ceremonial responsibilities, or used a mission school and rejected the missionary's political authority. A single household could contain such differences, and individuals changed their positions over their lifetimes. The sources are not capable of assigning a reliable private motive to every baptism. That uncertainty is a reason to analyze decisions, institutions, and recorded practices closely, not a reason to deny Indigenous religious thought or reduce it to either resistance or submission.

The geography of the chapter creates an important limit. Large parts of the continent had no sustained resident mission by 1821; elsewhere missions had been present for centuries. Even within one empire the reach of clergy was irregular, and political upheaval could close one mission while another congregation continued. The date 1821 usefully marks the end of Spanish colonial rule in Mexico and a significant renewal of obligations imposed on the Russian-American Company, but it does not mark a uniform religious settlement. Catholic, Protestant, Moravian, Lutheran, and Orthodox institutions were still being transformed by Indigenous participation. Subsequent histories of tribal political reform, religious revitalization, schooling, urban communities, and modern sovereignty cannot be understood without recognizing these earlier religious networks and their unresolved conflicts.

Chapter 54 therefore closes Part IX with a wider conclusion: Indigenous peoples were not merely the objects of rival empires' religious policies. They interpreted doctrines, selected teachers, established congregations, debated ceremonies, defended families, and sometimes rejected missions altogether. Those actions occurred under conditions ranging from negotiated exchange to extreme coercion. The history of Christianity in Indigenous North America is accordingly inseparable from the history of land, kinship, language, sovereignty, and colonial power. It is a field of continuing Indigenous life rather than a completed episode of European expansion. Chapter 55 turns to the diplomatic choices through which Native nations navigated the same imperial world.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that Christianity's expansion across Indigenous North America was not a simple replacement of Native religion by European missionary teaching. Native peoples encountered Christianity under strikingly different political conditions, from conquest and compulsory missionization to invitation, negotiation, and Indigenous-led movement, and they interpreted it according to their own experiences, languages, and aims. The resulting Native Christian traditions became part of Indigenous history.

Section 54.1 through 54.4 established the interpretive framework. Conversion, baptism, affiliation, participation, belief, and community transformation are different things, and the sources do not always allow them to be separated. Indigenous religious traditions varied widely and were bound up with political and community life, and Native listeners evaluated Christian claims in light of that knowledge. Missionary traditions differed institutionally and in their relationship to colonial states. Translators and catechists, usually unnamed, shaped what Christianity meant in Native languages, and every source type, from missionary letters to confraternity accounts to Native-authored writing, reflects its creator's purposes.

In Mexico, Nahua and Maya communities developed Catholic institutions through Native officials, catechists, scribes, and confraternities. Violent campaigns against idolatry, such as the Texcoco trial of 1539 and the Maní inquiry of 1562, coexisted with long-term accommodation in which towns gained substantial local control over religious life. Confraternities and saints' festivals tied devotion to community finances, mutual aid, and local politics. In the north, Jesuit missions among the Yaqui, Rarámuri, and O'odham were built through a mix of invitation, negotiation, and force, produced durable community institutions, and met recurrent resistance until the Jesuit expulsion of 1767 changed the setting.

In the Spanish borderlands, Franciscan missions in Florida depended on the consent of Native chiefs and fell into crisis through disease, labor demands, and conflicts like the Guale revolt of 1597 and the Timucua rebellion of 1656, before the mission chain was destroyed in 1704. Among the Pueblos, suppression of ceremonies before 1680 contributed to revolt, and the post-1692 settlement allowed a durable coexistence of Catholic and Pueblo practice. In California, Native people entered the missions for varied reasons that differed from the Franciscans' aims, endured harsh conditions, and responded with both adaptation and resistance.

In New France, Wendat, Mohawk, and other Haudenosaunee Catholics formed communities such as Lorette and Kahnawake whose members were both devout and Indigenous. The case of Kateri Tekakwitha illustrates both Native religious commitment and the limits of missionary-mediated sources. In New England, praying towns and Martha's Vineyard congregations developed Native-led Christian institutions and literacy, and King Philip's War brought violence against Native Christians. In the eighteenth century, Samson Occom's ministry, writings, and role in Brothertown showed that Native Christians could challenge colonial institutions from within Christianity.

The Moravian Lenape communities of the Ohio country illustrated the vulnerability of Native Christians caught between warring parties, as the Gnadenhutten massacre of 1782 demonstrated. In Greenland and Labrador, Lutheran and Moravian missions developed in tandem with trade, translation, and settlement change, and Inuit families shaped how they engaged with the stations. In Alaska, the Orthodox mission at Kodiak, begun in 1794, began amid the coercive fur trade, with missionaries sometimes opposing company abuses, while the major translation work followed after 1824.

Household and gender history showed that religious transformation occurred in families, funerals, naming, marriage, and education, as much as in churches. Across regions, women transmitted prayers, maintained images, and negotiated marriage customs, while generations and factions within communities interpreted change differently.

Section 54.19 compared patterns of change, coercive conversion, gradual adaptation, Indigenous-led movements, preservation of ceremonies, coexistence, and resistance, and argued that resisting colonial religious institutions is not the same as rejecting Christian belief. It assessed the term syncretism, noting its descriptive uses and its tendency to imply inauthenticity, and it affirmed that Native Christian identity is not a contradiction of Indigenous belonging.

Around 1821, Native Christianity was regionally diverse, contested, and unfinished. Some communities had maintained Christian institutions for nearly three centuries; some had lost them to war and disease; and most of the continent's peoples had not yet been touched directly by missions. New religious movements, treated in Chapter 62, were emerging in response. The chapter's evidence shows that colonial coercion and missionary authority shaped but did not determine Indigenous religious life, and that Native peoples remained the principal authors of their own religious histories.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter concludes Part IX by turning from the geopolitics of horses, raiding, captivity, and trade to a quieter but equally consequential transformation: the changing religious lives of Indigenous peoples. It shows that the history of a religion in a community is never only the history of its institutions. It is the history of translators, catechists, confraternity officers, mothers, ministers, hymn singers, and ceremonial leaders who decided, each in their own circumstances, what to take up, what to protect, and what to refuse.

The chapter also reveals how unevenly power was distributed. Missionaries and colonial officials often held force, funds, and institutions, and Native people sometimes paid for their choices with their lives, as at Gnadenhutten, or with their communities, as in Apalachee. Yet across the continent, Native peoples built institutions of their own, from town confraternities to Brothertown, and Indigenous languages became carriers of Christian text. These developments persisted well beyond 1821 and remain part of the lives of many Indigenous communities.

Later chapters build on these themes. Chapter 55 turns to Indigenous diplomacy and the balance of power, where religious communities such as Kahnawake and Moravian settlements appear as political actors. Chapter 62 returns to religious revitalization and unity, Chapter 64 to Cherokee constitutionalism, and Chapter 87 to boarding schools, which echo earlier struggles over children and religious instruction.

Finally, the chapter models a way of reading evidence. Sources written by missionaries cannot simply be trusted or dismissed; they must be read alongside Native-language texts, community records, and Indigenous memory. Doing so allows a more accurate history in which Christianity is neither an unchallenged success of missionary effort nor a mere mask over unchanged tradition, but one of the many arenas in which Indigenous peoples exercised judgment, adapted, and endured.
