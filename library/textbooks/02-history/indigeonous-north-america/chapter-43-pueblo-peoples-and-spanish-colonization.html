Pueblo Peoples and Spanish Colonization

Indigenous Peoples of North America: A Continental History

Chapter Opening

Spanish colonization of New Mexico was declared in 1598, but it was not accomplished by a declaration. For eight decades it had to be negotiated, fed, built, and defended through the labor, food, knowledge, and forbearance of Pueblo communities that Spanish officials claimed to govern and that never ceased to govern themselves.

At Abó, in central New Mexico, the roofless walls of a seventeenth-century church rise in red-brown sandstone beside the low mounded remains of the Pueblo town that supplied the labor to raise them. A short distance away stand the remains of similar churches at Quarai and at the site known as Gran Quivira, the Pueblo de las Humanas. Within a few years of the 1670s, all three communities were emptied. Today the walls are preserved as part of Salinas Pueblo Missions National Monument, and they pose a question that this chapter takes as its subject: what did it mean for Pueblo peoples to live for nearly eighty years inside a colonial order that had been planted in their homelands, and why did that order prove so much more fragile than the size of its buildings suggests?

The chapter begins in 1598, when Juan de Oñate led several hundred colonists, soldiers, and missionaries north from New Spain with a royal commission to settle the upper Río Grande, and it ends in the late 1670s, on the eve of the Pueblo Revolt of 1680. It is a period of roughly eighty years during which the Pueblo world was reorganized by a mission system, by tribute and labor demands, by the arrival of Old World livestock and crops, by recurring drought and epidemic disease, and by a long contest over who held authority to define religion, law, and community. The period is short enough to be studied in institutional and local detail and long enough to reveal how colonial institutions changed over several generations.

Two simplifications must be avoided. The first treats Pueblo peoples as passive recipients of a Spanish program, converted or crushed according to the intentions of governors and friars. The second treats Pueblo society as a sealed and unchanging tradition that simply endured behind a thin colonial surface. The evidence supports neither. Pueblo communities negotiated, withheld, complied, fled, adopted, adapted, and sometimes fought. The Spanish colonial government, for its part, was small, poorly funded, distant from the viceregal center at Mexico City, and divided between Franciscan missionaries and secular officials who quarreled bitterly over the same Pueblo people whose work sustained both. Colonial rule operated through coercion, but it also depended on cooperation that it could never fully command.

The chapter also insists on the diversity of the Pueblo world. Spanish writers used the word pueblo, meaning "town," for dozens of communities that spoke languages from different families and organized their governments, ceremonies, and kinship systems in different ways. A Tewa town on the Río Grande, a Keresan town on a mesa top, a Zuni town in the west, a Hopi village in northeastern Arizona, and a Tompiro town in the Salinas basin did not experience Spanish rule in the same way or on the same schedule. Much of the historical work of this chapter lies in following those differences.

The sources for this history are unevenly distributed and often troubling to use. Most surviving written records were produced by Spanish governors, friars, notaries, and royal officials, who wrote to justify their conduct, to request resources, or to accuse their rivals. Pueblo voices appear in these records mostly through translators, testimony taken under duress, and the reports of people who had reasons to misrepresent what they saw. Historians therefore combine these documents with archaeology, with the architectural evidence of mission sites and abandoned towns, with environmental data such as tree-ring records of drought, and with the historical knowledge that Pueblo nations themselves maintain. This chapter introduces students to reading each of those kinds of evidence critically.

Finally, the chapter has a defined boundary. It explains the colonial institutions, social pressures, and political tensions that existed before 1680. It does not narrate the organization, course, or consequences of the Pueblo Revolt, which is the subject of Chapter 44. Readers should regard the final sections of this chapter as an account of an unstable colonial order, not as a prediction. Historical actors in the 1670s did not know what would happen in 1680, and the sources show Pueblo and Spanish people alike making decisions under conditions that were uncertain, contested, and in many places desperate.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why the Spanish term "Pueblo" does not describe a single political nation, and distinguish among the major Pueblo language groups and regional communities at the end of the sixteenth century.
  2. Describe Pueblo systems of government, ceremonial leadership, agriculture, and exchange as they existed before sustained Spanish rule.
  3. Analyze the strengths and limits of Spanish administrative and missionary records, archaeology, and Pueblo historical knowledge as sources for colonial New Mexico.
  4. Trace the establishment of Spanish New Mexico from Juan de Oñate's expedition of 1598 through the founding of Santa Fe around 1609–1610.
  5. Evaluate the Acoma conflict of 1598–1599, distinguishing documented judicial sentences from uncertain claims about their execution and Spanish justifications from Pueblo experience.
  6. Explain how the Franciscan mission system operated, how it expanded unevenly across Pueblo regions, and how it transformed Pueblo space and labor.
  7. Assess competing interpretations of Pueblo conversion, adaptation, and religious coercion.
  8. Distinguish encomienda tribute from repartimiento labor and explain how each altered Pueblo households, agriculture, and community obligations.
  9. Analyze the roles of women, households, and neighboring Indigenous peoples, including Apache and Diné communities, in the colonial economy and regional politics.
  10. Compare local uprisings and acts of resistance before 1680, and explain why they did not produce a sustained regional coalition during that period.

  12. Evaluate the claim that Spanish colonial authority in New Mexico always depended on Pueblo cooperation and never eliminated Pueblo political and cultural agency.

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did Pueblo communities respond to nearly eight decades of Spanish occupation, and why did their responses differ from one community to another?
  2. What did Spanish declarations of possession and vassalage mean to the Spanish who made them, and what might they have meant to the Pueblo leaders who witnessed them?
  3. How should historians use colonial records written by governors and friars to reconstruct the experience of people those authors governed and judged?
  4. In what ways did the Franciscan mission system depend on Pueblo labor, Pueblo leadership, and Pueblo food production?

  9. How did environmental stress, epidemic disease, and regional insecurity interact with colonial demands to destabilize Spanish rule?

Historical Context

The Pueblo peoples of the late sixteenth century inhabited a region extending from the upper Río Grande valley and its tributaries westward across the Colorado Plateau to Zuni and the Hopi mesas, with communities also on the eastern edge of the Plains and in the Salinas basin east of the Río Grande. Their ancestors had built stone and adobe towns, farmed in a demanding semi-arid environment, and maintained trade and ceremonial relationships across great distances, as Chapter 18 explains. Chapter 30 surveys the Pueblo nations and their neighbors on the eve of sustained colonization. The present chapter assumes that background and recalls only what is needed to understand how Spanish rule was received.

Pueblo communities were not unfamiliar with Spanish power when Oñate arrived. Francisco Vázquez de Coronado's expedition of 1540–1542 had occupied the Tiguex towns on the Río Grande, with violent consequences examined in Chapter 42. Small expeditions in the early 1580s, led by Francisco Sánchez Chamuscado and Antonio de Espejo, had revisited the region. These entradas were short and left no permanent garrison, but they left memories of Spanish violence, Spanish demands for food and cloth, and Spanish interest in Pueblo towns as a source of labor, and they left Spanish descriptions of a populous, agricultural region that stimulated colonial ambitions in New Spain. By the 1590s, the silver economy of northern New Spain, described in Chapter 42, had extended Spanish settlement as far as Nueva Vizcaya, making a new northern province imaginable to officials and investors.

The legal and religious framework of the new colony reflected sixteenth-century Spanish imperial thought. The Crown claimed sovereignty over the territories its agents "discovered" and held that Indigenous people were free vassals who owed the Crown tribute and who should be instructed in Christianity. The Ordinances of 1573, issued under Philip II, had instructed colonizers to pursue "pacification" through peaceful settlement and missionary work rather than "conquest." In practice, those principles coexisted with the institution of encomienda, forced labor drafts, and military violence. Understanding this gap between imperial ideals and local practice is essential to reading the documents of New Mexico, because colonial officials routinely described their actions in the language of lawful pacification while engaging in demands that Pueblo communities experienced as coercion.

Spanish New Mexico was always a small and peripheral colony. Its Spanish and mixed colonial population, which included people of Spanish, Mexican Indian, African, and mixed descent, numbered in the low thousands at its largest before 1680, while Pueblo people greatly outnumbered them even after severe seventeenth-century population loss. The colony was separated from Mexico City by a long road, later called the Camino Real de Tierra Adentro, and its Franciscan missions depended on royal subsidies and supply caravans that arrived at intervals of years. These conditions meant that Spanish authority was thin, that colonists were dependent on Pueblo agricultural surplus and labor, and that conflicts among Spanish factions had real consequences for Pueblo communities.

The Pueblo World at the End of the Sixteenth Century

In the 1590s the Pueblo world was a patchwork of autonomous towns, not a unified political body. Spanish observers counted these communities in the dozens, and sixteenth-century Spanish writers sometimes spoke of more than a hundred settlements, though the numbers vary with who was counting and what counted as a distinct town. Modern estimates of total Pueblo population at the end of the sixteenth century range widely, from perhaps 60,000 to well over 100,000, and the uncertainty reflects the limits of the evidence, not a settled scholarly figure. What is clear is that the region was densely settled by the standards of the northern Spanish frontier and that its communities, by the time of sustained colonization, had already been reshaped by earlier population movements, drought cycles, and shifting alliances.

The Pueblo peoples spoke languages belonging to several unrelated families. Tewa, Tiwa, and Towa belong to the Tanoan family. Tewa-speaking towns lay along the Río Grande and its tributaries north of present-day Santa Fe, including Ohkay Owingeh (San Juan), Santa Clara, San Ildefonso, Nambé, Pojoaque, and Tesuque. Northern Tiwa speakers lived at Taos and Picuris, while Southern Tiwa towns, including those later known as Sandia and Isleta, lay along the middle Río Grande. Towa speakers lived at Jemez and at Pecos. Farther south and east, the Piro and Tompiro towns of the Río Grande and the Salinas basin also spoke Tanoan languages. Keresan-speaking communities, whose language is unrelated to Tanoan, included Cochiti, Santo Domingo (Kewa), San Felipe, Santa Ana, Zia, and Acoma, each independent. Zuni is a language isolate with no demonstrated relatives, and the Hopi language belongs to the Uto-Aztecan family, which extends far beyond the Southwest. Many of these communities are known today by both their Spanish-derived and their own names, and this chapter uses both where helpful, as Chapter 1 explains.

Spanish usage obscured these distinctions. The word pueblo, a Spanish word for a town or village, became the general name for the people who lived in such towns. It carried an assumption that these were a single kind of people, a view that facilitated administrative convenience and missionary planning. Pueblo communities distinguished themselves by language, by town, by clan and ceremonial society, and by history. They sometimes cooperated and sometimes quarreled. Their relationships to the land, to water sources, and to neighboring communities were distinctive. When this chapter uses "Pueblo" in the plural to refer to all these communities, it does so as a convenience while attempting to name specific towns and language groups whenever the evidence permits.

The Pueblo world also had regions with distinct histories of Spanish contact. The Río Grande towns around Tiguex had suffered during Coronado's expedition and could draw on a memory of that violence. Western communities at Zuni and Hopi had experienced shorter and less sustained contact in the sixteenth century. The Salinas towns east of the river and the Piro towns of the south were in contact with the Plains and Chihuahuan Desert regions in ways that would later affect their exposure to Apache raiding and to Spanish supply lines. These variations meant that when Oñate arrived, Pueblo leaders had different expectations, different histories, and different strategic interests.

Division I · Section 43.2

Pueblo Government, Community, and Ceremonial Authority

Pueblo towns governed themselves through institutions that combined ritual, political, and social functions in ways that Spanish officials found difficult to categorize. Authority in most communities rested not with a single ruler but with overlapping groups of leaders: religious officers who maintained the ceremonial calendar, councils of elders and society leaders, and officers with responsibilities for war, hunting, agriculture, or community works. The Spanish word cacique, a term borrowed from the Caribbean, was applied broadly to Pueblo leaders, often to people whose authority was religious as much as political and whose roles differed from town to town. Ceremonial leadership was not separate from governance. Maintaining the proper relationship among people, the land, and the seasons was understood as a collective responsibility that organized the community's labor, its use of water and fields, and its relationships with other towns.

Social organization differed across Pueblo regions. Among the Hopi, Zuni, and Keresan communities, descent was reckoned through clans traced through women, and clan membership structured marriage, ceremonial responsibility, and the use of land. Tewa-speaking towns were organized around dual divisions, often described in English as Summer and Winter moieties, with complementary leadership responsibilities. Other Tanoan towns and eastern communities had their own arrangements. Within communities, religious societies, whose membership and ceremonial knowledge were closely guarded, carried out ceremonies connected to rain, crops, curing, hunting, and war. Because specific ceremonial knowledge is not open to outsiders, and because Pueblo nations today decide for themselves what may be shared, this chapter describes Pueblo religious institutions at the level of public historical knowledge and avoids reproducing the esoteric details that some colonial records and later anthropological writings included without community consent.

Because authority was distributed, Spanish officials repeatedly discovered that an agreement with one Pueblo leader did not bind another. The Rio Grande Tanoan towns, the Keresan towns, Zuni, and the Hopi villages each managed their own affairs, and even towns that shared a language might disagree about alliances, land, or ceremonial obligations. Officers with responsibility for defense, often called war captains in the literature, handled relations with outsiders in many communities, while religious and clan leaders retained authority over ceremony and land. A Spanish governor who wished to obtain corn, labor, or obedience therefore had to deal with each town separately, and the oaths of obedience recorded in 1598 had to be repeated, in effect, through decades of town-by-town negotiation. This fragmentation limited what any single Pueblo leader could promise on behalf of others, and it also limited how far Spanish officials could rely on any one agreement.

Historical Vocabulary
Pueblo and Kiva

Pueblo. In Spanish, pueblo means "town" or "people." By the seventeenth century it had become the general colonial name for the agricultural, town-dwelling Indigenous communities of New Mexico and Arizona, and it later became an English-language ethnonym. In colonial documents the word can refer to a single settlement, such as "the pueblo of Pecos," or to the people of such a settlement.

Kiva. The term kiva is a Hopi word that is widely used in English for the ceremonial chambers of Pueblo communities, which may be built underground, semi-subterranean, or within the walls of a town, and whose forms differ among regions and periods. Kivas served as places for ritual, for meetings of ceremonial societies, and for certain community activities. Spanish colonial records seldom use the word; they speak instead of estufas ("stoves," referring to heated rooms) and, in the language of missionary suppression, of the places of "idolatry."

Potential misunderstanding. The word "kiva" can suggest a single standardized structure with a single function, but kivas varied in architecture and meaning, and not every Pueblo community used the term or the same forms. Nor were kivas merely "churches" of a Pueblo religion. Ceremonial space, community governance, and the maintenance of social relationships were interwoven, which is one reason Spanish attacks on ceremonial life struck at community institutions broadly.

Colonial rule introduced a second layer of town government. Spanish administrators and Franciscan friars promoted a system of annually chosen Pueblo officers modeled on Spanish municipal government, with titles such as governor (gobernador), lieutenant, constable, and church officers such as fiscal. Documents from the seventeenth century show Pueblo men holding these offices, which gave the Spanish a channel for demanding labor and tribute and gave Pueblo communities officers who could represent them to colonial authorities. Historians continue to discuss how these offices related to existing Pueblo political and ceremonial leadership. The evidence suggests that, in many communities, the colonial offices were integrated into an older political order and that existing leaders often found ways to remain influential, though the degree of integration varied by community and period.

Agriculture, Water, Exchange, and Regional Relationships

Pueblo agriculture centered on maize, beans, and squash, supplemented by cotton in warmer areas, by wild plants, and by hunting. The methods differed with the environment. Río Grande towns and some others used irrigation ditches drawn from rivers and streams. Farther west, farmers at Hopi, Zuni, and elsewhere relied on floodwater from seasonal runoff, spring-fed fields, and dry-farming techniques adapted to extremely limited rainfall. Storage of surplus corn in household and community rooms was a central strategy for surviving drought years, and the loss or diversion of stored corn would become one of the most consequential effects of Spanish tribute and requisition. Agricultural labor followed a seasonal calendar tied to ceremonial responsibilities, so that interruptions to the farming calendar reverberated through community life.

Pueblo towns were also exchange centers. Cotton textiles, maize, pottery, turquoise, salt, and other goods moved between towns and across the region. Exchange with Plains peoples was especially important: Apache communities and other Plains groups brought bison meat, hides, and other goods to trade with Pueblo farmers for corn, cotton blankets, and other products. Pecos, east of the Río Grande, functioned as a gateway for this trade, and Taos hosted gatherings where Pueblo and Plains peoples exchanged goods. Pueblo communities also had relationships with the Diné to the west, a people the Spanish in the 1620s called the "Apaches de Navajó," as well as with other peoples of the Plains and southern deserts. The colonial period would transform these relationships, because Spanish settlers, governors, and soldiers entered the same trade networks and sought control over them, as Sections 43.19 and 43.24 explain.

Textiles deserve particular attention because they became a principal object of colonial demand. Cotton was cultivated in the warmer parts of the Pueblo world, and Pueblo weavers, who in many communities were men, produced cotton cloth that circulated in exchange with Plains peoples and other Pueblos long before Spanish arrival. Sixteenth-century Spanish expeditions had already demanded cloth and clothing from Pueblo towns, and the later encomienda system converted a familiar Pueblo craft into a standing obligation. Pecos is often described as a gateway town in part because Plains peoples brought bison hides and meat there to exchange for such goods, and the gatherings at Pecos and Taos drew visitors from considerable distances. Because the trade was seasonal and relied on established personal relationships between Pueblo and Plains families, it could not easily be redirected by Spanish officials, though governors would try to profit from it.

These economic relationships formed part of an interconnected regional system. Disruption in one part, such as a drought on the Plains, a conflict between Apache bands, or a Spanish demand for corn at a Río Grande town, could affect others. Chapter 51 examines Apache history, and Chapter 49 treats the introduction of horses to the Plains. Here it is enough to recognize that Pueblo towns were embedded in networks of exchange that long predated the colony, that Pueblo leaders understood those networks, and that the Spanish colony entered a region of existing political and commercial relationships rather than an empty frontier.

Division I · Section 43.4

Reconstructing Colonial Pueblo History: Archaeology, Oral Traditions, and Spanish Documents

The documentary record of seventeenth-century New Mexico is substantial in some respects and deeply limited in others. Surviving records include reports by governors and friars, royal instructions, account books, legal proceedings such as trial records and investigations of misconduct, and Inquisition files. Many records from the colony were destroyed in 1680, when Pueblo forces burned the archives in Santa Fe, so the surviving documentation is skewed toward materials preserved elsewhere, such as in Mexico City, Seville, and the archives of religious orders. Other records, such as the proceedings of residencia investigations and accusatory depositions, survive because the colony's factions were suing one another. Their quarrels generated paper, but also systematic bias.

Every document must be read in light of its author. Friars writing to the Crown or to their order sought continued support for the missions and often exaggerated the numbers of conversions and the sincerity of Pueblo Christianity. Governors defending themselves minimized coercion and attacked the friars. Colonists seeking encomiendas or royal favor emphasized their services. Pueblo people appear in these records as witnesses, as defendants, as laborers, and occasionally as petitioners, but their words were recorded by Spanish scribes and often translated through interpreters. A statement attributed to a Pueblo person in a Spanish document is evidence of what a Spanish writer recorded, not a transcript in the modern sense.

Archaeology addresses some of these limitations. Excavations at mission sites, in Pueblo towns occupied during the colonial period, and in Spanish settlements allow researchers to study architecture, household goods, diet, construction, and the use of space. The ruins at Pecos, Abó, Quarai, and Gran Quivira, which are protected as public parks, have been among the most thoroughly studied. Archaeology can show where people built churches and kivas, what they ate, what goods they imported or made, and when settlements were abandoned. It cannot by itself identify what individuals believed, and, as Chapter 1 notes, material evidence must be interpreted carefully before it is connected to claims about identity or belief. Environmental data, especially tree-ring records that preserve patterns of annual rainfall, help establish the timing and severity of droughts.

Pueblo historical knowledge forms a third body of evidence. Pueblo communities preserve histories of migration, of colonial violence, and of resistance, transmitted through oral tradition and community memory. Acoma, for example, maintains its own understanding of the events of 1598–1599, and the Pueblo nations have long regarded the Spanish colonial period as part of an ongoing history, not a closed chapter. These histories do not always correspond with Spanish documentary accounts, and they are not intended to; they answer different questions and serve different purposes. A historian working responsibly with colonial New Mexico must listen to all these kinds of evidence, ask what each can and cannot show, and acknowledge where they conflict. Scholarship such as Joseph P. Sánchez's study of New Mexico in the seventeenth century and Heather B. Trigg's archaeological study of household and economy in early colonial New Mexico illustrate how documentary and archaeological work may complement one another.

Division II — Spanish Occupation and the Establishment of New Mexico · Section 43.5

Juan de Oñate and the Colonization Expedition of 1598

Juan de Oñate was a wealthy member of a prominent family in the silver-mining region of northern New Spain. In 1595 he signed a contract with the viceroy authorizing him to colonize the lands north of Nueva Vizcaya at his own expense, in return for titles, privileges, and the right to distribute encomiendas. After delays caused by administrative review, the expedition departed in early 1598 with several hundred men, women, and children, soldiers, Franciscan friars, wagons, and thousands of head of livestock. The expedition crossed the Río Grande near present-day El Paso, and on April 30, 1598, Oñate staged a formal act of possession in which he claimed the lands, and their inhabitants, for Philip II. Spanish colonists named the province New Mexico.

Oñate's contract followed a decade of unlicensed attempts to enter Pueblo country that had alarmed the Crown. In 1590–1591, Gaspar Castaño de Sosa led a colonizing party up the Pecos River into the Pueblo region without royal authorization and was arrested and returned to New Spain by an officer sent after him. In 1594–1595, the unauthorized expedition associated with Francisco Leyva de Bonilla and Antonio Gutiérrez de Humaña traveled through Pueblo country toward the Plains. According to the later testimony of the Indigenous participant Jusepe Gutiérrez, Humaña killed Bonilla during the expedition; the exact itinerary and fate of the remaining party are imperfectly documented. These episodes help explain why the viceroy and the Crown insisted on a formally licensed colonizer with financial guarantees. They also meant that, by 1598, some Pueblo communities had had additional, recent experience of Spanish parties that demanded food and quarters. The route of the 1598 expedition crossed the Chihuahuan desert and the dry stretch of the Río Grande corridor later called the Jornada del Muerto, a journey that tested the wagons and livestock before the colonists reached the first Pueblo towns in the summer of 1598.

That ritual possession was a legal performance, written down by notaries, and it asserted Spanish sovereignty over lands that none of the Pueblo communities had yet encountered under Oñate. The Ordinances of 1573 required peaceful treatment of Indigenous people, and Oñate's instructions contained language to that effect. Spanish law of the period also assumed that Indigenous nations owed obedience to the Crown and that Christian missionaries had a right to preach among them. These beliefs gave Spanish officials a legal vocabulary in which seizing food, requiring labor, or punishing resistance could be framed as lawful consequences of vassalage. The vocabulary did not require Pueblo consent, and the sources do not show that Pueblo communities understood themselves to have consented to subordination.

As the expedition moved up the Río Grande in the summer of 1598, Oñate summoned Pueblo leaders to ceremonies at which Spanish officials recorded that the leaders swore obedience and vassalage. A significant gathering took place at Santo Domingo in July 1598, with interpreters, including Mexican Indian and Pueblo intermediaries, translating Spanish statements. Spanish notaries described these gatherings as acts of submission. The Pueblo leaders who attended presumably had their own reasons: to learn the intentions of the newcomers, to avoid violence, to obtain trade or protection, or to preserve their communities' autonomy. The record cannot tell us what each leader understood. What can be established is that a Spanish declaration of sovereignty and a Pueblo act of participation were not equivalent to Indigenous consent to colonial rule, a distinction this chapter will keep in view.

Division II · Section 43.6

The First Spanish Settlement and Pueblo Responses

Oñate established the first colonial headquarters in the summer of 1598 at a Tewa town on the east bank of the Río Grande, which the Spanish renamed San Juan de los Caballeros, at the location of present-day Ohkay Owingeh. The settlers occupied a portion of an existing Pueblo settlement before moving, in the following year or so, to a site across the river that they called San Gabriel. Franciscan friars began a chapel and began religious instruction. The colonists immediately encountered the problem that would shape the early colony: the expedition had brought livestock and some supplies, but it could not feed several hundred people for long without Pueblo corn.

The colonists obtained food through a combination of gifts, trade, and requisition. Pueblo towns that stored corn in household and communal rooms had surpluses in some seasons, but they could not meet the demands of a large, new population without sacrificing their own security. Spanish accounts describe soldiers and officials taking maize and cotton textiles, sometimes with promises of payment, and describe Pueblo communities supplying provisions under pressure. Because the Spanish arrived with horses, iron tools, firearms, and an assertive willingness to use force, Pueblo leaders knew that refusal could have serious consequences. The Spanish for their part knew that the Pueblos could withhold food and abandon towns, and they relied on cooperation from the Tewa communities that had accepted them.

Communication was a persistent problem. Few Spanish colonists spoke any Pueblo language in 1598, and the languages of the Tewa, Keresan, Tiwa, Towa, Zuni, and Hopi towns were mutually unintelligible. The expedition relied on interpreters, including Indigenous people from central Mexico who had accompanied earlier parties, and on Pueblo individuals who learned some Spanish. These intermediaries held considerable influence, since their renderings determined what each side understood of oaths, demands, and warnings. A mistranslation, or a deliberate softening or sharpening of a statement, could shape events, and the Spanish record rarely reveals how translation was done. As friars gradually learned Pueblo languages, they became both interpreters and rival interpreters, with a stake in how communities were represented to the governor and the Crown.

The relocation to San Gabriel, across the river from San Juan, brought a larger Spanish settlement into the same agricultural landscape. Colonists needed houses, storehouses, a church, and fields, and Spanish accounts show that the construction and the cultivation of irrigated fields drew on Tewa knowledge of water and soils as well as on Pueblo labor. The Tewa towns that hosted the colonists thus bore the earliest and most continuous costs of colonization, and later documents refer to their resources being strained. The settlement also consumed timber, and its livestock grazed near Pueblo fields. These early burdens do not appear as a single dramatic event in the documents, but their cumulative effect was to establish that the colony's survival would be paid for in Pueblo corn, cloth, and work.

Pueblo responses to the settlers differed across communities and across time. Some leaders sought to maintain peaceful relations, provided food, and assigned workers, perhaps hoping that accommodation would limit Spanish demands or secure an ally against rivals. Others resisted or withdrew. Spanish writers noted that some communities left their towns for the mountains or the Plains when soldiers approached. The Spanish were not the only newcomers to bring disruption: the presence of a large body of settlers affected resources, water, and the regional movement of goods. The conflict at Acoma, examined next, shows what happened when communities and colonists disagreed about the terms of the exchange.

Division II · Section 43.7

Acoma, 1598–1599: Conflict, Punishment, and Colonial Terror

Acoma, a Keresan-speaking town, stood on top of a sandstone mesa rising more than a hundred meters above the surrounding plain, west of the Río Grande, in a location that had been a stronghold for generations. Spanish writers had encountered Acoma during earlier expeditions, and Oñate visited it in the fall of 1598 during a tour of Pueblo towns. According to Spanish accounts, Acoma leaders received him with some formality. In December 1598, Juan de Zaldívar, Oñate's nephew and a senior officer, led a party to Acoma to obtain provisions. The Spanish record states that a dispute arose over supplies and that Acoma warriors attacked the Spanish party on December 4, killing Zaldívar and a number of his men. Accounts give the number of Spanish dead as between about ten and thirteen, with a few survivors escaping.

The Spanish account presents the killing as an unprovoked act of treachery. Pueblo perspectives, including the historical knowledge maintained at Acoma, treat it differently, as a defense against demands for food and goods that the community had no obligation to meet and could not afford to meet in a winter of limited stores. The Spanish documents themselves reveal that the party came to take supplies. Whatever the precise sequence of events on the mesa, which only Spanish and Acoma accounts can reconstruct, the killing of Spanish officers was understood by Oñate and his council as a challenge to Spanish authority that required a decisive response.

In January 1599, Oñate dispatched Zaldívar's brother, Vicente de Zaldívar, with about seventy soldiers to attack Acoma. The assault lasted several days, from about January 21 to 24, and involved fierce fighting on the mesa and the burning of much of the town. Spanish accounts claim that many Acoma people were killed, with figures in Spanish sources reaching as high as around 800, and that some hundreds more were taken prisoner. These figures come from Spanish participants and cannot be independently verified. Acoma resisted fiercely, and the Spanish victory was achieved at heavy cost to the community, whose population, houses, and stores were devastated.

After the assault, Oñate convened a trial of the surviving Acoma captives in February 1599. The trial record, which survives, documents the proceedings and the sentences. Acoma men over the age of about twenty-five were condemned to have one foot amputated and to serve twenty years in personal servitude; younger men and older women were also condemned to twenty years of servitude; and children under about twelve were to be taken from their families and placed in Spanish custody, with some young girls assigned to be sent to convents in Mexico City. Two Hopi men who had been at Acoma were reported to have been sentenced to the loss of a hand and sent home as a warning. The sentence is thus documented, but the extent to which every part of it was carried out is less certain. Testimony in later investigations of Oñate's conduct and other Spanish accounts indicate that at least some amputations were carried out, and that a number of captives were distributed to colonists as servants. Precise numbers, however, remain uncertain, as do the later fates of many of the people sentenced. Students should distinguish carefully between a judicial sentence, which is documented, and the details of its implementation, which must be reconstructed from fragmentary and often contested testimony.

Oñate's March 1599 Report to the Viceroy

The report was written within weeks of the assault on Acoma and before the trial and sentencing had been fully reviewed by any outside authority. Oñate wrote with a clear purpose: to secure continued viceregal support, supplies, and reinforcement for a colony facing food shortages, and to justify the use of force.

Brief paraphrase (not a quotation): In the report, Oñate describes the land and its Pueblo inhabitants in terms of their agricultural wealth, their numbers, and their potential as a workforce and as converts. He recounts the Spanish obtaining formal oaths of obedience from Pueblo leaders. He then presents the killing of Zaldívar and his men at Acoma as a treacherous attack on Spaniards who had come in peace, and he describes the Spanish assault as a necessary and just response that was intended to make an example of Acoma and thereby secure peace in the province.

Analysis: The report is a valuable record of Spanish aims and of the framework within which Oñate understood his authority. It shows how the language of vassalage, treachery, and just war was used to justify violence. It also contains descriptive information about Pueblo towns and landscapes. Read carefully, it reveals pressures on the colony, including the dependence on Pueblo food and the colonists' insecurity.

It does not preserve Acoma testimony about the events of December 1598, and it cannot establish what Acoma leaders intended, what they understood of Spanish demands, or how their community experienced the assault and its aftermath. Numbers of casualties and captives are Spanish estimates. The document does not itself record the full course of the later trial and its implementation.

Perspective: The report represents the perspective of the Spanish governor and his need to justify force. Missing are the perspectives of Acoma leaders and survivors, of the Hopi men who were sentenced, of other Pueblo communities that watched events unfold, and of Spanish colonists and friars who may have disagreed with Oñate.

Division II · Section 43.8

The Early Colony in Crisis: Hunger, Discontent, and Royal Intervention

The colony that Oñate founded was shaky from the beginning. Many colonists had expected quick wealth, either in mines or in the rich towns that earlier expeditions had reported. Neither materialized. Disappointment, hunger, and quarrels with Oñate's government led to repeated complaints and, in 1601, to the departure of a number of colonists who left for New Spain. Oñate himself spent considerable time away on exploratory expeditions, including an expedition to the Plains in 1601 in search of the region called Quivira and a journey to the Colorado River and the Gulf of California in 1604–1605. While he was away, the colony relied on Pueblo food and labor and on the uncertain discipline of his subordinates.

The colony's instability affected Pueblo communities directly. Oñate's long absences on expeditions removed soldiers and horses from the settlement, and the expeditions themselves relied on supplies provided by Pueblo towns along the way. The journey to the Plains in 1601 began from the Pueblo country and depended on Pueblo corn and on Indigenous guides, and the return of the party in need of resupply placed new demands on the same communities. When colonists deserted in 1601 and Oñate's officers pursued them, the colony's remaining soldiers were occupied with Spanish disputes, leaving Pueblo towns to deal with a government that was simultaneously demanding and distracted. Spanish sources of the period are mostly the complaints of colonists and officials, and they say little about how Pueblo leaders assessed these years, though flight from towns and reluctance to supply food are reported.

Reports reaching the viceroy and the Crown raised concerns about the colony's cost and about the conduct of its governor. Colonists accused Oñate of cruelty, both to Indigenous people and to Spanish settlers, and of failing to find wealth. The Crown was also concerned that the enterprise had not paid for itself. Spanish officials debated whether to abandon the province, to take it over as a royal colony, or to retain it for other reasons. Oñate resigned in 1607, and the resignation was accepted the following year.

The decisive argument for retention was religious. Franciscan reports, which claimed thousands of baptisms, presented New Mexico as a mission field in which abandonment would betray converts and leave them to apostasy. A figure of about seven thousand baptisms was cited in the period, though this was a Franciscan claim whose meaning, in the absence of systematic instruction, is uncertain. The Crown decided to retain the province and to support the missionary program with royal funds. This decision had lasting consequences: it made New Mexico, in effect, a mission colony in which the Franciscans held a privileged position, and it set the stage for the recurring conflicts between friars and governors examined in Section 43.14. Oñate was later investigated and, in 1614, convicted by a colonial court of mistreating Indigenous people and colonists, including excessive cruelty at Acoma, and was sentenced to permanent banishment from New Mexico, four years of banishment from Mexico City and its surrounding district, and a fine of 6,000 ducats. On appeal the Council of the Indies also confirmed a ten-year suspension from the offices of governor and captain general. He subsequently petitioned for relief from parts of the sentence, and the question of his historical reputation has been disputed in New Mexico ever since.

Division II · Section 43.9

Santa Fe and the Construction of Colonial Government

In 1609 the viceroy appointed Pedro de Peralta governor and instructed him to establish a new capital. Peralta founded the Villa of Santa Fe, formally La Villa Real de la Santa Fe de San Francisco, around 1609–1610, at a site in the foothills of the Sangre de Cristo mountains on the Santa Fe River, near earlier Pueblo settlements. The government complex, later known as the Palace of the Governors, was begun soon afterward. Santa Fe housed the governor, officials of the colonial government, a small garrison, and a settler population that included a district known as Analco across the river, where Mexican Indian allies who had come north with the colonists from central Mexico lived.

Spanish government in New Mexico took the form of a governor, who also held the title of captain general, appointed by the viceroy with the approval of the Crown, and who held broad military, judicial, and administrative authority. Beneath him served a lieutenant governor, a cabildo, or town council, in Santa Fe, and district magistrates (alcaldes mayores), who supervised Pueblo towns. The professional garrison was small, consisting of a few dozen soldiers at any one time, supplemented by colonists who held encomiendas and owed military service with arms and horses. Spanish power therefore rested on a very limited coercive force. When governors needed more, they used Pueblo auxiliaries, who sometimes fought alongside the Spanish against Apache and other opponents, a fact that illustrates the dependence of colonial authority on Indigenous participation.

Spanish settlement was clustered rather than evenly spread. A northern district centered on Santa Fe and the surrounding valleys contained the villa, the governor, and many estancias, while settlers also took up lands along the Río Grande to the south, where Pueblo towns such as Isleta lay near Spanish holdings. Settlers lived in small, dispersed ranches and farms rather than in compact towns, so that Spanish households often lay close to Pueblo fields. Colonial officials known as alcaldes mayores supervised districts, collecting tribute, recruiting labor, and settling disputes, and Pueblo governors and other officers dealt with them directly. These district officials, with the friars, were the people Pueblo communities encountered most often, and their behavior shaped how colonial government was experienced. The great majority of Pueblo people lived in towns that no governor visited regularly.

Santa Fe was connected to central New Mexico and to the broader viceroyalty by the long road later called the Camino Real de Tierra Adentro. Supply caravans of wagons, operated for the Franciscan order, brought goods from Mexico City at intervals of several years, carrying vestments, tools, and necessities for the missions. Colonial officials and merchants also used the road to send hides, textiles, and other products south, and later to export captives. The road bound the colony to Chihuahua and the mining districts, where New Mexican goods and captives found markets, as Section 43.19 explains.

Division III — Franciscan Missions and Religious Transformation · Section 43.10

The Franciscan Missionary Project in New Mexico

Franciscan friars accompanied Oñate's expedition in 1598, and from that year until 1680 the Franciscan Order was the principal religious institution in New Mexico. The friars understood their task as the conversion of Pueblo people to Catholic Christianity: baptizing them, teaching doctrine, establishing parish-like communities, and altering practices that they regarded as incompatible with the faith. They believed that the work was urgent and that Pueblo religious traditions, which they called idolatry, endangered souls. Their conviction was sincere, and it was also bound up with the political interests of the Spanish Crown, which justified its claim to New Mexico partly through the duty to evangelize.

The mission system rested on a particular institutional structure. A friar lived at a principal town, called a mission seat or cabecera, where a church and an attached residence and workspace, the convento, were constructed, and he made periodic visits to nearby communities, called visitas. In 1616 the Franciscans organized the province as a Custody of the Conversion of St. Paul, headed by an official called the custos, who supervised the friars and held considerable authority. The Crown paid a stipend for each friar from the royal treasury and financed supply caravans, which gave the order a dependable material basis. A few dozen friars served at any one time, which meant that each was responsible for several towns and many hundreds or thousands of people, and that many communities saw a friar only occasionally.

Mission finances shaped Pueblo experience. The Crown paid the friars' stipends and financed the supply caravans that brought vestments, wine, tools, and other goods north at intervals of roughly three years, but the missions did not run on subsidy alone. Friars relied on Pueblo labor and food to feed themselves and their assistants, to build and maintain church complexes, and to care for mission livestock and fields. Franciscan reports describe mission herds and gardens tended by Pueblo workers, and complaints by governors and colonists repeatedly allege that friars used Pueblo labor for purposes beyond religious work. The line between legitimate service to the church and economic exploitation was disputed at the time, and the evidence generally reflects the rival accounts of the parties to the dispute.

The friars also trained Pueblo assistants. Franciscan reports claim that they taught Pueblo children and young men to sing, to serve at the altar, and to perform skilled work, and some individuals became sacristans, singers, and church officers. These roles gave certain Pueblo people influence and a source of local standing, and they created a group of community members familiar with both Pueblo and Spanish institutions. Some such individuals appear to have supported the friars, but the evidence also shows that church positions could be held by people who continued to take part in Pueblo ceremonial life. The result was an institution whose daily operation depended on Pueblo participation at every level.

The relationship between missionary authority and the colonial state was close but unstable. The Crown's patronage of the church, a system in which royal officials supported and supervised ecclesiastical work, meant that friars relied on governors for military protection and for support in disciplining communities, and governors relied on friars to justify the colony. Yet their interests were frequently opposed. Friars wanted Pueblo labor and time devoted to church building, instruction, and religious observance. Governors and settlers wanted tribute, labor, and goods for their own enterprises. Section 43.14 examines this struggle. For Pueblo communities, the practical effect was that two competing Spanish authorities, each with its own claims, made demands on the same people, sometimes offering opportunities to play one against the other, and at other times doubling the burden.

Division III · Section 43.11

The Expansion of Mission Communities

Missions did not spread evenly or all at once. In the first years after 1598, friars were concentrated in the Tewa and Keresan towns near the colonial centers. The Franciscan push intensified in the 1620s, under a new organization and with the arrival of additional friars, and extended outward across the Río Grande valley, eastward to Pecos and the Salinas basin, and westward to Acoma, Zuni, and the Hopi towns. The timing and character of mission establishment depended on the availability of friars, the attitude of local leaders, the proximity of colonial authorities, and the willingness of towns to accommodate or resist. Pecos, with its large population and strategic position, was among the earliest major mission sites in the east, and the monumental church there, built in the 1620s, shows the scale of the effort. In the Salinas basin, mission building at Abó and Quarai began in the 1620s and continued for decades, and a large church at Las Humanas was constructed in the 1660s.

In the west, missionary efforts began later and met a different response. Friars established missions at Zuni, including at Hawikuh, in the 1620s and at the Hopi towns of Awatovi, Shungopavi, and Oraibi by about 1629, but these missions were far from the center of colonial power, had few friars, and were vulnerable. Hopi and Zuni communities, with greater distance from Santa Fe and with fewer Spanish soldiers nearby, had more capacity to resist or to reject missionary demands, and episodes of violence against friars in the 1630s, discussed in Section 43.23, reflect that capacity. Acoma, for its part, accepted a mission and, from about 1629, undertook the construction of the large church of San Esteban, a project described below.

In the northern and central Rio Grande valley, missions were established earlier and more densely than in the west. Friars were resident at or regularly visited Tewa, Tiwa, Keresan, and Towa towns, and the mission at Pecos became one of the largest. Jemez, in the mountains to the west of the Río Grande, received missionary attention in the 1620s, and Spanish sources associate the Jemez uprising of 1623 with that early missionary pressure, though the surviving evidence is thin. Taos and Picuris, at the northern edge of the Pueblo world, also had friars by the 1620s or 1630s. In practice many communities were visitas, receiving a friar for scheduled visits, and the presence of a resident friar was a mark of a community's importance and also of its exposure to close supervision.

The mission system was also unstable. The number of friars fluctuated, and some missions were left vacant for periods because of deaths, reassignments, and the difficulty of recruiting men for a remote province. The Zuni and Hopi missions were disrupted by the violence of the 1630s, and missions in the west were served intermittently thereafter. Benavides's Memorial reflects an optimistic moment near the high point of mission expansion, and later decades saw a more mixed picture, with conflict between friars and governors, a growing sense of exhaustion, and the pressures of drought and raiding. Students should therefore avoid picturing a uniform mission network that grew steadily for eight decades.

Pueblo responses to missionization varied enormously. Some communities seem to have welcomed or at least tolerated friars, perhaps because of the material goods they brought, because of alliances with them, because a friar's presence might protect against other Spanish exactions, or because some people were attracted to aspects of Christianity. Others resisted openly, expelled or killed friars, or abandoned their towns. Many adopted a mixed approach, cooperating outwardly while maintaining their own ceremonial life. The chronology of mission construction therefore does not map neatly onto the chronology of Pueblo acceptance; a large church might signal a successful coercive labor system as much as a successful conversion.

Alonso de Benavides's Memorial, 1630

Creator: Fray Alonso de Benavides, Franciscan custos of New Mexico from about 1626 to 1629 · Date: Printed in Madrid in 1630; a revised version followed in 1634 · Type: A published report and petition addressed to King Philip IV, available in modern published editions and translations

Context: After returning to Spain, Benavides wrote and published a memorial describing the province of New Mexico, its Pueblo peoples, and the work of the Franciscans. The document was addressed to the king and intended to secure continued royal support for the missions. It combines geographic and ethnographic description with an account of conversions and with claims about miraculous events.

Brief paraphrase (not a quotation): Benavides reports that the friars, in roughly two decades of work, had baptized a very large number of Pueblo people, a figure on the order of tens of thousands, and that the converts lived in dozens of towns organized into a number of mission districts, with churches and instruction. He praises the diligence of the friars, describes churches built by Pueblo labor, and presents the Pueblo people as receptive to Christian teaching.

Analysis: The Memorial is among the most detailed contemporary descriptions of the missions at their height, and it identifies towns, languages, and mission locations. It shows how the Franciscans wished to present their work to the Crown and how mission success was measured by baptisms and church building. It also reveals, indirectly, Franciscan attitudes toward Pueblo religion and toward governors who interfered with the friars.

Limitations: The author was a promoter of the mission program who had every reason to emphasize success and to minimize failure, coercion, and Pueblo resistance. Baptism counts do not measure belief, and the reliability of the numbers is uncertain. The Memorial says little about Pueblo labor demands, which it treats as natural, or about Pueblo religious continuity. It also reports miraculous events that historians treat as part of the religious culture of the author, not as evidence of what happened.

Perspective: The source represents a Franciscan missionary leader writing to the king. Absent are Pueblo accounts of the missions, the perspectives of friars who doubted the mission's success, and the views of governors and colonists who disagreed with the Franciscans.

Division III · Section 43.12

Churches, Conventos, and the Transformation of Pueblo Space

The most durable physical legacy of the mission system is its architecture. Mission churches were built of adobe or, in the Salinas district, of local stone, with massive walls, timber roofs, and attached convento complexes that included living quarters, workshops, storerooms, and gardens. These buildings were among the largest structures in the colony. Building them required skilled direction, which friars provided, and a great deal of labor, which Pueblo communities provided. Stone and adobe had to be quarried or formed, timbers had to be cut in distant mountains and carried to the site, often by hand over many miles because draft animals were limited, and walls and roofs had to be raised. Pueblo builders brought their own architectural knowledge of adobe construction and masonry, and the resulting churches combined European plans with local building techniques.

The scale of the construction is illustrated by the church of San Esteban at Acoma, begun around 1629 and completed over a number of years. Tradition and some documentary evidence hold that Acoma laborers carried large timbers from distant mountains and hauled earth to the mesa top to build the church and its adjoining cemetery. The difficulty of the work, performed by a community that had been devastated three decades earlier, underscores the capacity of the mission system to demand heavy labor. It also reflects the capacity of Pueblo communities to build and, in time, to hold the church as their own: San Esteban remains in use today and is part of Acoma's religious life.

Archaeological evidence from Pecos, Abó, Quarai, and Las Humanas has shown how mission building proceeded in different phases and how it related to the existing town. At several mission sites researchers have found ceremonial chambers built in or near convento courtyards, a pattern interpreted in different ways. Some scholars see it as evidence of Pueblo people incorporating traditional ritual space into the new mission landscape, perhaps with the knowledge of friars and perhaps despite them. Others emphasize the possibility that friars permitted such structures as an accommodation or that their significance was limited. The evidence alone cannot settle the question, but it shows that churches did not simply replace Pueblo ceremonial spaces; the two coexisted, sometimes uneasily, within the same settlements.

Building churches was a long process. Quarrying stone or making adobe bricks, cutting and transporting roof beams from distant mountains, laying walls, and finishing interiors might occupy a community for years, and churches required continuing maintenance, since adobe walls and flat roofs needed regular repair. Friars directed the work, but Pueblo builders contributed expertise in adobe and masonry, and some communities took pride in the buildings. The construction timeline was also uneven. The first mission buildings at a site might be modest, and large churches came later; Las Humanas, for example, began its large church only in the 1660s. At Abó and Quarai, successive building phases show how mission ambitions grew, and the large churches visible today represent the end of long and costly processes that were disrupted by famine and abandonment. The ruins thus record a history of labor and its interruption.

Mission construction also altered the relationship between Pueblo towns and the surrounding landscape. Timber harvesting affected nearby forests, and the new large complexes changed the layout of towns. In some cases, the church stood at the edge of an existing plaza or settlement; in others, the position of the mission within a town suggests deliberate efforts to reshape sacred geography. Pueblo communities had long used architecture to express social and cosmological order, and the placement of a church within or beside a town was an assertion of a rival order, with which Pueblo people had to reckon in their daily lives.

Division III · Section 43.13

Catholic Conversion and the Persistence of Pueblo Religious Life

Franciscan practice centered on baptism, religious instruction, regular attendance at Mass, confession, marriage in the church, and the observation of the Catholic calendar. Friars taught children and adults prayers and doctrine, often in the Pueblo languages with the help of interpreters and, in some cases, with grammars and catechisms prepared in those languages. Church officials, often Pueblo men and boys, assisted as sacristans, singers, and teachers. Pueblo people took part in Catholic processions and feasts, which could be elaborate and which the friars supported.

At the same time, Pueblo ceremonial life continued. The evidence for this continuity includes Spanish complaints, trial records that describe Pueblo dances, ceremonies, and societies, and the archaeological evidence of kivas and ceremonial deposits. In many communities, people appear to have participated in Catholic practices while maintaining the ceremonies that they considered essential for rain, crops, health, and community well-being. The colonial documents describe this as idolatry, relapse, or hypocrisy; Pueblo people may have understood it as maintaining relationships and obligations that Catholicism did not replace and that they had no wish to abandon.

Friars also tried to reshape family and community life through the sacraments. Christian marriage, which required monogamy and church sanction, was promoted in place of Pueblo marriage practices, and unions not blessed by the church were condemned. Baptism of infants and of the dying was a priority, and friars recorded baptisms as measures of success. Burial practices changed as well: excavations at mission churches, including at Pecos, have recovered numerous burials placed in or near church buildings in Christian fashion, evidence that many Pueblo people accepted Christian burial and that mission cemeteries became part of the community landscape. Yet this evidence has to be read alongside the evidence for continued ceremonial life, and a Christian burial does not tell us about the beliefs of the person or of the mourners. In practice, different individuals within the same community almost certainly held different views of the church, and these views may have changed over a lifetime.

Selective adoption is a more accurate description than wholesale acceptance or rejection. Pueblo people incorporated elements of Christianity that made sense within their own frameworks, such as patron saints and festival days, and some Catholic imagery and practices were absorbed into Pueblo community life. In many Pueblo communities today, patron saint feast days remain important public occasions, a continuity that shows how deeply some Catholic forms became part of Pueblo life, even as Pueblo religious traditions continue alongside them. At the same time, the sincerity of individual Christians in the seventeenth century cannot be assumed or denied from documents alone. Some individuals may have embraced Christianity, others adopted it strategically, others resisted, and many probably held complicated and changing views.

Historical Debate

Conversion, Adaptation, and Religious Coercion in Colonial New Mexico

The question: How should historians characterize Pueblo participation in Catholic life in the seventeenth century? Did Pueblo people convert, outwardly comply under coercion, adapt selectively, or maintain religious traditions in ways that make the very category of "conversion" misleading?

Interpretation A — Conversion as real but partial: Franciscan sources, and some modern historians who read them carefully, emphasize that many Pueblo people were baptized, attended church, and participated in Christian life, and they argue that for some individuals and communities these practices carried genuine religious meaning, particularly over several generations.
Interpretation B — Coerced compliance and superficial adoption: Other historians stress that Christian participation took place under a system of forced labor, punishment, and surveillance, and that outward conformity does not indicate belief. In this view, baptism counts and church attendance reflect the power of the mission system more than Pueblo faith.
Interpretation C — Selective adaptation and coexistence: A third approach argues that Pueblo communities selectively adopted aspects of Catholicism while maintaining their own ceremonial life, sometimes by compartmentalizing the two, and that this strategy allowed continuity under pressure. Some scholars also note that the Spanish category of "religion" separates belief from social and political life in ways that Pueblo communities did not.

Evidence: The interpretations draw on Franciscan reports, trial and Inquisition records, the architecture and archaeology of churches and kivas, and the continued existence of both Catholic and ceremonial practices in Pueblo communities. Pueblo nations' own historical knowledge, much of which is not public, also informs how Pueblo scholars and community members interpret the period.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns interpretation and evidence rather than the existence of coercion, which is widely accepted. Historians differ on how to weigh Spanish documents that claim sincerity, on whether outward compliance can reveal inward belief, and on the proper analytical categories. The evidence is largely silent on the private religious views of ordinary Pueblo people.

Why it matters: How historians answer the question affects how they understand the mission system's impact, the sources of later resistance, and the long-term continuity of Pueblo religion. It also affects whether Pueblo people are seen as victims, as strategists, or as both.

Division III · Section 43.14

The Missionaries, the Governors, and the Struggle for Authority

Conflict between Franciscans and secular officials began early and persisted. The most prominent early episode involved Governor Pedro de Peralta and Fray Isidro Ordóñez, who led the Franciscans in the early 1610s. The two quarreled over jurisdiction, over the authority of church officials to discipline colonists and Pueblo people, and over the use of Pueblo labor. Ordóñez asserted ecclesiastical authority in ways that included claims connected to the Inquisition, and, according to the records, he excommunicated the governor and had him held for a time. Peralta left the province in 1614. The details are documented in Spanish sources that reflect the viewpoints of each side, but the larger pattern is clear: authority was contested between two institutions that each claimed to represent the Crown.

Similar conflicts recurred. Governors in the 1620s and later clashed with the friars over labor, tribute, and the regulation of Pueblo ceremonies. Governors engaged in commercial ventures, including trade in hides, textiles, salt, and captives, and used Pueblo labor and tribute for these enterprises, which the friars criticized as exploitative and as diverting labor from church work. Friars, for their part, used Pueblo labor and food in their own households and projects and were accused by governors and colonists of excessive punishments and of impeding the colony's economy. In the late 1630s, Governor Luis de Rosas operated textile workshops using Pueblo labor and clashed with the friars; he was killed in 1642 amid factional violence in Santa Fe. In 1659–1661, Governor Bernardo López de Mendizábal's disputes with the Franciscans included his willingness to permit Pueblo dances. Accusations of heretical propositions and suspected Judaizing were subsequently pursued by the Inquisition against him and his wife, Teresa de Aguilera y Roche; these proceedings cannot be reduced to disagreement over dances alone. Both were taken to Mexico City, where Mendizábal was imprisoned by the Holy Office in 1663 and died in custody on September 16, 1664. An ecclesiastical decision in 1671 rehabilitated his memory.

The reasons for these conflicts were structural. Both friars and governors drew their legitimacy from the Crown, but their jurisdictions overlapped. Friars claimed authority over Pueblo religious life and over the moral discipline of both Pueblo and Spanish residents, while governors held civil and military authority and were responsible for tribute and defense. Because Pueblo labor and food were the colony's principal resources, each side regarded the other's claims as a threat. Spanish officials also used the language of the Inquisition, excommunication, and royal authority as weapons in these disputes, and the participants wrote extensive accusations that survive in archives. These records are valuable because they contain testimony from many colonists and some Pueblo witnesses, but they were produced in adversarial proceedings, so they must be read with attention to who benefited from each version. In the Mendizábal case, for example, hostile colonists and friars gave testimony that shaped the Inquisition's proceedings, and the surviving record preserves their claims more fully than the governor's defense.

The consequences for Pueblo communities were complex. Conflict between Spanish authorities could weaken the system of control and open room for maneuver, and Pueblo leaders sometimes took advantage of divisions. Mendizábal's relatively permissive attitude toward dances, for instance, was a rare moment of official Spanish tolerance, and it was met with fierce Franciscan opposition. But the conflict also intensified demands, as each Spanish faction sought Pueblo labor and loyalty, and it produced waves of punishment and repression when one side prevailed. Pueblo people were thus caught between competing claimants, with their well-being depending on the shifting balance of power between them.

Multiple Perspectives

Labor, Religion, and Authority in the Mission Province

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns found in the sources. They are not quotations from any single named individual, and the Pueblo perspective in particular is reconstructed from indirect evidence.

From the perspective of Pueblo community leaders: Spanish demands for labor, food, and religious conformity threatened the capacity of the community to feed itself and conduct the ceremonies on which, in Pueblo understanding, its well-being depended. Leaders had to decide whom to satisfy, what to concede, and when to resist, and the division between governors and friars offered both dangers and openings.
From the perspective of Franciscan missionaries: Pueblo souls were at stake and Pueblo ceremonies were demonic idolatry. Governors and settlers, who demanded labor and tribute and who often tolerated Pueblo practices for convenience, were obstacles to conversion. Church building, instruction, and discipline required Pueblo labor and time that secular exploitation threatened to consume.
From the perspective of colonial governors: The governor held the Crown's authority over the province and required labor, tribute, and goods to govern, defend the colony, and, for many, to earn a return on their office. Friars who claimed jurisdiction over Pueblo labor and who punished those they judged guilty of idolatry undermined the governor's authority and the colony's economy.
From the perspective of encomenderos and settlers: Colonists who held tribute rights or sought labor depended on access to Pueblo communities. They resented the friars' claims on labor and, at times, their criticism of settler treatment of Pueblo people, and they often sided with governors in disputes.

Note on power: These groups did not hold equal power. Pueblo communities lacked the institutional and military resources of the Spanish factions, and the conflicts among Spaniards were fought largely over control of Pueblo labor, loyalty, and religious life, with Pueblo people bearing many of the costs.

Division IV — Forced Labor, Colonial Economy, and Everyday Life · Section 43.15

Encomienda and the Extraction of Pueblo Tribute

From the first years of the colony, Spanish officials granted encomiendas, rights to collect tribute from specified Pueblo communities, to colonists who had served the Crown. By the middle of the seventeenth century, a few dozen colonists held such grants, and the encomendero class formed the core of the settler elite. In exchange for tribute, an encomendero was supposed to provide military service and, in theory, protection and the support of religious instruction. Pueblo towns that were assigned to an encomendero owed a regular payment, which was commonly described as a cotton blanket or similar textile and a measure of maize from each household each year, though the specific amounts and forms varied by place and time, and the surviving documentation is imperfect.

The mechanics of tribute varied across the seventeenth century. Annual household obligations are documented, but collection could be divided into two installments, and accounts disagree about whether particular levies were gathered once or twice in a given year. Agricultural payments depended on the harvest, while the schedule for textiles could differ. Pueblo officers assembled the goods, and encomenderos or their agents received them, sometimes sending goods south for sale. Because tribute fell on households, the burden was heaviest on those with the smallest harvests, and in poor years, shortfalls could lead to demands for substitutes or to punishments. Encomenderos were supposed to provide military service and, in theory, to protect the communities assigned to them, but the evidence of effective protection is thin. The grants were also tied to the political economy of the colony, as an encomienda was a reward, a source of income, and a marker of status among settlers, which gave encomenderos an incentive to defend their rights against governors and friars who competed for the same resources.

Historical Vocabulary
Encomienda

Definition. A Spanish colonial grant giving a colonist, the encomendero, the right to receive tribute from a specified Indigenous community or group of communities, which the Crown regarded as its own vassals.

Historical context. The institution originated in the Caribbean and was adapted in Mexico and elsewhere, and in New Mexico it was initiated under Oñate. The Crown formally distinguished it from slavery and from ownership of land, and it prohibited encomenderos from demanding personal service, though enforcement was inconsistent. In New Mexico the grants were small compared to those of central Mexico, but they were important to the settler economy because tribute in textiles and maize was often one of the few sources of income and subsistence.

Why it matters. Encomienda converted the Pueblo agricultural and textile economy into a source of colonial revenue and made tribute a regular and legally sanctioned burden on every household.

Potential misunderstanding. An encomendero did not own the land or the people of the community, and encomienda was legally distinct from slavery. In practice, however, the distinction was often blurred, and Pueblo people experienced the demands as coercive and sometimes as burdens that exceeded legal limits.

Tribute payments had practical effects on Pueblo households and community resources. Cotton cloth was produced by skilled labor, largely by men in many Pueblo communities, and demands for blankets competed with the production of textiles for community use, ceremonial needs, and exchange. Maize payments drew on the stored surplus that communities depended on to survive drought and seasonal hunger. In years of poor harvest, the tribute burden fell on a thinner margin, and complaints of hardship appear in the documents. The burden also had a political dimension: tribute required Pueblo officers to organize collection, which linked community leaders to the colonial system and placed them in the difficult position of enforcing demands on their own people.

Encomienda's importance should not be exaggerated as the sole form of exploitation. It operated alongside labor drafts, church demands, and the commercial enterprises of governors and officials. Taken together, they created a layered system of obligations, none of which was fully regulated by the Crown's laws. The legal ideals of encomienda, protection and instruction in exchange for tribute, were rarely fulfilled in the way the Crown intended.

Division IV · Section 43.16

Repartimiento, Construction, and Compulsory Labor

Historical Vocabulary
Repartimiento

Definition. A system of compulsory, rotating labor drafts in which colonial officials allotted Indigenous workers to Spanish employers or projects for a limited period, usually in exchange for a small wage.

Historical context. The institution developed in New Spain and the Andes and varied widely by region. In New Mexico the term was used for a variety of labor requisitions, and in practice the line between repartimiento, personal service, and unpaid labor was often blurred. Governors, encomenderos, and friars all drew on Pueblo workers.

Why it matters. Repartimiento took laborers from their own fields, households, and ceremonial duties, and it was the principal mechanism through which churches, conventos, and public buildings were built.

Potential misunderstanding. Repartimiento is sometimes confused with encomienda. Encomienda was a claim to tribute from a community, while repartimiento was a draft of labor. In New Mexico both were applied to the same communities, and Pueblo people experienced them as overlapping demands.

Compulsory labor was perhaps the most pervasive burden imposed on Pueblo communities. Pueblo workers were drafted to build and repair churches and conventos, to construct and maintain government buildings in Santa Fe, to work colonists' fields and estancias, to herd livestock, to haul timber, to serve in friars' households, and to carry freight. Governors used labor in their commercial enterprises, including weaving workshops and the production of goods for export. The labor requirements fell most heavily on communities near Spanish settlements and mission seats, and the incidence of labor drafts varied with the demands of particular governors, friars, and encomenderos.

Labor was demanded through Pueblo officers, who were instructed to supply a certain number of workers for a given period. The allocation of work within the community was therefore handled by Pueblo leaders, which gave them a degree of control over who was sent but also made them responsible for meeting Spanish demands. Different Spanish actors made competing claims. Friars requested workers for church construction, for their kitchens and gardens, and for herding; governors requested labor for public buildings in Santa Fe, for transport, and for their own enterprises; and colonists with estancias sought laborers for fields and herds. Transport was a heavy burden because roads were long and carts and animals limited, so that carrying loads on foot or on the back was common. Spanish law prohibited many forms of personal service and required wages, and some governors sought to limit the friars' use of labor, but enforcement was inconsistent and generally depended on the personal inclinations of the governor. Pueblo communities attempted to protect themselves through petitions, delay, partial compliance, and, when necessary, flight.

Spanish law attempted to regulate compulsory labor by limiting its duration, requiring payment, and forbidding abuses, but the regulations were poorly enforced in a distant province with a small government. Pueblo workers were sometimes paid in small amounts of goods or not at all, and the time they spent away from their homes was a serious cost. The agricultural calendar was unforgiving: labor at planting, irrigation, or harvest time could not be postponed without harming crops, and labor demands that coincided with agricultural peaks directly threatened food security. The same men who were drafted to build a church were needed to prepare fields, repair ditches, and defend their towns.

Timing mattered as much as quantity. The Río Grande towns needed labor in spring to clean and repair irrigation ditches and to plant, and in late summer and autumn to harvest and store the crop, while the building season for churches and public works coincided with the same warm months. The surviving records do not establish how consistently Pueblo officers could negotiate the timing of drafts; demands from friars, governors, and colonists were not always coordinated, and a community might receive overlapping requests during important agricultural work. Where communities could not meet all of them, the consequences included reduced plantings, smaller stored surpluses, and, over time, greater vulnerability to drought. The remedies available were limited: petition, partial compliance, or withdrawal from the town.

Cause and Effect

Forced Labor and Economic Disruption in the Pueblo World

Long-term causes

The Spanish imperial system of tribute and labor extraction, the Crown's financial dependence on colonial enterprises that paid for themselves, and the religious mission to convert Indigenous people through a visible church-building program.

Short-term causes

The small size of the Spanish population, the absence of mines or other independent sources of wealth, the competition among governors, friars, and encomenderos for Pueblo labor, and the weakness of royal oversight.

Immediate context

The expansion of mission building in the 1620s and 1630s, the commercial ventures of governors, and the settler economy's dependence on Pueblo work.

Immediate effects

Reduced time for farming and community tasks, strain on stored food, disruption of household and ceremonial calendars, and growing resentment toward both friars and secular officials.

Long-term consequences

Heightened vulnerability to drought and famine in the 1660s and 1670s, erosion of the legitimacy of colonial institutions, and the accumulation of grievances that later shaped Pueblo political action.

Historical complexity

Labor demands did not cause famine or resistance by themselves. They interacted with drought, disease, raiding, and religious repression, and Pueblo communities varied in how heavily they were burdened and how they responded.

Why one cause is insufficient: Labor extraction made communities more vulnerable to environmental shocks, but the shocks themselves were independent of Spanish policy. The combination, not any single factor, produced the crisis examined in Division VI.

Division IV · Section 43.17

Livestock, Agriculture, Land, and Environmental Change

The Spanish brought sheep, goats, cattle, horses, mules, pigs, and chickens to New Mexico, along with wheat, barley, fruit trees, and other crops and with tools such as iron hoes, axes, and plows. Pueblo communities adopted some of these in varying degrees. Wheat and orchard fruit joined maize in some Pueblo fields, and sheep eventually provided wool that was woven into textiles. Iron tools were valued. The new crops and animals altered the agricultural economy and diet, though they were not adopted uniformly or at the same speed.

Livestock also created conflict. Spanish herds and flocks grazed on lands that Pueblo communities used for farming, hunting, and gathering, and unfenced animals damaged crops. Colonists established stock-raising estates, estancias, in the Río Grande valley and elsewhere, often on or near lands that Pueblo communities claimed or used, and competed for water and pasture. Disputes over land and water appear in the documents. The effects were not uniform. Pueblo towns near Spanish estancias experienced greater pressure than distant communities, and livestock could be a resource as well as a burden for Pueblo people who obtained and herded animals. Historians caution against assuming that every introduced technology or animal had the same effect across the Pueblo world.

The effects of livestock varied. Some Pueblo households acquired sheep, goats, or cattle, and wool became a resource for weaving, but the benefits were uneven. Communities with ample irrigated land and access to pasture could incorporate animals more easily than those with limited fields, and towns closest to Spanish estancias had the most disputes over crop damage and water. Spanish accounts of the period and later litigation indicate that colonists sometimes occupied lands that Pueblo communities considered their own, and that Pueblo officials complained about trespass. Irrigation also became an arena of cooperation and conflict, since Spanish settlers used Pueblo-style ditch systems and shared water sources with Pueblo farmers. In the arid Southwest, a few additional animals or a diverted stream could make a significant difference in a dry year, which is one reason that environmental pressures of the 1660s and 1670s were so serious.

Environmental change also reflected ordinary climatic variability, in which droughts and wetter periods alternated. Tree-ring evidence shows that the seventeenth century included periods of severe drought, especially in the 1660s and 1670s, discussed in Section 43.24. The Spanish presence interacted with this variability by increasing demands on stored food and on labor, by adding grazing pressure, and by drawing timber from nearby mountains. These human effects were real, but they should not be treated as the sole determinant of environmental conditions. Pueblo agriculture had long adapted to a variable climate, and Spanish demands reduced the margin of safety that adaptation relied upon.

Division IV · Section 43.18

Pueblo Households, Women, Children, and Social Continuity

The household was the basic unit of Pueblo economic life. Households produced and stored food, prepared meals, built and maintained dwellings, raised children, and fulfilled obligations to clans, ceremonial societies, and the community. Women's work was central: women ground maize, prepared and cooked food, made pottery, and in many communities held rights to houses and to important household resources, while men farmed, hunted, wove cloth, and performed much of the ceremonial and political leadership. These roles varied among communities, and the evidence for them in the seventeenth century is partial, drawn from Spanish records, archaeology, and later ethnographic and community knowledge.

Spanish colonial demands intersected with these household economies in several ways. Tribute and labor drafts removed men from their fields and households, increasing the burden on women and on the elderly and children who remained. Tribute demands for maize and cloth drew on household production and storage. Spanish friars sought to reshape family life through Christian marriage, restrictions on practices they regarded as sinful, and the instruction of children, who were taught doctrine and, in some places, music, reading, and craft skills. Some scholars argue that Franciscan attitudes toward gender, including the assignment of construction work to men in a context where women had traditionally had major roles in building and plastering houses, altered customary divisions of labor, though evidence on this point varies by community.

Kinship systems shaped how households absorbed colonial pressure. In matrilineal communities such as the Hopi, Zuni, and Keresan towns, clan ties through women structured access to land and houses and provided networks of mutual support, while other Pueblo societies relied on different kinship arrangements. Colonial institutions introduced new relationships alongside these, including godparenthood and the sponsorship of baptisms and marriages, and Pueblo people sometimes used such ties to build connections with Spanish individuals. Marriages between Pueblo people and members of the colonial population occurred, and mixed households and communities of servants and laborers also formed around Spanish settlements. The evidence on these developments is incomplete, and it comes largely from church records and legal documents, but it suggests a social world more varied than a simple division between Pueblo towns and Spanish settlements would indicate.

Women also appear in the documents as servants in Spanish households, as captives, and as targets of sexual violence by soldiers and officials, which friars and some officials sometimes condemned. Indigenous children were taken into Spanish households as servants, and the Acoma sentences of 1599 show that the removal of children from families was a feature of early colonial practice. At the same time, women remained keepers of household and community continuity, transmitting language, food practices, and ceremonial knowledge. Archaeological studies of colonial-period households suggest that Pueblo domestic life retained strong continuities in cooking, pottery, and architecture, with selective additions of Spanish goods and foods. These patterns of continuity offer an important counterweight to accounts that emphasize only transformation.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Seventeenth-Century Pueblo Household

Historians cannot reconstruct a particular household's daily life without inventing detail, but they can describe the kinds of conditions that shaped households across the Pueblo world, using documents, archaeology, and the knowledge of descendant communities. A household in a Río Grande town in the 1630s typically included several generations living in connected rooms of an adobe or masonry dwelling, with storage rooms holding maize, beans, squash, and other food, and with space for grinding, cooking, and craft work. Food came from irrigated fields, from gathering and hunting, and, increasingly, from small plots of wheat, fruit, and, in some households, a few sheep or goats.

The demands of the colonial system pressed on this household in several ways. Adult men might be absent for weeks on labor drafts. The household owed tribute in maize and cloth, and the yearly harvest had to be divided among tribute, church contributions, ceremonial obligations, and the household's own needs. Children might attend instruction at the mission or serve in the friar's household. Women continued to produce food and pottery, and in many households pottery shows the continued use of local forms alongside some Spanish-influenced ones. In years of drought, the household's stores, drawn down by tribute, were the line between survival and hunger.

This picture is assembled from patterns and not from the record of any one household, and its details differed by community and over time. The point for students is that colonial history happened inside households, where decisions about food, labor, children, and ceremony had to be made under pressure.

Division IV · Section 43.19

Commerce, Captivity, and Relations with Neighboring Indigenous Peoples

Colonization did not isolate the Pueblos from their neighbors; it changed the terms of their relationships. Apache bands and other Plains peoples continued to trade at Pecos and Taos, and Spanish governors and officials sought to enter and control this trade. Hides, bison products, and captives moved through these exchanges. Spanish colonists wanted hides and also Indigenous captives, whom they used as domestic servants and, in some cases, sold in the mining districts of Nueva Vizcaya. Governors' commercial ventures included the export of such goods down the Camino Real. Chapter 53 examines Indigenous slavery and captivity across the continent; for New Mexico it is enough to note that captive-taking, raiding, and exchange were intertwined and that Pueblo, Apache, Diné, and Spanish people all participated in various ways.

The geography of exchange shaped colonial politics. Pecos and Taos remained points of contact between Pueblo and Plains peoples, and governors and officials who wished to profit from hides and captives needed access to those markets. Spanish soldiers and traders joined the trade fairs, and Spanish demands for hides increased the pressure on Plains hunters and on Pueblo intermediaries. To the west, the Diné, whom Spanish writers of the 1620s called the Apaches de Navajó, were neighbors of the western Pueblos and the people of Acoma and Zuni; they maintained trade and sometimes conflict with those communities, and Spanish observers noted their farming as well as their raiding. Pueblo and Spanish forces sometimes campaigned together against Apache groups, and Apache and Diné groups sometimes raided Pueblo and Spanish settlements. These shifting alliances show that Pueblo people were neither passive victims of nomadic raiders nor loyal Spanish allies, but political actors whose relations with neighbors responded to their own interests and to colonial pressure.

Relations between Pueblo people and neighboring Apache and Diné communities were varied and changed over time. Trade and intermarriage connected some communities, while conflicts over resources, raiding, and captive-taking divided others. Pueblo men also served as auxiliaries in Spanish campaigns against Apache groups, and Apache groups raided Pueblo and Spanish settlements, particularly as drought and Spanish captive-taking intensified pressures in the 1660s and 1670s. It would be a mistake to portray Apache and Diné peoples simply as threats to the Pueblo world. They were trading partners, sometimes allies, sometimes enemies, and political actors with their own interests, including defense against Spanish and Pueblo enslavers. As Chapter 51 explains, Apache societies were themselves undergoing changes shaped by horses, trade, and Spanish pressure.

Spanish settlement thus altered regional political relations. Spanish demands for captives and hides encouraged raiding, Spanish alliances shifted the balance among Indigenous groups, and Pueblo dependence on Spanish protection against raiders was weakened when the colonial military proved unable to provide it. These shifts would become acute in the Salinas region and the western Pueblos in the 1670s, when raiding and famine combined to make many communities untenable.

Division V — Indigenous Political Agency, Accommodation, and Resistance · Section 43.20

Pueblo Leadership and the Negotiation of Colonial Power

Pueblo leaders did not simply submit to or resist Spanish authority. They negotiated, using a range of strategies. They supplied labor and goods in amounts intended to satisfy demands without exhausting community resources. They used the Spanish municipal offices to represent community interests. They appealed to governors against friars and to friars against governors or encomenderos, taking advantage of Spanish disunity. They withdrew from towns, as many Taos people did after the uprising of 1639, when they withdrew to the Plains, and they used distance and mobility as resources. In some cases, Pueblo leaders brought complaints to Spanish authorities, and Governor Mendizábal, in his dispute with the friars around 1660, is reported to have encouraged Pueblo people to bring complaints about Franciscan conduct.

Pueblo officers had several practical instruments. Town governors and other officers could submit petitions, appear before the governor or an alcalde mayor, or send delegations to Santa Fe. They could ask for reduced demands in a bad year, argue that a friar or encomendero had exceeded his rights, or request the return of captives and the protection of fields. Spanish governors who were at odds with the friars were often willing to hear such complaints, though their motives were political. A Pueblo officer's position was precarious: he might be blamed by his community for conceding too much and by the Spanish for conceding too little. Surviving documents give only fragments of these exchanges, but they show that Pueblo leaders understood the Spanish legal system well enough to use it when it served them.

Pueblo leaders also managed the relationship between the colonial and the ceremonial spheres. Officers appointed under the colonial system might also hold positions within ceremonial societies, and communities had to decide which aspects of ceremonial life to protect from Spanish view. The maintenance of secrecy around ceremonies, which later became a defining feature of Pueblo religious life, probably deepened under the pressure of Spanish repression. Leaders made difficult choices among accommodating the friars to protect the community, withholding information about ceremonies, and, in some cases, participating in resistance.

Differences among communities mattered. Towns close to Santa Fe and the main roads dealt with Spanish officials constantly and had less room to evade demands, while towns at a distance, such as those in the west, could sometimes avoid contact for long periods. Some communities had leaders who cooperated closely with friars, while others had leaders who resisted openly. Within communities, factions formed around different responses, and there is evidence of tension between officers who held colonial titles and those who held ceremonial authority. Historians should therefore avoid describing a single Pueblo political response. Pueblo leadership was plural, and the choices made in 1598, in 1632, in 1650, and in 1675 reflected local circumstances and the particular interests of individuals and groups.

These strategies show that Pueblo political agency operated within narrow constraints. Leaders had to balance risks: refusal could bring punishment, but compliance could undermine their authority with their own people. Communities differed in how well their leaders managed this balance, and leaders were not always unified. The evidence also shows that some Pueblo individuals collaborated with Spanish authorities for personal benefit or conviction, and that Pueblo communities experienced internal conflicts over how to respond to Spanish rule.

Division V · Section 43.21

Adaptation, Cultural Persistence, and the Limits of Conversion

Pueblo communities preserved ceremonial traditions and community institutions under colonial pressure through a variety of strategies. One was selective participation: communities took part in Catholic feasts and observances while continuing their own ceremonies. Another was reorganization: some ceremonies were conducted in more private settings, in smaller groups, or at different times, and some practices may have been adapted to avoid detection. A third was the incorporation of elements of Spanish life, such as saints' days, imagery, and the municipal offices, into Pueblo frameworks, so that new forms served existing community purposes.

Adaptation also left traces in everyday life and language. Pueblo people took Christian names, held church offices, sang in church choirs, and participated in processions on saints' days, while Spanish words for new animals, tools, and foods entered Pueblo languages. Communities appear to have fitted some Christian feast days into their existing calendars, a pattern that allowed public celebrations to continue under an acceptable guise. At the same time, adopting a Spanish word or a Christian name was not the same as accepting the worldview that came with it. Pueblo people selected what was useful or unavoidable, and the surviving evidence suggests that they also decided what to protect from outside view.

Responses varied regionally. In the Río Grande towns, where friars and colonists were more numerous and the government's reach was greater, participation in Catholic festivals and offices was more visible, and ceremonial life was more likely to be concealed. In the west, where friars were fewer and more distant, communities had more room to maintain public ceremonies, and violence against friars occurred more often, though the Spanish response also came more slowly. These differences cannot be reduced to simple formulas, since local leaders, personalities, and events mattered, but they illustrate why a single description of Pueblo religious adaptation would be misleading.

The limits of conversion are visible in the persistence of Pueblo ceremonial leadership, of kiva-based societies, and of the agricultural and seasonal rhythms that structured community life. Spanish friars repeatedly complained that baptized Pueblo people continued to take part in ceremonies they regarded as idolatrous, and trial records of the 1660s and 1670s describe a network of ceremonial leaders who retained significant authority. That authority was both spiritual and political, and it made Pueblo religious leaders potential centers of resistance, a fact that Spanish officials understood and that contributed to the escalating repression discussed in the next section.

At the same time, adaptation involved real change. Population loss, the abandonment of towns, the movement of people between communities, and the pressures of labor and tribute altered the demography and social organization of the Pueblo world. Some ceremonies may have been lost or modified, and the transmission of knowledge was disrupted by death and displacement. Persistence should therefore not be read as the absence of change. Pueblo traditions continued because people worked, often at great cost, to maintain them, and because they were adaptable enough to survive in altered form.

Division V · Section 43.22

Religious Repression and the Criminalization of Pueblo Traditions

Franciscan friars regarded Pueblo ceremonies as idolatry, and they frequently acted against them. Spanish records describe friars and officials entering kivas, seizing and destroying masks, prayer offerings, and other ceremonial objects, and punishing individuals identified as religious leaders. Punishments could include whipping, imprisonment, forced labor, and, in the most severe cases, execution. The jurisdictional distinction matters: a royal decree of 1571 formally excluded Indigenous people from prosecution by the Mexico City tribunal of the Holy Office. In remote New Mexico, however, Franciscan leaders exercised overlapping missionary, delegated ecclesiastical, and commissarial authority, and Indigenous ceremonial practices could still be punished as alleged sorcery or idolatry by local church and secular authorities. Such punishment should not automatically be described as a formal Inquisition trial of Pueblo defendants.

Spanish accounts of repression are specific enough to show what it looked like in practice. They describe the confiscation of masks and other ceremonial items, the burning or destruction of such objects, searches of kivas, and public punishments of people accused of leading ceremonies. Friars attempted to expose ritual leaders as impostors or sorcerers and sometimes used humiliation as a punishment. These acts were intended to demonstrate the power of the church and to deter participation, but they also exposed the friars' dependence on informants, since Pueblo people kept ceremonial knowledge secret. Because the surviving descriptions are written by the people who carried out the repression, they say little about the meaning the targeted objects and rites had for Pueblo communities and nothing, in most cases, about how people experienced the loss of sacred objects.

Repression was episodic and varied with the attitudes of governors and friars. Some governors tolerated Pueblo ceremonies, and Mendizábal's permissiveness in 1659–1661 illustrates both the existence of that tolerance and the fierce opposition it provoked. Other officials, and the friars who held sway in the 1660s and 1670s, pursued more aggressive suppression. The cumulative effect was the criminalization of practices central to Pueblo community life. Because Pueblo religion was integrated with agriculture, curing, and social organization, attacks on ceremonies were experienced as attacks on the community's survival, particularly in years of drought, when the Pueblo understanding of ceremony's role in bringing rain made suppression seem especially dangerous.

Repression also pushed Pueblo religious practice toward greater secrecy and may have strengthened the standing of leaders who protected it. It drove a wedge between communities and the Spanish who claimed to protect them. Evidence for this process comes mainly from the Spanish side, from friars' complaints and prosecutions, and its Pueblo dimensions must be inferred from patterns of behavior and from the continuity of ceremonial life. The escalation of repression in the 1670s, explored in Section 43.26, was a product of Spanish anxiety about the persistence of Pueblo religion and the weakness of their own control.

Division V · Section 43.23

Resistance before 1680: Local Uprisings and Colonial Retaliation

Pueblo resistance took many forms before 1680, from everyday evasion and flight to armed uprisings, and the sources preserve only part of it. Several episodes can be reconstructed, though in each case the evidence comes mainly from Spanish accounts and must be read with care. At Jemez, Spanish sources report an uprising in 1623, followed by Spanish military action in which a number of Jemez people were killed. The documents are limited, and they come from a Spanish perspective that interpreted the event as rebellion against the Crown and the church.

Many forms of resistance are almost invisible in the record. Communities delayed labor, delivered less than was demanded, hid corn, sent workers who were too few or too young, claimed illness, or withdrew to distant fields and mountains, and Spanish complaints about such behavior suggest that they were common. Such everyday actions did not require organization across towns, and they allowed communities to reduce burdens without risking the severe retaliation that open rebellion invited. Historians infer these practices from Spanish complaints and from the patterns of demand and response, and they cannot be dated or counted, but they form the background against which the better-documented uprisings should be understood.

In 1632, at Zuni, the Franciscan Francisco Letrado was killed at Hawikuh, and attacks on missionaries disrupted the early Zuni mission establishments. The sources document the interruption of missionary residence but do not establish a mass resettlement at Dowa Yalanne in 1632. In 1633, a friar at Awatovi among the Hopi, Francisco de Porras, was reported to have been poisoned, according to Spanish accounts. At Taos, in 1639–1640, Spanish sources record the killing of a friar and violence against Spaniards, followed by the flight of many Taos people to the Plains and a Spanish response. In each case Spanish records identify the targeted friars and the immediate aftermath but leave the Pueblo reasons only partly visible, though the context of labor demands, religious suppression, and abuse is evident in the documents.

Spanish responses to these episodes were typically punitive, but their effectiveness was limited. The destruction and disruption of the early Zuni missions show that colonial religious institutions could be rejected locally, while the Taos withdrawal from its pueblo shows how mobility and connections with neighboring peoples could provide refuge. In each case the Spanish authorities eventually reestablished some form of control, but the missions in the western pueblos were never again as secure as they had been, and the friars' numbers there remained small. The episodes also deepened suspicion between Spanish officials and Pueblo leaders. Each side drew its own lessons: Spanish officials concluded that harsh punishment was necessary, while Pueblo communities learned that Spanish authority could be challenged locally, and that distance and mobility offered protection.

A broader plot in 1650, during the governorship of Hernando de Ugarte y la Concha, reportedly involved people from Isleta, Alameda, San Felipe, Cochiti, and Jemez, together with Apache allies. Spanish investigators said the plan involved transferring horses to Apache allies for an attack; colonial authorities uncovered it before it could be carried out, and reports record the execution of nine implicated Pueblo men. These community names and details derive from later testimony and colonial investigations rather than a complete record of the participants’ own deliberations. Additional plans and conspiracies were reported in subsequent decades. The 1650 conspiracy demonstrates coordination across Pueblo and neighboring Indigenous communities, but the surviving evidence is fragmentary and adversarial.

Why did these resistance efforts remain local or limited? Several factors are apparent. Pueblo communities were politically autonomous, separated by language and local interests, and the idea of a coordinated, multi-community uprising required overcoming those divisions. Spanish retaliation was swift and violent, and it made the risks of resistance clear. Colonial authorities and friars maintained some Pueblo allies and informants, which made secret planning dangerous. And many Pueblo leaders probably calculated that accommodation, however costly, was preferable to the risks of war. By the 1670s, those calculations were changing, as Section 43.24 explains. It would be inaccurate to treat the earlier uprisings as steps toward 1680. They were distinct episodes, with their own causes, and their relationship to the later revolt is a question for Chapter 44.

Division VI — Crisis, Displacement, and the Limits of Spanish Rule · Section 43.24

Drought, Disease, Hunger, and Regional Insecurity

In the 1660s and 1670s the Pueblo world and the Spanish colony faced a cluster of mutually reinforcing crises. Tree-ring records from the Southwest indicate a period of severe and prolonged drought in the later 1660s and early 1670s, and Spanish documents describe failed harvests and widespread hunger in those years. Because Pueblo communities depended on stored maize to survive dry years, and because tribute, church contributions, and colonists' demands had already drawn on those stores, the margin for survival was thin. Hunger weakened people, drove some to abandon towns, and made demands for tribute still more burdensome.

Epidemic disease compounded the crisis. Spanish documents refer to epidemics at several points in the seventeenth century, with heavy mortality reported at certain times, though the diseases are seldom identified with confidence, and the figures are imprecise. Population decline over the century was substantial. Estimates for the Pueblo population fall from the wide range suggested for the late sixteenth century to a figure often cited as roughly 17,000 by about 1680, and the number of occupied Pueblo towns dropped markedly. The causes of this decline included introduced disease, famine, violence, and displacement, and their relative weight differed by region and period. Chapter 40 discusses the broader history of epidemic disease in North America and the cautions needed in explaining demographic collapse.

Colonial demands did not stop during the crisis. Spanish officials and friars continued to expect tribute and labor, and encomenderos sought their grants even as harvests failed. Documents of the period refer to hunger among Spanish settlers as well, and to the deaths of livestock in drought years, so the crisis was not confined to Pueblo communities. But the Pueblo towns bore the heaviest burden, because the colony's resources were drawn from their stores and because they had fewer alternatives. Where friars or officials took corn that households needed to survive, the demand could mean the difference between life and death, and Spanish records preserve reports of Pueblo people eating hides and other scarce resources during the worst years. The interaction of drought, tribute, labor, and raiding thus produced a downward spiral, in which each pressure made the others more severe.

Regional insecurity grew as raiding intensified. Apache groups, themselves under pressure from drought, from Spanish captive-taking, and from other changes, raided Pueblo and Spanish communities, and the weakened colonial military was unable to provide reliable protection. Western Pueblo missions were particularly exposed; Hawikuh was attacked in 1672, a friar was killed, and the mission there was abandoned. Spanish officials and Pueblo communities alike were in a position of growing vulnerability.

It would be wrong to attribute these crises to a single universal cause or to treat them as a prelude to an inevitable revolt. The crises had environmental, epidemiological, economic, and political dimensions, and they affected communities unevenly. Some towns, especially those with reliable water and distance from raiders, fared better than others. The deterioration did, however, weaken the colonial order's foundations, because it undermined both the Pueblo capacity to meet demands and the Spanish capacity to enforce them.

Division VI · Section 43.25

The Abandonment of the Salinas Pueblo Missions

The Salinas basin, east of the Manzano Mountains and the Río Grande, was home to the Pueblo communities of Abó, Quarai, and Las Humanas. Abó and the Pueblo de las Humanas, which lies at the site now known as Gran Quivira, were Tompiro-speaking towns, and Quarai was a Tiwa-speaking town. The region's inhabitants had long participated in trade with the Plains and with other Pueblos, and the basin's salt lakes were an economic resource. Franciscans established missions in the 1620s, and the communities built impressive churches. At Humanas, a large church was begun in the 1660s, during the very years that drought and hunger began to strain the region.

By the early 1670s the situation was severe. Spanish sources and archaeological evidence indicate that drought-induced famine hit the basin hard and that Apache raids added violence and danger. Reports speak of starvation, and bioarchaeological studies at Gran Quivira have documented evidence of nutritional stress. Spanish officials and friars described the pueblos as struggling to survive, while the colony itself could not provide food or effective protection. The pueblos' stored resources were depleted, and the possibility of replenishing them through trade was reduced by insecurity.

The Salinas pueblos were particularly exposed because they lay at the margin of the colony, relied on marginal agricultural conditions, and had long been linked to Plains trade. As drought reduced harvests, trade that might have supplied food was less reliable, and Apache raiding intensified at the same time. Spanish military support was distant, and the friars could not provide food in sufficient quantity. The famine and the raids affected communities differently: the Tompiro towns of Abó and Las Humanas were hit earlier and harder, while Quarai, nearer to the mountains, held out longer. This regional variation helps explain why abandonment took place in stages and not in a single evacuation.

In these conditions, communities made decisions about survival. Las Humanas was abandoned by 1672 after severe drought, famine, and attacks. Abó was abandoned in 1673, while Quarai remained inhabited longer and is generally dated as abandoned in 1678 by the National Park Service. Some records distinguish the departure of missionaries from the departure of the remaining inhabitants, so these dates should be treated as approximate local chronologies rather than a single district-wide evacuation. Survivors moved to other communities, particularly the Río Grande pueblos to the west, and some of them, in the events of 1680, would go south with Spanish refugees and settle near present-day El Paso, where communities such as Ysleta del Sur and Socorro del Sur trace their histories. The Salinas people were not simply victims of circumstances. They chose to leave, to relocate, and to join other communities, and these decisions reflect the strategic reasoning of people facing starvation and violence. The abandonment was nonetheless a profound loss, ending centuries of occupation in the basin.

Division VI · Section 43.26

Colonial Authority at the Breaking Point, 1675–1679

In 1675, Governor Juan Francisco Treviño, acting on charges that Pueblo religious leaders were practicing sorcery and had harmed Spanish friars and others, ordered the arrest of a large number of Pueblo men. Spanish sources report that about forty-seven were seized and brought to Santa Fe. According to the detailed account discussed by historian John L. Kessell, three were hanged, a fourth died by suicide, and other prisoners were sentenced to whipping, imprisonment, or servitude. Some later summaries instead describe four executions; the distinction between sentences, deaths by hanging, and the death in custody should therefore remain visible. Among those detained was Po'pay, a religious leader from Ohkay Owingeh (San Juan Pueblo).

The charges in 1675 emerged from a climate of anxiety. Spanish friars and officials believed that Pueblo religious leaders were using witchcraft against Spaniards and against Pueblo Christians, and a series of illnesses and deaths was attributed to such sorcery. These interpretations reflected both Spanish ideas about witchcraft and the genuine hostility between the friars and Pueblo ceremonial leaders. From a Pueblo point of view, the arrested men were religious specialists, whose duties included curing and ensuring rain in a period of drought and illness, and their arrest and punishment threatened the community's capacity to survive. The accusations thus involved fundamentally different understandings of illness, power, and responsibility, and the Spanish record gives only the prosecutors' side of the case.

The proceedings produced a confrontation. According to Spanish accounts, a large body of Pueblo men, including Tewa people from several towns, went to Santa Fe, armed, and demanded the release of the prisoners. The governor, recognizing his weakness, released them. The episode exposed both the Spanish dependence on Pueblo acquiescence and the willingness of Pueblo communities to act collectively in defense of their religious leaders. It also demonstrated that religious persecution, intended to strengthen colonial control, had instead revealed its limits. The details survive mainly in Spanish sources, and the interpretation of Pueblo motives must be inferred.

Treviño was succeeded in 1677 by Governor Antonio de Otermín, who inherited a colony in difficult condition: depleted by drought and hunger, strained by raiding, divided among its own factions, and facing Pueblo communities with sharply heightened grievances. The 1675 episode did not end Spanish efforts to suppress Pueblo ceremonies, nor did it resolve the conflicts between friars, governors, and settlers. Po'pay's later role, the organization of the uprising of 1680, and its consequences are the subject of Chapter 44. This chapter closes by recognizing that the late 1670s were a period in which the structural weaknesses of Spanish rule were visible, and in which the outcomes were not predetermined. Historical actors on both sides made choices under uncertainty, and the sources do not permit the conclusion that a revolt was inevitable.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Pueblo Institutions under Spanish Occupation, 1598–1679

What Changed

  • Pueblo population declined substantially, and the number of occupied towns shrank, with the Salinas basin emptied by the mid-1670s
  • Tribute, labor drafts, and the commercial demands of officials added new layers of obligation to household and community economies
  • Churches and conventos reshaped town landscapes, and Catholic offices, festivals, and instruction became part of community life
  • Spanish municipal offices were introduced, adding a colonial dimension to Pueblo political leadership
  • Livestock, wheat, orchard fruits, and iron tools entered Pueblo economies, and regional trade and raiding relationships shifted

What Stayed the Same

  • Pueblo towns remained autonomous communities with their own leadership, ceremonial societies, and kinship systems
  • Agriculture, water management, and the seasonal calendar continued to organize community life
  • Ceremonial life persisted, in many cases with increased secrecy, alongside participation in Catholic observances
  • Pueblo languages continued to be spoken, and domestic practices such as cooking and pottery retained strong continuity
  • Spanish authority remained dependent on Pueblo labor, food, and cooperation throughout the period

When and how change occurred: Change was uneven and gradual in most areas, with sharper breaks during the Acoma war, in the periods of intensive mission building, in the famine of the late 1660s and 1670s, and in the abandonment of the Salinas towns. Communities near Spanish centers experienced the most continuous pressure, while some distant communities experienced greater continuity. Why the pattern matters: It shows that the colonial order changed Pueblo life profoundly without eliminating Pueblo political and cultural agency, and that the institutions Spanish officials sought to govern remained the ground on which colonial rule depended.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the establishment, operation, and limits of Spanish colonial rule among the Pueblo peoples of New Mexico between 1598 and the late 1670s. Its central argument is that colonization was achieved through coercion and negotiation together, that it transformed Pueblo life, and that it never eliminated Pueblo political and cultural agency. Spanish institutions depended on the communities they sought to govern for food, labor, and cooperation, and that dependence was the colony's defining structural feature.

The Pueblo world that Spanish colonists encountered was diverse. Communities spoke languages from several families, governed themselves through overlapping ceremonial, kinship, and political institutions, and farmed and traded across a wide region. The Spanish term "Pueblo" concealed that diversity. Spanish colonization met varied responses because communities differed in their histories, locations, and strategies.

Juan de Oñate's expedition of 1598 initiated permanent colonization. Spanish ceremonies of possession and vassalage asserted sovereignty but did not constitute Indigenous consent. The war at Acoma in 1598–1599, with its assault and judicial sentences, demonstrated the willingness of Spanish officials to use extreme violence and left a lasting memory. Oñate's colony proved unstable, and the Crown retained it primarily on religious grounds. The founding of Santa Fe around 1609–1610 created an administrative center for a small colonial government whose power was limited.

The Franciscan mission system became the main institution of cultural and religious change. Friars established missions across Pueblo regions, supervised the construction of large churches with Pueblo labor, and sought to replace Pueblo religious practices with Catholicism. Conversion was uneven and contested. Historians debate how far Pueblo participation reflected belief, coercion, or selective adaptation, and the evidence supports the view that Pueblo communities incorporated some Catholic forms while maintaining ceremonial life.

Spanish economic demands, including encomienda tribute and repartimiento labor, altered household and community economies. Livestock, new crops, and the commercial ventures of officials added further changes. These burdens fell unevenly, and they interacted with environmental pressures. Women, children, and households bore the consequences in ways that must be reconstructed from limited evidence, and strong continuities in domestic life persisted.

Conflict among Spanish factions weakened colonial authority. Franciscans and governors repeatedly clashed over labor, jurisdiction, and Pueblo religion, and episodes such as the Peralta–Ordóñez dispute and the arrest of Governor Mendizábal showed the depth of the divisions. Pueblo leaders negotiated within this environment, using Spanish municipal offices, exploiting divisions, withdrawing when necessary, and protecting ceremonial life, though always under severe constraints.

Resistance before 1680 took many forms. Local uprisings at Jemez, Zuni, Hopi, and Taos, and a conspiracy in 1650, were met by Spanish retaliation, and they did not become a sustained regional coalition. The reasons included Pueblo political autonomy, linguistic and local differences, Spanish military and informant networks, and the calculations of leaders about risk. These episodes should be understood on their own terms and not as inevitable steps toward 1680.

In the 1660s and 1670s, drought, famine, disease, raiding, and intensified religious repression created a cumulative crisis. The Salinas missions were abandoned as communities made survival decisions, and Spanish authority proved unable to protect or sustain them. The arrest of Pueblo religious leaders in 1675 exposed the colonial order's weakness when Pueblo men confronted the governor and secured the release of prisoners. The late 1670s were a period of instability, but the sources do not show that a revolt was predetermined.

The historical sources for this period are mainly Spanish administrative, missionary, and legal records, which must be read critically for their purposes and biases. Archaeology, environmental data, and Pueblo historical knowledge supplement them. Responsible interpretation requires attention to what each kind of evidence can and cannot show, and to the Pueblo perspectives that Spanish documents seldom record directly.

The chapter prepares for Chapter 44 by establishing the institutions, grievances, and pressures that preceded the Pueblo Revolt. It also contributes to the textbook's broader argument that Indigenous nations were political communities with agency, that colonization was a contested process, and that Indigenous history continued through and beyond colonial rule.

Key Terms

Pueblo
A Spanish word for "town," used as the general colonial name for the town-dwelling Indigenous communities of New Mexico and Arizona, who spoke several unrelated languages and governed themselves independently.
Kiva
A Hopi word, widely used in English for the ceremonial chambers of Pueblo communities; forms and functions varied by community, and Spanish records often call them estufas.
Cacique
A term borrowed from the Caribbean and applied by Spanish writers to Pueblo leaders, often religious and political figures whose roles differed from town to town.
Encomienda
A Spanish grant of the right to collect tribute from an Indigenous community, assigned to a colonist who owed military service in return.
Encomendero
The holder of an encomienda grant.
Repartimiento
A system of compulsory, rotating labor drafts allotting Indigenous workers to Spanish projects or employers for limited periods.
Tribute
Regular payments in goods, in New Mexico commonly maize and cotton textiles, owed by Indigenous households to the Crown or to an encomendero.
Entrada
A Spanish military and exploratory expedition into a region not yet under colonial control.
Act of possession
A formal legal ceremony in which a Spanish leader claimed territory and its inhabitants for the Crown, recorded by notaries.
Pacification
The term adopted in the Ordinances of 1573 to replace "conquest," describing peaceful settlement and conversion, often at odds with actual practice.

Custos
The head of a Franciscan custody, with authority over the friars in the province.
Convento
The residence and workspace attached to a mission church, including quarters, storerooms, and courtyards.
Visita
A community visited periodically by a friar based at a larger mission seat.
Cabildo
A Spanish municipal council, as in the cabildo of Santa Fe.
Presidio
A Spanish military garrison; New Mexico's was small and often unable to provide protection.
Estancia
A Spanish ranch or farm, often for livestock, established near Pueblo lands.
Fanega
A Spanish measure of grain, used in tribute payments of maize.
Manta
A cotton blanket or textile, a common tribute item.

The blending or coexistence of elements from different religious traditions; historians debate how well it describes Pueblo Catholicism.

The label Spanish friars applied to Pueblo ceremonies, reflecting a Catholic view of them as false worship, not a Pueblo category.

The Catholic tribunal charged with prosecuting heresy; in New Mexico, Franciscan commissaries held its authority, and it was used in disputes among Spaniards.

A formal review of an official's conduct at the end of his term, a source of records about colonial abuses.
Camino Real de Tierra Adentro
The road linking Mexico City, northern New Spain, and New Mexico, used by colonists, caravans, and traders.

The process by which Pueblo people took up some Spanish and Catholic practices while retaining others, shaped by their own purposes.
Ohkay Owingeh
The name of San Juan Pueblo, a Tewa town beside which Oñate established the first Spanish headquarters in 1598.

MGU Library Research Project

Building a Source Dossier on a Colonial Pueblo Community

This project asks you to build a source dossier on a single Pueblo community or mission site during the seventeenth century, using library resources to compare different kinds of evidence.

  1. Choose a community or site. Select a town such as Acoma, Pecos, Taos, Zuni, Abó, Quarai, or Las Humanas.
  2. Collect documentary evidence. Using published translations and scholarly editions, find Spanish records that mention the community, and note the author, date, and purpose of each.
  3. Collect archaeological and environmental evidence. Consult site reports, National Park Service materials where relevant, and tree-ring or climate studies for the period.
  4. Consult Pueblo perspectives. Look for histories published by the community or by Pueblo scholars, respecting restrictions on ceremonial knowledge.
  5. Write a dossier essay. In a 3–5 page essay, compare the evidence, identify conflicts and gaps, and explain what each source contributes, using careful language such as "the sources indicate" and "this remains uncertain."

Library resources to use: Scholarly studies of seventeenth-century New Mexico, including Joseph P. Sánchez's Pueblos, Plains, and Province and Heather B. Trigg's From Household to Empire; published collections of translated colonial documents; park and museum materials; and works by Pueblo authors and community institutions.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows what colonization meant on the ground. A declaration of sovereignty in 1598 did not create a functioning colonial order; that order had to be built, supplied, and sustained, and each step involved Pueblo labor, Pueblo food, and Pueblo decisions. By following the missions, the tribute system, the household economy, and the political strategies of Pueblo leaders, the chapter reveals a colonial society that was both brutal and brittle.

The chapter also reveals larger processes that recur across the continent. Missions, tribute, labor drafts, and religious repression appear in other regions treated elsewhere in this textbook, and the Spanish dependence on Indigenous cooperation, the conflicts between church and state, and the interaction of colonial pressure with environmental stress are patterns that students will encounter again. At the same time, the Pueblo case has its own specificity: autonomous towns, a rich ceremonial life, and a long history of adaptation to a demanding environment.

The continuities that survived beyond this period are as important as the changes. Pueblo communities retained their languages, their ceremonial institutions, and their relationship to the land, even as they incorporated new elements and endured severe losses. The Spanish colonial period left Catholic churches and feast days that remain part of Pueblo life, and it left memories of violence that remain part of Pueblo historical knowledge. Chapter 44 builds on this foundation by examining the revolt of 1680, and later chapters follow the Pueblo nations into the eighteenth century and beyond.

Finally, the chapter models a way of reading colonial history. Documents written by the powerful must be read critically, archaeology and environmental evidence must be integrated, and Indigenous historical knowledge must be respected. Students who can do this will be better prepared to understand not only New Mexico but any history in which the voices of the colonized survive only partially in the records of the colonizer.
