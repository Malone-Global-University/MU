Indigenous North America as a Continental System

Indigenous Peoples of North America: A Continental History

Chapter Opening

Archaeologists working in the interior of North America repeatedly find objects that were made, or whose raw materials originated, hundreds or thousands of kilometers away: Yellowstone obsidian in Ohio, marine shell in the Plains and the Great Basin, scarlet macaws and copper bells from Mexico in the Pueblo Southwest, copper from the Great Lakes in the Southeast. Each of these objects records a journey, and each journey was made by people who knew where they were going and why.

No single object proves a continental system, and no single journey made one. Yet the accumulation of such evidence, together with linguistic maps, oral histories, ethnographic and documentary records, and the physical remains of fortifications, canals, fish weirs, and ceremonial centers, points to a conclusion that older textbooks often avoided. Indigenous North America before sustained European conquest was not a mosaic of sealed cultural areas, each living in isolation from the others. It was a continent of peoples who moved, traded, married, negotiated, fought, taught, and learned across great distances, through networks that overlapped, shifted, and sometimes collapsed.

This chapter is the capstone of Volume III. Chapters 25 through 35 surveyed the homelands and societies of the Northeast, the Haudenosaunee and Anishinaabe worlds, the Southeast, the Plains, the Southwest, the Great Basin and Plateau, California, the Northwest Coast, the Subarctic, and the Arctic. Chapter 24 surveyed the continent's major societies on the eve of the fifteenth century. This chapter asks a different question: how did those societies touch one another, and in what sense did their relationships together make up a continental system? It follows seven connected processes: trade, diplomacy, migration, language, warfare, ecological management, and communication.

The phrase "continental system" requires care. It does not mean a state, an empire, a single economy, or a centrally managed network. No ruler or institution coordinated Indigenous North America. The term is used here for a pattern of overlapping local, regional, and interregional networks through which goods, people, knowledge, obligations, and political messages regularly moved, and which shaped the choices of the societies within them. These networks differed in age, scale, and intensity. Some were seasonal and local, others were regional, and a few connected the Arctic coast with the Gulf of California or the Great Lakes with the Gulf of Mexico. Strong regional and national autonomy persisted within all of them.

The evidence for this history is uneven, and the chapter says so throughout. Archaeology and materials sourcing trace movements of durable objects but are nearly silent on perishable goods such as hides, feathers, foods, and textiles. Historical linguistics reveals relationships among languages and long-term contact but dates them imprecisely. Oral traditions preserve migrations, alliances, and diplomatic histories, and they must be read with attention to their own purposes and protocols. Written sources describing Indigenous exchange and diplomacy were mostly produced after European contact, so historians often use later evidence to illuminate earlier conditions, a practice that is powerful but carries real risks, examined in Section 36.12.

The chapter also keeps to its position in the book. It does not retell the regional histories of Chapters 25 through 35, and it treats earlier exchange systems such as the Hopewell world of Chapter 20 as deep precedent rather than as its own subject. It concentrates on the networks of the centuries before sustained European presence, especially from about 900 to 1500 CE, while reaching back where the evidence requires. Horses, firearms, and the fur trade, which transformed many networks, belong to later chapters, beginning with Chapter 49. Norse contact in Greenland and Newfoundland belongs to Chapter 37.

The central argument can be stated briefly. Europeans did not introduce long-distance trade, diplomacy, migration, warfare, or political communication to North America. They entered a continent already organized by such relationships, and they later reshaped, exploited, and in many places disrupted them. Understanding the older system is therefore a precondition for understanding every chapter in Volume IV and beyond.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Define "continental system" as used in this textbook and distinguish it from empire, state, centralized economy, and isolated culture area.
  2. Describe the principal river, coastal, mountain, and overland corridors along which people and goods moved before sustained European conquest.
  3. Explain why language families indicate historical relationships rather than political alliance, and how multilingualism and lingua francas supported interaction.
  4. Analyze migration as a recurring feature of Indigenous history, using specific cases and distinguishing among archaeological, linguistic, and oral evidence.
  5. Trace the movement of key materials, such as copper, obsidian, shell, turquoise, pipestone, and salt, and explain how archaeologists identify their sources.
  6. Compare exchange, diplomacy, and communication across several regions without assuming that one region's institutions represent the continent.
  7. Assess the roles of kinship, marriage, adoption, and personal obligation in making long-distance networks function.
  8. Evaluate the place of warfare, raiding, and captivity within continental connectivity, avoiding both romanticization and stereotype.
  9. Interpret Indigenous ecological management as historical evidence that much of the continent was a human-shaped landscape, and weigh scholarly disagreement over its extent.
  10. Analyze why networks centered on Chaco, Cahokia, and other centers reorganized between about 1100 and 1450 CE, identifying multiple causes.
  11. Evaluate a primary source for Indigenous communication networks, identifying author, audience, purpose, and limits.
  12. Explain how the pre-conquest continental system shaped the conditions Europeans encountered, without treating later outcomes as inevitable.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. What would count as evidence that a continent without a unifying state was nevertheless a connected system?
  2. How do rivers, coasts, mountains, deserts, and seasons both enable and limit movement, and why is geography an influence rather than a determinant?
  3. Why does shared language ancestry not necessarily mean shared politics, and what do multilingual societies reveal about connection?
  4. Why might migration be understood as ordinary Indigenous history rather than an exceptional event?
  5. In what ways were trade, marriage, diplomacy, and ceremony aspects of a single relational world rather than separate spheres?
  6. How can warfare and exchange coexist within the same networks, and what does that suggest about political competition?
  7. What does it mean to call a landscape "managed," and how much of the continent can historians show was shaped by human practice?

Historical Context

By the fifteenth century, the societies described in Volumes I through III had behind them long histories of adaptation. Volume I traced the peopling of the continent and the regional economies that developed after the Ice Age, including early exchange in the Archaic and in the ritual landscapes of Chapter 12. Volume II followed the spread of agriculture, the connections between Mesoamerica and the lands to its north, the irrigated and village worlds of the Southwest, and the earthwork and mound-building traditions of the East, including the Hopewell exchange networks of Chapter 20 and the Mississippian cities of Chapters 21 through 23. Chapter 24 surveyed the continent on the eve of the fifteenth century, and Chapters 25 through 35 gave regional portraits of Indigenous nations and homelands.

The centuries between roughly 900 and 1500 CE were a period of notable political and demographic movement. Large centers in the Southwest, the Mississippi Valley, and elsewhere grew and then declined or reorganized, in conditions that included climatic variability, local political change, and shifting agricultural opportunity. Peoples such as the ancestors of Inuit moved across the Arctic, Pueblo communities relocated and consolidated, and Athabaskan-speaking peoples established themselves in the Southwest. In the Eastern Woodlands, maize agriculture supported larger and more aggregated communities, and political relationships among them took new forms, including the confederal arrangements examined in Chapter 26. Indigenous North America on the eve of European contact was therefore not a static continent but one in the midst of its own ongoing transformations.

A synthesis of this scale has to manage numbers that no textbook can list in full. Hundreds of distinct nations and communities, speaking languages from several dozen families, were linked by networks of many kinds. The chapter therefore proceeds by process and by corridor rather than by people-by-people description, and its examples are illustrative rather than exhaustive. Students should read each example as one documented instance of a wider pattern, and should recall that absence of evidence for a connection in a particular place is often a consequence of what survives, not proof that the connection did not exist.

Section 36.1

Indigenous North America as a Continental System

Historians and anthropologists long organized North America into "culture areas," regions such as the Plains, the Southwest, or the Northwest Coast within which peoples shared broad environmental adaptations and cultural features. The framework was useful for teaching and for museum display, and this textbook uses it as an analytical convenience (Chapter 1). But culture areas can mislead when they are drawn as bounded containers. The Plains were not sealed from the Mississippi Valley, nor the Plateau from the Pacific coast. Peoples near the edges of each region shared features with their neighbors, and some of the most important historical developments occurred along the boundaries.

The concept of a continental system shifts attention from the containers to the connections. For the purposes of this chapter, a continental system exists where sustained flows of goods, people, knowledge, and obligations connect regions across substantial distances, and where those flows demonstrably shape the choices of the societies involved. That definition permits sharp differences of scale. A hunting family trading dried fish for plant foods with a neighboring village participated in the same logic as a ceremonial specialist who obtained marine shell from a distant coast, but the two operated in different networks of different reach. Networks were nested: local relationships fed regional ones, and regional ones connected, sometimes intermittently, to interregional ones.

Three cautions follow. First, a system is not a plan. No authority designed or administered the networks examined here, and they were produced by countless decisions made by households, leaders, specialists, and communities. Second, connection did not erase difference. Peoples who traded intensively might remain culturally and politically distinct, and some of the most active traders were also rivals. Third, systems changed. A network that carried obsidian across half the continent in one century might shrink in the next because of political change, environmental pressure, or shifting ceremonial priorities. The chapter's claim is not that Indigenous North America was uniformly integrated, but that it was pervasively connected and that connection was a basic condition of life for most of its peoples.

Historical Vocabulary
Continental System

Definition. In this textbook, a pattern of overlapping local, regional, and interregional networks, including exchange, kinship, diplomacy, migration, communication, and shared ecological practice, through which Indigenous peoples of North America were connected across large distances.

Historical context. The term is an analytical convention of modern scholarship. It is not an Indigenous category, and it describes relationships that Indigenous peoples understood in their own terms, often as kinship, reciprocity, alliance, or obligation.

Why it matters. It allows historians to describe connection without implying empire, and it corrects the older image of isolated tribes.

Potential misunderstanding. "System" does not mean uniform, centrally directed, or equally intense everywhere. Many regions were more connected to some neighbors than to others, and the pattern of connection changed over time.

The evidence for the system comes from several independent lines, and the strongest arguments depend on their convergence. Materials sourcing connects objects to distant origins. Language maps and language contact show where peoples lived near and interacted with each other over long periods. Oral traditions preserve accounts of migrations, alliances, and exchange. Early documentary sources, written mostly by outsiders from the sixteenth century onward, describe trade routes, trading partnerships, councils, and travelers in operation. No single line suffices, but when they agree the conclusion becomes secure. Where they diverge, this chapter notes the disagreement.

Section 36.2

Geography of Connection: Rivers, Coasts, Mountain Passes, Plains, and Portages

People moved through North America without wheeled vehicles and without domestic draft animals. Their principal power was human, supplemented in the north and on the Plains by dogs, which carried loads and pulled sleds and travois. Travel therefore followed the paths of least resistance: navigable waters, natural corridors, and trails worn by generations of walkers. Watercraft were crucial and diverse. Bark canoes served the northern forests and the Great Lakes, dugout canoes the Southeast and many river systems, plank and cedar canoes the Northwest Coast, sewn-plank canoes the Chumash of the Santa Barbara Channel, and skin-covered kayaks and larger boats the Arctic. Each was suited to its waters, and each built on specialized knowledge.

Seasons governed movement as much as terrain. In the north, frozen rivers, lakes, and sea ice could make winter the easiest season for long journeys by foot or sled, while summer favored canoe travel. On the Plains and in the Southwest, travel and gathering followed cycles of rainfall, harvest, and hunting. Many exchange events were tied to seasonal concentrations of food, such as salmon runs on the Columbia and other rivers, bison hunting, camas and acorn harvests, and the late-summer harvests of farming villages. At such times large numbers of people gathered, creating opportunities to trade, arrange marriages, settle disputes, and hold ceremonies.

Geography created possibilities, not outcomes. A major river did not automatically produce a trade route, and a mountain range did not automatically prevent contact. Choices about where to travel, whom to visit, and what to carry depended on relationships, political conditions, and the perceived value of what could be obtained. Environmental diversity encouraged exchange, because regions produced different goods and peoples sought what their own homelands lacked, but the form that exchange took varied enormously.

Section 36.3

Language Families, Linguistic Frontiers, and Multilingual Worlds

Indigenous North America's linguistic diversity was among the greatest of any comparable region of the world. Scholars classify its languages into several dozen families and isolates, among them Algonquian, Iroquoian, Siouan, Muskogean, Caddoan, Uto-Aztecan, Athabaskan, Salishan, Wakashan, and Eskimo–Aleut (Inuit, Yupik, and Unangan languages). These families are the product of long histories of divergence from ancestral languages, and their distributions record movements of peoples that are often older than any written source. Algonquian languages, for example, range from the Atlantic coast to the Plains, which is consistent with long histories of dispersal. Athabaskan languages are found across the Subarctic interior, on parts of the Pacific coast, and in the Southwest, where the Diné and Apache peoples speak Southern Athabaskan languages.

Language relationship, however, is not political relationship. Speakers of related languages could be rivals, and speakers of unrelated languages could be close allies and trading partners. Linguistic families therefore tell historians about deep ancestry and long-term contact, not about alliances, identities, or institutions. Historical linguistics can also identify areas where unrelated languages share structural features because of prolonged contact, suggesting centuries of close interaction. Linguists have identified such language contact zones, for example in the Pacific Northwest and in parts of the Southeast and California, and they provide indirect evidence of the density of ancient relationships.

In practice, many people spoke more than one language. Multilingualism was a normal condition in regions of dense contact, as when villages on a linguistic frontier included speakers of several languages or when trading families learned the languages of their partners. Some regions also developed shared means of communication across language boundaries. Mobilian Jargon, documented in the lower Mississippi Valley and Gulf region, served as a trade and diplomatic language among peoples of different languages, though its origins and antiquity are debated and the earliest evidence comes from colonial-era sources. Plains Sign Language, widely used among peoples of the Plains who did not share a spoken language, is documented in historic sources, and its antiquity is likewise uncertain. Trade jargons of the Pacific coast, including Chinook Jargon, are documented in the historic period, and scholars debate how much of their development predates contact. These examples are best treated as evidence of communicative strategies, with the caution that the form in which they are recorded is later than the period this chapter surveys.

Section 36.4

Migration as a Continuing Historical Process

Migration is a recurring theme in the histories examined in earlier chapters. Volume I traced the original peopling of the continent, and subsequent chapters recorded movements of agricultural peoples, the shift of population centers, and the relocations that followed political and environmental change. Treating these movements together reveals a pattern: relocation was an ordinary feature of Indigenous history, undertaken for reasons that included changing climate, resource opportunity, political conflict, ceremonial instruction, and the search for trade partners. It was not an aberration that happened only when outsiders arrived.

Several well-known cases illustrate the variety. Anishinaabe oral traditions, discussed in Chapter 27, describe a long movement westward from the Atlantic coastal region, marked by a series of stopping places and by guidance from sacred teachings. Historians use the tradition alongside archaeology and linguistics, and its chronology remains a matter of discussion. The Southern Athabaskan ancestors of the Diné and Apache peoples moved southward from the Subarctic interior to the Southwest, a migration discussed in Chapter 30; scholars disagree about its timing, with estimates ranging from roughly the fourteenth to the sixteenth century, and about whether movement was gradual, in stages, or in several streams. Numic-speaking peoples of the Great Basin, whose languages belong to the Uto-Aztecan family, spread across the region in a process whose timing and mechanisms remain debated. Thule-tradition communities, ancestral to the Inuit, expanded rapidly eastward from northwestern Alaska across the Arctic during the thirteenth century, according to recent radiocarbon and tree-ring dating of sites in northwest Alaska; the exact onset and the regional sequence of the movement remain under continuing study, and its relationship to earlier Dorset communities is treated in Chapter 35.

In the Southwest, the late thirteenth century saw the depopulation of the northern Four Corners region and the movement of Pueblo communities to areas along the Rio Grande, the Hopi mesas, Zuni, and elsewhere, a regional reorganization examined in Chapter 18. On the Plains, village-farming peoples such as the Pawnee, Mandan, and Hidatsa moved along river corridors in response to environmental and political conditions, and Siouan-speaking peoples have migration traditions that scholars are still working to reconcile with archaeology. In the Southeast and Mississippi Valley, communities shifted as chiefdoms rose and fell.

Historians evaluate migration claims by combining kinds of evidence. Archaeology shows changes in house forms, pottery, tool kits, and settlement pattern. Linguistics suggests where language families diversified. Oral traditions preserve how peoples understand their own movements, often with an emphasis on meaning, relationships, and obligation as much as on date. Genetic research can illuminate population relationships, but it cannot by itself determine cultural or political identity. When these lines of evidence agree, historians can speak with confidence; when they do not, they should speak of competing reconstructions. Migration also created connections: relocating peoples carried knowledge, kin, and obligations with them, and the relationships they established with new neighbors expanded existing networks.

Section 36.5

Trade and the Movement of Materials

The best-documented evidence for continental connection is the movement of materials. Lake Superior copper, mined and worked for thousands of years, appears in sites across the eastern half of the continent. Obsidian from sources such as Yellowstone in Wyoming, Glass Buttes in Oregon, and the Coso volcanic field in California was carried across great distances; Yellowstone obsidian reached Hopewell sites in Ohio. Marine shell from the Gulf of Mexico, the Atlantic, and the Pacific is found in interior burials and caches, including shell cups and ornaments in Mississippian contexts and Pacific shell beads in Great Basin sites. Turquoise from sources such as those near Cerrillos in New Mexico circulated widely within the Southwest and into the northern borderlands, although how far it traveled into central Mesoamerica is disputed (Section 36.9). Catlinite or pipestone from Minnesota, mica from the southern Appalachians, Knife River flint from North Dakota, and Alibates agate from the Texas Panhandle were all traded widely. Salt, hides, dried fish, native copper, ivory, soapstone vessels, and food products moved in countless smaller exchanges.

Archaeologists identify the origins of materials through sourcing studies. Obsidian can be matched to specific volcanic sources through the chemical signature measured by techniques such as X-ray fluorescence. Chemical analysis can characterize copper and clay, and specialists can identify shell to the species and thus to a likely sea. Strontium and other isotope studies can sometimes show where individuals grew up, revealing movements of people as well as objects. These techniques have transformed the field, but they have limits. They tell where a material came from, not who carried it, through how many hands, or how it was understood. And because perishable goods rarely survive, the surviving record is biased toward stone, shell, metal, and ceramics.

Historical Vocabulary
Reciprocity and Down-the-Line Exchange

Definition. Reciprocity refers to the exchange of goods, services, and hospitality within relationships of mutual obligation, in which the social bond is at least as important as the material value. Down-the-line exchange refers to movement of goods across great distances through successive transactions between neighboring groups, rather than by single long-distance journeys.

Historical context. Both patterns appear in the archaeological and ethnohistorical record. Some materials moved by direct expeditions or by specialists who traveled widely, while others passed through many intermediaries.

Why it matters. The distinction affects interpretation: a rare object far from its source need not imply direct contact between its source and destination communities.

Potential misunderstanding. Exchange was not simply a primitive form of a modern market. Many transactions were embedded in kinship, ceremony, and diplomacy, and goods could carry spiritual and political significance beyond practical use.

Exchange took several forms. Gift giving between leaders and trading partners cemented relationships and carried obligations of return. Down-the-line exchange moved goods through neighboring communities. Trade specialists and families traveled to distant partners and gatherings. Seasonal fairs and ceremonies drew people from many communities. Tribute and redistribution operated within chiefdoms and paramount political structures. Marriage exchanges moved goods along with people. These mechanisms often operated together, and a single community could participate in several at once.

Objects also carried differing kinds of value. Some goods, such as flint and obsidian for tools, salt, and foods, served practical needs. Others, such as copper, marine shell, pipestone, and feathers, held ceremonial, political, and spiritual significance, and they were often what leaders sought to display or distribute. Archaeologists use the term prestige goods for such items, with the caution that "prestige" is a modern label for objects whose meanings were specific to the communities that used them. Some regions also developed shell beads that functioned as a standard of value, including the beads of California and the dentalium shells valued on the Northwest Coast and traded widely. These did not make markets in the modern sense, but they show formal systems of value that supported exchange across language boundaries.

Section 36.6

The Great Lakes, St. Lawrence, and Eastern River Networks

The Great Lakes and St. Lawrence system formed one of the continent's most important water corridors, linking the Atlantic Northeast, the Subarctic forest, and the interior. Peoples of the Anishinaabe, Wendat, Haudenosaunee, and neighboring nations (Chapters 25 through 27) lived along or between its waters, and birchbark canoes, portage routes, and trails made travel possible across large distances. Copper from Lake Superior had circulated in the region for millennia, and by the period of this chapter people also exchanged maize, wild rice, fish, hides, shell, and stone. The northward spread of maize agriculture, which reached the Northeast around the beginning of the second millennium CE, created new relationships between farming peoples and neighbors in the north who relied on fishing, hunting, and gathering. Exchanges of agricultural produce for northern products, documented in better detail in the early seventeenth century, probably have earlier roots, though the earlier evidence is indirect.

The corridors extended eastward and southward. The Hudson–Mohawk and Susquehanna valleys connected the Atlantic coast to the interior. The Ohio River and its tributaries linked the Great Lakes to the Mississippi system, and in the Ohio Valley, Fort Ancient and other communities participated in networks that connected them to the Mississippi Valley and the Appalachians. Haudenosaunee communities in the Mohawk Valley and the Finger Lakes region occupied a strategic location between the Atlantic coast, the Hudson, the lower Great Lakes, and the St. Lawrence. Their political development, discussed in Chapter 26, took place in the middle of these corridors. The St. Lawrence Iroquoian communities that Jacques Cartier encountered in the 1530s, a threshold that belongs primarily to later chapters, were part of the same world of river exchange.

These networks were not purely commercial. They carried people who married into other communities, ceremonial knowledge, diplomatic messages, and political obligations. They also carried conflict. Communities that controlled critical portages or river junctions could benefit from their position, and rivals contested it. The Great Lakes and the eastern rivers therefore illustrate a general feature of the continental system: the same corridors served trade, diplomacy, migration, and warfare, and the relationships among these activities were close.

Specific routes can be named. The Ottawa River, Mattawa River, Lake Nipissing, and French River route linked the upper St. Lawrence with Georgian Bay and Lake Huron. The Fox and Wisconsin rivers, joined by a short portage at the site of present-day Portage, Wisconsin, connected Green Bay on Lake Michigan with the Mississippi, and the Chicago portage joined Lake Michigan with the Illinois River. Portages bypassed obstacles such as Niagara Falls, and the rapids of the St. Marys River at Sault Ste. Marie were a famous fishing and gathering place for Anishinaabe peoples. Many of these routes are described in detail only in seventeenth-century European records, when travelers adopted paths Indigenous guides already used. The geography itself, the archaeological sites along the routes, and Indigenous oral histories make earlier use highly probable, but the claim for any particular century has to be tested against local evidence.

The materials found along these corridors illustrate their reach. Flint Ridge flint from Ohio and Onondaga chert from western New York circulated widely in the Eastern Woodlands, and Lake Superior copper, marine shell from the Atlantic and Gulf coasts, and mica from the Appalachians appear at sites far from their sources. In the Ohio Valley, Fort Ancient communities (roughly 1000 to 1650 CE) lived in villages that participated in exchange with Mississippian neighbors to the west and south while maintaining their own distinctive traditions. Farther north, the palisaded Iroquoian villages of the lower Great Lakes and the St. Lawrence valley reflect both agricultural aggregation and the need for defense. Chapters 25 and 26 treat these communities in detail; the point here is that the same river and portage network that carried copper and corn also carried marriage partners, envoys, and war parties.

The relationship between farming and non-farming peoples shows how corridors structured partnership. Seventeenth-century sources describe Wendat communities, who farmed maize in large quantities, exchanging corn and other goods with Algonquian-speaking neighbors to the north in return for dried fish, furs, and other northern products, often through long-standing, personal partnerships. Because those descriptions were written after contact and during the early fur trade, historians treat them as evidence of relationships whose roots may be older, rather than as a direct description of the fifteenth century. The corridor network therefore illustrates the central claim of this chapter: connection rested on geography, but it was sustained by relationships that people created, maintained, and sometimes broke.

Section 36.7

Mississippi, Missouri, Gulf Coast, and Southeastern Connections

The Mississippi River and its tributaries formed the continent's central corridor. Cahokia (Chapter 22), at the confluence of the Mississippi, Missouri, and Illinois rivers, was well placed within it, and the Mississippian world of Chapters 21 and 23 developed along its network. Mississippian centers shared architectural forms, such as platform mounds, and ceramic and iconographic traditions, including a body of imagery archaeologists call the Southeastern Ceremonial Complex, which appears on shell, copper, and pottery across a wide area. Spiro in eastern Oklahoma, a Caddoan Mississippian center, contained imported shell, copper, and other goods. Etowah in Georgia and Moundville in Alabama were major centers in their own regions. The existence of shared imagery suggests common ceremonial concepts, while differences in the details show that regional communities adapted and reshaped what they shared.

The relationship between the Mississippian world and the Plains is a subject of active research. Communities along the Missouri, including the ancestors of the Mandan, Hidatsa, and Arikara, developed distinctive village traditions, and exchange between Plains and Mississippian regions is documented by materials such as shell and flint moving in each direction. Caddoan peoples in the southern Plains and the lower Mississippi region were well positioned between the Gulf Coast, the Southeast, and the Plains. Later documentary sources record Caddo exchange of items such as bois d'arc wood prized for bows and of salt, and these sources suggest earlier patterns while requiring the cautions discussed in Section 36.12.

By about 1300 to 1500 CE, Cahokia's regional dominance had ended and the Southeast and lower Mississippi Valley were organized into many chiefdoms, some very large, linked by alliances, tribute, marriage, exchange, and conflict. Their relationships were not uniform; some communities were closely tied, while others were rivals separated by buffer zones. Gulf Coast communities, including the Calusa of southwestern Florida, controlled rich marine resources and exercised influence over neighbors, and there is evidence for contacts across the Gulf and the Straits of Florida, though the scale of those contacts remains debated. The Southeast was thus connected to the interior and the coast, to the Plains and the Ohio Valley, and to the Gulf, forming a distinct but open region within the continental system. The later histories of the Cherokee, Muscogee, Choctaw, Chickasaw, and others (Chapter 28) developed from this setting.

Cahokia's material connections show how a regional center drew on a wide hinterland. Chert hoes made from stone quarried at Mill Creek in southern Illinois were distributed across the Mississippi Valley, supporting agricultural production, and galena, copper, and marine shell reached Cahokia from sources in the upper Mississippi region, the Great Lakes, and the Gulf. The site of Aztalan in southeastern Wisconsin contains mounds, a stockade, and pottery with strong Mississippian features, but archaeologists disagree about whether it reflects settlers from the Mississippi Valley, local people adopting new practices, or a combination of the two. This disagreement is typical of questions about Mississippian expansion: shared forms demonstrate contact, but they do not by themselves identify who carried them or how political relationships were organized.

Other Mississippian centers developed their own networks. Spiro, on the Arkansas River in eastern Oklahoma, flourished between roughly the thirteenth and fifteenth centuries and yielded engraved marine shell cups, copper plates, carved pipes, and textiles, many of them made from materials obtained over long distances. Etowah in northwestern Georgia is known for its mounds and for repoussé copper plates depicting figures in elaborate dress. Moundville, on the Black Warrior River in Alabama, was a major regional center whose population declined after about the thirteenth century, according to many archaeologists, as it became more concentrated on mortuary and ceremonial use. These sites differ in layout, size, and history, and the connections among them did not require a single political center. They were linked by exchange, shared religious imagery, and probably by marriages and alliances among leading families.

The Gulf Coast and the interior were joined by waterways and trails. Marine shell, especially the large whelk shells used for cups and ornaments, moved north from the Gulf, while Caddoan peoples of the southern Plains and the lower Mississippi occupied a position between several systems. Later sources associate Caddo communities with exchange in salt and bois d'arc wood, and with elaborate relationships of hospitality, which historians treat with the caution discussed in Section 36.12. In the Southeast generally, political relationships among chiefdoms fluctuated between alliance and rivalry, and wide regions of low population between major chiefdoms are sometimes described as buffer zones. The Southeast was thus a region of dense connection and considerable conflict, and the two were part of the same political landscape.

Section 36.8

Plains, Rockies, Plateau, and the Interior Corridors

The interior of the continent, from the Missouri River to the Columbia Plateau, was crossed by corridors that connected farming villages, bison-hunting communities, and fishing peoples. Chapter 29 described the Plains before the horse, with its river-valley village farmers and its hunters. Here the focus is on how these peoples were connected. Farming villages along the Missouri, such as those of the Mandan and Hidatsa at the Knife River, produced maize, beans, and squash in surplus and became centers where hunters and traders could obtain agricultural produce in exchange for meat, hides, and other goods. Knife River flint and Alibates agate, quarried from specific sources, were traded across wide areas. Bison drives and jumps, such as the long-used Head-Smashed-In site in Alberta, used since thousands of years ago, show highly organized communal hunting that required cooperation among many people and generated products for exchange.

West of the Plains, the Rocky Mountains and the Plateau were crossed by routes that connected the interior to the Pacific. Obsidian from Yellowstone and other sources moved widely, and Plateau peoples exchanged with the Great Basin, the Plains, and the Pacific coast. The Columbia River was the great artery of the Plateau, and the fisheries at Celilo Falls and The Dalles drew people from across the region for salmon harvests, which were also occasions for trade, marriage, and diplomacy. The place was an exchange center of long standing, probably over many centuries, with goods from the coast, the interior, and the Plains passing through it. Chapter 31 treats the Plateau and Great Basin in detail.

The Great Basin, often imagined as isolated because of its arid environment, was in fact crossed by routes that connected California, the Southwest, the Plateau, and the Plains. Marine shell beads from the Pacific coast appear in Great Basin sites, and Great Basin products moved outward. This is the kind of evidence that undermines the idea of environmental isolation: difficult terrain shaped the form of travel, but peoples with detailed knowledge of water sources and seasonal resources moved through it regularly. This section does not discuss the changes that horses would later bring to Plains and Plateau networks; those are the subject of Chapter 49.

Specific places show how exchange worked. The Dalles and Celilo Falls on the Columbia were described by early nineteenth-century visitors as a great trading place, and archaeological deposits in the area show human use over many thousands of years. People gathered at the falls to catch and dry salmon, and the same gatherings brought together people from the coast, the Plateau, and the Great Basin to trade, arrange marriages, and settle disputes. Yellowstone obsidian, quarried at sources used for roughly eleven thousand years, is found at sites across the northern Rockies, the Plains, and, in the Hopewell period, as far east as Ohio. These sourced materials are evidence of pathways that lasted for centuries, though they do not tell us whether a given piece traveled by a single journey or many exchanges.

The southern Plains were linked with the Southwest. Alibates agate from the Texas Panhandle is found on the Plains and in Southwestern pueblos, and Southwestern pottery appears at Plains sites. Spanish expeditions in the 1540s described exchanges between Pecos Pueblo and Plains peoples, in which bison products were traded for maize and other goods. That evidence is post-contact and the horse was not yet part of the system, but it indicates a relationship that archaeologists trace in earlier periods as well. In the northern Plains, the Mandan and Hidatsa trading villages were linked with hunting peoples to the west and with partners in the Great Lakes, and Knife River flint is found at sites across a very wide area.

Historians should be candid about what is not known. Seasonal expeditions by Plateau peoples to the bison country east of the Rockies are well documented after horses became available, so the patterns of earlier foot travel are less certain. The Crow Creek massacre (Section 36.14) shows that Plains villagers lived with serious threats, and that exchange and violence coexisted. The picture of the interior that emerges is of communities linked by trade and kinship but also by competition, in which the movement of materials depended on conditions that changed from one century to the next.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Trading Village on the Upper Missouri

Villages such as those of the Mandan and Hidatsa were not only centers of exchange; they were homes. Households lived in earth lodges built with timber frames and earth coverings, and families farmed garden plots along the river bottoms, with women playing central roles in agriculture, food processing, and the management of surplus. The same surplus that fed the village made it a place where visitors could be hosted, and the arrival of traders was an occasion with social meaning: visitors were received, lodged, fed, and drawn into relationships that often involved kinship or formal partnership.

Much of our detailed knowledge of such practices comes from historic-period observers, many writing in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, and from Indigenous oral histories. Historians use them cautiously for earlier periods, supported by archaeological evidence of large, permanent villages and widely sourced materials. The social history that emerges is one of ordinary people, women and men, participating in networks that gave them access to goods and knowledge from distant places, while the decisions about hospitality, partnership, and obligation remained rooted in household and community.

Section 36.9

Southwest–Mesoamerican Connections

Chapter 14 introduced the connections between Mesoamerica and regions to its north, and Chapters 15 through 18 described the Southwestern societies that participated in them. This section synthesizes that evidence rather than retelling it. Archaeological finds in the Southwest include scarlet macaws, found in large numbers at some sites and eventually raised at others, copper bells from western Mexico, marine shell from the Gulf of California and the Pacific, and cotton. Turquoise circulated extensively within the Southwest and northern Mexico, but whether Southwestern turquoise reached central Mesoamerica is disputed: isotopic analyses of sampled Aztec and Mixtec turquoise objects found that most did not match Southwestern deposits and were instead consistent with Mesoamerican sources, which overturned an older assumption. Chemical analysis of residues in vessels from Chaco Canyon has identified cacao compounds, indicating that cacao, which grows only in tropical lowlands, was imported across a very long distance, although interpretation of how it was used remains the subject of discussion. At Paquimé in northern Chihuahua, a center that flourished between about 1200 and 1450 CE, excavations revealed macaw breeding facilities, large quantities of shell, and distinctive ceramics.

Southwestern communities also shared architectural and ritual forms with regions to the south. Hohokam ballcourts, discussed in Chapter 17, resemble courts in Mesoamerica, and some imagery in Southwestern art has parallels in Mesoamerican iconography, such as feathered serpent motifs. Scholars interpret these similarities differently. Some stress direct contact and the movement of people or ideas; others emphasize local innovation and selective adaptation. The strongest conclusions are that goods and ideas moved across the border zone in both directions, that Southwestern peoples actively chose what to adopt and how to use it, and that the nature of the contact, whether through itinerant traders, down-the-line exchange, or ceremonial relationships, remains debated.

It is useful to state what each kind of evidence can and cannot establish. Macaw skeletons at Pueblo Bonito and at Paquimé, which numbered in the hundreds at the latter, prove that birds native to tropical Mexico were brought north and kept in captivity, and genetic studies are consistent with breeding in the Southwest in some periods; they do not show who carried the birds or whether Mesoamerican traders ever came north. Copper bells, made by casting techniques developed in western Mexico, appear in Hohokam, Mogollon, and Paquimé contexts after about 900 CE, but the Southwest did not take up casting itself, which suggests selective adoption of an object without the technology behind it. Shell from the Gulf of California was worked into bracelets and ornaments in Hohokam communities and distributed widely. Cotton was grown in the Sonoran Desert and traded. The theobromine detected in cylinder-jar fragments from Pueblo Bonito, dated to roughly 1000 to 1125 CE, is strong evidence for imported cacao, but not for how, how often, or by whom it was consumed.

The same caution applies to ballcourts and imagery. Hohokam ballcourts resemble Mesoamerican ones in form, and certain motifs, such as feathered serpents, recur in both regions, but resemblance can reflect borrowing, shared deeper ancestry, or independent development, and scholars continue to disagree. The turquoise case is instructive. For generations, writers assumed that the turquoise in Aztec and Mixtec objects came from the Southwest, and that assumption shaped theories of long-distance trade. Provenance analysis showed that the assumption was unsupported for the objects tested, and it reminded historians that attractive explanations require testing. The firmer evidence for Southwest–Mesoamerican connection lies in goods such as macaws, copper bells, shell, cotton, and cacao, and in the evidence that Southwestern communities made their own choices about what to take, what to modify, and what to ignore.

Influence did not mean political control. There is no evidence that Mesoamerican states ruled Southwestern communities, and the Southwestern evidence points to local political systems that adopted and transformed imported goods and ideas. Influence may have run in both directions, but the evidence for goods and ideas moving north is firmer than the evidence for movement south, and the older assumption that Southwestern turquoise supplied the cities of central Mexico can no longer be taken for granted. Placing the region within the continental system corrects both the image of the Southwest as an isolated outpost and the opposite image of it as a mere periphery of Mesoamerica. It was a distinct region with its own agricultural, architectural, and political traditions that was linked to its neighbors in several directions: northward to the Plateau and Great Basin, eastward to the Plains, westward to California and the Pacific, and southward into Mexico.

Section 36.10

California, the Plateau, and Pacific Exchange

California's extraordinary linguistic and ecological diversity (Chapter 32) was accompanied by dense networks of exchange. Shell beads, especially those made from Olivella shells, circulated widely, and Channel Islands communities produced shell beads in large quantities for exchange with mainland peoples. The Chumash built sewn-plank canoes, called tomol, that connected islands and mainland. Clam shell disk beads were used as a standard of value in many California communities, and magnesite beads from Pomo territory were also prized. Obsidian from sources such as Napa Valley, Casa Diablo, and Coso was traded for hundreds of kilometers, and acorn products, salt, fish, and other foods moved between ecological zones.

California's connections extended well beyond its own borders. Pacific shell traveled eastward into the Great Basin and the Southwest, and dentalium shells from the Northwest Coast reached California, where they were valued, notably among peoples of the north. Overland routes crossed the Mojave Desert and connected the Colorado River with the coast; Mohave and other peoples of the Colorado River were important participants in trade between the Southwest and California. The Sierra Nevada, Cascades, and river valleys connected California to the Plateau and Great Basin. California's linguistic fragmentation, rather than isolating communities, meant that many people regularly interacted across language boundaries, and trade and marriage ties joined communities that spoke very different languages.

The Channel Islands illustrate how specialized production could support exchange. On Santa Cruz Island and neighboring islands, Chumash communities made large quantities of Olivella shell beads, using small stone drills made from local chert, and exchanged them with mainland communities. Many archaeologists argue that bead production intensified in the centuries after about 1100 CE and that the beads functioned as a widely recognized form of valued exchange material, though the details of production, organization, and use remain debated. The beads found in Great Basin rockshelters and elsewhere come from this wider system, and sourcing and typology allow archaeologists to trace the changing popularity of different bead styles.

In northern California, peoples such as the Yurok, Hupa, and Karuk valued dentalium shells, obtained through exchange from the north, as a measure of wealth and used them in payments that settled disputes and marriages. Obsidian from Glass Mountain near Napa, Casa Diablo, and Coso reached many regions, and its distribution allows archaeologists to map the sphere of different exchange systems. Acorn flour, dried fish, salt, and other goods moved between ecological zones, and the Mohave Trail across the Mojave Desert and the Colorado River connected the Southwest with the coast. Owens Valley Paiute and other Great Basin peoples exchanged pine nuts and other products with California neighbors, according to ethnographic sources. Because much of the detailed evidence for such exchange comes from the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, scholars combine it with archaeological data, and distinguish between practices demonstrated for the period before contact and those that may have changed under colonial pressure.

Section 36.11

Northwest Coast, Subarctic, Arctic, and Bering Networks

On the Northwest Coast (Chapter 33), cedar canoes made the coastal waterways highways, and coastal peoples were connected with the interior by rivers and trails. Obsidian from sources such as Mount Edziza in northern British Columbia was traded widely, and the grease of the eulachon, a small fish, was traded inland along routes later called grease trails, which connected coastal communities with interior peoples. Copper, from sources in southern Alaska and elsewhere, was shaped into valued objects, including the shield-shaped "coppers" that became important in later Northwest Coast political and ceremonial life, and dentalium shells traveled far beyond the coast. Tlingit and neighboring peoples were linked with Athabaskan peoples of the interior through trade and marriage. The potlatch and related institutions, discussed in Chapter 33, brought neighboring communities together, and hosting such events established claims and relationships across village and sometimes language boundaries.

The Subarctic interior (Chapter 34) was crossed by rivers and portages, and peoples such as the Dene, Cree, and Innu used birchbark canoes in summer and snowshoes and sleds in winter. Native copper from the Coppermine River region was used by Dene and Inuit peoples. Goods moved between the boreal forest and the Arctic coast, and between the interior and both the Pacific and Hudson Bay regions. Subarctic peoples were mobile and tied to wide networks of kin and exchange that stretched across vast areas, with trade connecting them to farming peoples to the south.

In the Arctic (Chapter 35), the rapid eastward expansion of Thule-tradition communities during the thirteenth century created connections stretching from Alaska toward Greenland. Evidence for exchange across the Bering Strait, between Alaskan and Siberian peoples, includes imported goods in Alaskan sites, and historical sources describe trading fairs in the region; the antiquity of such fairs is uncertain, though exchange across the strait is much older. In the eastern Arctic, Inuit used iron from the Cape York meteorite in Greenland, a source of meteoritic iron that was exploited for tools. Ivory, soapstone, hides, and other goods moved along coasts and between coast and interior. These networks connected the Arctic to the rest of the continent through intermediaries and, at the threshold of sustained European contact, to the Norse in Greenland, a subject that belongs to Chapter 37.

On the Northwest Coast, obsidian from Mount Edziza, in northern British Columbia, has been found in sites hundreds of kilometers from its source, and the distribution of different obsidians has been used to map exchange among coastal and interior communities. The grease trails that connected the Nass River and the interior, and the trail linking the Nuxalk of the Bella Coola valley with Dakelh (Carrier) communities in the interior, were routes along which eulachon grease, dried fish, shells, and other goods moved. Later accounts describe the Chilkat Tlingit as intermediaries between the coast and interior peoples across passes such as the Chilkat and Chilkoot, a position that conferred influence, although those accounts date from the era of the maritime and fur trades. Haida canoe builders were known in the historic period for supplying canoes to mainland peoples. Together these examples show that coastal wealth and prestige were linked to the interior by chains of partnership and marriage.

In the Subarctic, native copper from the Coppermine River region and from the Copper River in Alaska was used and traded by Dene, Inuit, Ahtna, and neighboring peoples, and Ahtna copper moved toward Tlingit communities, where copper acquired great social and ceremonial importance. Chipewyan and other Dene peoples traveled across wide territories, and Cree and Innu networks connected the boreal forest to the Hudson Bay and Atlantic watersheds. Archaeologists note that the lack of durable materials in many Subarctic sites, and the dominance of perishable ones such as birchbark, hides, and wood, make the northern record particularly difficult to read.

In the Arctic, exchange across the Bering Strait is documented archaeologically by objects from Siberia in Alaskan sites and by Alaskan materials in Siberian ones, and the early Thule-related communities of northwestern Alaska were well placed to benefit. As Thule-tradition communities moved east, they carried a toolkit and knowledge of maritime hunting and also maintained connections to the west and with neighbors along the way. Meteoritic iron from the Cape York meteorites in northwestern Greenland has been identified in some sites of the eastern Arctic, evidence that people in the High Arctic valued and traded a distinctive source of metal. Walrus ivory, soapstone, and animal products moved between coastal and interior communities. The eastern Arctic expansion also brought Thule-tradition communities into contact with earlier Dorset communities and, eventually, with Norse in Greenland, subjects for Chapters 35 and 37.

Section 36.12

Kinship, Marriage, Adoption, and Personal Networks

Exchange, diplomacy, and migration were carried out by people connected to one another through relationships, and those relationships were often framed in the language and practice of kinship. Marriage between members of different communities created alliances and gave each side access to partners in the other's territory. Many Indigenous societies had clan or lineage systems that crossed village and sometimes national boundaries. In the Haudenosaunee Confederacy, for example, clans such as Turtle and Wolf include members in more than one nation, so that a person traveling to another nation could find clan relatives. Anishinaabe doodem (clan) relationships likewise linked communities across the Great Lakes, and Cherokee clans linked towns. On the Northwest Coast, marriages between lineages of different villages and the hosting of potlatches built alliances among leading families.

Adoption and fictive kinship extended relationships beyond blood and marriage. In several regions, ceremonies created kin ties between groups, and individuals could be adopted into families, replacing lost members. Trade partnerships often took the form of personal bonds of hospitality, in which partners hosted each other and exchanged gifts over years. Women were important in these networks as producers of goods, managers of household resources, and kin who connected communities through marriage. Understanding exchange as relationship helps explain why it often could not be reduced to price: the relationship was the point, and obligations continued over time.

Historical Vocabulary
Ethnohistoric Analogy

Definition. The use of descriptions and oral histories recorded after European contact to interpret archaeological evidence and conditions of earlier periods.

Historical context. Because most detailed written accounts of Indigenous exchange, kinship, and diplomacy date from the sixteenth century or later, historians often use them to illuminate earlier societies, while testing them against archaeology and linguistics.

Why it matters. It allows historians to give social meaning to material evidence, which otherwise cannot show how people organized relationships.

Potential misunderstanding. Practices recorded after contact may have changed because of contact, disease, and new trade, so using them uncritically can project a later period backward. Good analysis asks whether the practice is supported by earlier evidence or plausibly independent of European influence.

Archaeology can sometimes confirm the movement of people. Isotope studies have identified individuals buried at several major centers who spent their early lives elsewhere, which is consistent with migration, marriage, or other forms of mobility. Such evidence does not tell us why people moved, but it supports the view that personal networks were part of the regional systems of the period.

Section 36.13

Diplomacy, Councils, Confederacies, and Alliance Systems

Indigenous nations negotiated with one another through established institutions, ceremonies, and protocols, and these diplomatic practices were as varied as the societies that used them. The Haudenosaunee developed one of the best-known examples, the Great Law of Peace, which joined five nations in a confederacy governed by councils and based on consensus, with ceremonies of condolence and renewal. Haudenosaunee tradition attributes its founding to the Peacemaker, working with Hiawatha and Jigonsaseh. Estimates of when it occurred vary widely, from as early as the twelfth century to as late as the sixteenth, and many historians favor a date in the fifteenth century or later, a question treated in Chapter 26. Wampum, strings and belts of shell beads, were used to record and confirm agreements and to remind people of their terms, although scholars debate how elaborate wampum diplomacy was before European trade provided new tools and materials.

Other regions developed other diplomatic forms. In the Southeast, many communities organized political life around paired roles associated with peace and war, and later sources describe ceremonies for making peace. On the Plains, calumet ceremonies, in which a pipe was shared, created bonds between peoples who might otherwise be strangers or enemies; the pipe ceremony is documented in historic sources, and archaeological evidence for pipes is much older. On the Northwest Coast, the potlatch served as a public institution through which claims, titles, and alliances were validated before witnesses from other communities. In the Southwest, Pueblo communities maintained relationships through ceremony, exchange, and shared concerns. Large chiefdoms, such as those of the Mississippi Valley and the Southeast, managed relations with neighbors through tribute, marriage alliances, and warfare.

Diplomacy depended on communication, translation, and knowledge of protocol. Messengers carried news and invitations; gifts established seriousness; speeches used a language of kinship in which allies were called brothers or fathers. Diplomats needed to know the customs and interests of other nations, which meant that knowledge about distant peoples circulated widely. These institutions did not make the continent peaceful or uniform, and different peoples organized diplomacy differently. They show that political relations among Indigenous nations were structured, deliberate, and capable of handling conflict and cooperation, which is why European colonizers who arrived later found established diplomatic traditions to engage and, in many cases, to depend on (Chapter 55).

Section 36.14

Warfare, Raiding, Captivity, and Political Competition

Warfare was part of the continental system, and a history that omitted it would present an incomplete and falsely idealized picture. Archaeological evidence documents violence in many regions and periods. Palisaded villages appear in the Eastern Woodlands and the Plains; Cahokia itself was enclosed by a stockade rebuilt several times beginning around the twelfth century. Several sites preserve evidence of mass killing, including the Crow Creek site on the Missouri River in South Dakota, dated to about 1325 CE, where the remains of roughly five hundred people were found, and Southwestern sites that show episodes of violence in earlier periods. Oral traditions and early documents describe raiding, revenge, and warfare between communities, and many societies had institutions for warriors and for the care of the dead.

The causes of warfare were varied. They included competition for land and resources, especially in times of environmental stress or population concentration; revenge for killings; the pursuit of status by leaders and warriors; conflicts over trade routes and access to prized goods; and political rivalry among chiefdoms and confederacies. Captivity and adoption were practices in several regions: captives might be killed, enslaved, or adopted to replace lost family members, and in some societies captive-taking was tied to ceremony and kinship. On the Northwest Coast, slavery was an institution within some societies and raiding for captives was recorded. These practices differed greatly from region to region and are best studied in the regional chapters, including Chapter 47 for the Haudenosaunee mourning wars of the seventeenth century and Chapter 53 for captivity and slavery in the colonial period.

Warfare and exchange were not opposites. Peoples at war in one period or season might trade in another, and truces often protected trade fairs and ceremonies. Alliances in war could develop into diplomatic and economic partnerships. Warfare also influenced where people lived, how they built their villages, and which routes they used. At the same time, historians should avoid portraying pre-contact North America as either a peaceful paradise or a place of perpetual war. The evidence indicates that conflict was periodic and regionally variable, intensifying in some places and periods and receding in others, and that peace-making was as much a part of Indigenous political practice as war.

Cause and Effect

Why Did Major Networks Reorganize between about 1100 and 1450?

Long-term causes

Agricultural intensification and population aggregation made some communities dependent on a limited set of crops, centers, and political arrangements, which could be vulnerable to environmental or political stress.

Short-term causes

Climatic variability, including episodes of drought documented by tree rings in the Southwest, along with political rivalry, local conflict, and shifts in leadership and ceremonial authority.

Immediate trigger or context

Varied by region. In the northern Southwest, a prolonged late-thirteenth-century drought coincided with depopulation. In the Mississippi Valley, the reasons for Cahokia's decline are still debated and may involve flooding, political change, conflict, and other factors.

Immediate effects

Relocation of populations, abandonment of some centers, fortification and conflict in some areas, and the rise of new centers in regions such as the Rio Grande, the Southeast, and northern Mexico.

Long-term consequences

More decentralized and multi-centered networks in many regions, with continued exchange, new political forms such as confederacies, and communities whose relocations shaped the homelands described in Chapters 25 through 35.

Historical complexity

No single cause explains these changes, and different regions followed different paths. Environmental stress interacted with political, social, and ceremonial factors, and the outcomes were not inevitable.

Historical complexity: Students should resist the older idea of "collapse" as a simple endpoint. Communities at Chaco, Cahokia, and elsewhere did not vanish; they reorganized, moved, and in many cases continue as living nations whose histories include these centuries.

Section 36.15

Communication Across Distance

Communication was the connective tissue of the continental system. At the most basic level, people communicated by traveling and talking. Messengers carried news, invitations, and diplomatic messages between communities, and gatherings for trade, ceremony, and harvest were opportunities for news to spread. Multilingual individuals, including traders, kin through marriage, and captives or adoptees, served as interpreters. Where languages differed, sign systems and trade languages (Section 36.3) bridged the gap. Trails and river routes, maintained by use, were a physical form of communication; some, such as long-distance trails later followed by European travelers, had been used by Indigenous people for generations. The roads associated with Chaco Canyon are among the most formal examples of constructed routes in North America, though scholars debate their functions, which may have been ceremonial and symbolic as well as practical.

Indigenous peoples also developed means of recording and transmitting information beyond speech. Wampum belts and strings preserved the memory of diplomatic agreements for those trained to interpret them. Pictographs and petroglyphs marked places and recorded events. Plains peoples later kept winter counts, pictographic calendars that recorded a notable event for each year, although the earliest surviving examples date from the eighteenth century and the antiquity of the practice is uncertain. Specialists in oral tradition trained to preserve histories, genealogies, and ceremonial knowledge with precision. Some peoples drew or carved maps; Inuit of East Greenland, for example, carved wooden coastal maps recorded in the historic period, and Indigenous informants later provided European explorers with drawn and spoken geographic information.

Information about distant places and people spread through these channels, often well ahead of the travelers themselves. Knowledge of routes, resources, and political conditions was an asset, shared within relationships of trust. The continental system was thus an information system as well as an exchange system, and that fact helps explain how news and goods could move among peoples who had never met. The same channels would later carry news of European arrival, new goods, and disease, a subject examined in Chapter 40.

Primary Source Window

Álvar Núñez Cabeza de Vaca's Account of His Overland Journey (1530s; published 1542)

Creator: Álvar Núñez Cabeza de Vaca, a Spanish survivor of the failed Narváez expedition to Florida · Date: events of c. 1528–1536; narrative published in 1542 · Type: Travel narrative (the Relación) written for the Spanish crown

Context: After the Narváez expedition collapsed on the Gulf Coast in 1528, a handful of survivors, including Cabeza de Vaca, traveled for years through the lands of many Indigenous peoples, across the Gulf Coast region, the southern Plains, and northern Mexico, before reaching Spanish forces in western Mexico in 1536. The exact route is debated among historians. The account was written afterward, partly to explain events to the Spanish crown. The journey occurred after the Spanish conquest of Mexico but before Spanish colonization of the regions he traversed, so the account is a rare early outsider's record of networks that were largely Indigenous in organization.

Paraphrase, not a quotation. As the account is generally understood, the travelers moved from group to group, often passed along by hosts who guided them to neighboring peoples. They were received as healers, and communities escorted them and in some cases had heard of them before they arrived. The narrative describes people exchanging goods such as hides, stones, and ornaments, and it mentions valued objects of non-local origin, including items of copper and stones that readers have often interpreted as turquoise. It also describes communication among peoples of different languages, including by signs, and the presence of interpreters.

This is a summary of widely discussed features of the narrative and does not reproduce its wording. Students who wish to study the text should consult a reliable edition and translation.

Analysis: The account is valuable for what it suggests about how Indigenous networks of hospitality, exchange, and communication operated: travel by guided passage from one community to another, reliance on interpreters and sign, the circulation of non-local materials, and the speed with which news of unusual visitors traveled. It indicates that a foreign traveler could cross a vast region by moving through existing relationships among peoples.

Limitations: Cabeza de Vaca wrote from memory years later, for a Spanish audience, with reasons to emphasize his own role, and he did not understand most of the languages he heard. His identifications of peoples and places are difficult to match to later nations, and some of his interpretations, such as of particular stones, are uncertain. The account represents the experience of a small group of outsiders and cannot show how the networks looked from the perspective of Indigenous participants. It also comes from after the beginnings of Spanish activity in the Americas, so it cannot be treated as an entirely uncontaminated record of the pre-contact system.

Questions for students:

  1. What does the source suggest about how travelers could move through unfamiliar territories?
  2. Who created it, and under what circumstances?
  3. What purposes might have shaped how the narrative was written?
  4. What can historians learn about Indigenous communication and exchange from this source?
  5. What can historians not establish from it alone, and what other kinds of evidence would help?
Section 36.16

Ecological Management as Continental History

The landscapes Europeans encountered were, in many regions, the product of long Indigenous stewardship. Indigenous peoples across the continent used fire to shape habitats, increase the yields of useful plants, improve hunting, and reduce the risk of destructive fires. California peoples managed oak woodlands, grasslands, and basketry plants through burning and pruning. Plains peoples used fire to influence grasslands and the movement of bison. In the Eastern Woodlands, burning, together with agriculture, shaped forests and clearings in many regions, though scholars debate the extent. Anishinaabe peoples tended wild rice beds, and Plateau and Coast Salish peoples cultivated camas. On the Northwest Coast, people constructed clam gardens, rock-walled terraces that increased shellfish production, and fish weirs on rivers and coasts controlled and harvested fish in many regions. In the Southwest, the Hohokam built extensive irrigation canals in the Sonoran Desert (Chapter 17), and Pueblo farmers developed water-management systems suited to their environments. Farmers across the East cultivated the Three Sisters of maize, beans, and squash and managed fields and surrounding forests.

This management was not only local. Knowledge of plants, animals, fire, and water was shared along trade and kinship networks, and crops, techniques, and varieties moved across regions. Management also created the resource surpluses that supported exchange, such as salmon, acorns, maize, and bison products. In this sense ecological management was continental history: it shaped where populations could live, what goods were available, and how political power was organized. Later chapters, particularly Chapters 133 through 135, return to Indigenous environmental knowledge and stewardship in the modern world.

The idea that North America before Europeans was untouched wilderness is a long-standing myth, and the evidence of Indigenous management refutes it. Yet historians should not replace one simplification with another. The scale and effects of Indigenous land use varied greatly by region, population density, and period, and in some places human activity may have caused local environmental damage or depletion. Scholars continue to debate how much of the landscape in a given region reflected human management compared with climate and natural processes. The sound conclusion is that Indigenous peoples were active shapers of their environments across the continent, using knowledge of great sophistication, and that the extent of their influence is a subject of ongoing research in which Indigenous knowledge is increasingly central.

The evidence for management differs by practice. Fire is documented in ethnographic records, early descriptions, and pollen and charcoal studies. In California, the evidence for burning to maintain oak woodlands, grasslands, and basketry plants is strong, and some of the best documentation comes from the knowledge of present-day tribal members. In eastern North America, charcoal records show increased burning near Native settlements in many places, and early observers described open, park-like woodlands, but studies in some regions, including parts of New England, indicate that climate was a more important influence than burning. Fire could also work together with bison on the Plains to keep grasslands open, though the interplay among fire, grazing, and climate remains an area of research.

Some of the clearest evidence concerns structures. Clam gardens, rock-walled terraces built in the intertidal zone along the coast from southeastern Alaska to Washington, include examples dated to at least several thousand years old, and experimental work has found that they can increase clam growth and numbers. Fish weirs are preserved at sites such as the Boylston Street Fishweir in Boston, about five thousand years old, and salmon weirs and traps are documented on rivers across the Pacific coast and elsewhere. Camas, whose bulbs were harvested, roasted in earth ovens, and traded, was tended in meadows in the Pacific Northwest, often with burning. Anishinaabe peoples and others harvested wild rice in ways that sustained the beds, and in the Great Lakes region archaeologists have identified ridged agricultural fields and garden beds of earlier centuries.

The Hohokam canal systems of the Salt and Gila valleys carried river water to fields over distances of many kilometers and were maintained over centuries, a scale of engineering that required organized labor and shared rules. Pueblo farmers used floodwater, check dams, and field placement to farm in a dry land. Bison drives such as Head-Smashed-In in Alberta, used for thousands of years, used the terrain and the coordinated work of many people. Management also implied rights and responsibilities: in many societies, families or communities held rights to particular fishing stations, berry grounds, clam gardens, or hunting territories, and governed their use. Not every effect was beneficial or sustainable, and some local environmental damage and resource pressure appear in the record, including in debates over the effects of intense settlement at Cahokia. The balanced conclusion is that management was widespread, varied, and knowledgeable, and that scholars must examine each region on its evidence.

Section 36.17

Technology and Knowledge in Motion

Practical knowledge also traveled the continent's networks. Maize, domesticated in Mesoamerica, spread northward over centuries, and its adoption involved the development of varieties adapted to shorter growing seasons and different climates. Beans and squash followed, and in the Northeast the combination of crops that supported large villages became established over time. The bow and arrow spread across much of the continent during the first millennium CE and in many regions replaced the atlatl. Pottery traditions developed in several regions and spread through contact and migration. Watercraft, from birchbark canoes to kayaks and plank canoes, were regional technologies refined over time. Architectural forms, including pit houses, earth lodges, longhouses, and plank houses, were adapted to local materials and climates. Methods of preserving food, such as drying, smoking, and pounding meat, made long-distance travel and winter survival possible. Native copper was cold-hammered and annealed into tools and ornaments in the Great Lakes and elsewhere, and copper bells from western Mexico show metalworking traditions from farther south reaching the Southwest, though not smelting traditions in the north.

Historians approach the spread of technology with care. The older concept of diffusion often implied that ideas flowed from centers to passive recipients, but it is now understood that adoption involved selection, adaptation, and sometimes independent invention. Communities decided what to adopt, modified it to fit local needs, and sometimes rejected it. Knowledge was also often held by specialists, such as canoe builders, navigators, healers, and farmers, and its transmission depended on teaching relationships. Technological change thus illustrates the same principle as exchange: networks carried possibilities, and communities made their own choices.

The history of agriculture shows both movement and independent invention. Maize came from Mesoamerica and reached the Southwest early, but it became a major crop in the East only much later, spreading widely by about 900 to 1100 CE and changing the economies of communities there. In the north, farmers developed varieties adapted to short growing seasons, including the northern flint maize associated with the Northeast. Beans arrived in the Northeast later than maize, perhaps by the late thirteenth century or after, and the combination of maize, beans, and squash then supported denser villages. At the same time, eastern North American peoples had domesticated their own crops, including sunflower, squash, and several seed plants, thousands of years earlier, which means that farming was not a single idea that spread from one source but a set of traditions that sometimes converged. This history is treated in Chapter 13.

Other technologies followed their own paths. The bow and arrow, which appears early in the Arctic, spread across much of the continent during the first millennium CE and gradually replaced the atlatl in many regions, with timing that varied from place to place. Pottery appeared independently in several regions, and distinctive traditions, such as the shell-tempered pottery associated with Mississippian communities, spread through contact and migration. Watercraft such as birchbark canoes, plank canoes, dugouts, and kayaks were regional responses to different waters, each embodied in specialist knowledge of materials and construction. Pueblos, earth lodges, longhouses, plank houses, and snow houses were local adaptations rather than variations on a single model, though builders in neighboring regions borrowed ideas from one another.

Metal shows the importance of choice. Peoples of the Great Lakes and elsewhere cold-hammered and annealed native copper into tools, ornaments, and ceremonial objects for thousands of years, which is a sophisticated technology that works metal without smelting. Cast copper bells from western Mexico reached the Southwest after about 900 CE, but regional communities did not adopt casting. Whether that reflects lack of access to ores, lack of need, or other considerations is unclear, but it demonstrates that technologies did not spread automatically. Knowledge was often held by specialists, such as navigators, canoe builders, healers, and master farmers, and passed on through apprenticeship and kinship, and some knowledge was protected by ceremony. For historians, the movement of technology is therefore evidence about relationships among people as much as about things.

Section 36.18

Ceremony, Sacred Geography, and Intellectual Networks

Religious and intellectual life was also a connecting force. Sacred places drew visitors from many communities. The pipestone quarries of Minnesota, where catlinite was extracted, were regarded as sacred by many peoples who visited to obtain stone, and the quarry is documented in historic sources as a place associated with peace among those who came. Chaco Canyon appears to have been a ceremonial center that drew people from a wide region, though scholars debate its role. Mississippian centers shared iconography that suggests common religious ideas, including figures such as winged beings and symbols such as the cross in a circle. Earlier, Hopewell centers (Chapter 20) created ceremonial landscapes that connected communities across the East. Ceremonies such as the potlatch and the calumet, discussed earlier, were institutions where religion, politics, and exchange merged.

Intellectual traditions circulated as well. Narratives of an earth-diver creating land, trickster figures such as Raven, Coyote, and Nanabozho, and cosmological themes appear in many regions, reflecting some combination of common ancient origins, contact, and parallel development, and historians generally avoid assuming one explanation. Ritual knowledge, medicinal expertise, songs, and ceremonial rights could be exchanged, purchased, or granted. The ceremonial specialists who made pilgrimages or sought rare materials for sacred purposes were among the continent's travelers. Sacred geography, the identification of particular places with creation, ancestors, and spiritual power, gave landscapes meaning for peoples whose territories included them, and made them destinations for travelers who came to honor them. These facts suggest that the continental system was not only an economy but also a field of shared and contested religious and intellectual life.

Specific cases illustrate how sacred places and ceremony connected communities. The pipestone quarries in southwestern Minnesota, where catlinite was removed for pipes, were used by many peoples, and Dakota, Lakota, and other traditions treat the place as sacred. Historic sources describe the quarry as a place where people from different nations could gather, and pipes made of the stone were used in ceremonies that created kinship and peace. Chaco Canyon is interpreted by some scholars as a pilgrimage and ceremonial center, drawing visitors from a large region, with great houses aligned to solar and lunar cycles and roads converging on the canyon; others emphasize resident elites, regional politics, or other functions. The deposits of turquoise, shell, and cylinder jars in great houses indicate ritual importance, but the scale and nature of the gatherings remain debated.

The Southeastern Ceremonial Complex is the best-known example of shared religious imagery. Engraved shell gorgets and cups, copper plates, and painted pottery at Spiro, Etowah, Moundville, and other centers depict winged human figures, serpents, hands with eyes, and other motifs. Scholars interpret the imagery by comparing it with later oral traditions of Southeastern and Plains peoples, such as the Caddo and Osage, an approach that carries the risks of ethnohistoric analogy. The shared imagery indicates connection and common ideas, while regional variation shows reinterpretation. At Cahokia, isotope studies suggest that some people buried at the site had grown up elsewhere, evidence consistent with visitors or migrants, and some scholars have proposed that the center attracted pilgrims, though how many and why remain open questions.

Ritual knowledge was itself an object of exchange. Plains peoples such as the Mandan and Hidatsa transferred sacred bundles and their associated rights from one owner to another through ceremony and payment. On the Northwest Coast, dances, songs, and names were acquired through inheritance, marriage, and purchase. Anishinaabe Midewiwin teaching, known from historic sources, involved the transmission of medicine and ritual knowledge to trained members. Narratives of an earth-diver, tricksters such as Raven, Coyote, and Nanabozho, and the Thunderbird appear in many nations in forms that differ in detail, and scholars debate how much reflects deep common origins, contact, or parallel development. Landscape features, such as Bear Lodge (Devils Tower) in Wyoming, have meaning for several nations. These facts show that the continent's networks included the movement of ideas, rights, and sacred responsibilities, and that people crossed boundaries to seek them.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Networks of Exchange and Connection over Two Millennia

What Changed

  • The centers of exchange shifted, from Hopewell ceremonial centers to Chaco, Cahokia, and later chiefdoms and regional hubs
  • The materials and objects of greatest prestige changed, as did the ceremonial contexts in which they were used
  • Political forms varied, including paramount chiefdoms, confederacies, and village-based councils
  • Agricultural change, population movement, and environmental conditions altered which regions were densely connected

What Stayed the Same

  • Long-distance movement of valued materials such as copper, shell, obsidian, and stone
  • The importance of kinship, hospitality, and reciprocity in making exchange work
  • The use of major river and coastal corridors
  • The close relationship among exchange, ceremony, and diplomacy

Why the pattern matters: Change was uneven and regionally specific, and it was not a smooth progression. Networks expanded and contracted, but the underlying practice of connection persisted. Those who experienced change most directly were residents of the centers that rose and declined; those in smaller communities often experienced greater continuity in local practices and kin networks.

Section 36.19

A Connected Continent Before Sustained Conquest

The preceding sections show a continent whose peoples were connected through overlapping systems of exchange, kinship, language contact, migration, diplomacy, conflict, communication, ecological practice, and ceremony. The picture is one of strong regional and national autonomy combined with real interdependence. Communities did not need a central authority to maintain connections, and the networks were durable because they rested on relationships and mutual interest. They were also vulnerable: networks that relied on particular centers, particular routes, or particular relationships could be disrupted when those failed.

Europeans did not create the long-distance trade, diplomacy, warfare, mobility, and political communication of North America. They entered networks that already existed, and they used them. Later chapters show how European traders, missionaries, and officials depended on Indigenous guides, interpreters, diplomatic protocols, and commercial networks, and how new goods, diseases, and pressures altered those networks. The chapters that follow, beginning with the Norse in Greenland and Newfoundland (Chapter 37) and the voyages that began in 1492 (Chapter 38), should be read with this background in mind. The outcomes of contact were shaped by the structure of the Indigenous world that Europeans encountered, not simply by European intentions.

That recognition should not be read as inevitability. The pre-conquest continental system did not determine what would follow, and historians must avoid reading later events back into it. It was a dynamic system with its own tensions and its own histories of change, whose peoples would respond to colonization with the same agency and strategies they had long applied to their neighbors.

Historical Debate

How Integrated Was Indigenous North America?

The question: Did the networks described in this chapter amount to integrated regional or continental systems that shaped the politics and economies of the societies within them, or were they better understood as many separate, shifting, and largely small-scale relationships whose long-distance reach was occasional?

Interpretation A — Integrated systems: Some scholars emphasize the scale and regularity of exchange and the sharing of ritual and iconographic traditions, arguing that centers such as Chaco and Cahokia drew in resources and people from wide hinterlands and shaped political and economic life across large regions. They use network analysis and other methods to argue for structured, interdependent systems.
Interpretation B — Local and episodic connection: Other scholars emphasize that much exchange was small-scale and embedded in local relationships, that rare imported objects may overstate how regular long-distance trade was, and that surviving durable materials give a biased picture. They caution that apparent regional systems may be the product of archaeologists' categories as much as of ancient reality.
Interpretation C — Nested and fluctuating networks: A third approach, widely adopted, sees networks of different scales that grew and contracted over time, with some regions and periods more integrated than others. Many scholars, including Indigenous scholars, also emphasize that relationships of kinship and reciprocity are central, and that frameworks developed for modern economies may not capture them.

Evidence: Materials sourcing, settlement and architectural evidence, shared iconography, linguistic contact, oral histories, and early written accounts are used by all sides, but they are interpreted differently.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns scale (how much material moved and how regularly), interpretation (whether shared symbols imply shared institutions), and terminology (whether "system" is an appropriate concept).

Why it matters: How historians answer shapes whether Indigenous North America appears as a set of separate regional histories or an interconnected continent, and whether regional centers are seen as political cores or as ceremonial and exchange hubs with limited authority. This textbook adopts the third approach, while recognizing that the first two identify real features of the evidence.

Student task: Choose one example from this chapter, such as the movement of Yellowstone obsidian, the Chaco cacao residues, or the Pacific shell found in the Great Basin. Explain how each interpretation would read the evidence, and identify what additional evidence would help decide among them.

Section 36.20

Chapter Review and Historical Thinking Checkpoint

This chapter has argued that Indigenous North America before sustained European conquest functioned as a continent of overlapping political, economic, linguistic, ecological, and intellectual networks. As students move from Volume III to Volume IV, they should be able to explain how the seven processes examined here, trade, diplomacy, migration, language, warfare, ecological management, and communication, worked together, and why none of them can be understood in isolation.

Students should also be able to use the chapter's habits of mind: asking what kinds of evidence support a claim about connection; distinguishing between movement of objects and movement of people; recognizing when later sources are being used to interpret earlier periods; and avoiding both the image of isolated tribes and the image of a single integrated system. These habits will recur throughout the rest of the textbook. The review materials that follow summarize the chapter and offer questions for study, discussion, and research.

Chapter Summary

Chapter 36 has examined Indigenous North America as a continental system: a pattern of overlapping networks through which goods, people, knowledge, and obligations moved across large distances before sustained European conquest. The chapter argued that no empire or central authority unified the continent, and that no single network covered all of it, but that connection was nevertheless pervasive, and that strong regional and national autonomy persisted within it.

The geography of connection rested on river systems, coastlines, mountain passes, portages, and trails, traveled by watercraft of many kinds and on foot, with dogs as the principal draft animals. Seasons shaped movement, and environmental diversity encouraged exchange, but geography created possibilities rather than determining outcomes.

Language families, contact zones, multilingualism, and lingua francas such as Mobilian Jargon and Plains Sign Language, whose antiquity is uncertain, revealed both deep historical relationships and everyday communication across boundaries. The chapter stressed that language relationship does not equal political alliance.

Migration appeared as a recurring part of Indigenous history, illustrated by Anishinaabe traditions, the Athabaskan movement to the Southwest, Numic expansion, the thirteenth-century eastward expansion of Thule-tradition communities across the Arctic, and Pueblo relocations. Historians evaluate such movements by combining archaeology, linguistics, oral tradition, and genetics, acknowledging disagreement over chronology and mechanisms.

Trade moved copper, obsidian, shell, turquoise (chiefly within the Southwest and northern borderlands), pipestone, flint, salt, and countless perishable goods through reciprocal gift exchange, down-the-line exchange, specialists, fairs, tribute, and marriage. Sourcing techniques allow archaeologists to trace materials, though they cannot reveal who carried them or how they were understood. Regional sections showed how these exchanges operated in the Great Lakes and East, the Mississippi and Southeast, the interior, the Southwest and Mesoamerica, California and the Pacific, and the North.

Kinship, marriage, adoption, and personal obligation made networks work. Diplomacy, including councils, confederacies, wampum, calumet ceremonies, and potlatches, provided institutions for peace and alliance, while warfare, raiding, and captivity were also part of political competition. The two were interwoven, and neither should be exaggerated.

Communication depended on travelers, interpreters, trails, and recording systems such as wampum and pictographs. The sources for it largely date from after contact, which requires careful use of ethnohistoric analogy. The Primary Source Window on Cabeza de Vaca illustrated both the value and the limits of such evidence.

Ecological management, including cultural burning, wild rice and camas tending, clam gardens, fish weirs, irrigation, and agriculture, showed that much of the continent was a human-shaped landscape, while historians continue to debate the extent of this shaping. Technology and knowledge moved through the networks, and communities selected and adapted what they received. Ceremony, sacred geography, and shared intellectual traditions formed another layer of connection.

The chapter's Cause and Effect and Timeline features showed that major centers such as Chaco and Cahokia lost their regional roles between roughly 1100 and 1450 CE through multiple, regionally varied causes, and that networks reorganized rather than disappeared. The Historical Debate showed that scholars differ about how integrated the continent was, and this textbook adopts a nested-network interpretation.

Finally, the chapter positioned the Indigenous continental system as the context for the encounters of Volume IV. Europeans entered a world of existing trade, diplomacy, warfare, and communication, and the outcomes of encounter were shaped by that world. Chapter 37 begins that story with the Norse in Greenland and Vinland.

Mapping a Material Across the Continent: A Source Dossier

This project asks you to trace the movement of one material discussed in this chapter and to assess how well the evidence supports claims about its circulation.

  1. Choose a material. Select one such as Lake Superior copper, obsidian from a named source, Olivella or dentalium shell, turquoise, pipestone, or Knife River flint.
  2. Identify the evidence. Using library resources, find scholarly publications and museum or tribal resources on where the material was found, how its source was identified, and how it was used.
  3. Map it. Create a map showing source areas and find locations, distinguishing secure sourcing from inference and noting the probable corridors involved.
  4. Evaluate interpretation. Explain what scholars and Indigenous communities say about its meaning, and note whether the interpretation depends on later documents.
  5. Write your findings. In a 3–5 page essay or equivalent presentation, assess what the evidence can and cannot establish, using careful language such as "scholars generally agree" or "this remains debated."

Library resources to use: Archaeological journals and reference works, museum collection catalogs, tribal historic preservation and cultural resource office publications where available, and the MGU History Library's materials on Indigenous exchange and archaeology.

Chapter Reflection

Chapter 36 closes Volume III by changing the scale of the question. After eleven chapters of regional portraits, it asks how those worlds were connected, and the answer is that connection was a basic condition of Indigenous life. Trade, kinship, language, migration, diplomacy, warfare, ecological practice, and ceremony tied communities together across great distances, without a central authority and without erasing the autonomy of the nations involved.

The chapter also reveals what kind of history Indigenous history is. It is not a story of isolated peoples awaiting contact, nor of a single integrated civilization. It is a history of many peoples who created and maintained relationships over centuries, who adapted to changing environments and political circumstances, and who produced institutions for peace, exchange, and communication that were sophisticated and enduring.

What survived beyond the period of this chapter is as important as what changed. The relationships of kinship and reciprocity, the diplomatic protocols, the knowledge of land and water, and the networks of communication did not disappear when Europeans arrived. They shaped how Indigenous nations engaged colonial powers, and they continue to shape Indigenous political and cultural life today.

With this chapter, Volume III ends and the textbook turns to encounter. The Norse voyages, the Columbian voyages, and the conquests that followed entered a continent whose peoples had long experience of managing relations with strangers, neighbors, and rivals. Reading Volume IV with this background allows students to see Indigenous peoples as actors in those events, working within, extending, and defending a world that was already their own.
