The Pueblo Revolt of 1680

Indigenous Peoples of North America: A Continental History

Chapter Opening

In the first days of August 1680, messengers moved between Pueblo communities scattered across hundreds of miles of mesa, river valley, and desert. They carried news that, if Spanish officials later understood it correctly, was to be conveyed with a counting device as well as with words: a cord tied with knots, one to be undone each day until the appointed morning. When that morning came, communities that spoke at least six mutually unintelligible languages acted within days of one another to end eighty years of colonial rule.

The uprising that followed is among the most consequential Indigenous victories in the history of North America. Between August 10 and the end of that month, Pueblo forces killed several hundred Spanish colonists and a large majority of the Franciscan friars in New Mexico, besieged the provincial capital of Santa Fe, and compelled the governor and the surviving colonists to abandon the province altogether. Spain had governed New Mexico, in one form or another, since the expedition of Juan de Oñate in 1598. For the next twelve years, no Spanish government operated along the upper Río Grande. The colonial order that Chapter 43 described, with its tribute demands, labor drafts, mission compounds, and campaigns against Pueblo ceremonial life, was overthrown not by a rival European power but by the people it had been built to rule.

This chapter treats that event as a political and religious revolution rather than as an isolated explosion of violence. The distinction matters. An outburst of rage can be explained by grievance alone. A coordinated movement spanning many distinct communities requires something more: leaders willing to risk their lives, a way to communicate across long distances without being detected, a shared understanding of objectives, and a decision about how to act on a single schedule. Explaining how the Pueblo peoples met these requirements, and why they were able to meet them in 1680 rather than in the earlier years when Spanish officials discovered similar plots, is the first task of the chapter. Its second task is to examine what Pueblo communities did with the independence they won. Its third is to follow the contested Spanish return between 1681 and 1696, which was neither a simple restoration nor a swift one.

The chapter therefore has three principal movements. The first covers the crisis of colonial rule, the organization of the revolt, and the events of August 1680, and occupies Divisions I through III. The second, in Division IV, covers the years from 1680 to 1692, when Pueblo communities governed themselves, rebuilt settlements, reorganized ceremonial life, and negotiated relations with one another and with neighboring peoples. The third, in Division V, covers the Spanish attempts to return, from Governor Antonio de Otermín's failed expedition of 1681 to the campaigns of Diego de Vargas and the renewed Pueblo uprising of 1696. Division VI then assesses the consequences and historical significance of the revolt, including the very different ways it has been remembered.

The evidence for these events is unusual and uneven. The richest surviving documentary record was produced by the people who lost: Spanish governors, friars, and soldiers who wrote reports, took testimony from captured Pueblo men, and compiled journals of their campaigns. Those documents are indispensable, but they were created for Spanish audiences and Spanish purposes, often to justify the conduct of the officials who wrote them. They record Pueblo words only through translators and interrogators, frequently under duress. Pueblo communities preserve their own historical traditions, shared according to community protocols and not always available to outsiders. Archaeology offers a third body of evidence, and in recent decades excavations at revolt-era settlements have changed how historians understand the independence years. A responsible account must read these sources against one another rather than treating any single one as an unquestioned baseline.

The chapter also connects to the wider history of the continent. The Pueblo Revolt occurred in the same decades as the great seventeenth-century contests in the Northeast and Great Lakes examined in Chapters 45 through 48, and it shared with them an essential feature: Indigenous political actors shaped the terms on which European colonies survived. In the Southwest, the revolt redirected relations among Pueblo, Apache, Diné, Ute, and Spanish communities, contributed to the dispersal of horses explored in Chapter 49, and set a precedent for the more negotiated colonial relationship that developed after 1696.

Nothing in this chapter is intended to treat the revolt as an inevitable consequence of Spanish misrule, nor to treat its aftermath as a foregone return to colonial normality. Those who planned the uprising in 1680 could not know whether it would succeed. Those who negotiated, fought, or fled during the 1690s faced choices whose outcomes remained uncertain. The history of the revolt is a history of decisions made under pressure by people who understood their circumstances far better than later narratives sometimes allow, and it deserves to be read with attention to that uncertainty.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain the structural conditions, immediate pressures, and organizational achievements that allowed the Pueblo peoples to launch a coordinated uprising in August 1680.
  2. Distinguish among the terms revolt, rebellion, revolution, and sovereignty, and assess which best describes the events of 1680 to 1696.
  3. Assess what historians can reliably establish about Po'pay and other Pueblo leaders, distinguishing documented evidence from later embellishment.
  4. Analyze why participation in the revolt varied among Pueblo communities and why the Pueblo peoples cannot be treated as a single political unit.
  5. Trace the military course of the uprising across the northern, central, western, and southern regions of the colony, including the siege of Santa Fe and the Spanish retreat.
  6. Evaluate Spanish documentary testimony, including interrogations of captured Pueblo men, as evidence for Pueblo intentions, beliefs, and decisions.
  7. Interpret archaeological evidence from revolt-era settlements such as Patokwa, Boletsakwa, and Kotyiti, and explain what it reveals about Pueblo reconstruction during the independence years.
  8. Describe everyday life, religious revitalization, and political organization in Pueblo communities between 1680 and 1692.
  9. Compare the Spanish campaigns of 1681 to 1682, 1689, 1692, 1693 to 1694, and 1696, explaining why Spanish authority returned and why its character changed.
  10. Contextualize the revolt within the wider colonial history of North America and assess competing interpretations of its significance.

Historical Context

The Pueblo peoples of the seventeenth century were the heirs of a long agricultural tradition in the northern Southwest, traced in Chapter 18 and developed in Chapter 30. Their communities lay along the upper Río Grande and its tributaries, on the high mesas of Acoma and Zuni, and farther west on the Hopi mesas in present-day northeastern Arizona. They spoke languages from several families: Tewa, Tiwa, and Towa, which are related members of the Tanoan family; Keresan, a language family with no demonstrated relatives; Zuni, also without known relatives; and Hopi, a Uto-Aztecan language. Each community was politically autonomous. Pueblo political life rested on religious societies, clan and kinship networks, and community councils, and no single authority governed the Pueblo world as a whole.

Spanish colonization of this region began with Juan de Oñate's expedition of 1598 and the establishment of a province called New Mexico, whose capital was moved to Santa Fe in the early seventeenth century. Chapter 42 examined the earlier Spanish entradas and the northern frontier of New Spain. Chapter 43 traced the institutions of colonial rule: the encomienda, which assigned Pueblo tribute to Spanish holders; labor drafts for church construction and household service; the mission system administered by the Franciscan order; and campaigns against Pueblo ceremonial practices. Chapter 43 also showed that the colonial state was internally divided, with the governors and the Franciscans frequently quarreling over jurisdiction and over who could command Pueblo labor. This chapter takes that institutional history as established and returns to it only where it bears directly on the causes of the revolt.

By 1680 the Spanish colonial population of New Mexico was small. Published estimates vary and generally place the population in the low thousands, roughly 2,000 to 4,000 people depending on the source and who was counted, a population that included Spanish-born and New Spain-born settlers, people of mixed Spanish, Indigenous, and African ancestry, and Indigenous and mixed-ancestry servants and auxiliaries brought from central Mexico. Pueblo populations, by contrast, numbered in the range of 17,000 or more, though estimates vary and reflect the heavy losses from epidemic disease, famine, and warfare examined in Chapter 40. The imbalance in numbers did not translate into Pueblo military dominance in earlier decades, because Spanish soldiers possessed firearms, armor, and horses, and because Spanish authorities could exploit divisions among Pueblo communities. But it meant that a coordinated Pueblo effort, if one could be organized, would face a thinly dispersed and poorly supplied colonial population.

That population was dispersed. Spanish settlers lived on ranches and farming estates, called estancias, along the Río Grande and its tributaries, and in the villa of Santa Fe, while Franciscan friars lived in mission compounds attached to individual pueblos, often without a military escort. This pattern depended on Pueblo acquiescence and made the colony highly vulnerable to a sudden, simultaneous attack. The supply line connecting New Mexico to the rest of New Spain ran along the Camino Real, a route of many hundreds of miles to Mexico City that supply wagons traveled only at intervals of years. When the colony was in crisis, relief was far away.

The decade before 1680 brought several overlapping pressures that Section 44.4 examines in detail: severe drought and hunger, epidemic disease, intensified raiding by Apache and other neighboring peoples, and a new Spanish campaign against Pueblo ceremonial leaders. Earlier Pueblo conspiracies, including a plot reported around 1650 and other plans uncovered in the following decades, had shown that organized resistance was possible and that it could be discovered and punished. The task of the leaders of 1680 was therefore not simply to resent Spanish rule but to avoid the mistakes of earlier efforts.

Section 44.1

The Pueblo Revolt as a Historical Problem

Few events in North American history have been described with so many different words. Spanish officials of the seventeenth century called it a rebellion, an uprising, an apostasy, and a treason against the Crown and the Church. Modern Pueblo communities and many scholars often call it a revolt, a revolution, or a war of liberation. The vocabulary is not merely a matter of taste. A rebellion presupposes a legitimate authority against which subjects rise. A revolution suggests a transformation of the political and social order. A war of liberation presupposes that the colonial regime was an illegitimate imposition. Each term carries an argument about what the events meant, and the argument cannot be settled by vocabulary alone.

This textbook uses "the Pueblo Revolt" as the conventional name of the event while treating it, for the reasons developed below, as a coordinated Indigenous political and religious revolution. That interpretation is not universal, and Section 44.32 presents the principal alternatives. What can be said at the outset is that the uprising was not a spontaneous riot, that it succeeded in expelling an established colonial government, and that its effects reached well beyond the battles of August 1680.

Historical Vocabulary
Revolt, Rebellion, Revolution, and Sovereignty

Revolt is a broad term for a violent collective act of resistance against an authority. It does not by itself imply success, planning, or lasting change, and it is the conventional label for this event in English.

Rebellion describes a revolt by people considered subjects against the authority to which they owe allegiance. Spanish officials used the term because they regarded the Pueblo peoples as vassals of the Crown. Using it without qualification adopts the colonizers' point of view. The Pueblo peoples had not consented to Spanish rule in a form that Pueblo political thought would have recognized as binding allegiance, and from a Pueblo perspective the colonial relationship was imposed by force.

Revolution describes a fundamental restructuring of political and social order, usually carried out through organized collective action. Applied to 1680, the term reflects the scale of the transformation: removal of a government, reorganization of religious life, and reconstitution of communities.

Sovereignty refers to inherent political authority, as defined in Chapter 1. In this chapter, the term matters because the revolt restored Pueblo self-government as a practical matter, even though Pueblo communities had always been politically autonomous in their own understanding.

Potential misunderstanding: "Revolution" in the modern sense often implies a single centralized movement with a unified ideology. The Pueblo revolt was a coalition of independent communities, and its leaders did not govern a unified state. The term is used here in its looser sense of fundamental political change, not as a claim that the Pueblo peoples became a nation-state.

The Sources and Their Limits

The documentary record of the revolt is dominated by Spanish sources. Governor Otermín wrote reports and letters during and after the uprising. Spanish officials took testimony from Pueblo prisoners, notably during the expedition of 1681 to 1682, and from colonists who had escaped. Later, Diego de Vargas kept daily journals during his campaigns that survive in detail and that record his meetings with Pueblo leaders. English translations of many of these documents were published in the twentieth century, and they remain the foundation of the narrative history.

These sources are valuable but carry particular risks. Otermín wrote partly to explain his conduct to superiors who might hold him responsible for losing the province. Interrogations were conducted by victorious or aggrieved Spanish officers, with Pueblo witnesses speaking through interpreters and sometimes under threat. The questions asked reveal what Spanish officials already believed or wished to establish, such as whether the revolt was a religious conspiracy, whether specific leaders were to blame, and whether Pueblo people could be induced to return to obedience. Pueblo witnesses had their own reasons to shape testimony: to protect their communities, to deflect blame, to flatter or mislead the interrogators, or to tell Spanish officers what they wished to hear.

Pueblo historical traditions offer a different vantage. Many Pueblo communities preserve narratives about the revolt, and Pueblo scholars and writers have published accounts that emphasize the movement's religious meaning and its role in preserving Pueblo ways of life. These traditions are shared according to community protocols. Certain religious knowledge is not intended for outsiders, and scholars working with Pueblo communities must respect those boundaries. For that reason the absence of a detail from published sources should not be read as evidence that Pueblo communities do not know it.

Archaeology provides a third source of evidence whose importance has grown. Excavations at revolt-era settlements have revealed how Pueblo people built and arranged communities during the independence years, a subject on which Spanish documents are almost silent because Spanish officials were absent. Modern scholarship has drawn on all three bodies of evidence. Andrew Knaut's study of the revolt emphasized the colonial conditions and the Spanish record. Matthew Liebmann's archaeological research on the revolt-era villages, discussed in Section 44.20, emphasizes the reorganization of Pueblo communities during independence. Michael Wilcox has argued that older scholarship, including the work of archaeologists, tended to assume the dominance of Spanish conquest and to underappreciate Pueblo continuity and agency. Together these works show how much the story depends on which sources are privileged.

Section 44.2

Colonial Domination and the Accumulation of Grievances

Chapter 43 described the institutions through which Spain governed the Pueblo peoples. The question here is narrower: how did those institutions create conditions in which an uprising became thinkable, and why were those conditions not sufficient by themselves to produce one?

The material burdens were considerable. Under the encomienda, Pueblo communities owed tribute, typically in woven cotton mantas, maize, and other goods, to encomenderos who held the grants. Pueblo households also supplied labor under the repartimiento system for Spanish households, for construction, and for the mission compounds. Franciscan friars drew on Pueblo labor to build and maintain churches, to tend mission fields and herds, and to serve in friary households. The resulting demands competed with the labor Pueblo families needed for their own agriculture, in a region where harvests were uncertain even in good years. In bad years the tribute exacted from households that had little surplus could be devastating.

The religious burden was in some respects heavier. Franciscan policy required conversion, baptism, church attendance, and the abandonment of Pueblo ceremonial life, which friars characterized as idolatry. Pueblo religious practice was not a separable private belief. It was bound up with agriculture, rainfall, community governance, and the social order, and ceremonial societies played political and economic roles as well as spiritual ones. Spanish campaigns against kiva ceremonies, masks, and ritual objects therefore struck at the institutions through which Pueblo communities understood and sustained themselves. Friars periodically conducted raids on kivas and confiscated or destroyed ceremonial items, and the colonial government at times punished those accused of continuing the old religion.

Political interference compounded these pressures. The Spanish system required Pueblo communities to elect or appoint officers on a Spanish model, including governors and other officials who served as intermediaries with colonial authorities. This arrangement gave Spanish officials a point of leverage and gave some Pueblo individuals access to Spanish institutions. But it also created tension within Pueblo communities between officeholders accountable to Spanish officials and religious leaders who held authority within the community's own system. Rivalry between governors and Franciscans, described in Chapter 43, sometimes opened space for Pueblo communities to play one against the other, but it also meant that Pueblo people were caught in the quarrels of their rulers and sometimes punished for them.

Yet grievance alone does not explain the revolt. Pueblo communities had endured these burdens for decades, and earlier efforts to organize resistance had failed. Historians therefore look for the additional conditions that transformed discontent into coordinated action: an intensified crisis of legitimacy, specific events that produced leaders and martyrs, and a means of organizing across communities without detection. The sections that follow address those conditions in turn. This approach also guards against two common errors. One treats the revolt as inevitable, as though Spanish exploitation mechanically produced a rising. The other treats the revolt as the work of a single charismatic figure who stirred up an otherwise passive population. The evidence supports neither view.

It is also worth remembering that Pueblo communities were not uniformly hostile to everything Spanish. Pueblo people had adopted Spanish livestock, crops such as wheat, and iron tools, and some individuals served in colonial offices or as auxiliaries in Spanish campaigns against Apache raiders. Some became sincere or strategic Christians. Others practiced Christian and Pueblo rituals side by side. The coalition of 1680 had to be built among people whose experiences of colonial rule were varied, and the leaders who built it did so by emphasizing the shared costs of Spanish domination while accommodating the particular interests of individual communities.

Section 44.3

The Religious Crisis and the Arrests of 1675

In 1675, Governor Juan Francisco Treviño ordered a campaign against Pueblo religious leaders that would become a pivotal episode in the history of the revolt. The immediate background was a charge of witchcraft. Franciscan friars and Spanish officials believed that Pueblo ritual specialists had used sorcery to harm Spanish people, including at least one friar who had fallen ill and was thought to have been bewitched. In Spanish legal and religious thought, sorcery was both a crime and a manifestation of idolatry, and Treviño's government pursued the accused with unusual vigor.

According to Spanish accounts, Treviño's officers arrested dozens of Pueblo religious leaders, with the number usually given as 47. They were taken to Santa Fe and tried. Four were condemned to death. Most accounts report that three were hanged and that a fourth died by suicide in custody, and the remaining prisoners were publicly whipped and imprisoned. Among those punished was a man from Ohkay Owingeh known to the Spanish as Popé, whose name in Tewa is rendered Po'pay. Details of the trials, of the identities of individual prisoners, and of the exact charges remain partly uncertain, because the records survive in fragmentary or later copies, and they reflect a Spanish legal process rather than any Pueblo account.

What happened next is among the clearest indications of how seriously the arrests were regarded by Pueblo communities. Spanish sources report that a large group of armed Pueblo men from the northern pueblos, especially Tewa communities, converged on Santa Fe and demanded the release of the prisoners. Treviño, facing a hostile gathering and with limited soldiers at his disposal, released them. The episode shows that Pueblo communities could act collectively across pueblo boundaries when a core interest was threatened, and that Spanish military capacity was thin enough that a show of force could reverse a governor's decision. It also shows that Spanish officials knew, from this point on, that a coordinated Pueblo response was possible.

The consequences extended beyond the immediate confrontation. The arrested men were religious leaders, and their punishment attacked institutions that carried political as well as spiritual authority. In Pueblo communities, the leaders of ceremonial societies helped decide matters of agriculture, communal labor, and relations with outsiders. Humiliating or killing them was, in effect, an assault on part of the community's government. It also transformed individuals into leaders with a personal stake in resisting Spanish power. Po'pay, according to Spanish testimony, withdrew from Ohkay Owingeh after his release and settled at Taos, a northern pueblo with a strong tradition of independence from Spanish authority, where he began the work of organizing that became the revolt.

The 1675 arrests also reveal how limited the religious conquest had been. After roughly eight decades of missionary effort, Pueblo religious specialists were still practicing, and Spanish officials still regarded them as dangerous. Some friars interpreted this as evidence of Pueblo duplicity. A more probable reading, and one supported by Pueblo accounts and by archaeological evidence from kivas, is that Pueblo people had never abandoned their ceremonial life and had accommodated Catholic practice without surrendering their own. The revolt of 1680 was thus not a sudden return to older religion, as Spanish writers sometimes suggested, but an effort to remove the colonial apparatus that threatened an existing and ongoing tradition.

Section 44.4

Drought, Hunger, Raiding, and the Collapse of Colonial Legitimacy

The religious crisis of 1675 unfolded against a background of environmental and military stress. The late 1660s and 1670s brought some of the worst conditions in the colony's history. Tree-ring evidence indicates severe drought in these decades, consistent with Spanish reports of crop failure and famine. In a region where agriculture depended on limited rainfall and runoff, a succession of dry years reduced harvests, drove up the cost of tribute in human terms, and left households with little reserve. Spanish documents record deaths from hunger among Pueblo people, and some sources describe people abandoning communities in search of food.

Epidemic disease compounded hunger. Chapter 40 examined the demographic catastrophe that accompanied European colonization across the continent. In New Mexico, epidemics recurred across the seventeenth century, and malnourished populations were more vulnerable to illness. The combined effect of hunger and disease was a decline in Pueblo population that deprived communities of labor and weakened their ability to meet colonial demands. Several communities in the Salinas district southeast of the Río Grande, including pueblos with large mission churches, were abandoned in the 1670s, with their inhabitants dispersing to the Río Grande and other regions. The reasons were multiple, including drought, disease, and pressure from raiders, and historians continue to debate the relative weight of each.

Raiding by Apache peoples intensified as drought pressed on the resources of mobile peoples as well. Apache groups had long traded and sometimes fought with Pueblo communities, and the relationship was not uniformly hostile. But in the 1660s and 1670s, raids on pueblos and Spanish settlements increased. A raid in 1672 at Hawikuh, among the Zuni, killed a Franciscan friar and was followed by the abandonment of that community. Spanish officials were unable to protect either Pueblo communities or Spanish settlers. For Pueblo people, this was a double burden: they were expected to supply tribute and labor to a government that was failing to provide the security it claimed as part of its legitimacy.

This failure was not only practical but ideological. Spanish colonial rule rested partly on a claim that the Catholic faith brought spiritual power and material blessings. A prolonged drought, disease, and military insecurity called that claim into question. Pueblo religious leaders, who held that ritual practice sustained rainfall and harvest, had an explanation for the crisis: the suppression of ceremonies had angered the powers on which the community's welfare depended. This interpretation, reported in Spanish sources and consistent with Pueblo religious thought, gave the leaders of resistance a compelling argument. The restoration of proper ceremonial life was not merely a cultural preference but a condition of communal survival.

At the same time, the same pressures made revolt dangerous. Communities weakened by hunger and disease had fewer resources to sustain a war. The decision to rise required leaders to judge that the costs of continued submission exceeded the risks of resistance, and that judgment could not be taken for granted. The conditions of 1680 were, in short, enabling rather than determining. They lowered the credibility of the colonial regime and strengthened the position of those who argued for resistance, but a revolt still required deliberate organization.

Historical Debate

Why Did the Revolt Succeed in 1680?

The question: Earlier Pueblo conspiracies had been discovered and suppressed. Why did the effort of 1680 succeed, at least in expelling Spanish authority? Historians generally agree that no single cause explains the outcome and differ over which factors deserve the greatest weight.

Interpretation A — Crisis and legitimacy: Some scholars emphasize the convergence of drought, famine, disease, raiding, and the religious repression of 1675. In this view, the colonial government had lost the capacity to command or protect, and Pueblo communities found a shared interest in removing it.
Interpretation B — Leadership and organization: Others stress the skill of the organizers: the use of runners, the building of a coalition among independent communities, and the willingness to accelerate the timetable when the plot was discovered. In this view, the revolt succeeded because it was well designed.
Interpretation C — Spanish vulnerability: A third approach points to the dispersal of the Spanish population, the small number of soldiers, the isolation from reinforcements, and the missionaries' reliance on Pueblo goodwill. In this view, a simultaneous strike could not be met.
Interpretation D — Religious and cultural cohesion: Others emphasize the shared religious framework that gave participants a common purpose. The restoration of ceremonial life provided a language and an organization that bridged differences among pueblos.

Evidence: Spanish testimony supports the importance of the 1675 events and the use of runners. Environmental evidence, including tree rings, supports the picture of prolonged drought. Spanish population estimates support the view of vulnerability. Evidence for religious cohesion is mostly from Spanish testimony and later Pueblo tradition, which makes it harder to assess independently.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement is mainly one of interpretation and emphasis rather than of fact. The interpretations are not mutually exclusive, and most historians combine them.

Why it matters: Emphasizing crisis tends to treat the revolt as a response to external pressure. Emphasizing leadership and religion tends to present it as an act of political design. How scholars weigh these elements affects how they understand Pueblo agency.

Division II Po'pay, Pueblo Leadership, and the Organization of Revolt Sections 44.5–44.10 · Leadership, diplomacy, and revolutionary preparation
Section 44.5

Po'pay of Ohkay Owingeh and the Emergence of Revolutionary Leadership

No individual is more closely associated with the revolt than Po'pay, yet the reliable evidence about him is slight. Spanish sources spell his name Popé. The Tewa-language form, Po'pay, is the one now preferred by Pueblo communities and used in this textbook. He came from Ohkay Owingeh, the pueblo the Spanish called San Juan de los Caballeros, the first capital of the colony under Oñate. Spanish testimony taken after 1680 identifies him as a religious leader, and his punishment in 1675 among men accused of sorcery supports that identification. After his release he lived at Taos, according to those same sources, and it was from Taos, and from the circle of leaders around him, that the organization of the uprising was reportedly directed.

Almost everything else about his life is uncertain. His birth date, his age in 1680, his family connections, and the details of his religious training are not securely documented. Spanish testimony offers vivid but unverifiable claims. The most elaborate source, the testimony of Pedro Naranjo taken in December 1681 and examined in Section 44.19, says that Po'pay claimed to receive instruction from spirit beings in a kiva at Taos. Whether Po'pay said this, whether Naranjo reported it accurately, and what such a claim would have meant within Pueblo religious thought are all questions on which scholars exercise caution. The same testimony and other Spanish sources describe Po'pay after 1680 as an authoritarian ruler who imposed his will by threat. That portrait may contain truth, but it also served Spanish purposes: it supported the view that the revolt was the work of a tyrant who had coerced otherwise peaceful communities.

Historians reasoning from the evidence therefore distinguish three layers. The first is what can be established with reasonable confidence: Po'pay was a Tewa religious leader from Ohkay Owingeh, punished in 1675, who lived at Taos and was identified by multiple Spanish sources as a leading organizer of the revolt. The second layer consists of claims that are plausible but depend on Spanish testimony, including the claims about his religious visions and his later authority. The third consists of embellishments that arose in later narratives, both Spanish and modern, which have sometimes portrayed him as either a heroic founder or a despot. Pueblo traditions, and the work of Pueblo authors, emphasize his role as a defender of Pueblo religion and community, and that perspective is a necessary corrective to the Spanish portrait.

It is also important to place Po'pay in context. Pueblo political practice did not center on individual rulers. Authority was distributed among religious societies, clan leaders, and community officers, and a movement requiring the agreement of many communities could not have depended on one man's command. Po'pay's role is best understood as that of a catalyst and coordinator, someone with the standing, the resources, and the personal motivation to bring other leaders together. The success of the revolt depended on many other individuals whose names are mostly lost or survive only in fragments of Spanish testimony. To attribute the revolt to Po'pay alone would repeat a common error of narratives that reduce complex movements to the personalities of their most famous figures.

Section 44.6

The Pueblo Coalition: Leaders, Communities, and Alliances

A revolt across many independent communities required leaders in each of them. Spanish sources name several individuals alongside Po'pay. Luis Tupatú is identified with Picuris, a northern Tiwa community. Alonso Catiti is identified with Santo Domingo, a Keresan pueblo on the Río Grande that held considerable influence in the central region. A Taos associate appears in later accounts as Jaca and in Pedro Naranjo's testimony as El Saca; the identification and spellings require care. Domingo Naranjo, associated with Santa Clara, also participated in the movement, but genealogical scholarship identifies him as of mixed African and Indigenous ancestry, not necessarily a Pueblo person by descent. Other individuals appear in Spanish records as war captains, messengers, or organizers in their respective communities. Some participants had Spanish names or titles and experience as officers in the colonial system, a point of considerable importance.

The prominence of men who held colonial offices shows how the revolt drew on existing institutions as well as on opposition to them. Pueblo governors and war captains, who held their positions within the colonial system, also had experience in communicating with Spanish officials, in mobilizing community labor, and in organizing defense against raiders. Some of these skills were readily converted to the purposes of the revolt. Likewise, individuals who understood Spanish language and practice could interpret Spanish plans and anticipate Spanish responses. Their knowledge of the colonial system was an asset in dismantling it.

Leadership operated at several levels. At the level of the individual pueblo, religious leaders, clan elders, and officeholders had to agree to participate and to ensure that the plan was kept secret from the friar and any Spanish officials in residence. At the regional level, leaders in neighboring communities had to coordinate. At the broadest level, the movement required a way to agree on timing and on common objectives. Spanish sources suggest that Taos, with its position at the northern edge of the Pueblo world and its history of independence, served as a gathering point, and that the Tewa pueblos, with their numbers and their proximity to Santa Fe, were central to the planned attack on the capital. The central and western communities were linked through trade and ceremonial relationships.

Existing relationships among communities provided the infrastructure. Pueblo peoples had long traded goods, including maize, cotton textiles, salt, turquoise, pottery, and hides, across community and language lines. They intermarried, attended each other's ceremonies, and joined in common defense against raiders. The Spanish colonial period had not eliminated these links. Archaeological and ethnographic evidence indicates that the connections persisted in forms that Spanish officials did not always notice. This network allowed information to travel and allowed leaders to find one another, and it explains how a movement could be organized without leaving much trace in colonial records before the moment of discovery.

The coalition also included relationships with non-Pueblo peoples, although the evidence is limited and the interpretation contested. Spanish sources report that Apache groups were involved in some way in the revolt, in the sense of either cooperating or taking advantage of the disorder. Relationships between Pueblo people and Apache groups varied by community and period, and generalizations require care. Chapter 51 examines Apache histories in detail. What can be said here is that the revolt took place in a regional environment of interacting peoples, and that the Pueblo leaders had to take neighboring groups into account as potential allies, rivals, or threats.

Section 44.7

Building Unity across Pueblo Nations

It would be a mistake to speak of the Pueblo peoples as a single nation in 1680. The Spanish term "Pueblo" simply means "town" or "village," and it was applied to a collection of communities that were politically independent, spoke different languages, and had distinct histories. Within the Tanoan group, Tewa, Tiwa, and Towa are languages as different from one another as English is from Dutch or perhaps more so. Keresan, Zuni, and Hopi are unrelated to these and to each other. The communities had varied traditions of governance and ceremonial organization, and in the past they had at times been rivals or enemies. The achievement of 1680 was to find common ground among them for a specific, limited purpose, and not to create a new political unity.

Participation was not uniform. The northern Tewa and Tiwa communities, along with Taos and Picuris, appear to have been among the most committed and the earliest to act. Keresan communities of the central region, the Jemez and Pecos peoples, and the Tanos of the Galisteo Basin joined in significant numbers, according to Spanish accounts. The western pueblos of Zuni, Acoma, and the Hopi towns participated in the uprising, and their involvement is treated separately in Section 44.14. Among the southern Río Grande communities, including the southern Tiwa of Isleta and the Piro-speaking pueblos farther south, the pattern was more varied. Some of their members appear to have joined the revolt, while others remained with, or withdrew with, the Spanish, a subject developed in Section 44.16.

Several factors help explain the variation. Geography mattered: communities closest to the dense Spanish settlements and mission compounds of the middle Río Grande were under the most direct supervision, while the northern and western communities, farther from colonial administrative centers, had somewhat more room to organize. Local histories mattered as well. Some pueblos had experienced especially harsh repression, such as the punishment of religious leaders, while others had more cooperative relationships with particular friars or officials. Factional divisions within communities mattered, because within any given pueblo some individuals may have favored resistance and others accommodation or avoidance. Relations with Apache neighbors mattered too, since communities facing the most severe raiding had different interests than those less exposed.

Unity was therefore constructed, not given. The leaders of the movement had to persuade or pressure communities to join, to keep silent, and to act on a common schedule. Spanish testimony indicates that persuasion was not always gentle. Reports describe individuals who refused or threatened to inform being killed, and Spanish authors made much of this coercion to portray the revolt as a conspiracy imposed on unwilling communities. Pueblo traditions and later historians emphasize rather the shared interest and the collective decision. The truth probably includes both elements. A plot involving dozens of communities and many hundreds of participants could not succeed if a few individuals informed, so secrecy was enforced, sometimes violently, while the underlying support for resistance was widespread enough to make the plot possible.

The differences among communities also help explain what happened after the revolt. Because the movement was a coalition of independent communities united against a common enemy, it faced predictable difficulties when that enemy departed. The leaders could not simply convert wartime authority into a durable government over all Pueblo communities, and the pueblos returned to managing their own affairs. Section 44.21 examines the limits of revolutionary unity in this period.

Section 44.8

Runners, Knotted Cords, and Revolutionary Communication

The most striking feature of the plot, from the perspective of organization, is that dozens of communities agreed to act at about the same time. That required a way to transmit instructions and set a date across a vast region without relying on any medium the Spanish could intercept or understand. Spanish sources describe the method. Messengers, often described as runners, traveled between pueblos carrying cords of fiber with knots tied in them. The number of knots indicated the number of days until the appointed day, and each community was to untie one knot each day. When the last knot was undone, the uprising would begin.

The best-known account of this practice comes from the testimony of Pedro Naranjo, taken in 1681, who described the cord and its use. Other Spanish sources, including statements from other prisoners and from officials reconstructing the plot, mention messengers, signals, and the transmission of information between communities. No knotted cord from 1680 is known to survive, and the Spanish accounts that describe them were written afterward, in most cases to reconstruct what had occurred. Historians therefore treat the cord as a plausible and well-attested method while recognizing that the details rest on a single dominant source. Knotted cords and other mnemonic devices were used elsewhere in the Americas, and counting devices of this kind are consistent with Pueblo practices of marking time through ceremonial calendars and observation of the sun, though Pueblo traditions may preserve their own accounts of how the date was communicated.

The organizational problems the system solved were considerable. Runners had to cover long distances, between communities separated by mountains, rivers, and desert, within a time that preserved the schedule. They had to evade Spanish patrols, avoid arousing suspicion among friars and officials, and be trusted by the leaders in the communities they visited. The message had to be understood the same way everywhere, which the physical counting device helped ensure. And the system had to maintain secrecy in communities where informants might be present. Using a counting device with no written text had the advantage that if a runner were captured, the knots would mean little to a Spanish interrogator who did not know the context.

Pueblo traditions regarding running deserve mention. Long-distance running had a long history in Pueblo communities, in ceremonial contexts and in communication. The use of runners in 1680 drew on this existing tradition. The revolt was commemorated in later centuries through relay runs, including events that recalled the runners of 1680, a form of historical memory that reflects the enduring significance of this feature of the revolt. These commemorations are modern and should not be confused with historical evidence, but they show how the community has remembered the event.

The communications system, finally, shows that the revolt cannot be explained as a simple spontaneous outbreak. It was a project that took planning, time, and trust. Months, at least, of quiet preparation preceded the day of the uprising, during which leaders traveled, argued, and persuaded. The fact that Spanish officials learned of the plot only at the last minute indicates how effective the secrecy was, and it indicates how little the colonial government understood of the communities it governed.

Section 44.9

Discovery, Arrests, and the Acceleration of the Uprising

Spanish sources report that the uprising was originally planned for August 13, 1680. On the eve of the revolt, however, the Spanish learned of the plot. The details of how they learned differ among accounts. Spanish reports describe warnings from Pueblo informants, including individuals who held office in communities near Santa Fe and who were evidently unwilling to take part, and the capture or interrogation of messengers. Some accounts say the warning reached Governor Otermín on about August 9, which led him to order the arrest of suspected participants and to send messages to officials and missionaries in outlying districts.

The precise sequence matters less than the response of the Pueblo leaders. According to the Spanish sources, when the leaders learned that the plot had been discovered, they decided to begin the revolt immediately rather than wait for the planned date. The uprising started on August 10. This decision shows the organizers' judgment. Postponing might have allowed the Spanish to arrest the leaders, seize the initiative, and reinforce vulnerable settlements. Advancing the date meant that some communities, which had been counting down to August 13, had to be notified at once and had to act with little preparation. Spanish accounts suggest the revolt began almost simultaneously in many places, which implies that the messenger system was able to deliver the revised timing quickly.

The acceleration also reveals the internal discipline of the movement. Spanish sources report that the leaders enforced secrecy by punishing informants. Pueblo officials who were reported to have warned the Spanish were, according to testimony, killed. Among them, reports say, was a governor of San Juan who was a relative of Po'pay by marriage, a point Spanish writers emphasized to show the ruthlessness of the movement. As with other claims in this testimony, the story serves the Spanish narrative of a tyrannical leadership, though it is also consistent with the severe risks that discovery posed to the organizers and with the general pattern of enforcing secrecy in clandestine movements.

Governor Otermín's response to the warning was inadequate to the scale of the crisis. He had limited troops and limited ability to communicate with the scattered Spanish population. Some alerts reached estancias and missions in time, and some did not. Spanish officials attempted to gather settlers in more defensible locations, particularly Santa Fe, and sent messengers to friars and local officers. But many colonists and almost all missionaries in the outlying pueblos were isolated. The Pueblo leaders' decision to advance the date thus exploited the gap between the colonial government's information and its capacity to act on it.

This episode also shows how contingency shaped the outcome. Had the plot been discovered earlier, or had Otermín been able to act on the warning more decisively, the history of New Mexico might have followed a different course, as with earlier conspiracies that were suppressed. The revolt succeeded in part because its leaders adapted quickly to an unexpected disruption. Historians should therefore resist the temptation to see 1680 as inevitable. A well-planned movement could still have failed, and the decisions made on August 9 and 10 were consequential.

Section 44.10

The Revolutionary Program: Religion, Sovereignty, and Expulsion

What did the leaders of the revolt want? Spanish sources frame the answer in terms that reflect Spanish concerns: apostasy from Christianity, the murder of priests and settlers, and the rejection of royal authority. Pueblo interpretations, and the testimony the Spanish themselves gathered, point to a program that can be summarized as the removal of Spanish control and the restoration of Pueblo ceremonial and political autonomy.

The religious dimension was central. Testimony from Pedro Naranjo and others reports that the leaders ordered the destruction of Christian objects such as crosses, images, and church furnishings, the abandonment of Christian marriage and the Spanish language, and the return to Pueblo religious practice, including the rebuilding of kivas. Section 44.19 examines this testimony critically. For present purposes, the point is that Spanish accounts convey a program of religious restoration, and the physical evidence of destroyed or damaged missions corroborates that churches were targeted in the revolt. At the same time, the evidence does not support the claim that all Spanish elements were rejected. Pueblo communities kept livestock, wheat, iron tools, and other goods introduced by the Spanish, as later archaeological evidence shows.

The political dimension was equally important. The expulsion of Spanish officials and soldiers from the province was a clear objective, and it was pursued with the siege of Santa Fe and the pursuit of retreating colonists. The end of tribute and forced labor followed directly. Beyond these immediate aims, the evidence for a unified political program is limited. The revolt did not produce a new constitution, a common government, or a single leader with recognized authority over all communities. Its political achievement was negative, in the sense of removing a colonial regime, and positive in the sense of restoring the autonomy of individual communities.

Historians distinguish reported directives from demonstrated policies. Many of the directives attributed to Po'pay come from Spanish testimony taken from people who had reasons to emphasize the radical and coercive character of the movement. Some of those claims, such as destroying churches and killing missionaries, are independently supported by evidence. Others, such as an alleged order to divorce Christian spouses or to bathe in rivers to remove baptism, appear only in testimony and are plausible expressions of a desire to reverse conversion, but their enforcement and extent cannot be independently established. Careful historians treat such claims as evidence of what Spanish interrogators learned and what some Pueblo participants said, rather than as proof of a comprehensive policy.

The revolutionary program was thus a combination of immediate military aims and longer-term hopes for restoring a way of life. It did not anticipate every problem. The leaders did not necessarily know how they would deal with a Spanish counterattack, how they would govern across communities after the colonists left, or how they would manage the economic transition. As Divisions IV and V show, those problems proved significant. But the clarity of the core aims, expulsion and restoration, helps explain the coherence of the movement in its initial phase.

Division III The Revolt and the Expulsion of Spanish Rule Sections 44.11–44.17 · August 1680 and its immediate aftermath
Section 44.11

August 10, 1680: The Uprising Begins

On the morning of August 10, 1680, Pueblo communities across the northern Río Grande began coordinated attacks. The targets were the instruments of colonial rule: the mission compounds where friars lived, the estancias where Spanish settlers farmed and raised livestock, and individuals who held colonial offices. Spanish sources report that in the first hours of the revolt several friars and numerous settlers were killed in communities across the northern district. Among the earliest reported deaths were those of friars at Tesuque and at other pueblos near Santa Fe, and settlers on estancias in the surrounding valleys.

The timing was crucial. The colony was organized around dispersed settlement. Families lived at distance from one another, and friars were typically alone or with one or two companions in communities that vastly outnumbered them. An attack launched simultaneously across the district meant that no settlement could come to the aid of another, and that news of one attack would spread only as fast as survivors or messengers could travel. Surprise, in this context, was a decisive military advantage. By the time warning reached a settlement, it might already be under assault.

The revolt's violence was directed at specific categories of people, though not with perfect discrimination. Spanish sources describe the killing of friars, soldiers, and male settlers, and report that women and children were in some cases taken captive or killed, in other cases spared. The killing of friars was a direct attack on the institution of the mission and carried religious meaning, in addition to its military function. The scale of casualties, detailed in Section 44.17, was large in proportion to the small Spanish population. Spanish accounts understandably emphasize atrocity, and it is difficult to establish from them the exact circumstances of individual deaths. But the broad pattern of coordinated lethal attacks on missions and settlements is not in doubt.

Spanish survivors in the outlying districts fled toward Santa Fe, and the movement of refugees was itself a crucial part of the revolt. Some made it through, often in disorder and with few possessions. Others were intercepted. Pueblo forces took horses, livestock, weapons, and other goods, strengthening their own capacity to continue the campaign. Spanish accounts note that Pueblo fighters made use of captured horses and equipment, and they record that some Pueblo warriors had experience with Spanish weapons and tactics from service as auxiliaries.

The first day set the pattern for the rest of the month. Pueblo forces seized the initiative and held it. Spanish officials in Santa Fe received reports of attacks from many directions and could do little to respond beyond preparing defenses. The remaining sections of this division follow the revolt region by region before returning to its climax at Santa Fe.

Section 44.12

The Northern Río Grande: Taos, Picuris, and the Tewa Towns

The northern Río Grande was the heartland of the uprising. Taos and Picuris, the northernmost pueblos, lay at the edge of the colony and had a history of resistance. Taos in particular had been involved in earlier conflicts with Spanish authority, and its position near the mountains and the plains gave it relationships with Apache and other neighbors as well as with other Pueblo communities. Spanish sources associate the leadership of the revolt with Taos, and the attacks on Spanish settlers and missionaries in the Taos valley were among the first and most thorough.

The Tewa pueblos to the south of Taos, on the Río Grande and the Chama, occupied a strategically critical position. They were numerous, they lay between the northern pueblos and Santa Fe, and they were the home of many of the leaders of the movement, including Po'pay. The Tewa communities took part in the attacks on missions and estancias in their districts and in the movement of forces toward the capital. Their experience with Spanish authority had been particularly marked by the 1675 arrests, which had involved many Tewa people, and their motivation was correspondingly intense.

Spanish administration in the north was concentrated in the district known as the Río Arriba. Alcaldes mayores, who were provincial officials with judicial and military responsibilities, supervised the district, and Spanish settlers occupied estancias along the river valleys. The revolt tore this structure apart. Spanish sources describe the killing of alcaldes mayores and the destruction of estancias, with settlers fleeing south in groups, where possible, toward Santa Fe. The survivors' testimony, taken in the following weeks, provides much of what is known about the northern events, and as always it reflects the experiences and perspectives of those who escaped.

One result of the northern attacks was a flow of refugees into Santa Fe, which increased both the number of people the capital had to defend and the burden on its food and water supplies. Another was the loss of communication with the Spanish in the north. Governor Otermín received reports, but they were fragmentary, and he had to decide how to defend Santa Fe without a clear picture of the revolt's scope. The decisions he made, and the pressure of refugees seeking shelter, shaped his strategy in the days that followed.

The northern campaign shows how a movement can combine centralized objectives with local initiative. The leaders agreed on a common goal and timing, but individual communities attacked their own local targets, drawing on knowledge of the terrain and of local Spanish personnel. This approach made the movement difficult to counter. The Spanish could not defeat the revolt by capturing a single leader or seizing a single stronghold, and the loss of control in one district did not depend on events in another.

Section 44.13

The Central Pueblo Region: Keresan, Jemez, and Pecos Communities

The central region lay between the northern pueblos and the southern Río Grande settlements, and its pueblos controlled much of the ground that connected them to Santa Fe. The Keresan-speaking communities of Cochiti, Santo Domingo, San Felipe, Santa Ana, and Zia lay on or near the Río Grande and its tributaries. The Towa-speaking people of Jemez lived to the west in the mountains. Pecos, also Towa-speaking, lay east of the Sangre de Cristo foothills and had long served as a trading center between the Río Grande pueblos and the plains. The Tano pueblos of the Galisteo Basin, south of Santa Fe, completed the picture. Together these communities controlled the roads and river crossings by which Spanish forces and refugees might move.

Spanish sources report that communities of the central region joined the revolt in large numbers. Santo Domingo, the leading Keresan pueblo, was prominent, and Alonso Catiti is associated with that community in Spanish testimony. The pueblos of the region attacked their missions and Spanish residents, and some fighters moved toward Santa Fe to join the siege. Jemez and the Tano communities were also reported to be involved in the assault on the capital. The evidence for the participation of Pecos is more mixed in the Spanish record, and Pecos's later relations with Spanish officials, including cooperation with Vargas in 1692, suggest that its stance may have differed from that of some neighbors at different moments.

The strategic geography of the central region deserves emphasis. A force moving from the Río Grande toward Santa Fe had to pass through terrain the pueblos knew intimately, and the roads that served Spanish settlers and supply trains were vulnerable. Control of river crossings and approaches allowed Pueblo forces to isolate Santa Fe from potential reinforcements in the south and to prevent the escape of Spanish groups from the Río Arriba. The Tano communities, whose pueblos lay on the routes into Santa Fe from the south and east, were especially well placed to participate in the siege.

The central pueblos also illustrate the importance of preexisting relationships. Keresan communities had trade, kinship, and ceremonial ties to neighbors of other languages. Jemez had strong connections with Keresan and Tewa neighbors. These relationships helped cement the coalition and allowed forces from different pueblos to operate together at Santa Fe, though language differences must have complicated command and communication. The Spanish sources do not describe a unified command, and the siege seems to have been conducted by contingents from many communities acting in concert rather than under a single general.

For the Spanish, the loss of the central region meant that the capital was isolated. Their communications to the south were threatened, the pueblos that had formerly furnished provisions and labor now stood against them, and the sense of encirclement intensified. These developments set the stage for the siege.

Section 44.14

The Western Uprising: Zuni, Hopi, and Acoma

The revolt extended far beyond the Río Grande corridor. To the west, the high mesa pueblo of Acoma, the Zuni communities, and the Hopi towns joined the uprising. Their participation is evidence of the reach of the communications network that had been built, and it demonstrates that the coalition included communities with different histories and different relationships with the Spanish.

Acoma had been the site of one of the most severe episodes of Spanish violence in the early colony, when Oñate's forces destroyed the pueblo and punished its people in 1599. That memory, preserved in Acoma tradition, formed part of the background for its participation. Spanish sources report that the community rose in 1680 and killed its resident friar, and that it remained outside Spanish control for the following years. Acoma's mesa-top location offered natural defensive advantages.

The Zuni communities had experienced Spanish presence since the Coronado expedition of 1540, a subject belonging to Chapter 42, and they had endured both mission activity and raiding. In 1672 Hawikuh, a Zuni town, had been attacked by Apache raiders in an event that killed a friar and led to its abandonment. In 1680, Zuni communities took part in the revolt and, according to later sources, withdrew to the defensible mesa of Dowa Yalanne, known to the Spanish as Corn Mountain, where they remained for much of the independence period before returning to the valley town of Halona. The move illustrates how Pueblo communities responded to the threat of Spanish return by selecting defensible locations, a pattern examined archaeologically in Section 44.20.

The Hopi towns, on three mesas in what is now northeastern Arizona, were the farthest from the Río Grande. Spanish missions had been established among the Hopi in the 1620s and 1630s, but Spanish presence was limited, with a handful of friars and little military or settler support. In 1680 the Hopi rose and killed their resident friars, and they destroyed or abandoned the missions. Unlike most other Pueblo communities, the Hopi were not subsequently brought back under Spanish colonial control, and they have remained independent of Spanish administration since 1680, a fact of lasting consequence discussed in Section 44.31. The Hopi experience illustrates that Spanish power was thin at the periphery of the colony and that the revolt succeeded in permanently ending Spanish presence in some regions.

The western uprising had distinct objectives and conditions. Distances made coordination with the Río Grande more difficult, and western leaders had to act on information received by runner. The western communities were less directly threatened by Spanish military response, since Spanish forces after the revolt were concentrated far to the east and south. Their participation also reflected local priorities, including the desire to end tribute and missionary pressure and to restore religious practices, in settings where the Spanish presence had been relatively limited. The western pueblos thus show both the unifying power of the revolt and the variety of local circumstances in which it operated.

Section 44.15

The Siege of Santa Fe and Governor Otermín's Defense

By the middle of August, Santa Fe had become the last refuge of Spanish authority in the north. Refugees from the Río Arriba and from estancias along the nearby valleys had gathered in the villa, where Governor Otermín and his officers concentrated the defense in and around the Casas Reales, the governmental complex that included the governor's residence. The population within the town probably numbered well over a thousand people, including soldiers, settlers, women and children, and Native servants and auxiliaries. Provisions were limited, and the crowding that followed strained both food supplies and morale.

Pueblo forces began to arrive around August 13, and the siege took shape over the following days. Spanish accounts describe growing numbers of Pueblo fighters, largely from the Tewa, Tano, and Keresan communities, occupying the town's outskirts, burning houses and the church, and tightening the circle. Spanish estimates of the besiegers run to a few thousand, but these figures reflect the fear of those inside the walls and should be treated cautiously. What the sources do establish is that the Pueblo force was large enough to threaten the Spanish position and to maintain the siege for about a week.

A revealing episode occurred during the siege. According to Otermín's account, a Tano man named Juan (often called Juan el Tano), who had lived among the Spanish and earlier served as a messenger, came to the Spanish position bearing an ultimatum symbolized by two crosses, one red and one white. He offered Otermín a choice that the crosses symbolized: the red cross meant war, the white cross meant peace and the departure of the Spanish from the province. Spanish sources interpret the offer as an ultimatum, and it suggests that the Pueblo leaders were prepared to allow the Spanish to leave. It shows, too, the use of Christian symbols in Pueblo diplomacy, which complicates any idea that the revolt rejected everything Spanish and Catholic in its form and communication. Otermín rejected the offer.

The siege turned decisively against the Spanish when Pueblo forces cut the town's water supply. Santa Fe depended on a stream, and when that source was interrupted, the thirst of people and animals became acute. Otermín faced a choice between remaining in a position that was becoming untenable and attempting to break out. On or about August 20, he ordered a sortie against the besiegers. Spanish sources report that the attack inflicted heavy losses, with figures on the order of several hundred Pueblo dead, and captured a number of prisoners who were subsequently executed, although Spanish casualty reports are the principal basis for these numbers and may be inflated. The sortie did not break the siege, though it did demonstrate that Spanish forces retained fighting capacity and that the Pueblo force could be hurt.

Facing continued thirst and with the countryside in Pueblo hands, Otermín decided to withdraw. The Spanish evacuated Santa Fe on about August 21, moving south along the Río Grande with their remaining people, animals, and what goods they could carry. The decision abandoned the capital and, effectively, the northern province. Otermín's later reports defended it as the only course that would save the survivors, and it is hard to see what alternatives he had once the water was cut. For students of the revolt, the siege of Santa Fe demonstrates the culmination of the plan: Pueblo forces had isolated and then forced out the seat of colonial government.

Section 44.16

The Southern Río Grande and the Fractured Colonial Retreat

While the north was in revolt, the settlements of the southern district, known to the Spanish as the Río Abajo, experienced the crisis differently. This district lay downriver of Santa Fe and included Spanish estancias and the pueblos of the southern Tiwa, such as Isleta, and the Piro communities farther south. The Spanish lieutenant governor, Alonso García, was in charge of the district. When the revolt began, he gathered settlers from the surrounding area and, according to Spanish reports, assembled them at Isleta.

The events in this region demonstrate that the Pueblo peoples did not behave uniformly. Some southern Tiwa and Piro people appear to have joined in attacks on Spanish settlers and missionaries. Others did not, and some left with the Spanish. Spanish sources describe Pueblo people of Isleta and the Piro communities accompanying the colonists south, and Spanish officials emphasized their loyalty. Historians should consider the possible motives. Some may have been Christians who sincerely valued their relationship with the friars. Some may have feared Spanish reprisal if they stayed or Pueblo reprisal if they were seen as collaborators. Some may have been coerced to move. The evidence does not allow a confident answer for most individuals, and different people probably acted for different reasons.

García, with the Río Abajo settlers and their Pueblo companions, began moving south along the Camino Real. They did not know the fate of Otermín and the northern survivors, and Otermín did not know theirs. Each believed the other might have been destroyed. Reports and messengers eventually established contact, and in early September the two groups joined in the desolate country on the way to El Paso del Norte. The meeting is noteworthy for the trouble Otermín had in locating García, which highlights the isolation and uncertainty that characterized the retreat.

The journey itself was punishing. Thousands of people, some on horseback and many on foot, traveled across arid country with limited provisions. Survivors were often weak from the siege and from the loss of family members. Spanish sources report suffering, hunger, and some deaths during the retreat. Pueblo communities along the route generally did not attack the column in force, and Spanish accounts suggest that the southern pueblos were largely abandoned or in disorder as the refugees passed. Some of the settlements the Spanish had known along the river were destroyed or burned.

The southern retreat also demonstrates how the revolt divided communities. Pueblo people who accompanied the Spanish became part of a displaced population that settled in the El Paso region, where, as Section 44.24 notes, new communities such as Ysleta del Sur and Socorro del Sur were established with Tiwa and Piro refugees. Their descendants remained in that region. The history of these people shows that the revolt's outcomes varied even among Pueblo communities and that unity in 1680 coexisted with division. Any account that treats the Pueblo peoples as identical in action and fate misses the complexity of the event.

Section 44.17

The Spanish Withdrawal and the Victory of August 1680

By early September, Spanish authority in New Mexico had collapsed. The surviving colonists, perhaps around two thousand people when counting Native servants and allies, were on their way to El Paso del Norte, and no Spanish garrison remained in the province. The scale of losses was heavy. Spanish sources and modern summaries report more than 400 Spanish colonists killed, and 21 of the province's Franciscan friars killed, out of a total of about 33 in the colony. These figures derive from Spanish accounts and should be understood as attributed estimates rather than a complete demographic accounting. They do not include Native servants and allies who may have died, and they do not include Pueblo casualties, for which there is no reliable overall figure.

The magnitude of the victory must be assessed carefully. Militarily, the Pueblo coalition had accomplished its principal goal: it had driven the colonial government out and ended the immediate threat of tribute and forced labor. It had done so by combining surprise, coordination, and knowledge of terrain. Politically, the victory restored the autonomy of individual communities and opened the way to religious revitalization. But the victory was also incomplete. The Spanish had not been destroyed, they retained a base at El Paso del Norte, and the Crown regarded the loss of the province as a problem to be remedied. The leaders of the revolt did not necessarily expect the Spanish to return, and they could not be certain they would not.

The deaths of the friars deserve particular attention because they carried symbolic and practical meaning. The mission system depended on the presence of the friars in the pueblos. Their deaths, and the destruction of mission compounds, ended that system in practice. For the Franciscan order, the loss of so many members was a calamity, and the later campaign to return to New Mexico was driven partly by the desire to recover the missions and avenge the dead. For Pueblo communities, the killing of friars removed the most immediate agents of religious pressure and did so in a way that conveyed an unambiguous rejection of missionary authority.

Multiple Perspectives

How Different Groups Experienced the Revolt of 1680

Note: the viewpoints below are analytical reconstructions based on the kinds of evidence discussed in this chapter. They are not quotations from named individuals.

From the perspective of a Pueblo religious and political leader: The revolt was a defense of a way of life that had been under sustained attack, culminating in the punishment of ceremonial leaders in 1675. The restoration of ceremonies was necessary for the welfare of the community, and the departure of Spanish officials was a precondition for it. Leaders also had to weigh the risks of Spanish retaliation and the need to maintain secrecy and unity.
From the perspective of a Franciscan missionary, as reflected in surviving accounts: The uprising was an apostasy and a betrayal by people to whom the friars had devoted decades of work. The deaths of colleagues were martyrdom. Many friars could not conceive that Pueblo communities might have grounds for resisting the mission, and some understood the revolt as evidence of a diabolical influence among those who clung to old religion.
From the perspective of a Spanish colonist household: The revolt meant sudden loss of property, family members, and security. Households on isolated estancias had little warning, and survivors faced a perilous flight and a future in exile. Many colonists blamed the governor or the friars for the crisis, and others blamed the Pueblo communities.
From the perspective of a Pueblo Christian or Native servant who accompanied the Spanish: Choices were constrained by family ties, religious commitment, fear of reprisal, and, in some cases, compulsion. Those who left with the colonists gave up their homes and fields, and their place in the new settlements near El Paso del Norte was uncertain. Their experience shows that the revolt divided communities as well as uniting them.
Cause and Effect

Colonial Crisis and Revolutionary Coordination

Long-term causes

Decades of tribute, forced labor, religious coercion, and political interference under the encomienda and mission systems, combined with declining Pueblo populations and the vulnerability of a dispersed colony.

Short-term causes

Drought and famine in the 1660s and 1670s, epidemic disease, intensified raiding, and the Spanish inability to provide security.

Immediate trigger and context

The 1675 arrests of religious leaders, which produced leaders with personal motives and demonstrated that collective Pueblo action could succeed, followed by five years of organizing and the near-discovery of the plot in August 1680.

Event or transformation

A coordinated uprising beginning on August 10, 1680, followed by the siege of Santa Fe and the Spanish withdrawal.

Immediate effects

Deaths of hundreds of Spanish colonists and most friars, the collapse of the mission system, and the end of tribute and forced labor.

Long-term consequences

Twelve years of Pueblo independence, religious revitalization, and a Spanish return in the 1690s on altered terms.

Historical complexity: No single factor explains the revolt. Grievance existed for decades without producing a successful uprising. The crisis of the 1670s lowered the legitimacy of colonial rule, the 1675 arrests created leaders, existing networks supplied the means, and the discovery of the plot forced a decision that, in this case, helped rather than harmed the movement. A different sequence could have produced a different result.

Section 44.18

The Expulsion of Spanish Institutions and the Restoration of Pueblo Authority

The twelve years between 1680 and 1692 are the least documented and, in some respects, the most important period of the revolt. Spanish officials were absent, and Spanish documents say little about conditions in the province, relying on testimony from the few Pueblo individuals who came to El Paso or were captured during expeditions. The picture that emerges from these documents, from Pueblo traditions, and from archaeology is of communities engaged in reconstruction after a revolution.

The most immediate change was the removal of colonial institutions. Tribute and labor demands ended, since there were no encomenderos or officials to enforce them. The mission system ended in practice with the death or departure of the friars. Pueblo officials who had served as governors under the Spanish system no longer answered to a colonial superior. Spanish accounts from the expeditions of 1681 to 1682 describe churches burned, damaged, or turned to other uses, and Spanish furnishings and religious objects removed or destroyed. Archaeological work at mission sites has documented evidence of burning and dismantling at some churches, though the pattern is not uniform, and some structures were reused or left standing.

Santa Fe itself was transformed. Spanish reports from the 1690s describe the former capital as occupied by Pueblo residents, among them Tano people from the Galisteo Basin and others, who adapted the colonial buildings for their own use and fortified the town. The Casas Reales, which had been the center of Spanish government, became part of a Pueblo settlement. This reoccupation is a striking symbol of the reversal of power, and it also had practical consequences, since it made Santa Fe a stronghold that the Spanish would have to retake by force in 1693.

Historical Vocabulary
Kiva and Estufa

Kiva is a term, borrowed from Hopi, for a ceremonial chamber in a Pueblo community, often (though not always) built partly or wholly below ground. Spanish writers called such chambers estufas, meaning "stoves" or "heated rooms," reflecting their interior warmth.

Historical context: Kivas served as meeting places for religious societies, as sites of ceremony, and as spaces for men's work and instruction. They were central to the religious and political life of the community. Franciscan friars regarded them as centers of idolatry and conducted raids on them, and some Pueblo communities built kivas out of sight or within residential structures during the mission period.

Why it matters: The reconstruction of kivas after 1680 is among the clearest signs of religious revitalization, and the arrangement of kivas in revolt-era villages is important archaeological evidence.

Potential misunderstanding: Kivas are not "churches" in the sense of weekly worship halls, and their functions varied among communities. The word is also used for ancient structures in the Ancestral Pueblo world, discussed in Chapter 18, whose uses may not correspond exactly to those of the seventeenth century.

It is important not to overstate the rejection of everything Spanish. Pueblo communities kept sheep, goats, cattle, and horses, which had become integral to their economies, and they used iron tools, wheat, and other imports. Some Christian practices and symbols continued among individuals and families, and Pueblo diplomatic gestures, such as the crosses sent to Otermín, borrowed Christian forms. The revolution was against Spanish domination and religious coercion, not against every element of Spanish material culture. This selective approach is consistent with the Pueblo pattern, evident long before 1680, of adopting useful elements while maintaining core institutions.

Restoring Pueblo authority also required decisions about governance. Communities reasserted the authority of their own religious and political leaders, and the offices established under the Spanish system, such as governor, may have continued in modified form or been subordinated to older structures. The evidence is thin, and practice undoubtedly varied among communities. What is clear is that the end of colonial supervision restored to each pueblo control over its own labor, land, harvests, and ceremonies, which was the central achievement of the revolt.

Section 44.19

Religious Revitalization and the Reconfiguration of Pueblo Ceremonial Life

Religious revitalization lay at the heart of the revolt's meaning for many participants. With the missionaries gone, Pueblo communities were free to practice ceremonies without fear of raids and punishment. Kivas were reopened or built, masks and other ritual objects were brought out, and ceremonial cycles resumed in public. Communities that had maintained their ceremonies in secret during the mission period could now perform them openly. The resumption of ceremonies was understood by participants as essential to the welfare of the community, including rainfall and harvest, a view that is documented in both Spanish testimony and Pueblo tradition.

The most detailed account of the religious program comes from the testimony of Pedro Naranjo, which describes directives attributed to Po'pay. The testimony includes claims that crosses and images were to be destroyed, that the names of God, Mary, and the saints were not to be spoken, that the Spanish language was to be abandoned, that Christian marriages were to be dissolved, and that people were to bathe with a plant-based soap in order to wash away baptism. Naranjo also said that the dead were to be honored and the old ceremonies restored. Historians read this as a valuable but problematic source, as the feature below discusses.

Interpreting the religious changes requires attention to Pueblo religious thought. The directives against Christian symbols and names may be read as an effort to cleanse communities of the colonial religion and to reestablish a ritual order in which ceremonies could be effective. Washing away baptism has an obvious logic in this framework. At the same time, some Pueblo individuals and families continued to value Christian elements, and the revolution did not produce uniform religious practice. Catholic objects were treated in different ways: some were destroyed, some hidden, some reused. In the long run, as Section 44.31 notes, Pueblo communities developed forms of religious life in which Catholic and Pueblo practices coexisted, a situation Chapter 54 examines more fully.

Spanish accusations that Po'pay compelled people to follow his religious directives by threat deserve a critical reading. Such accusations served the Spanish narrative that the revolt was the work of a tyrant who forced innocent people to renounce their faith. There is evidence of conflict over these matters among Pueblo people, but the existence of conflict does not establish that revitalization was coerced from the outset. Pueblo traditions emphasize the community's choice and the sustaining power of ceremony. The most defensible reading is that revitalization was a collective project, carried out with varying degrees of enthusiasm and disagreement.

Section 44.20

Building New Pueblo Settlements: Patokwa, Boletsakwa, and Kotyiti

Archaeology has transformed knowledge of the independence years. Where Spanish documents are silent, excavations have revealed how Pueblo communities settled and built in the 1680s and early 1690s. The work of Matthew Liebmann and colleagues on revolt-era villages in the Jemez and Cochiti areas of the northern Río Grande region has been especially important. Among the sites studied are Patokwa and Boletsakwa, associated with the Towa-speaking people of Jemez, and Kotyiti, associated mainly with Keresan-speaking people from several communities. These settlements were built during or just after the revolt, in many cases on high mesas and other defensible locations; their later occupation histories differed. Patokwa was established about 1681, while tree-ring evidence places the construction of Boletsakwa around 1683. Both were associated with Jemez/Towa communities. Kotyiti, constructed in the early 1680s in the Cochiti region, brought together Keres-speaking and Tewa-speaking residents. The three villages shared a notable dual-plaza arrangement with a central room block; scholars interpret this as potentially expressing Pueblo social and ceremonial principles and accommodating newcomers, although some details of individual ceremonial structures remain uncertain.

The locations themselves tell a story. Mesa-top and otherwise defensible sites were chosen for visibility and protection. This pattern is consistent with the expectation of a Spanish return and with the danger posed by raiding neighbors. Building new villages in difficult terrain demanded labor, planning, and cooperation, and it entailed leaving established communities, fields, and in some cases the former sites of mission churches. People who might have preferred to stay where they were chose to move, and the settlements show the effort that went into the move.

The layout of the villages offers further evidence. Researchers have documented settlement plans arranged around plazas, with rooms in blocks, and with ceremonial spaces integrated into the plan. At some sites, scholars see signs of deliberate reorganization of community life, including changes in how space was arranged, how households related to public ceremonial areas, and how groups of different origins were accommodated. At Kotyiti in particular, the evidence has been interpreted as indicating the coming together of people from multiple communities, which raises questions about how leadership and cooperation were managed. Interpretations vary, and scholars caution that the dating of occupation and the identity of builders require careful analysis.

Material evidence from the sites also bears on Pueblo selectivity. Pottery, tools, and food remains show how people adapted to the period, and they include evidence of continued use of Spanish-introduced animals and goods alongside Pueblo traditions. This evidence supports the view that independence was not a rejection of all things Spanish, and it fits the documentary indications discussed in Section 44.18. It also suggests that people were making deliberate choices about what to keep, change, or abandon.

Section 44.21

Po'pay's Authority and the Limits of Revolutionary Unity

The unity that made the revolt possible did not automatically survive the Spanish departure. Spanish sources, drawing on testimony from captured Pueblo people and on rumor, portray a Pueblo world in disorder after 1680, with Po'pay acting as a tyrant, rivals contesting his authority, and communities falling into dispute. These portrayals must be handled with caution because they fit the Spanish interest in showing that Pueblo people would welcome the return of Spanish rule. But the broad outlines are plausible, because every revolutionary coalition faces difficulties once the common enemy has gone.

The evidence indicates that Po'pay's authority was real but limited. He had standing as a religious leader and as an organizer, and his directives, as reported by Spanish sources, were influential in some communities. But authority in the Pueblo world depended on consent and on the standing of leaders within their own communities, and no mechanism existed to enforce a regional order. Spanish testimony reports that Po'pay's authority waned, that he was challenged, and that leaders in other communities, including figures such as Luis Tupatú of Picuris and Alonso Catiti of Santo Domingo, exercised significant influence. By the time Vargas arrived in 1692, Spanish sources indicate that Po'pay was no longer living, though the date and circumstances of his death are not securely documented.

Disagreements among Pueblo people reflected real differences of interest. Communities that had suffered less from Spanish rule, or that had stronger ties with particular friars or officials, may have been less committed to maintaining the revolutionary program. Others faced greater threats from Apache raiders or from neighboring communities and had different priorities. There were also divisions over religion: those who retained attachments to Christianity were in tension with those who insisted on its removal. The Spanish record, with its emphasis on discord, probably exaggerates, but it likely reflects real disputes.

The difficulty of maintaining cooperation has to be set against the achievements. The coalition held together long enough to expel the Spanish and to prevent their return for a decade. Communities defended themselves against the expeditions of the 1680s, and cooperation persisted in some regions. The failure to produce a durable pan-Pueblo government reflects the structure of Pueblo political life, in which autonomy was a value, and it should not be read as a failure of the revolution. The Pueblo world remained a constellation of self-governing communities with shifting alliances and shared interests, rather than a single formal federation.

Historians therefore distinguish documented disagreements from Spanish narratives that exaggerated Pueblo disorder. The documented cases include changes in leadership, communities declining to follow certain directives, and local conflicts. The exaggerations include claims that the revolution collapsed into chaos and that Pueblo people longed for Spanish rule. The truth lies in a more complicated and more human picture of people governing themselves under difficult circumstances and disagreeing as self-governing people do.

Section 44.22

Inter-Pueblo Diplomacy, Migration, and Regional Conflict

The independence years were a period of intense regional interaction. Pueblo communities renewed relations among themselves, adjusted to population movements, and managed relationships with neighboring peoples. The evidence for these developments is fragmentary, but it suggests a dynamic and sometimes tense environment rather than either uninterrupted harmony or inevitable collapse.

Movement of people was a hallmark of the period. The construction of new villages brought people from different communities together, as at Kotyiti. Some communities relocated to mesa tops. Zuni people withdrew to Corn Mountain, and some Pueblo people went to live in more remote locations, including areas to the west and northwest where they interacted with Diné communities. Archaeological research on small defensive stone structures in the Dinétah region, the homeland of the Diné in northwestern New Mexico, has identified evidence that Pueblo refugees lived there in the later seventeenth and early eighteenth centuries, particularly after the Spanish return. These refugee communities fostered exchange between Pueblo and Diné peoples, with consequences for Diné cultural history that Chapter 51 and later chapters consider.

Relations with Apache peoples and other neighbors were complicated. Some Pueblo communities allied with Apache groups, and some experienced conflict with them. The departure of the Spanish removed one source of tension, and opened new opportunities for trade and alliance, but raiding continued and affected communities differently. Ute and other peoples were also part of the regional picture. The pattern shows that Pueblo communities negotiated within a field of competing interests rather than within a closed Pueblo world.

Relations among Pueblo communities were not free of friction. Spanish observers in 1692 reported that some communities were hostile to others and that alliances had shifted since 1680. Pecos and some other communities were more willing to negotiate with Vargas, while others were determined to resist. These differences reflected the positions of individual communities and not a simple division along language lines. They show that the coalition of 1680 did not continue unchanged, and that Pueblo politics after the revolt involved realignments.

The overall picture is of a period of adjustment and uncertainty. Pueblo leaders had to decide how to cooperate against a possible return, how to manage resources and land, and how to deal with neighbors. The Spanish documents give glimpses of these issues, mostly through the lens of intelligence-gathering by officials planning a return. They do not tell the whole story, and the gaps remind historians that Pueblo history in this period is only partly recoverable from the Spanish archive.

Section 44.23

Everyday Life under Pueblo Self-Government

What did independence mean in daily life? For most Pueblo people, it meant that the rhythms of agriculture, family, and ceremony were no longer subject to colonial demands. Households grew maize, beans, and squash, as their ancestors had, and they also grew crops introduced by the Spanish, including wheat and, in some places, fruit trees. They tended livestock, including sheep, goats, cattle, and horses, which had become important sources of food, hides, wool, and transport. Without tribute and labor drafts, the produce of fields and flocks remained within the community, easing one of the principal burdens of the colonial period.

Craft production continued. Women made pottery, and men and women wove textiles, working with cotton and, increasingly, wool. The skills of the colonial period, including the use of iron tools and, in some cases, new weaving techniques, remained available. Trade networks that had been disrupted by Spanish control could be revived, and goods moved among Pueblo communities and between Pueblo and neighboring peoples. Horses and other livestock left by the departing Spanish became part of regional exchange networks, and the broader spread of horses across the Plains and beyond, discussed in Chapter 49, was influenced by the collapse of Spanish control in New Mexico, though historians debate the extent and timing.

Family and community life were reorganized around the resumption of ceremonial life. The ceremonial calendar structured the year, and the participation of men and women in religious societies, in preparation for ceremonies, and in community work shaped social relationships. Children grew up without mission schooling and in an environment where Pueblo religious teaching could be openly transmitted. Marriage practices were affected by the rejection of Christian marriage, as the testimony of Naranjo suggests, though the extent of change is uncertain, and many families probably continued existing arrangements.

The conditions of life were not uniformly good. Resettlement in new villages, particularly on mesas, entailed hardships, including distance from fields and water. Defensive needs imposed costs. Environmental conditions, which remained variable, continued to affect harvests. Disease and hunger had not disappeared. Spanish expeditions and rumors of Spanish return created anxiety, and violence from raiding and from the expeditions of the late 1680s affected specific communities severely. Independence was valuable and also precarious.

Everyday Life and Social History

Pueblo Communities during Independence

Imagine the difference between a household in 1675 and the same household in 1685. In 1675, a family owed tribute in cloth and corn, supplied labor to the friary and perhaps to a Spanish estancia, and conducted its most important ceremonies in secret. In 1685, the same family could keep its harvest, hold ceremonies openly, and make decisions with neighbors about defense and settlement, though it might have moved to a new village and worried about a Spanish return. The change was real, but it did not end hardship, and it did not remove the need to cooperate with neighbors and to adapt.

Because the Spanish records of this decade are thin, historians draw on archaeology, Pueblo tradition, and the occasional testimony of captured Pueblo people to reconstruct daily life. Questions that remain open include how labor was divided between men and women in the new villages, how communities handled shortages, how children were taught, and how people who had held Christian beliefs or Spanish offices were treated. The evidence is more suggestive than complete, and students should treat reconstructions of everyday life in this period as provisional.

Section 44.24

Spanish Exile at El Paso del Norte

The refugees from New Mexico settled in the vicinity of El Paso del Norte, a mission and settlement on the Río Grande established in the preceding decades at the southern end of the route from Santa Fe. The place was not prepared to receive thousands of displaced people. Supplies were scarce, housing was improvised, and the refugees depended on provisions sent from the south and on the efforts of Fray Francisco de Ayeta, who had organized relief caravans and now lobbied on their behalf with viceregal authorities in Mexico City. Spanish sources describe hunger, disease, and discord during the first months of exile.

The displaced population was diverse. It included Spanish-born and New Spain-born families, people of mixed ancestry, Native servants and auxiliaries from central Mexico, and the Pueblo people, mostly southern Tiwa and Piro, who had accompanied the colonists. Governor Otermín organized settlements for the Pueblo refugees, and communities took shape that were named for their places of origin: Ysleta del Sur, for Isleta; Socorro del Sur; and Senecú del Sur. These settlements, which survive today in some form, combined Pueblo and Spanish colonial elements and illustrate the divided consequences of the revolt. Their relationship to the colonial authorities was one of both service and dependence, and the degree of choice available to their inhabitants is a matter of historical debate.

The colonial government continued to function in exile, and its principal aim was the recovery of New Mexico. Officials debated the means, including whether to launch a military expedition, how large a force would be needed, and who would bear the cost. The Crown regarded the loss of the province as serious, partly because of its religious significance, and partly because of concern about the northern frontier of New Spain and about foreign powers. In the mid-1680s, the French expedition of La Salle on the Gulf coast heightened Spanish anxiety about the security of the northern borderlands, and strengthened the argument for reasserting control in New Mexico.

Not all colonists wished to return. Many had lost property and relatives and were reluctant to risk further hardship. Some left the El Paso region for settlements in Nueva Vizcaya, in what is now northern Mexico. Governor Otermín faced criticism and accusations about his conduct during the revolt, and he had to contend with colonists' resentment. Relations between colonists and the Franciscans, who pressed for return, were sometimes strained. The exile thus had its own internal politics and pressures, and the impetus for reconquest came from a combination of imperial, religious, and personal motives rather than from a single source.

The exile also affected the Indigenous peoples of the El Paso region. Manso, Suma, and other groups lived in the area, and the arrival of thousands of newcomers strained resources and altered relationships. Spanish sources report episodes of unrest among these groups in the early and mid-1680s, and some scholars see these as part of a wider pattern of Indigenous resistance on the northern frontier in the years following the Pueblo Revolt, though the connections between these events and the Pueblo Revolt itself are debated. The revolt thus sent ripples beyond the Pueblo world, a theme Chapter 68 considers in a later period.

Section 44.25

Otermín's Return Expedition, 1681–1682

In the autumn of 1681, Governor Otermín led an expedition north from El Paso del Norte with the aim of reestablishing Spanish authority. The force was small: a company of Spanish soldiers, some auxiliaries from among the Native allies, and supply wagons. Otermín expected that Pueblo communities, faced with a Spanish army, might submit, and he carried instructions to seek their return to obedience and to recover Christian practice. The expedition moved up the Río Grande into the southern pueblos.

At Isleta, Spanish forces found the pueblo largely abandoned or lightly defended, and they took possession. The expedition took a large number of Pueblo people captive and removed them to the south. Spanish accounts give a figure of several hundred, and these captives were later settled in the El Paso area, forming the nucleus of Ysleta del Sur. The treatment of these people as captives complicates the picture of the southern settlements as simple refuges: some of the Pueblo people of Ysleta del Sur were taken south against their will during Otermín's expedition, while others had accompanied the retreat in 1680.

The expedition's advance north revealed the state of the province. Pueblo communities along the route had withdrawn from their villages or burned them, and they did not offer submission. Spanish soldiers reported finding evidence of damaged churches and the signs of Pueblo religious practice, including kivas and ritual objects. Otermín's officers questioned Pueblo prisoners, including Pedro Naranjo, whose testimony provided much of the detailed information on the revolt. The pueblos of the central region were not prepared to submit, and the Spanish force, small and limited by supply difficulties, reached the Cochiti area before turning back.

Otermín decided to withdraw in January 1682, for reasons Spanish accounts explain in terms of supply shortages, cold weather, the strength of Pueblo forces, the condition of his animals, and the danger of being cut off. His officers disagreed about the decision, and the expedition has been criticized by contemporaries and historians alike. The practical reality, however, was that the force was inadequate to subdue a region of many pueblos prepared to resist, and that the Spanish could not hold ground or feed an army in the province. The withdrawal confirmed the Pueblo victory.

The expedition produced lasting consequences. It generated a major body of documentary evidence, including the testimony of Naranjo and other prisoners. It added to the population of the El Paso settlements. And it demonstrated to Pueblo leaders that the Spanish could be resisted. For Spanish officials, it showed that a larger and better-supplied force would be needed and that reconquest would require sustained commitment. The next decade saw several smaller and more violent operations, but no successful reoccupation until Vargas.

Section 44.26

Domingo Jironza Petriz de Cruzate and the Violence of the 1680s

In the years after Otermín's expedition, Spanish officials in El Paso sent several smaller campaigns north. Domingo Jironza Petriz de Cruzate, who served as governor in the mid-1680s and again at the end of the decade, led or authorized raids on Pueblo communities that were intended to punish resistance, gather intelligence, take captives, and test the possibility of return. These operations were often brutal and had limited strategic effect, but they shaped relations between Spanish and Pueblo communities and kept the possibility of return alive.

The best-known episode occurred in 1689, when Cruzate's forces attacked Zia, a Keresan pueblo on the Jemez River. Zia had become a significant center of resistance, and Spanish accounts describe it as fortified. The Spanish assault, supported by Native allies, overwhelmed the defenses and burned the pueblo. Spanish reports give the number of Pueblo dead as several hundred, with figures around 600 appearing in the sources, and they report the capture of dozens of prisoners. These figures come from Spanish reports and may not be precise, but they indicate a massacre of large scale. The event stands as one of the most violent episodes of the period, and it had lasting significance in Zia's memory and in the experience of neighboring communities.

Spanish violence of this kind served several purposes. It aimed to terrorize Pueblo communities into submission, to punish those held responsible for the deaths of 1680, and to demonstrate the continuing power of Spanish arms. It also reflected the practices of colonial warfare, in which the taking of captives, who could be held as servants, was an accepted element. The Spanish expeditions drew on Native auxiliaries, whose participation shows that colonial forces were not exclusively Spanish and that the revolt had divided Indigenous peoples.

Pueblo communities responded in several ways. Some resisted fiercely, as at Zia. Others withdrew to defensible positions. Some sent messages, and some individuals traveled to El Paso to open negotiations. Spanish records indicate that diplomacy accompanied violence, and that some Pueblo leaders were prepared to consider terms. The relationship between military coercion and negotiation is a recurring theme of the 1690s, and the violence of the 1680s helped create the conditions in which some communities were willing to negotiate with Vargas.

At the same time, Spanish authorities did not yet have the capacity to reoccupy and hold the province. They lacked sufficient troops, supplies, and colonists, and they faced other demands on the northern frontier, including Indigenous unrest in other regions. The Crown's willingness to commit resources increased in the early 1690s, and the appointment of Vargas as governor in 1691 signaled a more determined approach.

Section 44.27

Diego de Vargas and the Campaign of 1692

Diego de Vargas Zapata Luján Ponce de León y Contreras was appointed governor of New Mexico in 1691 and arrived in El Paso del Norte with a mandate to recover the province. His daily journals, which survive in extensive form, are among the most valuable sources for the period, though like all official records they present events from his perspective and serve his reputation. In August 1692 he set out from El Paso with a force of soldiers, some Franciscans, and Native auxiliaries, intending to test Pueblo resistance and to receive submissions if possible.

The campaign was a combination of military display and diplomacy. Vargas advanced up the Río Grande and reached Santa Fe in mid-September. Pueblo residents, among them Tano people, occupied the town and its fortified buildings. According to Vargas's journal, he demanded surrender and promised pardon for the rebels who submitted and returned to the Catholic faith, while threatening force against those who did not. After negotiations, and with Spanish forces positioned for assault, the occupants agreed to submit, and on September 14 Vargas formally took possession of the villa in a ceremony that included prayers, the raising of a standard, and declarations of allegiance to the Crown.

Vargas then toured the pueblos, visiting communities across the north and center and reaching as far as Zuni and the Hopi towns, where he received statements of obedience and had children baptized. In many communities leaders met him with expressions of submission, and Vargas recorded these meetings as evidence that the province had been peacefully restored. Later Spanish and New Mexican tradition celebrated this episode as a bloodless reconquest, and commemorations of it persist in Santa Fe. Historians have argued that this tradition oversimplifies the events.

Why did so many communities submit? Pueblo leaders had reasons to be cautious. A Spanish army was present, and past expeditions had been violent. Some communities faced threats from Apache neighbors or rival Pueblo groups and may have hoped for Spanish military support. Others calculated that a formal submission would buy time and avoid immediate attack. For some, prior relations with the Franciscans or with Spanish officials made accommodation acceptable. Declarations of submission were thus tactical in many cases, and they did not necessarily express acceptance of the restoration of the colonial order. A distinction between ceremonial possession and effective control is central: Vargas had gone through the forms of possession, but he lacked the colonists, supplies, and force needed to govern.

Vargas returned to El Paso in the winter, taking some captives and refugees, and began preparing a colonizing expedition. The year 1692 therefore marked the beginning, not the end, of the reconquest. The terms on which the Pueblo communities had submitted would be tested when the colonists arrived.

Section 44.28

The Violent Reoccupation of Santa Fe, 1693

In October 1693, Vargas led a colonizing party north from El Paso del Norte. It included soldiers, Franciscans, and several dozen families of settlers, along with livestock and supplies, amounting to some hundreds of people by Spanish count. They reached Santa Fe in mid-December and found the town occupied by Pueblo residents who had no intention of surrendering it. The Pueblo occupants, who included Tano people and others, refused to leave and made clear that they regarded the submission of 1692 as no basis for the return of Spanish colonists.

The standoff lasted about two weeks in bitter winter conditions. The Spanish colonists, camped outside the walls, suffered from cold and shortage of supplies, and some people and animals died. Vargas negotiated, and Pueblo representatives from the surrounding region came and went. Spanish accounts report that Pueblo communities, including Tewa and Keresan peoples, sent forces to support the occupants. The Spanish position was precarious, and Vargas had to decide between withdrawing and assaulting.

On December 29, Spanish forces attacked and captured the town after fighting. Spanish accounts report that dozens of defenders were killed in the assault, that a large number of men, women, and children were taken prisoner, and that about seventy of the captured men were executed in reprisal. Surviving captives were distributed among Spanish soldiers and settlers as servants, under terms the Spanish called temporary but that in practice resembled slavery. The numbers come from Spanish reports and are generally accepted as indicating the scale, though precision is limited. The episode, which Chapter 53 places in the broader history of captivity and servitude, was a brutal demonstration that the Spanish return would not be peaceful.

The assault changed control of the provincial capital and began the re-establishment of Spanish government. Spanish colonists moved into Santa Fe, repairing and occupying the buildings, and the town resumed its role as capital. But the cost was high. The violence deepened Pueblo hostility, and reprisals spurred further resistance. Many Pueblo communities that had submitted in 1692 now reconsidered. The assault, in other words, ended one confrontation but did not secure the province, and the sequence of 1694 and 1696 followed from it.

Section 44.29

The Campaigns of 1694 and Continuing Pueblo Resistance

The reoccupation of Santa Fe did not end Pueblo resistance. In 1694, Vargas led a series of campaigns to bring the surrounding pueblos under control. The operations took him to the Tewa country, where refugees from several communities had gathered on a defensible mesa near the Río Grande, to the Jemez country, and to other strongholds. The campaigns combined siege, assault, negotiation, and the use of Native auxiliaries, notably Pueblo allies such as people from Pecos, whose cooperation illustrated the divisions among Pueblo communities.

At the mesa refuge near San Ildefonso, often called Black Mesa, Pueblo defenders occupied a naturally strong position. Vargas attempted to take it and then settled into a protracted blockade, which lasted for months. The defenders suffered shortages but repeatedly resisted assault, and the Spanish also faced the problem of sustaining their forces. Eventually negotiations led to the surrender of the refugees, who agreed to return to their pueblos and accept Spanish authority, though under conditions that left many tensions unresolved. The case shows how mesa-top refuges allowed Pueblo communities to negotiate from relative strength.

In July 1694 Vargas attacked the Jemez stronghold of Astialakwa, on a mesa in the Jemez mountains. Spanish accounts report that his forces stormed the position, killing dozens of defenders, with a figure of about eighty-four often cited, and capturing hundreds of people. The Jemez had been among the most persistent opponents of Spanish rule, and the battle was among the largest of the reconquest. As with other events, the numbers come from Spanish reports. The captives were removed and, as in Santa Fe, distributed among colonists and soldiers.

Spanish victories in 1694 were real but not decisive. Many Pueblo people remained beyond Spanish reach. Some had fled to remote areas, including the Dinétah region and the Hopi mesas, where Spanish forces could not follow. Some communities negotiated submissions, and some Pueblo leaders cooperated with Vargas for reasons of their own, including rivalry with neighbors and hope of protection. Vargas learned that victory in battle did not produce political acceptance. The Spanish held strong points but lacked the means to administer the countryside, and Pueblo communities retained the ability to resist or to disappear into the landscape.

The campaigns also expanded Spanish colonization. Vargas encouraged settlers to establish new communities, including a villa near the Tewa pueblos in 1695, intended to extend Spanish presence in the north. These settlements added to the pressures on Pueblo lands and labor and intensified the tensions that would soon erupt in a second revolt.

Section 44.30

The Pueblo Uprising of 1696 and the End of the Revolt Era

On June 4, 1696, a new uprising broke out among Pueblo communities in the north and center of the province. Spanish sources report that Pueblo people attacked missions and settlements and killed five Franciscan friars and about twenty-one other Spaniards. The participants included Tewa communities, Taos and Picuris, and some Keresan and Jemez communities, though accounts of which pueblos took part vary. The uprising did not extend to all Pueblo communities, and some, including Pecos and some Keresan communities, remained aligned with the Spanish or neutral.

The causes were rooted in the experience of reoccupation. Spanish colonists, soldiers, and friars had returned and resumed demands for labor, tribute in practice if not always in name, and religious conformity. The violence of 1693 and 1694 had embittered many communities, and captives had been taken from families. Famine, caused by crop failures and the disruption of planting by military operations, added hardship. Pueblo leaders who had submitted in 1692 saw that the return of Spanish rule meant the return of burdens they had overthrown in 1680. The 1696 uprising was thus a reaction to the realities of reconquest and a continuation of the revolt.

Compared with 1680, the 1696 movement was less coordinated and less successful. Spanish forces were present in the province and were able to respond. Vargas mounted a campaign against the rebel communities, pursuing them into the mountains. Many Pueblo people fled. Spanish accounts describe the movement of Taos and Picuris groups eastward to the plains and the retreat of others to remote locations, including the Hopi mesas and the Dinétah region, where Pueblo refugees continued to live among or alongside the Diné. Tano and other Tewa-speaking refugees at Hopi established a village that persisted as Hano, or Tewa Village, according to Hopi and Tewa tradition, illustrating how the revolt reshaped the map of Pueblo communities.

Vargas's campaign restored a measure of Spanish control in the north, but it did not eliminate Pueblo resistance or restore the earlier colonial order. The Spanish, short of resources, had to settle for partial submission, and they encountered continued reluctance. Vargas himself was replaced as governor in 1697 and faced legal difficulties in the following years. In the years after 1696, Pueblo communities generally did not mount large coordinated uprisings, though local resistance and flight continued. The Hopi community of Awatovi, which accepted missionaries, was destroyed by other Hopi in 1700, an episode showing that disputes over religion and Spanish contact continued to divide Pueblo communities.

The end of the revolt era was therefore gradual and uneven. By the late 1690s, Spanish authority was reestablished in much of the Río Grande region, but under conditions that differed from those of 1680. The combination of Pueblo resistance, Spanish exhaustion, and a changing regional balance of power produced a new, negotiated relationship. Division VI examines that outcome.

Division VI Consequences, Historical Significance, and Memory Sections 44.31–44.33 · Transformation and legacy
Section 44.31

The Reconstructed Colonial Order and Pueblo Autonomy

The Spanish government that returned to New Mexico in the 1690s was not the one that had been expelled in 1680. Historians generally agree that the relationship between Pueblo communities and Spanish authorities changed, though they differ on how much. The change can be examined in several domains: religion, labor and tribute, political institutions, and military relations.

In religion, Franciscan missions were reestablished, and friars again lived in Pueblo communities. But the intensity of efforts to suppress Pueblo ceremonial life appears to have diminished compared with the decades before 1680. Spanish officials and friars had learned, at heavy cost, that extreme repression could provoke revolt, and the conflicts between governors and friars that had undermined colonial stability were moderated in practice. Pueblo communities maintained kiva ceremonies, generally without open interference, while participating in Catholic observances, including feast days. This dual religious life, in which Pueblo people observed Christian ritual and maintained their own, became characteristic of Pueblo communities in the following centuries, an example of the broader Indigenous Christian adaptation and religious transformation examined thematically in Chapter 54.

In labor and tribute, the old encomienda system was not reestablished in its earlier form, and the burden of colonial demands on Pueblo communities lightened relative to the period before 1680, though forced labor and other obligations did not disappear. Spanish settlers needed Pueblo cooperation for agriculture, defense, and trade, and the colonial economy became more dependent on exchange and negotiation. The Spanish population grew, and land disputes between Pueblo communities and settlers became a continuing feature of colonial life, but the relationship was shaped by the recognition that Pueblo communities could resist.

In politics, Pueblo communities retained their own governing structures, including religious leadership and community officers on the Spanish pattern. The annually chosen community governors and other offices that had been established under Spanish rule continued and became integrated into Pueblo political life. Pueblo leaders negotiated with Spanish officials on matters of land, labor, and justice, and the Crown recognized, at least in principle, Pueblo rights to lands and to the protection of royal law. The exact legal documents establishing such rights have a contested history, and their interpretation has been the subject of litigation and scholarship in later centuries.

Militarily, Pueblo communities became allies as well as subjects. The Spanish relied on Pueblo auxiliaries in campaigns against Apache, Ute, and other raiders, and later against the Comanche peoples discussed in Chapter 50. The alliance reflected shared interests in defense against raiding and gave Pueblo communities influence in colonial affairs. The Spanish population also relied increasingly on people of mixed and Indigenous ancestry living outside Pueblo communities, whose history belongs to later chapters on captivity and servitude.

Outcomes varied by community. The Hopi remained outside sustained Spanish colonial control, and their villages were never brought back under mission rule. Zuni returned to the valley after the 1690s and had a mission restored, though relations remained tense. Acoma resumed contact with Spanish authority. Many communities in the Río Grande valley experienced Spanish administration again, but with the changes described above. The southern Tiwa and Piro communities near El Paso del Norte developed in separate directions. The revolt therefore left a legacy of diversity rather than a single outcome for the Pueblo peoples.

Section 44.32

The Meaning and Historical Legacy of the Pueblo Revolt

The Pueblo Revolt has been interpreted in many ways. For Spanish officials, it was a rebellion and an apostasy, an act of treason against the Crown and an offense against the Church. This interpretation shaped the narrative of the reconquest, which celebrated the return of the province as the restoration of legitimate order and, in later New Mexican tradition, as a peaceful and providential event. Annual festivals in Santa Fe that commemorate Vargas's entry have been the subject of public controversy in recent decades, reflecting disagreement over how the reconquest should be remembered.

For Pueblo communities, the revolt is part of a history of liberation and continuity. Pueblo accounts emphasize the defense of religion and community, the courage of those who risked their lives, and the survival of Pueblo ways of life under pressure. The revolt has been remembered through oral tradition, through commemorative events, and through Pueblo scholarship and writing. In 1980, the three hundredth anniversary of the revolt was marked by public events, including a relay run, and later anniversaries have also been observed.

The memory of Po'pay has gained wide public recognition. In 2005, a statue of Po'pay, created by the Jemez Pueblo sculptor Cliff Fragua, was placed in the National Statuary Hall Collection in the United States Capitol, representing New Mexico. The statue's installation reflected the revolt's growing acceptance as a significant event in American history, and also the choice of the state to honor an Indigenous leader. Commemoration of this kind raises questions about how historical figures are represented, including the limits of the evidence about Po'pay and the diversity of Pueblo views of him.

Scholarly interpretation has also evolved. Earlier narratives often emphasized the revolt as a dramatic episode in the story of Spanish colonization, followed by the reconquest, with less attention to the independence years or to Pueblo agency. Recent work, including archaeological research and critical reassessments of the historiography, has emphasized the revolt as a transformative event in Pueblo history and has questioned narratives that treat Spanish conquest as the central fact. Michael Wilcox has argued that scholars' assumptions about conquest shaped the interpretation of the Pueblo past, and others have emphasized the importance of Pueblo continuity.

The revolt's wider significance lies partly in comparison. It is among the most successful Indigenous uprisings against European colonial rule in North America, and one of the rare instances in which an established European colonial government was expelled from a province for an extended period. It belongs alongside the Indigenous diplomatic and military achievements examined in Chapters 45 through 48 and 55 through 58, in which Indigenous nations shaped the course of colonization. It also illustrates a recurring pattern: colonial authorities could not rely on military force alone, and the terms of colonization were negotiated.

Historical Debate

What Did the Pueblo Revolt Ultimately Achieve?

The question: The Spanish returned to New Mexico by 1696 and remained until 1821, when Mexican independence ended Spanish rule. Was the revolt, then, a failure? Or did it achieve something lasting?

Interpretation A — A temporary military victory: Some interpretations emphasize that the revolt did not end Spanish colonization, that the Spanish returned and reasserted control, and that the independence years were an interlude. On this view the revolt's lasting effects were limited.
Interpretation B — A lasting religious and cultural success: Others stress that Pueblo religion and community life survived and were secured by the revolt, which prevented the mission system from eliminating Pueblo ceremonial life. On this view the revolt achieved its most important goal.
Interpretation C — A transformation of the colonial relationship: A third view holds that the revolt forced Spain to adopt a more accommodating policy, shifting the balance of power between colonizers and Pueblo communities and producing a more negotiated order.

Evidence: Evidence includes the reestablishment of Spanish government in the 1690s, the changed intensity of anti-ceremonial campaigns, the end of the encomienda in its old form, the continuity of Pueblo religion into the present, and Pueblo traditions about the meaning of the revolt.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns the criteria for success. Historians who measure success by political independence reach different conclusions than those who measure it by cultural survival or by changes in colonial policy. The question of counterfactuals also arises: how would Pueblo history have differed without the revolt?

Why it matters: How historians answer shapes whether the revolt is seen as an episode of defeat or as a central event in the survival of Pueblo communities. The answer also bears on the broader question of how to evaluate Indigenous resistance to colonialism.

Student task: Write a short argument for each interpretation using specific evidence from this chapter, and then explain which criteria of success you find most persuasive and why. Identify one piece of evidence that would help resolve the disagreement.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that the Pueblo Revolt of 1680 was a coordinated Indigenous revolution that temporarily overthrew Spanish colonial rule, created opportunities for religious and political revitalization, and permanently altered the relationship between Pueblo communities and the Spanish colonial state. It has treated the events in three movements: the organization and execution of the uprising, the years of Pueblo independence, and the contested Spanish return through 1696.

The causes lay in a long accumulation of colonial burdens, including tribute, forced labor, religious coercion, and political interference, as Chapter 43 established. But grievance alone did not produce the revolt. The crisis of the 1660s and 1670s, with its drought, hunger, epidemic disease, and raiding, undermined the legitimacy and capacity of the colonial government, and the arrests of 1675 produced leaders with personal motives and demonstrated that collective Pueblo action could compel a Spanish governor to yield.

Po'pay of Ohkay Owingeh emerged as an important organizer, but the evidence about him is thin, and the revolt depended on many other leaders from communities including Taos, Picuris, and Santo Domingo. The coalition built on trade, kinship, ceremonial ties, and the skills of men who had served in colonial offices. It joined communities that spoke different languages and governed themselves independently, and participation varied, particularly among the southern Río Grande pueblos.

Communication was a central achievement. Runners carrying knotted cords, as described in Spanish testimony, allowed distant communities to act on a common schedule. When Spanish officials discovered the plot, the leaders advanced the date to August 10, 1680, a decision that preserved the element of surprise. The uprising struck missions and estancias across the northern, central, and western regions almost simultaneously.

The military course culminated in the siege of Santa Fe. Pueblo forces isolated the capital, cut its water supply, and compelled Governor Otermín to withdraw south with perhaps two thousand survivors. Spanish sources report that more than 400 colonists and 21 friars died, figures that should be treated as attributed estimates. The southern retreat showed that the Pueblo peoples did not act identically, as some southern Tiwa and Piro people accompanied the Spanish.

During the independence years, Pueblo communities removed colonial institutions, revived ceremonial life, and built new villages. Archaeological work at Patokwa, Boletsakwa, and Kotyiti has shown that these communities were planned, defended, and ceremonially organized, and has made the independence era a recoverable part of the revolution's history. Everyday life changed through the end of tribute and labor demands, though hardship, defense, and uncertainty persisted. Unity was constructed and fragile, and Po'pay's authority had limits.

The Spanish return was slow and violent. Otermín's expedition of 1681 to 1682 failed, the assault on Zia in 1689 showed Spanish brutality, and Vargas's campaign of 1692 achieved ceremonial possession through negotiation rather than effective control. The retaking of Santa Fe in December 1693, the campaigns of 1694, and the uprising of 1696 demonstrated that submissions were tactical and that resistance continued.

The reconstructed colonial order that followed differed from the one that had been overthrown. Campaigns against Pueblo ceremonies lost much of their earlier intensity, the encomienda was not reestablished in its old form, and Pueblo communities became allies as well as subjects. The Hopi remained outside Spanish control, and refugee communities created new settlements. The outcomes varied across the Pueblo world.

The revolt has been interpreted as rebellion by Spanish officials, as liberation and continuity by Pueblo communities, and as a pivotal event by modern scholars. Public commemorations, including the statue of Po'pay in the United States Capitol, show its continuing significance, and debates over how the reconquest is remembered show that its meaning remains contested.

The central lesson is methodological as well as historical. Understanding the revolt requires reading Spanish documents critically, drawing on archaeology and Pueblo tradition, resisting both inevitability and single-cause explanations, and recognizing that Indigenous people were political actors whose decisions shaped the colonial world. These habits of mind apply throughout the chapters that follow.

Key Terms

Pueblo
A Spanish word for "town," applied to Indigenous communities of the northern Southwest with compact, often multistory villages, and to their people; each was politically autonomous.
Revolt
A violent collective act of resistance against an authority; the conventional name of the 1680 event, with no implication about its success or planning.
Revolution
A fundamental restructuring of political and social order through organized collective action; used here for the transformations of 1680 to 1692.
Sovereignty
Inherent political authority to govern, which Pueblo communities exercised before and after Spanish rule.
Encomienda
A Spanish grant that entitled a holder to tribute from Indigenous communities in exchange for nominal protection and instruction in Christianity.
Repartimiento
A system of assigned Indigenous labor for Spanish households, public works, and missions.
Franciscan mission
A religious establishment in or near a Pueblo community, staffed by friars of the Franciscan order, with a church, a convent, and agricultural properties.
Extirpation
The campaign by missionaries and officials to eradicate Indigenous religious practice, including raids on ceremonial chambers.
Kiva (estufa)
A Pueblo ceremonial chamber, called estufa by the Spanish, used by religious societies for ritual and instruction.
Alcalde mayor
A Spanish provincial official with judicial, administrative, and military duties in a district.
Estancia
A Spanish ranch or farming estate, typically isolated and dependent on Indigenous labor.
Villa
A Spanish chartered town, such as Santa Fe, with civil institutions and a settler population.
Camino Real
The royal road linking New Mexico to Mexico City, the principal route of supply and of the 1680 retreat.
Knotted cord
A cord with knots used, according to Spanish testimony, to count the days until the uprising.
Revitalization
A deliberate effort to restore and renew cultural and religious life, as in the revival of Pueblo ceremonies after 1680.
Apostasy
The abandonment of a religion; the Spanish term for Pueblo rejection of Christianity, which reflects colonial and Catholic assumptions.
Interrogation testimony
Statements recorded from captives by Spanish officials, valuable but shaped by the circumstances of custody and translation.
Auxiliary
An Indigenous or allied fighter serving alongside Spanish forces, including Pueblo allies in the 1690s.
Reconquest (reconquista)
The Spanish term for the recovery of New Mexico in the 1690s, which implies restoration of a prior legitimate order and reflects the colonial perspective.
Ceremonial possession
A Spanish ritual act of claiming territory through formal speech, prayer, and symbols, which could occur without effective control.
Revolt-era village
An archaeological term for communities such as Patokwa, Boletsakwa, and Kotyiti, built in the years after 1680.
Astialakwa
A mesa stronghold of the Jemez, stormed by Vargas in July 1694.
Dinétah
The homeland of the Diné in northwestern New Mexico, where Pueblo refugees lived in the late seventeenth and early eighteenth centuries.
Tano
The Tewa-related people of the Galisteo Basin who participated in the revolt and occupied Santa Fe; some later settled at Hopi.
Autonomy
Self-governance of each Pueblo community, the basis of Pueblo political life and a source of both strength and fragility in the coalition.
Tewa, Tiwa, Towa
Related Tanoan languages spoken in different Pueblo communities, distinct from each other and from Keresan, Zuni, and Hopi.
Corn Mountain (Dowa Yalanne)
A mesa near Zuni that served as a refuge during the independence years.
Ysleta del Sur
A community near El Paso founded by Tiwa people from Isleta who left with or were taken by Spanish forces.

Chapter Reflection

The Pueblo Revolt matters because it shows that Indigenous political action could change the course of colonization. A colonial government with a century of institutions behind it was expelled by communities that had no common state, no single language, and no standing army. The outcome was the product of planning, shared religious commitments, and decisions made under pressure, and it cannot be explained by Spanish weakness alone. That makes the revolt a powerful corrective to narratives in which Indigenous peoples appear mainly as victims of inevitable conquest.

The chapter also reveals how much of the story lies outside the colonial archive. The independence years, which occupy a decade between the fighting of 1680 and the Spanish return, are poorly documented in Spanish sources and are recoverable mainly through archaeology and Pueblo tradition. Treating those years as part of the revolution rather than as a gap changes how the entire episode is understood. It also demonstrates why historians must read different kinds of evidence against one another and be explicit about whose voices are missing.

The consequences extended well beyond 1696. The revolt shaped the terms of coexistence between Pueblo communities and Spanish colonists, contributed to the movement of people and horses across the region, and helped secure the survival of Pueblo religious and political institutions that persist today. These themes recur in Chapters 49 through 51, which examine horses and Apache history, and in Chapters 54 and 55, which examine religious transformation and Indigenous diplomacy. The chapter also invites comparison with the contests of the Northeast and Great Lakes examined in Chapters 45 through 48, where Indigenous nations likewise shaped the terms of colonial presence.

Finally, the revolt's afterlife reminds students that history is remembered as well as recorded. Spanish officials, Pueblo communities, New Mexican festival organizers, and modern museums have all told the story differently, and these differences are part of the history. Understanding why the Pueblo Revolt continues to matter requires attention both to what happened in 1680 and to the many ways it has been interpreted since.
