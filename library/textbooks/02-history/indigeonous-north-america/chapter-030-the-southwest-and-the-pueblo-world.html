The Southwest and the Pueblo World

Indigenous Peoples of North America: A Continental History

Chapter Opening

Hopi tribal historical and cultural materials describe the ruins scattered across the mesas and canyons of the Colorado Plateau not as abandoned places but as footprints: places ancestors passed through, lived in, and deliberately left behind as part of a long migration that fulfilled a covenant and brought clans together at Hopitutskwa, the Hopi homeland. A visitor trained to see an archaeological site as a ruin, a place where something ended, encounters a landscape that official Hopi sources describe instead as a record of where something continued.

This difference in framing is not a minor matter of word choice. It goes to the heart of what this chapter sets out to explain. The preceding chapters in this volume's companion sequence on the ancient Southwest, together with Volume II's chapters on early farming communities, Mogollon pottery and settlement, Hohokam irrigation engineering, and the broad world of Ancestral Pueblo architecture and community life, left the region's story at a moment of profound transformation. During the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries, long-established communities across the Colorado Plateau, the Mogollon highlands, and the Sonoran Desert reorganized themselves. People who had lived for generations at places such as Mesa Verde moved. Great houses and canyon centers that had anchored regional life for centuries saw their populations disperse toward new areas along the Rio Grande, toward the Hopi mesas, and toward other parts of the Southwest. Hohokam canal systems that had irrigated the Gila and Salt River valleys for a thousand years gave way, by the fifteenth century, to different patterns of settlement and community organization among the ancestors of later Akimel and Tohono O'odham communities.

It would be easy, and it would be wrong, to read this transformation as decline. A textbook that ended the story of the ancient Southwest with dispersal and then jumped directly to Spanish expeditions arriving in the sixteenth century would imply that the centuries in between were simply empty, a gap between one civilization's "collapse" and another's "discovery." That implication is historically false. The people who left Mesa Verde, Chaco, and the Hohokam canal towns did not vanish. They moved, often toward kin, toward known trade partners, toward places with reliable water, and toward communities that were themselves growing and changing. They carried agricultural knowledge, ceremonial traditions, clan histories, and social memory with them. Over the following two centuries, their descendants and the communities they joined built the Pueblo towns along the Rio Grande and at Hopi, Zuni, and Acoma; sustained and adapted O'odham desert farming and water management across the Sonoran Desert; and, during this same broad period, saw Athabaskan-speaking peoples, ancestors of the Diné and various Apache communities, establish themselves across large parts of the northern and eastern Southwest.

By the time Spanish expeditions reached the region in the sixteenth century, the Southwest they encountered was not a landscape of ruins inhabited by a few scattered survivors of a vanished golden age. It was a dense, dynamic, and highly diverse Indigenous world: dozens of autonomous Pueblo towns speaking several unrelated languages; expanding Diné communities building new kinds of homes and economies in the Dinétah region of the San Juan Basin; Apache peoples establishing distinctive and varied ways of life across the southern Plains borderlands, the mountains, and the desert; and O'odham communities whose knowledge of desert agriculture and water had been refined over centuries. These peoples traded with one another, intermarried, borrowed ceremonial knowledge, competed for resources, occasionally fought, and built the homelands their descendants hold today.

This chapter asks how historians, working with archaeology, linguistics, environmental evidence, and the historical knowledge that Pueblo, Diné, Apache, and O'odham communities themselves maintain and share, can responsibly describe this process of reorganization and community formation. It asks in particular how to avoid two tempting but misleading shortcuts. The first shortcut draws a straight, confident line from a single archaeological "culture," such as the people archaeologists call Ancestral Pueblo, directly to a single modern nation, as though Mesa Verde were simply "the ancestors of" one specific Pueblo community today. The second shortcut treats oral tradition and archaeological or linguistic reconstruction as rival, competing accounts in which one must be right and the other wrong. Neither shortcut survives careful examination of the evidence. Modern Pueblo communities frequently trace ancestry to several different archaeological regions and migration histories, which came together through centuries of movement, intermarriage, and community formation. Diné clan histories describe varied origins, including some clans whose traditions connect them to older Southwestern peoples. Oral tradition and archaeological or linguistic reconstruction are distinct bodies of historical evidence that may converge, diverge, or illuminate different dimensions of a shared, complicated past; neither should automatically be assumed to confirm or to contradict the other.

This chapter accordingly does not simply narrate "what happened" in the fifteenth-century Southwest, as though a single settled timeline were available to recite. It also teaches a method: how to think about cultural affiliation, migration, and community formation when the evidence is varied, when descendant communities themselves describe multiple and sometimes historically layered origins, and when any single-origin story risks flattening a past that was, in reality, built from many directions at once.

The chapter proceeds region by region and people by people, examining the Rio Grande Pueblo world, the western Pueblo communities of Hopi, Zuni, and Acoma, O'odham desert communities, Diné homelands in formation, and the expanding world of Apache peoples, before turning to the farming systems, trade networks, and patterns of both cooperation and conflict that connected all of these communities into a single, interdependent regional world. It closes at the threshold of sustained Spanish colonization, examined in later chapters, leaving the reader with a clear picture of the Southwest not as a prelude to conquest but as a fully functioning, internally diverse Indigenous world in its own right.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why the reorganization of Southwestern communities after the thirteenth century should be understood as migration and continuity rather than collapse or disappearance.
  2. Distinguish among the major historically identifiable peoples of the late precolonial Southwest, including Rio Grande and western Pueblo communities, Akimel and Tohono O'odham communities, Diné communities, and various Apache peoples.
  3. Describe the linguistic, political, ceremonial, and economic diversity among Pueblo communities, and explain why "Pueblo" names a broad historical category rather than a single nation, language, or government.
  4. Evaluate the methodological problem of connecting archaeological "cultures" to living Indigenous nations, and explain why the relationship is frequently many-to-many rather than one-to-one.
  5. Analyze Hopi migration tradition as a form of historical knowledge that interprets archaeological landscapes as evidence of ancestral movement rather than as abandoned ruins.
  6. Assess the evidence, and the scholarly uncertainty, surrounding the timing and routes by which Athabaskan-speaking peoples became part of the Southwest.
  7. Explain how O'odham communities maintained and adapted desert agricultural and water-management knowledge following the Hohokam archaeological period.
  8. Compare Rio Grande irrigation agriculture, Hopi dry farming, and O'odham floodwater farming as distinct solutions to different environmental problems.
  9. Describe the regional trade networks that connected Southwestern peoples to one another and to the Plains, California, northern Mexico, and Mesoamerica before sustained European contact.
  10. Analyze the mixture of exchange, intermarriage, ceremonial sharing, competition, and conflict that characterized relationships among Pueblo, Diné, Apache, and O'odham communities.
  11. Identify the limitations of archaeological, linguistic, and oral historical evidence for reconstructing Southwestern migration and community formation.
  12. Describe the Southwest as it existed on the eve of sustained Spanish colonization, as a foundation for understanding the colonial encounters examined in later chapters.

Historical Context

This chapter builds directly on the archaeological foundations established earlier in this textbook. Volume II traced the origins and spread of agriculture across the Southwest, the early village-farming communities that adopted maize, beans, and squash, the distinctive pottery and settlement traditions of Mogollon communities in the mountainous borderlands of present-day Arizona, New Mexico, and northern Mexico, the irrigation engineering and trade networks of Hohokam communities in the Sonoran Desert, and the architecture, agriculture, and community organization of the Ancestral Pueblo world centered on the Colorado Plateau, including major centers such as Chaco Canyon and Mesa Verde. Those chapters carry the primary responsibility for describing archaeological method, dating, architecture, and the deep history of Southwestern farming societies, and this chapter does not repeat that material in detail.

What this chapter must explain is what happened next. During the thirteenth century, a combination of pressures, examined later in this chapter's Cause and Effect feature, led communities across the Colorado Plateau to disperse from long-occupied centers. Over the following two centuries, their descendants, often joined by migrants from other regions and absorbed into new community configurations, built the historically identifiable Pueblo world that Spanish expeditions encountered in the sixteenth century: dozens of towns along the Rio Grande and its tributaries, and additional communities at Hopi, Zuni, and Acoma farther west, with ancestral Keresan communities also present in the area that would later become Laguna Pueblo after its formal establishment in 1699. Meanwhile, in the Sonoran Desert, the elaborate canal systems that Hohokam communities had built and maintained for roughly a thousand years saw reduced use by the fifteenth century, even as communities ancestral to later O'odham peoples continued to occupy the region, adapting their farming and water-management practices to new circumstances.

At the same time, new peoples were becoming part of the Southwestern world. Linguists classify Diné and Apache languages as Athabaskan, part of a much larger language family whose other branches are spoken primarily across the Subarctic interior of present-day Alaska and Canada, examined in Chapter 34. The presence of Athabaskan-speaking communities in the Southwest by the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries represents one of the most significant population developments in the region's precolonial history, and this chapter treats it as a central subject rather than a brief aside.

By the time this chapter's narrative reaches its close in the sixteenth century, the Southwest was an intricate mosaic: autonomous Pueblo towns organized around clans, kivas, and ceremonial societies; O'odham communities practicing refined desert agriculture across the Sonoran Desert; Diné communities establishing farming, hunting, and trading economies in the Dinétah region of the San Juan Basin; and various Apache peoples occupying a wide range of environments from the southern Plains borderlands to the mountains and deserts farther south and west, with economies that varied considerably from community to community. These peoples were connected by extensive trade networks moving turquoise, shell, cotton, pottery, feathers, and food across hundreds of miles, and by patterns of intermarriage, ceremonial exchange, and both cooperation and conflict that this chapter examines directly.

This chapter's geographic and chronological boundaries are therefore specific. Geographically, it covers the Colorado Plateau, the Rio Grande watershed, the Little Colorado River region, the Mogollon Rim, the Sonoran Desert, the Gila and Salt River valleys, and the San Juan Basin, while acknowledging connections extending into the southern Plains, California, and northern Mexico. Chronologically, it covers roughly the period from the great population movements of the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries through the eve of sustained Spanish colonization in the late sixteenth century. The Pueblo Revolt of 1680, Spanish missionization, and the broader colonial history of New Spain's northern frontier belong to later chapters in this textbook's fourth volume, and this chapter deliberately stops short of that colonial narrative.

Section 30.1

The Southwest as an Indigenous World

The term "Southwest," as used throughout this chapter, refers to a region defined by its own rivers, plateaus, mountain ranges, and deserts, not by the modern boundaries of Arizona, New Mexico, or the United States-Mexico border. Before any such boundaries existed, the lands historians today call the Southwest formed a connected, if ecologically varied, world stretching from the high, forested rim of the Colorado Plateau in the north to the hot, arid floor of the Sonoran Desert in the south, and from the Rio Grande watershed in the east to the Little Colorado River and the edges of the Great Basin in the west.

Within this broad region, local environments differed enormously. The Colorado Plateau, cut by canyons and crowned by mesas, supported piñon-juniper woodland and, at higher elevations, ponderosa pine forest, with growing seasons shaped by elevation and unpredictable precipitation. The Rio Grande and its tributaries carved a long, fertile corridor through otherwise arid country, offering reliable water for irrigation in many stretches. The Mogollon Rim marked a transition zone between the Colorado Plateau and the lower desert basins to the south, supporting mixed woodland and grassland environments suited to both farming and hunting. The Sonoran Desert, by contrast, received the bulk of its moisture in two separate rainy seasons each year and required farmers to master floodwater and canal irrigation techniques capable of capturing brief, intense runoff from the Gila and Salt Rivers and smaller washes. Farther north and east, the San Juan Basin and the plains bordering the Southwest presented still different conditions, supporting economies that combined farming with hunting and long-distance mobility.

No single economy, language, or political system unified this region before European colonization, any more than afterward. This chapter's central task is to describe how, within this environmentally diverse landscape, distinct peoples, Pueblo communities of several linguistic traditions, O'odham desert farmers, Diné households establishing new homelands, and Apache communities adapting to a range of environments, built the historically identifiable Southwest encountered by Spanish expeditions in the sixteenth century. The region's connections extended well beyond its own boundaries as well: trade routes linked the Southwest to the southern Plains, to California, and, through long chains of exchange, to Mesoamerica, a set of relationships examined further in Section 30.10 and in Chapter 14.

Section 30.2

From Ancestral Worlds to Living Pueblo Communities

Chapter 18 examined the architecture, agriculture, and community organization of the Ancestral Pueblo world at length, including the rise and eventual decline of Chaco Canyon's regional influence and the continued growth of communities such as Mesa Verde into the thirteenth century. This chapter begins where that account ends: with the dispersal of Colorado Plateau communities during the mid-to-late thirteenth century and the question of where, and as what, those communities continued.

The language historians use to describe this period matters a great deal. Older popular accounts sometimes described Mesa Verde and similar sites as having been "abandoned" by a people who then "disappeared," language that implies an ending rather than a continuation. The National Park Service and allied scholarship today generally describe this period instead as one of migration: communities moved from the Colorado Plateau toward regions including the Rio Grande valley, the Hopi mesas, and the Zuni and Acoma areas, carrying agricultural knowledge, ceremonial practices, and social organization with them. Far from disappearing, these communities' descendants became part of the very Pueblo nations that exist today, many of which maintain explicit historical and ceremonial relationships to Mesa Verde, Chaco, and other ancestral places.

This migration was not a single, simple relocation of one people from one place to another. It involved multiple migration streams, over more than a century, converging on a smaller number of destination regions. Communities that arrived at a given destination, whether a Rio Grande valley location or the Hopi mesas, did not necessarily share a single prior origin. Archaeological evidence, including variations in pottery styles, architectural techniques, and material culture, suggests that migrants from different parts of the Colorado Plateau and surrounding regions settled alongside one another, and, over time, together built new, often larger communities that combined elements of several earlier traditions. This pattern, of multiple origins converging into single later communities, is central to why this chapter resists simple one-to-one connections between archaeological regions and modern Pueblo nations, a problem examined in full in this chapter's Historical Method feature.

The governing lesson of this section, and one that should guide students through the rest of this chapter, is that migration can represent continuity rather than collapse. A community that moves, especially when it carries its language, its ceremonial knowledge, its agricultural techniques, and its own understanding of its history with it, has not ended. It has changed location and, often, has joined with other communities to form something new while remaining connected to what came before. Hopi migration tradition, examined at length in this chapter's Primary Source Window, offers one of the clearest and most explicit Indigenous articulations of exactly this principle: migration as the fulfillment of a historical and spiritual obligation, not as a response to defeat or failure.

Section 30.3

The Rio Grande Pueblo World

By the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, dozens of Pueblo towns lined the Rio Grande and its major tributaries, from the northern valleys near present-day Taos southward through the Albuquerque Basin and beyond. These towns were not provinces or districts of a single Pueblo nation. Each was an autonomous political community, with its own leadership, its own ceremonial calendar, and its own relationships, sometimes close and sometimes distant, with neighboring towns.

Linguistically, the Rio Grande Pueblo world was itself divided among several distinct and largely unrelated languages. Tewa-speaking communities were concentrated in the northern Rio Grande valley. Tiwa-speaking communities, themselves divided into northern and southern groups with related but distinct dialects, were located both near Taos and Picuris in the north and near present-day Isleta and other towns farther south. Towa was spoken at Jemez and at Pecos, a large town at the eastern edge of the Pueblo world with close trading ties to Plains peoples; Pecos remained a major Towa-speaking Pueblo well into the colonial period, and its remaining residents did not join their Towa-speaking relatives at Jemez until 1838. Towa continues to be spoken at Jemez Pueblo today. Keres-speaking communities, whose language is not closely related to Tewa, Tiwa, or Towa, were located at Santo Domingo, Cochiti, San Felipe, Santa Ana, Zia, and, farther west, at Acoma, discussed in the following section alongside other western Pueblo communities.

Despite this linguistic diversity, Rio Grande Pueblo towns shared certain broad features of economy and settlement, even as the particulars varied from town to town. Agriculture centered on maize, beans, and squash, supplemented by cotton in warmer locations, and relied on a combination of river floodplain farming, small-scale irrigation diverted from the river and its tributaries, and dry farming on terraces and in areas where seasonal rainfall and careful field placement could sustain crops without constant irrigation. Towns were typically built of adobe or stone masonry, organized around one or more central plazas, and included semi-subterranean or partially enclosed ceremonial structures.

Political authority in Rio Grande Pueblo towns was generally organized around a combination of civil leadership, often associated with particular clans or religious societies, and ceremonial leadership connected to specific ritual responsibilities. No single Rio Grande Pueblo town held authority over the others, though towns maintained networks of trade, intermarriage, ceremonial exchange, and, at times, shared defensive concern in the face of outside pressure. This textbook resists describing the Rio Grande Pueblo towns as a unified political bloc; they were a set of related but independent communities whose interactions, examined further in Section 30.11, ranged from close cooperation to occasional friction.

Section 30.4

Hopi, Zuni, Acoma, and Western Pueblo Worlds

West of the Rio Grande valley, several additional Pueblo communities developed their own distinct histories, languages, and ceremonial traditions. These western Pueblo communities, while sharing certain broad features with their Rio Grande neighbors, including agriculture based on maize, beans, and squash, kiva-centered ceremonial life, and clan-based social organization, should not be collapsed into a single "western Pueblo culture." Each maintains its own specific history.

Hopi and the Mesas

The villages that came to be known as Hopi are situated on and around three mesas extending southward from Black Mesa in northeastern Arizona. Hopi oral tradition describes the formation of these communities as the outcome of a long process of clan migration, understood within Hopi tradition as the fulfillment of a covenant between the Hopi people and Maasaw, the guardian of the earth, under which different clans traveled extensively, in some accounts across much of the continent, before converging at the mesas. According to this tradition, each clan's arrival, its particular migration route, and the order in which clans settled at the mesas carry historical and ceremonial significance that continues to shape Hopi village organization and ritual responsibility today. This chapter's Primary Source Window examines this tradition, and the Hopi Tribe's own contemporary presentation of it, in detail.

Hopi agriculture adapted distinctively to the arid, high-elevation environment of the mesas, relying primarily on dry farming techniques that took advantage of natural runoff, careful field placement in washes and at the base of mesas, and drought-resistant crop varieties, a system examined further in Section 30.9. Hopi villages, while sharing language, in most cases, and broad ceremonial traditions, functioned as largely autonomous communities, each with its own specific history, leadership, and ceremonial responsibilities.

Zuni

The Zuni people, speaking a language unrelated to any other Pueblo language and considered a linguistic isolate within North America, occupied the Zuni River valley in what is now west-central New Mexico. Zuni towns, several of which existed in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries before later consolidation, maintained extensive trade relationships extending toward the Gulf of California, from which shell was obtained, and toward the Rio Grande Pueblo world and the southern Plains. Zuni ceremonial life, agriculture, and social organization developed along lines distinct from both the Hopi mesas and the Rio Grande towns, reflecting Zuni's own specific historical trajectory rather than a generic "western Pueblo" pattern.

Acoma

Acoma, built atop a high sandstone mesa in west-central New Mexico, speaks a Keresan dialect, connecting it linguistically, though not politically, to the Keresan-speaking towns of the Rio Grande valley despite considerable geographic distance from those towns. Acoma is frequently described, with appropriate historical caution about competing claims to such distinctions, as one of the longest continuously occupied settlements in North America, reflecting a community history that extends back through the period of Colorado Plateau migration examined in Section 30.2.

A related but chronologically distinct case is the area later occupied by Laguna Pueblo, southeast of Acoma. Ancestral Keresan-speaking populations lived in this area by at least the fourteenth century, and portions of the later Laguna settlement area contain fifteenth-century remains, reflecting the same broad pattern of Colorado Plateau-connected migration examined in Section 30.2. However, the modern Pueblo of Laguna, also known as Kawaik, was not established as a formally organized Pueblo community until 1699, well after the Pueblo Revolt of 1680 and the subsequent Spanish reconquest, and the National Park Service specifically notes that no such pueblo existed at the time of Coronado's expedition in 1540. This chapter accordingly treats ancestral Keresan communities in the Laguna area as part of the broader precolonial Southwest, while reserving the history of Laguna Pueblo itself, as a named political community, for the colonial-era chapters where it belongs.

Taken together, Hopi, Zuni, and Acoma demonstrate the same lesson that applies to the Rio Grande Pueblo towns: "Pueblo" describes a broad category of related but politically and, in most cases, linguistically distinct communities, not a single people. Each of these communities' migration and settlement histories, while sharing the broad pattern of Colorado Plateau dispersal and convergence examined in Section 30.2, followed its own specific course.

Section 30.5

Communities, Clans, Ceremony, and Political Authority

Having surveyed the major Pueblo communities geographically, this section examines the institutions that structured life within them, while resisting the temptation to describe a single universal "Pueblo constitution." Pueblo towns organized political and religious authority through overlapping institutions: clans, which traced descent, in many though not all Pueblo communities, through the mother's line; kivas, ceremonial structures, often partially below ground level, used for religious observance, instruction, and, in many communities, governance-related deliberation; and ceremonial societies, whose members held specific ritual responsibilities tied to agriculture, weather, healing, warfare, or other dimensions of community life.

Hopi village organization offers a useful, well-documented example of why local specificity matters, provided students do not then assume that Hopi patterns describe all Pueblo communities. Hopi clans are generally matrilineal, with clan membership, certain forms of property such as farmland, and specific ceremonial responsibilities passing through women. Political and ceremonial authority at Hopi has historically been organized primarily at the level of the individual village rather than through a single overarching Hopi government, with village chiefs, often drawn from particular clans holding traditional leadership responsibilities, and ceremonial leaders directing the complex annual cycle of Hopi religious observance. This village-centered pattern reflects Hopi's own specific history of clan migration and settlement, examined in Section 30.4, rather than a generic Pueblo template.

Other Pueblo communities organized authority differently. Some Rio Grande towns maintained forms of dual leadership dividing civil and religious authority between separate officials, with religious societies playing a central role in selecting civil leaders and overseeing agricultural and ceremonial timing. Descent rules, the relative authority of specific clans, and the degree to which ceremonial societies controlled civil affairs varied from town to town, reflecting each community's own history and composition, including the particular mix of earlier migrant populations that had joined to form it.

This variation should not be read as a sign of political immaturity or incompleteness. Pueblo communities, across this diversity of specific institutions, shared a broad underlying principle: that political authority was closely integrated with ceremonial and agricultural responsibility, and that community decision-making generally emphasized consensus, established ritual calendars, and the maintenance of relationships with the land, water, and ancestral beings understood to sustain the community's welfare.

Section 30.6

O'odham Worlds after the Hohokam Era

Chapter 17 examined Hohokam irrigation engineering, settlement, trade, and ceremonial life in the Sonoran Desert in detail, tracing a tradition of canal construction and desert agriculture that sustained large communities along the Gila and Salt Rivers for roughly a thousand years. By the fifteenth century, the elaborate, large-scale canal systems associated with the Hohokam archaeological period saw reduced use, and the large platform mound centers that had anchored regional ceremonial and political life in earlier centuries were no longer maintained in the same way.

A note on terminology is useful before proceeding. "Hohokam" is the conventional archaeological label for the material culture and canal-building tradition of this period, adopted from scholarly usage rather than from the communities themselves. O'odham communities, including the Salt River Pima-Maricopa Indian Community, generally use the O'odham term "Huhugam" to refer to their own ancestors, a term that carries a specifically ancestral and genealogical meaning within O'odham tradition rather than functioning simply as a synonym for the archaeological label. This chapter uses "Hohokam" when referring to the archaeological period and material record as such scholarship generally describes it, and "Huhugam" when referring specifically to O'odham ancestors as O'odham communities themselves understand that relationship.

As with the Colorado Plateau migrations examined in Section 30.2, the transition from the Hohokam archaeological period should not be described as the "disappearance" of a people. The Tohono O'odham Nation and allied Akimel O'odham communities today explicitly identify their Huhugam ancestors as the desert farmers of this earlier period, while emphasizing that the relationship involves continuity of knowledge, practice, and homeland rather than an unbroken, unchanged repetition of Hohokam-era settlement patterns. Desert environmental knowledge, including an intimate understanding of seasonal rainfall, flash flooding, and the specific plants and animals of the Sonoran Desert, continued to be developed, applied, and transmitted across this transition.

What changed, and what continued, remains an active area of archaeological and historical inquiry, and this chapter presents that uncertainty honestly rather than resolving it artificially. Communities ancestral to later O'odham peoples appear to have shifted toward smaller-scale settlements and a mix of canal irrigation, floodwater farming, and seasonal movement between desert village sites and agricultural fields, a pattern well suited to the Sonoran Desert's two-season rainfall regime. Akimel O'odham ("River People") communities continued to farm along the Gila and Salt Rivers using irrigation methods with clear continuities to earlier Hohokam practice, while Tohono O'odham ("Desert People") communities developed a distinctive economy centered on floodwater farming in desert washes, combined with extensive seasonal gathering, hunting, and movement between summer field villages and winter well villages suited to the limited and scattered water sources of the open desert.

The precise degree of population continuity between Hohokam-era communities and later O'odham peoples, whether through direct descent, absorption of Hohokam populations by incoming groups, or some combination of both processes, remains debated among archaeologists, and this chapter does not resolve that debate. What can be said with confidence is that O'odham communities of the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries possessed a deep, historically rooted body of desert agricultural and environmental knowledge, that they maintained a continuous presence across the Sonoran Desert landscape associated with Hohokam settlement, and that they developed their own late-precolonial history rather than simply persisting as a diminished remnant of an earlier civilization.

Section 30.7

Diné Homelands, Migration, and Community Formation

The Diné, commonly known by the exonym Navajo, developed a distinctive homeland and way of life in the San Juan Basin of the Four Corners region during the centuries examined in this chapter. Diné historical and ceremonial tradition describes the Dinétah, the area bounded loosely by the San Juan River and its tributaries, as the heart of the original Diné homeland, a landscape bound up with Diné origin narratives, ceremonial geography, and clan histories.

This chapter presents Diné historical knowledge and archaeological and linguistic reconstruction side by side, as distinct bodies of historical evidence that may converge, diverge, or illuminate different dimensions of the past, rather than as a contest in which one account confirms or disproves the other. Diné tradition describes the people's origins through a sequence of worlds and the activities of Holy People, alongside detailed clan histories explaining how different clans joined the Diné people over time, including some clan traditions that describe connections to earlier Pueblo or other Southwestern peoples, reflecting the same pattern of absorption and community formation already examined among Pueblo communities. Navajo Nation educational and cultural-resource materials describe a Dinétah period, prior to sustained European contact, during which Diné communities combined farming, hunting, and gathering with developing networks of exchange and leadership, adapting flexibly to the resources available across the San Juan Basin's varied terrain.

Archaeological and linguistic evidence addresses a different set of historical questions. Linguists classify the Diné language as part of the Southern Athabaskan branch of the larger Athabaskan language family, whose other branches are spoken much farther north, across the Subarctic interior of present-day Alaska and western Canada, examined in Chapter 34. This linguistic relationship indicates a historical connection between Southern Athabaskan speakers and Northern Athabaskan-speaking peoples, implying some form of population movement, over an extended and not precisely datable period, from northern regions toward the Southwest. Archaeological evidence for early Diné sites in the Dinétah region, including distinctive dwelling types and material culture, becomes more clearly identifiable from approximately the fifteenth or sixteenth century onward, though some archaeologists argue for an earlier presence not yet clearly visible in the archaeological record.

This chapter does not treat Diné historical tradition and archaeological or linguistic reconstruction as a contest in which one account must defeat the other. Diné oral tradition speaks to questions of meaning, identity, clan relationship, and the people's own understanding of their origins and obligations to their homeland, questions that archaeology and linguistics are not designed to answer. Archaeology and linguistics speak to questions of material chronology, population movement, and language relationship, questions that oral tradition, which compresses and structures time differently, does not attempt to answer in the same terms. Both are necessary for a full historical understanding, and this chapter's Historical Debate feature examines the specific, unresolved question of chronology and route in greater depth.

Section 30.8

Apache Peoples and the Expanding Athabaskan World

Diné communities were not the only Athabaskan-speaking peoples to become established in the Southwest during this period. Several distinct peoples, generally grouped under the broad exonym "Apache" though never unified as a single nation, established homelands across a wide arc of territory extending from the southern Plains borderlands through the mountains of present-day New Mexico and Arizona and into northern Mexico. These peoples, including communities later known to outsiders as Jicarilla, Mescalero, Chiricahua, Western Apache, Lipan, and others, should be understood as distinct communities with their own histories, dialects, and ways of life, related to one another and to the Diné through language but not forming a single political or cultural unit.

The economies of these Apache communities varied considerably according to environment and historical circumstance. Some communities, particularly those established on the southern Plains borderlands, developed economies centered substantially on bison hunting and seasonal mobility, maintaining trade relationships with Rio Grande Pueblo towns, examined further in Section 30.11, that exchanged bison products, hides, and meat for maize, cotton goods, and other Pueblo products. Other Apache communities, particularly in mountainous and forested regions, combined hunting and gathering with small-scale farming where terrain and water permitted. This economic diversity among Apache peoples parallels the diversity already observed among Pueblo and O'odham communities and should caution students against assuming that shared language implies a shared economy or way of life.

The timing and routes by which Apache peoples became established in the Southwest remain, like the parallel Diné case, a subject of ongoing scholarly inquiry rather than settled fact. Archaeological and linguistic evidence generally places the broader southward movement of Southern Athabaskan-speaking peoples, encompassing the ancestors of both Diné and Apache communities, within a wide window spanning roughly the fourteenth through sixteenth centuries, though scholars differ on more precise dating, routes of movement, and the degree to which this process involved a few large migrations as opposed to a longer, more gradual pattern of small-scale movement and settlement. National Park Service historical summaries generally place the broader Apachean presence in the Southwest as becoming clearly established by the fifteenth or sixteenth century, while cautioning that earlier, less visible occupation cannot be ruled out.

This chapter deliberately stops its treatment of Apache history at the threshold of sustained contact with Spanish and, later, Mexican and American forces. The deeper and better-documented history of Apache migration, economy, diplomacy, and warfare across the colonial and early national periods, together with the sustained conflicts of the nineteenth century often called the Apache Wars, belongs to Chapter 51 and later chapters in this textbook's fifth and seventh volumes. The purpose of this section is to establish Apache peoples as an integral, already well-established part of the Southwestern world described throughout this chapter, not to anticipate their later history.

Section 30.9

Farming, Water, and Environmental Knowledge

Having surveyed the major peoples of the late precolonial Southwest individually, this section draws a direct comparison among their agricultural systems, in order to demonstrate a central historical point: Southwestern agriculture was not a single technological tradition applied uniformly across the region, but a set of distinct solutions, each refined over generations, to the specific environmental challenges of a particular place.

Rio Grande Pueblo communities relied heavily on a combination of floodplain farming along the river itself, where periodic flooding deposited fertile silt and sustained relatively high soil moisture, and small-scale irrigation, in which communities diverted water from the river or its tributaries through hand-dug ditches to fields that would otherwise receive insufficient moisture. This approach required cooperative labor to construct and maintain irrigation works and cooperative management to allocate water among fields and households, responsibilities often integrated into the civil and ceremonial leadership structures examined in Section 30.5.

Hopi agriculture, by contrast, developed in an environment with far less reliable surface water. Hopi farmers practiced dry farming, planting maize deep in sandy soil where subsurface moisture persisted longer, selecting fields at the mouths of washes where runoff from summer storms would naturally concentrate, and developing maize varieties specifically suited to germinate from greater planting depths and to withstand the mesas' short, unpredictable growing season. This system required precise knowledge of local microenvironments, accumulated and transmitted over generations, rather than the cooperative labor of large-scale irrigation.

O'odham agriculture in the Sonoran Desert combined elements of both approaches but adapted them to a still more extreme environment. Akimel O'odham communities along the Gila and Salt Rivers maintained canal irrigation systems with clear roots in earlier Hohokam practice, examined in Section 30.6. Tohono O'odham communities, located away from permanent rivers, developed floodwater farming techniques that captured the brief, intense runoff produced by the desert's distinctive two-season rainfall pattern, directing storm water through carefully placed brush dams and field alignments into fields planted with drought-tolerant crop varieties, including distinctive desert-adapted beans and squash, timed precisely to the desert's summer monsoon season.

Diné and Apache communities, for their part, generally combined farming, where terrain and water allowed, with hunting, gathering, and, in the case of many Apache communities, substantial reliance on bison and other game, reflecting both the more limited agricultural potential of much of their territory and a way of life that placed greater emphasis on mobility and seasonal use of varied resources. This should not be read as a less sophisticated adaptation than Pueblo or O'odham farming; it represents a different, equally deliberate response to different environmental conditions and different historical circumstances.

The lesson of this comparison is that no single phrase, such as "Southwestern agriculture" or "desert farming," adequately describes the range of environmental knowledge developed across this region. Each system required its own specialized understanding of soil, water, climate, and plant biology, accumulated through generations of careful observation and experimentation, and each remains a significant historical achievement in its own right.

Section 30.10

Trade, Roads, and Regional Exchange

The diverse peoples examined in this chapter were connected to one another, and to peoples far beyond the Southwest, through extensive networks of trade and exchange, conducted without pack animals or wheeled vehicles, relying instead on human carrying before the later arrival of Spanish horses. These networks nonetheless moved substantial quantities of goods across considerable distances, organized through established trails, seasonal trading gatherings, and long-standing relationships between specific communities.

Turquoise, mined at several locations across the Southwest, including sites in the Cerrillos Hills near present-day Santa Fe, moved substantial distances within the region itself; isotopic analysis of turquoise recovered from ancestral Zuni sites in the El Morro Valley, for example, confirms a Cerrillos origin. Southwestern and Mesoamerican societies unquestionably took part in long-distance exchange, examined further in Chapter 14, but the older hypothesis that Southwestern turquoise supplied the turquoise found in major Aztec and Mixtec artifacts has been challenged by isotopic provenance studies, which instead indicate that most tested Mesoamerican turquoise mosaics derive from sources within Mesoamerica itself. This chapter accordingly treats Cerrillos and other Southwestern turquoise as a well-documented item of regional exchange, while treating its specific role in Mesoamerican assemblages as an open scientific question rather than an established fact. In the opposite direction, marine shell originating from the Gulf of California moved northward and eastward through O'odham and Hohokam-descended communities and onward into the Pueblo world, where it was worked into beads, pendants, and other ornaments. Cotton, cultivated in the warmer parts of the Southwest, particularly in Hopi and O'odham territory, was woven into textiles that circulated widely, including toward Pueblo communities in cooler areas less suited to cotton cultivation. Pottery, distinctive in style from region to region, moved along these same routes, and archaeologists use its distribution as one important form of evidence for reconstructing trade relationships.

Pueblo towns, particularly Pecos at the eastern edge of the Rio Grande world and Taos farther north, served as important points of exchange between the agricultural Pueblo world and Plains peoples, who brought bison products, hides, and other goods to trade for maize, cotton textiles, pottery, and turquoise, a relationship examined further in Section 30.11 and in Chapter 29's treatment of the Plains before the horse. Westward, trade routes connected Hopi and Zuni to California, through which shell and other coastal goods moved, and southward, routes connected the Southwest to communities in northern Mexico and, through extended chains of exchange, to Mesoamerican civilizations.

These exchange networks mattered for reasons beyond the specific goods they moved. They sustained diplomatic relationships, spread agricultural and technical knowledge, facilitated the ceremonial exchange examined in the following section, and, as later chapters note, provided established routes that Spanish expeditions would later use for their own purposes. The Southwest on the eve of sustained colonization was not an isolated region but a well-connected node within a much larger continental, and ultimately hemispheric, system of exchange.

Section 30.11

Diplomacy, Conflict, Migration, and Shared Landscapes

The peoples examined throughout this chapter did not live in separate, sealed compartments. Pueblo towns, O'odham communities, Diné households, and Apache bands interacted constantly, through relationships that combined cooperation and competition in ways this chapter presents honestly rather than resolving into a single, simplified pattern of either harmony or hostility.

Trade, examined in the previous section, was one major form of interaction, but it was accompanied by others. Intermarriage between Pueblo communities and neighboring Diné, Apache, or other groups occurred with some regularity, and the archaeological and traditional record of clan formation, particularly among the Diné, reflects the absorption of individuals and sometimes entire family groups from Pueblo and other communities into new kinship networks, a pattern consistent with the broader theme of community formation through convergence examined throughout this chapter. Ceremonial knowledge, including particular ritual practices, dances, and religious concepts, moved between communities as well, sometimes adopted and adapted rather than simply copied, reflecting ongoing cultural exchange alongside linguistic and political distinctness.

At the same time, competition and conflict were real and should not be minimized in an effort to present a uniformly cooperative picture. Pueblo towns, Apache bands, and other communities at times competed for access to water, agricultural land, hunting territory, and control of trade routes, and raiding for goods or captives occurred among various Southwestern peoples as it did in many regions across the continent, examined in the continental context of Chapter 53. Some Rio Grande Pueblo towns maintained defensive architecture and settlement patterns that suggest ongoing concern about raiding or conflict with neighboring peoples, including, in some periods, other Pueblo communities as well as Apache groups.

The historically accurate picture, then, is one of a shared, overlapping Southwestern landscape in which distinct peoples maintained separate identities, languages, and political structures while engaging continuously with one another through exchange, intermarriage, ceremonial borrowing, and, at times, competition or conflict. This pattern should not surprise students who have followed this textbook's treatment of other regions; it mirrors the combination of interdependence and rivalry documented among Indigenous nations across the continent in Chapter 55's broader discussion of Indigenous diplomacy. What makes the Southwestern case distinctive is the particular combination of settled agricultural towns, desert farming communities, and more mobile hunting, gathering, and mixed-subsistence peoples sharing a single, ecologically demanding region, well before Spanish colonization introduced livestock and transformed the region's economies in ways examined in Chapters 42 and 43.

Section 30.12

The Southwest before Sustained Spanish Colonization

By the middle of the sixteenth century, when the first substantial Spanish expeditions reached the region, the Southwest this chapter has described was a fully formed, internally diverse, and well-connected Indigenous world. Dozens of autonomous Pueblo towns, speaking several unrelated languages and organized through distinct combinations of clan, kiva, and ceremonial-society governance, lined the Rio Grande valley and occupied the mesas and valleys farther west at Hopi, Zuni, and Acoma, with ancestral Keresan communities also present in the area that would, after the colonial upheavals of the following century, become Laguna Pueblo. Akimel and Tohono O'odham communities farmed the Sonoran Desert using irrigation and floodwater techniques refined over centuries, maintaining historical and cultural continuity with the Hohokam past while developing their own distinct later history. Diné communities had established farming, hunting, and trading economies across the Dinétah region of the San Juan Basin, building clan networks that incorporated people and traditions from multiple sources. Several distinct Apache peoples occupied a wide range of environments from the southern Plains borderlands through the mountains and deserts farther south, with economies adapted to their particular circumstances.

These peoples were connected by extensive trade in turquoise, shell, cotton, pottery, and food; by patterns of intermarriage and ceremonial exchange; and by a shared, if sometimes contested, use of a demanding and varied regional landscape. None of this diversity, interconnection, or historical depth was a precondition that existed merely to be discovered or interrupted by the Spanish expeditions that reached the region beginning in 1539 and 1540. It was, rather, the outcome of centuries of Indigenous historical development entirely on its own terms: migration understood and carried out as continuity rather than collapse, the convergence of multiple origins into new communities, the refinement of agricultural systems suited to some of the most demanding farming environments on the continent, and the construction of social, political, and economic relationships among peoples who would, in the centuries that followed, confront Spanish, and later Mexican and American, colonization together and separately.

This chapter stops here deliberately. Francisco Vázquez de Coronado's expedition of 1540 to 1542, the Spanish northern frontier of New Spain, the sustained colonization that followed Juan de Oñate's 1598 expedition, the missionization and forced labor imposed on Pueblo communities, and the Pueblo Revolt of 1680 are each the subject of dedicated treatment in Chapters 42, 43, and 44. Readers who proceed to those chapters should carry with them the picture this chapter has constructed: not a vacant or declining landscape awaiting European arrival, but a populous, sophisticated, and historically dynamic Indigenous world whose peoples would meet colonization as established nations defending homelands, networks, and ways of life built over many centuries of their own historical agency.

Historical Method

From Ancestral Site to Living Community: Cultural Affiliation without One-to-One Descent

This chapter has repeatedly resisted a simple and tempting shorthand: the idea that a single archaeological "culture," such as Ancestral Pueblo or Hohokam, maps directly onto a single modern Indigenous nation, as though Mesa Verde were "the ancestors of" one specific Pueblo community and no other, or as though the Hohokam canal builders were "the ancestors of" a single O'odham community alone. This feature explains why that shorthand fails and what should replace it.

Archaeological evidence from the period of thirteenth- and fourteenth-century migration shows that communities arriving at a given destination, whether in the Rio Grande valley or at the Hopi mesas, frequently came from more than one point of origin on the Colorado Plateau and surrounding regions. Pottery styles, architectural details, and other material evidence at many later Pueblo sites show a blending of traditions associated with more than one earlier archaeological region. This evidence supports a model in which modern Pueblo communities often have legitimate historical and ceremonial relationships to several different ancestral regions and sites, rather than a single exclusive point of origin.

Diné clan histories illustrate a related but distinct version of the same principle. Clan traditions describe the incorporation of groups and individuals from varied origins, including, in some traditions, connections to earlier Pueblo or other Southwestern peoples, over the course of Diné community formation. This means that a simple model in which "Athabaskan migrants" and "established Southwestern farmers" remained two entirely separate populations understates the degree of interaction, absorption, and shared ancestry that developed over time.

This chapter distinguishes two related but separate ideas. The first is the broad historical notion of an ancestral or cultural relationship between a present-day community and an earlier people, place, or archaeological tradition, the kind of relationship this chapter has described throughout in discussing Pueblo, O'odham, Diné, and Apache connections to earlier Southwestern populations. The second is "cultural affiliation" as a specific, defined legal term under the Native American Graves Protection and Repatriation Act (NAGPRA), examined fully in Chapter 125, which requires a reasonable connection, established through geography, oral tradition, biological evidence, linguistics, folklore, material culture, or other lines of evidence, between human remains or cultural items and a particular federally recognized Indian Tribe or Native Hawaiian organization, based on a shared group identity that can be reasonably traced historically. The NAGPRA legal standard is narrower and more specific than the general historical idea of ancestral relationship, and does not require that a connection be the only such connection, or that it take the form of straightforward, unbroken descent from a single population. Students should not treat the two ideas as interchangeable: a people can hold a meaningful historical or ceremonial relationship to an ancestral site without that relationship necessarily meeting NAGPRA's specific legal definition in a given case, and vice versa.

Method in practice:

  1. When examining a claim that connects an archaeological site to a modern community, ask whether the evidence supports exclusive descent, partial descent, or a broader cultural and ceremonial relationship.
  2. Recognize that more than one modern nation may legitimately hold a historical relationship to the same ancestral site or region.
  3. Treat a descendant community's own account of its relationship to an ancestral site as significant historical evidence, to be considered alongside, not automatically subordinated to, archaeological and linguistic reconstruction.
  4. Avoid language implying single, exclusive lines of descent when the evidence instead points toward convergence, absorption, and multiple origins.
Historical Vocabulary
Pueblo

"Pueblo," from the Spanish word for "town" or "people," was applied by Spanish explorers and colonists to the settled, town-dwelling Indigenous communities they encountered along the Rio Grande and farther west, and it has remained in use ever since, both as a general descriptive term and, within United States federal Indian law, as a specific designation for the federally recognized nations descended from these communities.

As this chapter has emphasized, "Pueblo" does not name a single people, language, or government. It groups together dozens of historically distinct, politically autonomous communities, speaking several unrelated languages, including Tewa, Tiwa, Towa, Keres, Hopi (itself part of the Uto-Aztecan language family, unlike its Pueblo neighbors), and the linguistic isolate Zuni. What unites these communities under the broad term "Pueblo" is a shared, though variously expressed, pattern of settled town life, agriculture, kiva-centered ceremonialism, and clan-based social organization, developed through the specific regional histories examined throughout this chapter.

Students should use "Pueblo" as this chapter does: as a useful umbrella term for a historically related but internally diverse set of communities, always specifying the particular community, language, or region under discussion whenever precision matters.

Historical Vocabulary
Hisatsinom and Ancestral Pueblo

Ancestral Pueblo is the general scholarly term, examined fully in Chapter 18, used by archaeologists to describe the farming, architecture-building peoples of the Colorado Plateau whose major centers included Chaco Canyon and Mesa Verde. The term replaced an earlier label, Anasazi, a Diné-derived term meaning roughly "ancient enemies" or "ancestors of our enemies," which many Pueblo people consider inappropriate as a description of their own ancestors, since it originates outside Pueblo tradition itself. This textbook accordingly avoids "Anasazi" as a default term, using "Ancestral Pueblo" for general archaeological reference.

Hisatsinom is a Hopi term, meaning roughly "people of long ago," used within Hopi tradition and by the Hopi Tribe to describe Hopi ancestors specifically, including those associated with sites across the Colorado Plateau connected to Hopi clan migration traditions. Hisatsinom is not a simple substitute for "Ancestral Pueblo" in all contexts; it is a community-specific term reflecting Hopi tradition and should be used primarily when discussing Hopi perspectives on ancestral sites, rather than applied indiscriminately to every community or every archaeological site across the Colorado Plateau.

This distinction matters because other Pueblo communities maintain their own specific terms and understandings of their ancestors, which may differ from Hopi tradition in emphasis and detail even where the underlying archaeological evidence overlaps. Students should recognize "Ancestral Pueblo" as the general scholarly term for cross-community or archaeological discussion, and "Hisatsinom" as a Hopi-specific term appropriate within Hopi contexts.

Hopi Migration, Ancestral Footprints, and the Meaning of Archaeological Places

Creator/Community: The Hopi Tribe · Source type: Contemporary official tribal presentation of cultural tradition and land relationship · Context: Modern explanatory material describing Hopi migration tradition, the covenant with Maasaw, and the significance of ancestral sites across Hopitutskwa, the Hopi homeland

Context: The Hopi Tribe maintains and shares, through its own cultural and historic preservation materials, an account of Hopi origins and migration in which different clans undertook extensive journeys, understood as the fulfillment of a covenant with Maasaw, before converging at the mesas of northeastern Arizona. This tradition identifies ancient village sites, shrines, springs, trails, and inscribed images across a wide area of the Southwest and beyond as evidence of these migrations.

Paraphrase of the tradition's central claim: Hopi tribal materials describe ancestral sites, such as abandoned villages, petroglyphs, and other traces left across the landscape, as "footprints" documenting the clans' migrations, rather than as abandoned ruins marking the end of a people's presence in a place.

Analysis: This framing is historically significant for two reasons. First, it offers a direct, living Indigenous historical interpretation of the archaeological landscape examined throughout this chapter, one that understands dispersal from the Colorado Plateau not as decline but as the deliberate, meaningful fulfillment of migration. Second, it demonstrates that a community's own historical tradition can carry specific evidentiary content, identifying particular sites, routes, and the order of clan arrival, that complements and enriches the archaeological record examined in Chapter 18 and this chapter's Historical Method feature.

Limitations: This source is a contemporary presentation of a living tradition, prepared for public and educational purposes, rather than a fifteenth-century written document or a precise, dated archaeological itinerary. Oral tradition of this kind, as Chapter 1 explained, often structures time and meaning differently than a chronological historical record, compressing or symbolically representing events in ways appropriate to its own purposes. Historians should not treat it as a substitute for archaeological dating, nor dismiss it as merely symbolic; it functions best as a distinct and valuable form of historical evidence, to be read alongside, not instead of, other evidence.

Perspective: This source represents a Hopi tribal perspective, and specifically a form of knowledge maintained collectively within Hopi communities and shared under Hopi tribal authority. It does not represent the perspectives of Zuni, Acoma, Rio Grande Pueblo, O'odham, Diné, or Apache communities, each of which maintains its own distinct historical and migration traditions not represented in this excerpt.

Historical Debate

When Did Athabaskan-Speaking Peoples Become Part of the Southwest?

The question: When, by what routes, and through what process did the ancestors of the Diné and various Apache peoples become established in the Southwest? Was this a relatively rapid movement of a few large groups, or a longer, more gradual process involving many smaller movements over a wider span of time?

Interpretation A — A relatively late, archaeologically visible arrival: Some archaeologists emphasize that clearly identifiable Diné and Apache archaeological sites, including distinctive dwelling types and material culture associated with the Dinétah region, become reliably datable only from approximately the fifteenth or sixteenth century onward, suggesting that a securely documented Southern Athabaskan presence in the Southwest is a comparatively late development relative to the deep history of Pueblo and Hohokam-era occupation.
Interpretation B — An earlier but less archaeologically visible presence: Other scholars argue that Southern Athabaskan-speaking peoples may have been present in parts of the Southwest earlier than the clearest archaeological evidence suggests, on the grounds that highly mobile populations with less durable material culture, or populations occupying areas not yet extensively excavated, may leave a thinner or later-appearing archaeological signature than their actual historical presence would warrant.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on overlapping archaeological, linguistic, and traditional evidence: dated archaeological sites in the Dinétah region and elsewhere, the linguistic relationship between Southern and Northern Athabaskan languages, which implies a historical connection but does not by itself establish precise timing, and Diné and Apache oral traditions, which describe origins and migrations in their own terms and are not designed to yield calendar dates.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns chronology and the interpretation of an uneven archaeological record, not whether Athabaskan-speaking peoples became part of the Southwest, a point on which all available evidence agrees.

Why it matters: The precise timing affects how historians describe the relationship between incoming Athabaskan-speaking peoples and already-established Pueblo and O'odham communities, including how much overlap, interaction, and mutual influence occurred during the period of Pueblo community formation examined earlier in this chapter. This textbook does not resolve the debate; it presents the fourteenth through sixteenth centuries as the period during which Athabaskan-speaking peoples became clearly established in the Southwest, while noting that earlier, less visible occupation remains a live possibility.

Student task: Drawing on the evidence summarized above, explain what kind of new evidence, archaeological, linguistic, or traditional, might help resolve this debate, and explain why definitive resolution may remain difficult regardless of future discoveries.

Continuity and Change Over Time

The Southwest, 1250 and 1500

What Changed

  • Major Colorado Plateau centers such as Mesa Verde were no longer the primary population centers of the region, their communities having migrated toward the Rio Grande and the western Pueblo mesas
  • Large-scale Hohokam canal systems and platform mound centers in the Sonoran Desert gave way to different, more dispersed settlement and farming patterns
  • Athabaskan-speaking peoples became an established part of the Southwestern population, occupying the Dinétah region and territories extending across the southern Plains borderlands and surrounding mountains and deserts
  • New, often larger Pueblo towns formed through the convergence of migrants from multiple earlier regions, producing communities with blended material traditions

What Stayed the Same

  • Maize, bean, and squash agriculture remained the economic foundation of Pueblo and O'odham communities, adapted to each region's specific water conditions
  • Clan-based social organization and kiva-centered or comparable ceremonial life continued among Pueblo communities, even as specific communities and their composition changed
  • Extensive regional trade in turquoise, shell, cotton, pottery, and other goods continued across the Southwest, while broader exchange networks connected Southwestern communities with the Plains, California, northern Mexico, and Mesoamerica
  • The Southwest remained a region of multiple distinct peoples and languages rather than consolidating into a single political or cultural system

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both the substantial reorganization and the underlying continuities prevents two opposite errors: treating the fifteenth-century Southwest as a simple, unbroken extension of earlier Ancestral Pueblo and Hohokam societies, or treating the thirteenth-century migrations as a rupture so complete that later Pueblo, O'odham, Diné, and Apache communities bear no meaningful historical relationship to what came before.

Cause and Effect

Why Did Populations Reorganize after 1300?

Long-term causes

Generations of agricultural intensification on the Colorado Plateau had placed sustained pressure on soils, fuelwood, and water resources in some areas, while regional social and political relationships, including the legacy of Chaco's earlier regional system, had grown increasingly strained.

Short-term causes

A documented period of severe and sustained drought during the later thirteenth century reduced agricultural yields across much of the Colorado Plateau, placing acute stress on communities already facing longer-term pressures.

Effect

Communities across the Colorado Plateau dispersed over the following decades, with population moving toward the Rio Grande valley, the Hopi mesas, and the Zuni and Acoma regions, where water and agricultural conditions, and existing or developing community networks, offered better prospects.

This chapter's response

Treating migration as a complex response involving environmental pressure, social and political strain, and, according to Hopi and other traditions, deliberate fulfillment of migration obligations, rather than attributing the entire reorganization to drought alone.

Historical complexity: Archaeologists caution against treating drought as a simple, sufficient explanation on its own. Other regions experiencing comparable drought conditions did not see population movement on the same scale, suggesting that social, political, and possibly religious factors combined with environmental stress to produce the specific pattern of migration this chapter describes. Hopi tradition, for its part, frames at least some migration as the fulfillment of covenant obligations rather than simply a response to hardship, a perspective this chapter treats as a meaningful part of the historical picture rather than as incompatible with environmental explanation.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Year in a Pueblo Farming Community, circa 1500

Note: the description below is a carefully reconstructed composite drawn from archaeological and ethnohistorical evidence about Rio Grande Pueblo agricultural and ceremonial life in general. It does not describe a specific documented town, family, or year, and specific practices varied considerably among individual Pueblo communities.

Life in a Rio Grande Pueblo farming community followed a calendar bound closely to agriculture and ceremony. Early spring brought the clearing and preparation of fields along the river's floodplain and the maintenance of irrigation ditches, work generally organized cooperatively among households sharing a given water source. Planting of maize, beans, squash, and, in warmer locations, cotton followed as soil and water conditions allowed, accompanied by ceremonies intended to secure favorable growing conditions and to mark the beginning of the agricultural cycle.

Through the summer months, households tended fields, managed irrigation flow, and supplemented farming with hunting, particularly of rabbits and other small game, and the gathering of wild plants, including piñon nuts in years of good harvest farther from the valley floor. Women generally held primary responsibility for processing harvested crops, preparing food, and, in many communities, held significant roles in household and clan organization, while also producing pottery, an activity whose stylistic traditions, passed down within families and communities, provide much of the material evidence archaeologists use to trace trade and migration patterns examined throughout this chapter.

Autumn brought harvest, storage preparation, and further ceremonial observance connected to the agricultural cycle's completion, along with trading expeditions or the hosting of trading partners from Plains communities bringing bison products in exchange for maize, cotton goods, and pottery. Winter months saw reduced agricultural labor but continued ceremonial activity, including the instruction of children in religious knowledge and the preparation, within kivas and ceremonial societies, for the coming year's ritual calendar.

Throughout the year, children learned agricultural, craft, and ceremonial knowledge primarily through direct participation alongside adults rather than through separate, formalized instruction, a pattern of knowledge transmission common across many of the communities examined in this chapter and consistent with the broader historical pattern of oral and practical education discussed in Chapter 1.

A final caution is necessary. Much of the specific detail historians use to reconstruct scenes such as this one derives from early colonial-era documentation and later ethnographic study, both of which postdate the period this chapter examines and were often produced by outside observers. Such sources are valuable, but institutions and practices they document cannot be assumed to have existed in identical form in every Pueblo community circa 1500, nor projected backward or across communities without caution. This composite should be read as a reasonable, evidence-informed reconstruction, not as a documented account of any specific fifteenth-century household.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced the transformation of the Southwest from the archaeological world of Chaco, Mesa Verde, Mogollon communities, and Hohokam canal builders, examined in Volume II, into the historically identifiable Pueblo, O'odham, Diné, and Apache worlds encountered by Spanish expeditions in the sixteenth century. The chapter's central argument has been that this transformation represents migration, reorganization, and community formation, not decline or disappearance, and that modern Indigenous nations of the Southwest frequently hold legitimate historical relationships to more than one ancestral region or archaeological tradition.

The chapter examined the dispersal of Colorado Plateau communities during the thirteenth century and the subsequent growth of Rio Grande Pueblo towns, organized around Tewa, Tiwa, Towa, and Keres languages, and of western Pueblo communities at Hopi, Zuni, and Acoma, which, despite some shared linguistic relationships, such as the Keresan connection between Acoma and the Rio Grande Keresan towns, maintained distinct migration histories and ceremonial and political institutions. It examined the continuation and adaptation of Hohokam-era desert farming knowledge among Akimel and Tohono O'odham communities, and the establishment of Diné communities in the Dinétah region of the San Juan Basin, alongside the development of several distinct Apache peoples across a wide range of environments from the southern Plains borderlands through the mountains and deserts farther south.

Throughout, the chapter has emphasized methodological caution: resisting simple one-to-one connections between archaeological "cultures" and modern nations, treating oral tradition and archaeological or linguistic evidence as distinct bodies of evidence that may converge, diverge, or illuminate different dimensions of the past, and presenting genuine scholarly uncertainty, particularly surrounding the timing and routes of Athabaskan-speaking migration, honestly rather than resolving it artificially.

The chapter also compared the distinct agricultural systems developed across the region, Rio Grande irrigation and floodplain farming, Hopi dry farming, and O'odham canal and floodwater farming, demonstrating that no single technological tradition describes Southwestern agriculture as a whole. It examined extensive regional exchange in turquoise, shell, cotton, pottery, and other goods, moved across hundreds of miles without pack animals or wheeled transport, as well as the broader networks that connected Southwestern peoples with the Plains, California, northern Mexico, and Mesoamerica. It examined, with appropriate honesty about both cooperation and conflict, the relationships among Pueblo, Diné, Apache, and O'odham communities, including trade, intermarriage, ceremonial exchange, and periodic competition or conflict over land, water, and resources.

The chapter closed at the threshold of sustained Spanish colonization, describing a Southwest that was populous, internally diverse, economically sophisticated, and thoroughly interconnected, the product of centuries of Indigenous historical development conducted entirely on its own terms. This foundation prepares readers for the colonial encounters examined in Chapters 42 through 44, in which these same peoples confronted Spanish missionization, forced labor, and, ultimately, organized resistance in the Pueblo Revolt of 1680.

These themes, migration as continuity, the limits of archaeological-to-modern correspondence, the diversity hidden within broad regional categories such as "Pueblo" or "Apache," and the combination of interdependence and conflict among neighboring peoples, recur throughout this textbook's treatment of Indigenous North America and should inform how students approach the regional chapters that follow.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Pueblo
A broad historical and linguistic category describing dozens of distinct, politically autonomous, town-dwelling Indigenous communities of the Southwest, not a single people or government.
Hisatsinom
A Hopi term for ancestral people, used within Hopi tradition to describe Hopi ancestors specifically, distinguished from the general archaeological term "Ancestral Pueblo."
Ancestral Pueblo
The general scholarly term for the archaeological peoples of the Colorado Plateau, examined fully in Chapter 18, whose descendants are connected to multiple modern Pueblo communities through complex migration rather than single-line descent.
Kiva
A ceremonial structure, often partially below ground level, used among Pueblo communities for religious observance, instruction, and, in many communities, governance-related deliberation.
Clan
A kinship group, often but not universally matrilineal among Pueblo communities, that structures descent, property, ceremonial responsibility, and, in some communities, political leadership.
Dry farming
An agricultural technique, developed extensively by Hopi farmers, relying on deep planting, careful field placement, and natural runoff rather than irrigation.
Floodwater farming
An agricultural technique, developed by Tohono O'odham farmers, capturing brief, intense desert runoff through field placement and small diversion structures.
Irrigation agriculture
Farming sustained by water deliberately diverted from a river or stream through constructed ditches or canals, practiced along the Rio Grande and by Akimel O'odham communities along the Gila and Salt Rivers.
Dinétah
The region of the San Juan Basin associated with early Diné history and considered, within Diné tradition, the heart of the original Diné homeland.
Athabaskan
A large language family whose southern branches are spoken by Diné and Apache peoples and whose northern branches are spoken across the Subarctic, examined further in Chapter 34.
Cultural affiliation
Under NAGPRA, a reasonable connection between human remains or cultural items and an Indian Tribe or Native Hawaiian organization based on shared group identity, narrower than the broader historical idea of ancestral relationship used elsewhere in this chapter.
Migration tradition
Structured historical and ceremonial knowledge, such as Hopi clan migration narratives, describing a people's movement across the landscape and its historical and religious significance.
Desert adaptation
The range of agricultural, water-management, and subsistence strategies developed by O'odham and other Southwestern peoples to farm successfully in arid environments.
Matrilineal
A descent system, common though not universal among Pueblo communities including Hopi, in which clan membership and certain property pass through the mother's line.
Ceremonial society
An organization within a Pueblo community holding specific ritual responsibilities tied to agriculture, weather, healing, warfare, or other aspects of communal life.
Hohokam legacy
The historical and cultural inheritance, including irrigation knowledge and homeland continuity, that O'odham communities trace to the archaeological period scholars label Hohokam.
Huhugam
The O'odham term for ancestors, used by O'odham communities to describe the desert-farming peoples of the period archaeologists call Hohokam, carrying a specifically genealogical and ancestral meaning within O'odham tradition.
Regional exchange network
The interconnected trade routes that moved turquoise, shell, cotton, pottery, and other goods across the Southwest and linked Southwestern communities with the Plains, California, northern Mexico, and wider Mesoamerican exchange systems.
Turquoise trade
The long-distance exchange of turquoise within the Southwest, including material from sources such as the Cerrillos Hills, documented as far as the Zuni area by isotopic analysis. Earlier claims that Southwestern turquoise supplied major Aztec and Mixtec assemblages have been challenged by isotopic provenance research pointing instead to Mesoamerican sources.
Borderlands
Regions where the homelands or economic activity of different peoples overlapped or came into regular contact, such as the southern Plains borderlands associated with several Apache communities.
Covenant
Within Hopi tradition, the sacred agreement between the Hopi people and Maasaw, understood as the basis for Hopi migration and settlement at the mesas.
Footprints (migration evidence)
A term used within Hopi tradition to describe ancestral sites, trails, shrines, and artifacts understood as evidence of clan migration rather than as abandoned ruins.
Dispersal
The historically preferred term for the thirteenth-century movement of Colorado Plateau populations, chosen over "abandonment" or "collapse" because it does not imply ending or failure.
Linguistic isolate
A language, such as Zuni, with no known relationship to any other language family, complicating any attempt to link it linguistically to a single migration history.
Akimel O'odham
"River People," O'odham communities practicing canal irrigation along the Gila and Salt Rivers with clear continuities to Hohokam-era practice.
Tohono O'odham
"Desert People," O'odham communities practicing floodwater farming and seasonal movement between field and well villages across the open Sonoran Desert.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has recovered a period of Southwestern history that is too often skipped over entirely: the two and a half centuries between the dispersal of Chaco and Mesa Verde communities and the arrival of Spanish expeditions, during which the historically identifiable peoples of the Southwest, Rio Grande and western Pueblo communities, Akimel and Tohono O'odham communities, Diné households in the Dinétah homeland, and several distinct Apache peoples, built the nations and homelands that persist to the present day.

The chapter's larger lesson extends well beyond the Southwest itself. Migration, properly understood, is rarely a simple story of ending and beginning. It is usually a story of continuity carried forward through change, of communities converging from multiple origins to form something new while remaining connected to what came before, and of historical knowledge, whether archaeological, linguistic, or oral, illuminating different facets of a single complicated past. These lessons apply as much to the Haudenosaunee, Anishinaabe, and Plains peoples examined in neighboring chapters of this volume as they do to the Pueblo, Diné, Apache, and O'odham worlds examined here.

This chapter also modeled a specific historical method that students should carry forward: resisting the temptation to draw a single, clean line between an archaeological culture and a modern nation, and instead asking what the full range of available evidence, including the historical knowledge that descendant communities themselves maintain, actually supports. That method will recur throughout this textbook, particularly in Volume IV's treatment of early colonial encounters, where the question of who inherited which homelands, relationships, and historical claims becomes directly relevant to understanding how Pueblo, Diné, Apache, and O'odham peoples confronted Spanish colonization.

Readers proceeding to Chapter 31 and the chapters that follow should carry forward the central image this chapter has built: not a landscape of ruins awaiting discovery, but a populous, diverse, interconnected Indigenous world, built by many hands and many histories, standing fully formed at the threshold of a colonial era that is the subject of this textbook's next volume.
