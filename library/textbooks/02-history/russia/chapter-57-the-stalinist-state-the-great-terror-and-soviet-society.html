Part X — Stalinism and the Second World War

The Stalinist State, the Great Terror, and Soviet Society

A History of Russia: From the Eurasian World to the Russian Federation

Chapter Opening

This chapter distinguishes three things that are often run together: the public trials that drew the world's attention, the secret mass operations that struck hundreds of thousands of ordinary people, and the society that kept working, studying, marrying, and building around both.

Butovo lies on what was then the southern edge of Moscow, on an estate that had passed through aristocratic and monastic hands before the security police took it over. Between August 1937 and October 1938, according to research by the Memorial society and archival records, more than twenty thousand people were shot there and buried in long trenches. Many were ordinary: peasants, workers, priests, railway employees, teachers, and people of Polish, German, Latvian, and other minority backgrounds, alongside party officials and former Bolsheviks. Their families were commonly told only that the person had been sentenced to "ten years without the right of correspondence," a formula that concealed the execution. Not until the mid-1950s did relatives begin to learn what it meant, and the site's history was not publicly documented until the late Soviet and post-Soviet period.

This chapter studies how a state that proclaimed socialist equality, popular participation, and social advancement also built a system of dictatorship, mass imprisonment, executions, and pervasive fear. Its central historical question is how these features coexisted, and how the same decade could produce both the Stalin Constitution and the Great Terror, both new opportunities for young specialists and the arrest of their predecessors. The chapter concentrates on the years 1934 to 1939, with background from the late 1920s and early 1930s necessary to explain the party, security, and camp institutions that Chapters 54 through 56 introduced.

The chapter's first task is to separate related but different phenomena. The Moscow Trials of 1936, 1937, and 1938 were public, staged proceedings against prominent figures, widely reported in the Soviet and foreign press. The "mass operations" of 1937 and 1938, carried out under secret NKVD orders, were far larger. They targeted categories of people defined by past status, nationality, or alleged connections abroad, and they were administered through quotas, regional three-person panels, and summary procedures. A third dimension was the purge of institutions: the party apparatus, the state administration, economic management, and the Red Army. A fourth was the Gulag, which received many of those not executed. Treating these as one undifferentiated event obscures how the repression worked and who experienced it.

The chapter also resists two simplifications. One reduces Stalinism to the personality of Stalin; the other treats Soviet society as a mass of passive victims. Historians have shown that Stalin was central, approving execution lists and shaping policy, but that the terror also depended on institutions, quotas, bureaucratic incentives, local initiative, mutual denunciation, and widespread ideological belief that internal enemies existed. At the same time, the evidence for large-scale killing and imprisonment is strong and must not be softened. The chapter keeps both points in view: central direction and institutional participation, repression and social mobility, fear and sincere commitment.

The evidence has changed dramatically since 1991. For decades, historians relied on émigré memoirs, defector accounts, the published trial transcripts, and the testimony of survivors. After the opening of Soviet archives, researchers gained access to NKVD orders, Politburo decisions, execution lists, internal statistics, and interrogation files. These sources transformed estimates of scale and clarified how decisions were made, but they also carry their own limits: the files were produced by the agency that carried out the repression, often under pressure to show results, and many contain fabricated testimony. This chapter therefore treats archival statistics, trial transcripts, memoirs, and survivor testimony as different kinds of evidence with different strengths.

The chapter connects Russian and Soviet history to wider currents. Comparable mass violence occurred elsewhere in the 1930s, and the Soviet Union was embedded in an international system that included the Comintern, the Spanish Civil War, and growing fear of Germany and Japan. Foreign observers debated whether the Moscow Trials were genuine. And the terror was not confined to the Russian Soviet Federative Socialist Republic: it struck Soviet Ukraine, Belarus, Georgia, Kazakhstan, Central Asia, the Far East, and other regions, and it fell especially heavily on certain minorities. By the chapter's end, students should be able to explain how the system functioned, who it affected, what historians know and dispute, and why it remains a charged subject of memory in the Russian Federation and in the other successor states.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how the relationships among Stalin, the Communist Party, the state apparatus, and the security police sustained dictatorship beyond one man's personality.
  2. Describe the nomenklatura system and the Politburo and Secretariat as instruments of appointment, patronage, and control.
  3. Analyze the legal framework of political repression, including Article 58 and the tension between formal legality and arbitrary practice.
  4. Distinguish the public Moscow Trials from the secret mass operations of 1937–1938 and explain the purposes of each.
  5. Trace the escalation of repression from the Kirov assassination of 1934 to the end of the extraordinary operations in 1938.
  6. Evaluate archival estimates of arrests, executions, and camp populations, identifying what they establish and what remains uncertain.
  7. Explain the operation of NKVD Order No. 00447 and the national operations, including quotas, troikas, and categories of targeted people.
  8. Assess the consequences of the party, administrative, and military purges for the composition and functioning of Soviet institutions.
  9. Describe the Gulag as an administrative institution, a system of coerced labor, and a human experience, distinguishing it from other forms of forced labor.
  10. Interpret the 1936 Constitution and the simultaneous expansion of repression as evidence of the contradictions of the regime.
  11. Analyze social mobility, denunciation, belief, and fear as features of everyday Soviet life.
  12. Compare major historiographical interpretations of the Great Terror and explain how archival evidence changed them.

  7. Why could many citizens simultaneously experience upward mobility, ideological commitment, and terror?

Historical Context

By 1934, Stalin had defeated the Left, United, and Right oppositions inside the party (Chapter 54), and the party had been committed to forced industrialization and collectivization (Chapters 55 and 56). Those campaigns had themselves involved extensive coercion: dekulakization and the deportation of peasants to "special settlements," grain requisitions, the famines of 1932–1933, and the expansion of the camp system. They also left a legacy of social dislocation, rural hostility, and a regime acutely conscious of resistance, real and imagined. The mechanisms and habits of that earlier coercion, including the categorizing of people as "class enemies," administrative sentencing, and quotas, formed the immediate background to the Great Terror.

The Bolshevik party had been a centralized, hierarchical organization from the Civil War onward, and the ban on factions adopted in 1921 (Chapter 53) narrowed the scope for open disagreement. The security police had a long lineage from the Cheka of 1917 through the GPU and OGPU, and Red Terror practice (Chapter 52) set precedents for extrajudicial punishment. In the early 1930s the regime also faced a changing international environment: Hitler came to power in Germany in 1933, Japan consolidated its position in Manchuria, and Soviet leaders spoke of capitalist encirclement. Many officials believed war was coming and that internal enemies would ally with external ones. Such beliefs did not by themselves cause the terror, but they shaped how evidence was interpreted and how threats were imagined.

Socially, the Soviet Union in the mid-1930s was a country of rapid urbanization, scarcity, crowded housing, and large-scale population movement. Millions of peasants had moved to cities and construction sites; a new generation of workers, technicians, and administrators was being trained. The regime promoted rapid promotion of workers and Communists into managerial and technical roles (vydvizhenie), and the first Five-Year Plans had created an expanded state bureaucracy and an enlarged economic administration. The relaxation of some policies after 1933, including rhetoric about "a more cheerful life," and the claim in 1934 that the Congress of Victors had marked socialist success, formed the paradoxical backdrop against which a new wave of repression began.

Terminology matters here. "The Great Terror" (a phrase popularized by Robert Conquest's 1968 book) and "the Great Purge" are conventional names for the period of roughly 1936 to 1938, but they can imply a single campaign. Soviet documents distinguished "purges" (chistki) of party membership, criminal prosecutions, "mass operations" of the NKVD, and administrative sentences. The word "Stalinist" in this chapter refers to the political system as it operated under Stalin's leadership, not to a claim that every action originated in his individual decisions. Finally, "Soviet" and "Russian" are not interchangeable: the repression took place across the USSR, and some of its heaviest local patterns occurred outside the Russian Soviet Federative Socialist Republic.

Section 57.1

The Stalinist State and the Concentration of Political Power

The Soviet political system of the 1930s had two overlapping structures. The formal state consisted of soviets at every level, the Council of People's Commissars (Sovnarkom), the commissariats, and the courts. The party consisted of the Central Committee, the Politburo, the Secretariat, and regional and local committees. In practice the party directed the state: party committees supervised state officials, and party decisions were transmitted as government decrees. Historians therefore often speak of a "party-state," a system in which formal state institutions existed but political authority lay mainly in the party hierarchy.

Stalin's position within this system rested on the offices and networks he had accumulated during the 1920s (Chapter 54). As General Secretary he controlled the Secretariat, which managed personnel and communications between the center and the provinces. He did not hold the formal post of head of government until 1941; Vyacheslav Molotov chaired Sovnarkom from 1930, and Mikhail Kalinin was the nominal head of state. Stalin's authority was personal and informal as well as institutional: it depended on his ability to arbitrate among colleagues, to approve or reject proposals, and to command the loyalty of regional leaders who owed their careers to the center.

The concentration of power was real but not total. Scholars emphasize that a regime proclaiming centralized control still faced practical limits: poor communications across a vast territory, shortages of trained officials, local leaders who protected clients or exaggerated results, and a bureaucracy that could slow, distort, or exceed central instructions. Understanding the Great Terror requires holding both facts together. Stalin and his colleagues could set campaigns in motion and approve large numbers of death sentences, yet the campaigns were carried out by thousands of party and police officials whose choices, incentives, and fears shaped outcomes.

For this reason the chapter does not treat Stalinism only as a matter of Stalin's character. The system's features, including emergency legal procedures, a security police with broad powers, a party that regulated careers, and an ideology that interpreted dissent as class enmity, would have shaped events under any leader of the same movement, though historians disagree on how far. The question of Stalin's personal responsibility, and the evidence for it, is examined directly in §57.22.

The workings of the center can be traced in surviving records. Stalin's correspondence with Molotov and Kaganovich, much of it written while Stalin vacationed in the south, shows him commenting on drafts, ordering personnel changes, and pressing colleagues to act against officials he distrusted. The logbook of visitors to his Kremlin office, published from the archives, records who met him and for how long, and shows that policy was discussed in small, shifting groups rather than in formal sessions. The party congresses and Central Committee plenums, by contrast, increasingly served to ratify decisions and to stage unanimity. The Seventeenth Congress of 1934, officially called the "Congress of Victors," celebrated the achievements of collectivization and industrialization, yet within a few years a large share of its delegates and elected leaders had been arrested (§57.2).

Two further features of the system deserve emphasis. First, the regime's institutions overlapped and competed: party secretaries, state commissariats, the NKVD, and the procuracy each had their own chains of command and interests, and the leadership often used one to check another. Second, the system depended on information produced by its own agents. Reports from the NKVD, from party inspectors, and from the press shaped the leadership's picture of the country, and officials at every level had incentives to present themselves as vigilant and their rivals as suspect. A state that relies on such information can come to act on a picture of threat that is partly generated by its own procedures, a dynamic that helps explain the escalation of 1936 and 1937.

Section 57.2

The Politburo, Secretariat, Nomenklatura, and Stalin's Inner Circle

The Politburo was the party's highest policy body, a small group of full and candidate members who met irregularly and increasingly decided matters by informal consultation and written polling. In the 1930s its leading figures included Molotov, Lazar Kaganovich, Kliment Voroshilov, Sergo Ordzhonikidze, Anastas Mikoyan, and, after Kirov's death, Andrei Zhdanov. Historians such as Oleg Khlevniuk, using archival records of Stalin's correspondence and meeting schedules, have shown that Stalin worked closely with a small circle, often away from Moscow in the south during the summer, and that his colleagues proposed, drafted, and executed many measures. The circle was neither a collective check on Stalin nor merely his instrument; its members shared his assumptions about enemies and threats, competed for his favor, and were themselves vulnerable.

That vulnerability was real. Ordzhonikidze, head of heavy industry and one of Stalin's oldest associates, died by suicide in February 1937 after the arrest of colleagues and relatives and a tense dispute over the treatment of economic managers. Members of the Central Committee elected at the 17th Party Congress of 1934 suffered heavily; Nikita Khrushchev stated in 1956 that of 139 members and candidates elected at that congress, 98 were arrested and shot. Historians treat that figure as a starting point and have refined the numbers, but the pattern of severe losses among senior figures is not in doubt.

The nomenklatura system tied this elite to a wider body of officials. Lists of positions, from factory directors to provincial party secretaries, were assigned to the appointing authority of specific party committees. Appointment, promotion, and dismissal required party approval, which meant that the party controlled careers across the whole administration. The system created loyalty and dependence: those appointed owed their positions to patrons, and patrons relied on clients. It also created networks that could be attacked as "family circles" or conspiracies, a charge that frequently appeared in the 1937 campaign against regional party leaders.

The practice of patronage was central. Regional party first secretaries, such as those governing large provinces and union republics, held enormous local power: they supervised industry, agriculture, and local police, and they placed trusted subordinates in key positions. Contemporaries and later historians sometimes compared them to provincial "princes." The center needed such figures to implement its policies, but it also distrusted them. In 1937 Stalin and his colleagues attacked "family circles" (semeistvennost), meaning networks in which officials protected clients and concealed failures. Many regional secretaries were then accused of just that, and the accusation proved fatal for numbers of them. The same patronage ties that had given a leader local control became evidence of conspiracy once the leadership decided that conspiracy existed.

Nikolai Yezhov's career shows how personnel work and repression connected. He rose through the Central Committee apparatus, became a Secretary of the Central Committee and chairman of the Party Control Commission in 1935, and in 1936 took over the NKVD. His combination of party and police posts illustrates the interpenetration of the two hierarchies: the party leadership directed the security police, and the security police investigated the party's own members. For the officials involved, the system created strong incentives to demonstrate vigilance and severe penalties for appearing lenient.

Historical Vocabulary
Nomenklatura

Definition: A list of important positions whose appointment required approval by a specified party committee, and, by extension, the class of officials who held such positions.

Historical context: The system took shape in the 1920s and was broadly in place by the 1930s, with lists for the central committee, regional committees, and lower levels. It governed the careers of party officials, managers, military commanders, and many professionals.

Why it matters: Nomenklatura gave the party control over personnel without formal legislation, tied officials to their sponsors, and made mass replacement of cadres possible during the purges. The word later became a shorthand for the Soviet privileged stratum; students should distinguish the original administrative meaning from that broader, often polemical use.

Section 57.3

Law, Political Crime, and the Institutions of Repression

The principal legal instrument for political prosecution was Article 58 of the 1926 criminal code of the Russian republic, copied with variations by other republics. It listed "counterrevolutionary" offenses in numbered paragraphs, including treason, espionage, armed rebellion, terrorism, wrecking (deliberate sabotage of industry or transport), and "anti-Soviet agitation." The definitions were broad. Wrecking could cover almost any industrial failure, and agitation could include private remarks. A 1934 law on treason added liability for adult family members of certain offenders. Because prosecution depended on interpretation of intent, accusations could be shaped to fit campaigns.

Several institutions processed cases. Regular courts handled many offenses; the Military Collegium of the Supreme Court tried major political cases, including the Moscow Trials. The Procuracy, a system of prosecutors under a USSR Procurator, was meant to supervise legality, and Andrei Vyshinsky, appointed Procurator in 1935, was its best-known head. Yet from 1934 the NKVD's Special Council (Osoboe soveshchanie) could impose sentences of exile or camp confinement administratively, without a trial. In 1937–1938 the regional "troikas" and national "dvoikas" went further (§57.10). The coexistence of codified procedure and extrajudicial sentencing is one of the defining features of the period.

Vyshinsky's legal writing justified practices that departed from earlier norms, but it is often misdescribed. The phrase that confession is the "queen of evidence" is popularly attributed to him. His published discussion of evidence in fact rejected that maxim in its traditional form, which had treated a confession as proof requiring no further support, and insisted that confessions must be tested against other evidence. At the same time he assigned confessions exceptional weight in cases of political conspiracy, where, he argued, the nature of clandestine organizations meant that participants' own testimony would often be the most direct evidence available. The qualification mattered less in practice than the emphasis: combined with interrogation methods that included sleep deprivation, threats against relatives, and, as the Central Committee later acknowledged, physical coercion, the weight placed on confessions rewarded investigators who produced them, and the requirement of corroboration was routinely satisfied by other coerced testimony. The formal legality of the system therefore offered little protection to defendants. Students should take care not to describe the system as simply lawless: the regime used law as an instrument, and the tension between legal form and arbitrary practice was part of how the terror was presented and justified.

Other legal developments widened the reach of repression. A decree of April 1935 extended criminal liability for certain offenses, including theft and violence, to children from the age of twelve. The 1934 treason law, which made adult family members of a defector liable to punishment, introduced the principle of collective responsibility that would reappear in Order No. 00486 of 1937 against wives of "traitors" (§57.12). The Special Council of the NKVD, established in 1934, could initially impose up to five years of exile or camp confinement without a trial, and its powers were later broadened. In 1937–1938 the troikas and dvoikas, discussed in §§57.10 and 57.11, effectively replaced judicial procedure for the largest groups of victims.

Formal legality was not simply a facade. The regime enacted codes, published decrees, and retained courts and prosecutors, and many ordinary criminal cases were processed through these institutions. Some officials took legal norms seriously and, at moments, resisted the demands of the security police. Yet the system gave priority to political expediency over procedure whenever the leadership defined a case as a matter of state security. The inconsistency between declared law and practice is one reason that the later rehabilitation of victims (Chapters 61 and 65) required the review of tens of thousands of case files rather than a simple annulment of sentences.

Section 57.4

The Cult of Personality and Stalinist Political Legitimacy

The cult of Stalin grew rapidly in the 1930s. Press, posters, speeches, and public ceremonies presented him as Lenin's heir, the "father" of the people, the architect of industrialization, and a nearly infallible leader. Birthday celebrations, naming of places and institutions after him, and ritual praise at party meetings made declarations of loyalty a routine feature of public life. Stalin himself sometimes expressed discomfort with excesses of flattery while allowing the practice to expand. His image also shaped the political language of the trials: defendants were portrayed as traitors to the leader as well as to the country.

Legitimacy rested on more than the cult. The regime claimed to be building socialism, defending the revolution, and modernizing a backward country; many citizens accepted some or all of these claims, particularly those who benefited from education, jobs, and promotion (§57.19). Foreign threats and the memory of the Civil War reinforced the argument that vigilance was necessary. The leadership also presented repression as a defense of the people against saboteurs, spies, and conspirators, so that terror and legitimacy were bound together rather than opposed.

Public ritual extended to the campaigns of repression. Mass meetings demanded severe sentences, newspapers printed letters from workers condemning "enemies," and loyalty required public participation. Chapter 58 treats the broader culture of socialist realism, education, and propaganda; here the point is that political rituals made participation visible and tied ordinary people to the system's claims, even where private belief was uncertain.

Several landmarks mark the cult's growth. Stalin's fiftieth birthday in December 1929 was the first major occasion of public praise. By the mid-1930s his name had been attached to cities, factories, and canals, and school children were taught the slogan of gratitude to him for a happy childhood. In 1938 the party published the Short Course of the history of the All-Union Communist Party (Bolsheviks), a canonical account in which Stalin appeared as Lenin's closest collaborator and in which the defeated oppositionists were portrayed as traitors from the beginning. It became a compulsory text in party education and was issued in huge printings. Its version of the past was thus inseparable from the political trials: rewriting history and condemning enemies were parts of the same project. The Stalin Prizes, by contrast, were not instituted until 20 December 1939, and belong to the later consolidation of the cult.

Historians disagree about how the cult functioned. Some stress its manufactured, coercive character; others note that it drew on older Russian and revolutionary traditions of reverence for leaders, on real admiration for the leadership's achievements among some citizens, and on the desire for a unifying figure in a society undergoing rapid change. The evidence of private opinion is limited, because candid speech was dangerous, and police summaries of public mood record both what people said and what the police wished to report. What is clear is that public avowals of devotion became an expected element of political life, so that silence or insufficient enthusiasm could itself be noticed.

Section 57.5

The NKVD: Organization, Surveillance, and Political Control

In July 1934 the OGPU, the political police that had succeeded the Cheka, was merged into a new all-Union People's Commissariat of Internal Affairs (NKVD). The commissariat's Main Directorate of State Security (GUGB) handled political cases, counterintelligence, and surveillance, while other directorates supervised ordinary police, border troops, fire services, and the Gulag. The merger gave a single agency control of investigation, arrest, imprisonment, and forced labor. Its personnel numbered in the hundreds of thousands, with a network of informers drawn from workplaces, apartment buildings, and institutions.

Surveillance relied on reports, wiretaps, mail interception, and denunciations from ordinary citizens as well as paid informers. Files accumulated on people with previous political affiliations, such as former members of other socialist parties, former oppositionists, former officers of the old army, and people with relatives abroad. These files proved decisive in 1937, when categories of past status became the basis of arrests. The NKVD also developed practices of constructing "cases" in which separate individuals were linked as members of alleged conspiracies, with investigators pressing detainees to name accomplices.

The NKVD also reflected a distinctive institutional culture. Its officers called themselves Chekists, claiming descent from Felix Dzerzhinsky's Cheka of 1917 (Chapter 52), and they cultivated an identity as the "sword and shield" of the revolution. The organization was divided between the security apparatus proper and units responsible for ordinary policing, border guarding, firefighting, and the camps, so that in the field the same commissariat could handle a street theft and a case of alleged espionage. Special departments of the NKVD inside the army and navy monitored the loyalty of military personnel, which later enabled the military purge (§57.14).

Insecurity also marked the security services themselves. Many NKVD officers who had made their careers under Yagoda were removed after 1936, and the agency was purged again in 1938–1939. In June 1938 Genrikh Lyushkov, the NKVD chief in the Far East, crossed the border into Japanese-controlled territory to avoid arrest, an episode that the leadership used to reinforce fears of treachery within the apparatus. The agency's officers thus acted under pressure from above, with the knowledge that failure or apparent leniency could end in their own arrest.

Section 57.6

The Assassination of Sergei Kirov and the Escalation of Repression, 1934

On 1 December 1934, Leonid Nikolaev shot Sergei Kirov, the Leningrad party leader, in the party headquarters at the Smolny Institute. Kirov was a prominent member of the Politburo and had been popular, particularly in the Leningrad organization. Nikolaev, a party member who had lost his job and had personal grievances, was arrested at the scene, tried in a closed session of the Military Collegium, and executed at the end of December.

The government responded within hours. A decree of 1 December ordered that cases of alleged terrorism be investigated within ten days, heard without the participation of defense counsel or prosecutor, and not be subject to appeal or petition for clemency, with death sentences carried out immediately. This emergency procedure became a template for later repression. Zinoviev, Kamenev, and others were arrested in connection with the case, and thousands of people in Leningrad with former opposition or "bourgeois" backgrounds were deported from the city in early 1935. Press campaigns portrayed a wider conspiracy.

Historians distinguish the facts. It is well documented that Stalin and the security police exploited the assassination to justify new repression, and the emergency decree and subsequent campaign are on the record. The claim that Stalin or the NKVD organized the murder has been argued since the 1950s, often citing apparent lapses in Kirov's protection. Archival research since 1991, including by Khlevniuk and by Getty and Naumov, has not produced documentary proof of such an order, though some questions about the investigation remain and some scholars continue to regard the matter as open. Students should therefore state the exploitation as established and the orchestration as unproven.

The circumstances of the killing have fed long-standing suspicion. Nikolaev had been detained near Kirov's offices in the weeks before the shooting and released; Kirov's bodyguard was killed in a vehicle accident the day after the assassination, while being taken for questioning; and the Leningrad NKVD leadership was later accused of negligence. Such details are cited in many accounts, and they raise legitimate questions. Yet each admits other explanations, including administrative carelessness, and the surviving files, including those opened after 1991, have not yielded a document or credible testimony showing that Stalin ordered the killing. The historian's task is to keep three propositions separate: that the killing occurred, that the regime exploited it, and that the leadership organized it. The first two are established; the third is unproven.

The exploitation followed a clear sequence. In December 1934 the press announced the discovery of a "Leningrad center" of former Zinovievites, and by January 1935 Zinoviev and Kamenev had been tried in secret and sentenced to prison terms of ten and five years respectively. A closed letter of the Central Committee circulated to party organizations urged vigilance and drew lessons from the killing. In 1935 the so-called Kremlin affair led to the arrest of Kremlin staff accused of plotting against the leadership. Zhdanov succeeded Kirov as Leningrad party leader. The immediate effect was to create, in the party's imagination, a conspiracy that linked past oppositionists with terrorism, a link on which the later trials would build.

Section 57.7

Party Verification, Expulsions, and Political Enemies, 1933–1936

Purges of party membership were not new. The party had conducted chistki since the early 1920s to remove people judged unreliable, corrupt, or passive, and a purge in 1933 expelled a substantial minority of members. Beginning in 1935, a "verification of party documents" examined records to detect people who had entered by fraud or concealed past affiliations, and in 1936 an "exchange of party cards" repeated the exercise. These campaigns removed large numbers of members and, importantly, generated files on people expelled for supposed ties to oppositionists or "alien elements."

The campaigns also revealed a gap between the center's assumptions and local practice. Provincial party officials often protected their own members, negligently handled records, or conducted the exercise in a perfunctory way. The center, in turn, criticized them for lack of vigilance. Historians such as J. Arch Getty have argued that this friction, in which the leadership grew convinced that local organizations sheltered hidden enemies, contributed to the radicalization of 1936–1937. Others stress the leadership's earlier readiness to use coercion. What both agree on is that expulsion became a step toward arrest: an expelled party member was a ready target.

The treatment of former oppositionists followed a progression. After 1934, former leaders of the opposition who had already recanted were again accused, now of complicity in terrorism. A secret letter of the Central Committee in 1935 and 1936 spoke of a "Trotskyite-Zinovievite" conspiracy. Public and internal rhetoric thus linked party discipline to criminal prosecution, preparing the ground for the trials.

The statements of the leadership during these campaigns were not uniformly harsh. In December 1935, Stalin made a widely quoted remark that a son does not answer for his father, a statement intended to reassure the children of former "class enemies" who were seeking education and jobs. Yet the same years saw the introduction of collective liability in some laws and, from 1937, the arrest of relatives. The contrast illustrates the regime's habit of announcing limits that its own practice did not observe, and it warns students against taking a leadership pronouncement as an accurate description of what happened.

The campaigns also affected ordinary members. Those expelled lost not only party status but often their jobs, since many posts required membership. Expulsion procedures included public "checking" of a member's biography in meetings, where past associations, relatives, and conduct could be raised. A wrong answer, an undeclared relative abroad, or an old acquaintance with an oppositionist could be grounds for removal. This public examination of private histories made denunciation and self-exculpation a regular part of party life, and it prepared the ground for the larger accusations of 1937.

Section 57.8

The Moscow Show Trials, 1936–1938

Three major public trials took place in Moscow's House of Unions, presided over by Vasily Ulrikh with Vyshinsky as prosecutor. In August 1936, sixteen defendants including Zinoviev and Kamenev were accused of forming a "Trotskyite-Zinovievite terrorist center," of the murder of Kirov, and of plots against other leaders. All were sentenced to death and executed. In January 1937, seventeen defendants including Georgy Pyatakov and Karl Radek faced charges of leading a "parallel anti-Soviet Trotskyite center," sabotage of industry, and collaboration with foreign powers; thirteen were shot, while Radek and others received prison terms. In March 1938, twenty-one defendants, among them Nikolai Bukharin, Aleksei Rykov, Nikolai Krestinsky, and the former NKVD head Yagoda, were tried as the "bloc of Rights and Trotskyites"; eighteen were sentenced to death.

The accusations were fabricated. Defendants confessed in open court to crimes including terrorism, espionage for Germany and Japan, and sabotage, and the confessions were produced through pressure during interrogation, which included threats to relatives, sleep deprivation, and beatings, along with promises of leniency. Trotsky, exiled abroad, was named as the chief organizer. The trials had several purposes: to discredit former opposition leaders, to explain economic difficulties by attributing them to wreckers, to dramatize the existence of an internal enemy, and to justify wider repression to the party and the public. A rare moment of resistance occurred in 1938 when Krestinsky briefly retracted his confession in court before reversing it the next day. Bukharin's testimony has been interpreted in several ways, as capitulation, as an attempt to protect his family, or as coded resistance, and the debate shows the difficulty of reading coerced speech.

Each trial must be understood in its own chronological and political context. The first, in August 1936, followed months of preparation. Zinoviev and Kamenev, already imprisoned since 1935, were interrogated for weeks, and accounts based on the archives indicate that they were pressed to confess in return for assurances about their lives and families, assurances that were not kept. The trial opened on 19 August; the verdict of death for all sixteen defendants was announced on 24 August, and the sentences were carried out the next day. During the proceedings the names of other leaders, including Bukharin, Rykov, and Mikhail Tomsky, were mentioned, and Tomsky died by suicide on 22 August. In late September, a telegram sent by Stalin and Zhdanov from the south declared that the NKVD was "four years late" in unmasking the Trotskyite-Zinovievite conspiracy and demanded the appointment of Yezhov in place of Yagoda, which signaled that the investigation would be widened.

The second trial, in January 1937, shifted the accusations from politics to the economy. Georgy Pyatakov, a deputy commissar of heavy industry and a former oppositionist, and Karl Radek, a prominent journalist and Comintern figure, were among the defendants. They were accused of organizing wrecking in mines, chemical works, and railways, including accidents that had killed workers, and of acting with Trotsky and agents of Germany and Japan. Some claims could be checked abroad: Pyatakov testified to a clandestine flight to Oslo to meet Trotsky, but Norwegian authorities and press reported that no such foreign aircraft had landed at the airport. Of the seventeen defendants, thirteen were shot; Radek and a few others received ten-year prison sentences and were killed in prison in 1939. The trial gave the public an explanation for industrial accidents and shortages and placed responsibility on named individuals, and it deepened the pressure on the economic commissariats, including Ordzhonikidze's.

The third trial, in March 1938, was the largest. The twenty-one defendants included Bukharin, Rykov, Krestinsky, the diplomat Khristian Rakovsky, former NKVD chief Yagoda, Uzbek leaders Akmal Ikramov and Faizulla Khodzhaev, and several physicians. They were accused of treason, espionage, wrecking, terrorism, and of causing the deaths of leading figures, including Maxim Gorky, through "medical murder." Krestinsky's brief retraction at the opening session showed that the script could fail; the next day, after the intervention of the interrogators, he withdrew it. Bukharin admitted general political responsibility while contesting specific accusations, and he left a letter addressed to a future generation of party leaders, which his widow, Anna Larina, preserved by memory until it could be published. Eighteen defendants were executed, with Bukharin and Rykov shot on 15 March 1938. By then the mass operations had already claimed far more victims than the three trials combined.

The public trials also had lesser echoes. Throughout 1937, regional and local show trials in provincial towns tried officials and managers for local "wrecking," and press reports of them spread the language of conspiracy to the whole country. The trials thus supplied the public vocabulary of the terror, while the secret operations supplied its mass. Historians differ about the leadership's primary purpose: some stress the elimination of potential rivals, others the need for scapegoats for economic difficulty and for a demonstration of vigilance, and others the preparation of public opinion for war. These explanations are not mutually exclusive, and the documents show the leadership closely supervising the scripts, with Stalin editing drafts of the indictments and reviewing the interrogation records.

Russia and the World

International Reactions to the Moscow Trials

The trials were reported worldwide, and foreign opinion divided. Some visitors and journalists accepted the proceedings as genuine. The German writer Lion Feuchtwanger, who attended in 1937, wrote that the confessions seemed credible, and some diplomats concluded that a real conspiracy might have existed. Others were skeptical from the start. A commission of inquiry chaired by the American philosopher John Dewey, convened in Mexico in 1937 at the request of Trotsky's supporters, concluded that the charges were false, though the commission's critics noted its origins. Western communist parties generally defended the trials, while many socialists and liberals broke with Moscow over them.

The trials also affected the international communist movement. Foreign communists living in the USSR, including many émigrés from Poland, Hungary, Germany, and other countries, were arrested in large numbers during 1937–1938; the Polish Communist Party was dissolved by the Comintern in 1938. The terror therefore also reached beyond the Soviet population, linking domestic repression with the Comintern and with the foreign-policy fears discussed in Chapter 58.

Section 57.9

The Great Terror of 1937–1938: Decisions, Escalation, and Scale

The leadership did not announce a plan for a mass terror. The sequence of decisions was visible in documents and was incremental. At a Central Committee plenum in February–March 1937, Stalin and Yezhov argued that enemies had infiltrated the party and state, that wreckers and spies were active, and that success in building socialism paradoxically made class enemies more desperate. Stalin also criticized party leaders for complacency and for protecting subordinates. Regional secretaries were told to review their organizations. During 1937 the Politburo approved NKVD requests for operations against designated categories of people, and Stalin and his colleagues signed lists of party and state officials proposed for sentencing by the Military Collegium, in total comprising tens of thousands of names in one frequently cited tally.

Archival records compiled by the NKVD and later published give figures that scholars use as a baseline, while noting that they cover only recorded cases. For 1937 and 1938 combined, the NKVD statistical series records 1,575,259 arrests by the security police, which this chapter rounds to about 1.6 million. About 87 percent of these, roughly 1.37 million, were classified as political (counterrevolutionary or state-security) cases; the remainder were arrests for other offenses handled by the NKVD. The same series records 681,692 death sentences, the great majority of which were carried out. The mass operation under Order No. 00447 accounted for 767,397 sentenced, of whom 386,798 were sentenced to death. The national operations accounted for several hundred thousand more arrests. These numbers exclude deaths in custody not recorded as executions, and they do not capture the earlier and later repressions. They also leave aside deported groups and the larger number of people harmed by the arrest of relatives. Estimates beyond the NKVD records vary, and students should attribute any figure to its source.

The decisions of 1937 unfolded over several months. After the February–March plenum, at which Stalin warned of "enemies" in party posts and Bukharin and Rykov were expelled from the party, investigators moved against regional and economic leaders. On 2 July 1937 the Politburo adopted a decision instructing regional party and NKVD leaders to register former kulaks and criminals who had returned from exile or camp, and to prepare lists of those to be shot or deported. Out of this decision came Order No. 00447. The mass operations thus began not as a spontaneous police initiative but with a central decision, although the details of lists, quotas, and methods were left to the NKVD and the regions.

The central leadership also managed the repression of the elite. Lists of persons proposed for sentencing by the Military Collegium, with names drawn from party, state, military, and economic ranks, were submitted to Stalin and other Politburo members, who approved them in batches, sometimes marking particular names. A frequently cited tally of these lists counts tens of thousands of names approved between 1937 and 1938. Although the lists were for the top ranks, they show that the leadership participated in the process on an individual basis, not merely through general directives.

The campaign changed phases. During the second half of 1937 the operations expanded rapidly, as regional leaders reported on their quotas and requested more. By early 1938 the leadership began to express concern about excesses: in January 1938 a Central Committee plenum condemned "mistaken" expulsions from the party and the persecution of members on weak evidence, a decision that did not stop the arrests but began to redirect them toward the NKVD's own apparatus. The terror also had a demographic profile. A large majority of those executed were men; many were in middle age; and while ethnic Russians formed the largest group in absolute terms, certain minorities with real or alleged foreign ties were represented in far greater proportion to their share of the population (§57.11).

It is essential to separate categories when using numbers. The 1,575,259 arrests of 1937–1938 include both political and other cases; the 681,692 death sentences were overwhelmingly political; the figures for Order No. 00447 and the national operations overlap with these totals and should not be added to them. The total of victims of Stalinist repression across the entire 1930s and 1940s, including deportations, famine, and camp mortality, is a different calculation altogether, and historians present different estimates depending on definitions. Students should therefore name the statistical series, the years, and the category whenever they cite a number.

Section 57.10

NKVD Order No. 00447 and the Secret Mass Operations

Order No. 00447, signed by Yezhov on 30 July 1937 after Politburo approval earlier that month, launched what is often called the "kulak operation." Its targets were broad: former kulaks who had returned from exile or camp, former members of non-Bolshevik parties, former tsarist officials, clergy and sectarians, former White soldiers, and persons with criminal records. Those arrested were to be placed in one of two categories. The first, to be shot, was reserved for the most dangerous; the second involved imprisonment in camps for eight to ten years. The order set regional limits, with initial totals of roughly 270,000 people, about 76,000 of them in the first category, and the operation was to be completed in four months.

Procedure was entrusted to regional "troikas," panels of the regional NKVD chief, the party secretary, and the procurator, who reviewed lists of cases prepared by NKVD officers and issued sentences without hearing the accused. Regional leaders requested, and received, increases in their quotas; the limits were raised repeatedly, and the operation was extended into 1938. Records show roughly 767,000 convictions and about 387,000 executions under the order, far above the initial figures. These numbers come from NKVD reports and should be treated as the best documentary baseline rather than a complete count.

The operation's origins lie partly in the regime's perception of a residual hostile population. Hundreds of thousands of peasants had been deported as kulaks in 1930–1932, and many had escaped, returned, or been released, while others had entered towns and industrial sites. After the 1936 Constitution formally restored civil rights to former "disenfranchised" groups, party and police leaders worried that these people could vote, organize, or work against the regime. The NKVD compiled registers of former kulaks, former members of other parties, former White officers, and clergy. The Politburo decision of 2 July 1937 and Yezhov's order of 30 July converted these registers into target lists, with an operation to begin on 5 August in most regions.

The procedure combined central quotas with regional drafting. Each region received limits, divided into those in the first category (to be shot) and the second (to be imprisoned), and NKVD officers drew up lists of names and short statements of charges. Troikas approved cases in bulk, sometimes hundreds in a single session, from files that might contain little more than a questionnaire and a brief summary. Sentences were then implemented, with executions carried out at night in prisons, forest sites, or purpose-built grounds, and families were often informed only of a "sentence without right of correspondence." Records of troika sessions, preserved in regional archives, show how routine and rapid this process became.

The initial limits proved only a starting point. Regional leaders, including those of Moscow oblast and several Siberian and Ukrainian regions, asked the center for higher limits, and the Politburo repeatedly authorized increases and extended the operation's deadline well into 1938. In practice, local organizations sometimes overfulfilled quotas by arresting people who did not belong to the designated categories, including ordinary criminals, workers who had criticized managers, and people denounced by neighbors. Overall, about half of those sentenced under the order were sentenced to death, and the rest to camp terms. The order also encouraged "chain" arrests, since interrogators sought names of accomplices to fill lists. The operation reached from large cities to remote villages, and its victims included peasants, workers, craftsmen, priests and believers, former soldiers of the old army, and people with criminal records. Clergy and active believers were prominent among the victims, and the arrests also hit Buddhist and Muslim religious personnel in some regions.

Historians such as Nicolas Werth and Paul Gregory have emphasized the planned, quota-based character of the operation, with the center setting the numbers and the regions implementing and exceeding them. Others have drawn attention to the role of local initiative, social tensions, and denunciation in determining who actually appeared on the lists. The documents support both observations: the center's role in initiating the operation and setting its framework is clear, and the regional dynamics shaped its course. The statistical series on Order No. 00447, with 767,397 sentenced and 386,798 sentenced to death, comes from NKVD summary reports; individual regional counts can differ slightly depending on the date of the report.

Historical Vocabulary
Troika

Definition: A three-person extrajudicial panel. In 1937–1938, regional troikas, typically composed of the NKVD chief, the party secretary, and the procurator, sentenced people to death or camp terms on the basis of NKVD files.

Historical context: The term originated in earlier campaigns; the troikas of 1937–1938 were established by Order No. 00447 and abolished in November 1938. Troikas operated alongside two-person "dvoikas" in the national operations.

Why it matters: Troikas show how repression bypassed courts. The term should not be confused with the Russian word for a three-horse carriage, or with the later Soviet-era "troika" of leaders.

Section 57.11

The National Operations and the Search for Foreign Enemies

Alongside Order No. 00447, the NKVD conducted a series of "national operations" directed at people of specific nationalities associated with foreign states. One of the earliest, and the largest, authorized by Order No. 00485 in August 1937, targeted persons of Polish origin accused of belonging to a "Polish military organization" and of espionage. It was followed by operations against Germans, Latvians, Finns, Estonians, Romanians, Greeks, Chinese, Koreans, and others, as well as against railway workers with connections to Harbin. According to NKVD records, the Polish operation produced about 143,810 arrests, 139,835 convictions, and 111,091 death sentences; these three figures should not be conflated, because arrests, convictions, and sentences of death are different stages of the process. The national operations together accounted for several hundred thousand arrests and a proportionally high rate of death sentences.

The logic combined security fears with ideology. Border regions and minorities with kin states across the frontier were considered vulnerable to foreign recruitment, and NKVD officials produced allegations of vast spy networks. Many of the accused were Soviet citizens of long residence; others were political émigrés or Comintern members. The operations also overlapped with deportations of whole populations from border zones, notably the removal of Koreans from the Far East in 1937, which Chapter 58 discusses within nationality policy. This chapter restricts itself to the mass arrests and killings.

The national operations followed a distinct procedure. The earliest, directed at Germans employed in defense industries, began in July 1937 (Order No. 00439). The Polish operation followed in August 1937 under Order No. 00485, and further orders applied the same model to Latvians, Finns, Estonians, Romanians, Greeks, Bulgarians, Chinese, and others, and to so-called "Harbintsy," Soviet railway employees and their families who had returned from the Chinese Eastern Railway in Manchuria. Instead of troikas, cases were handled by a "dvoika" of the NKVD chief and the Procurator, who approved lists of cases submitted in bound volumes called "albums." In Moscow, the albums were signed by Yezhov and Vyshinsky, which meant that central officials reviewed cases by the thousand on the basis of brief summaries.

The Polish operation illustrates the logic. The NKVD alleged the existence of a Polish Military Organization (the POV), a clandestine group that had existed during and after the First World War and had been dissolved in the early 1920s. Investigators claimed that it had been reconstituted and had infiltrated Soviet institutions, and they used the claim to justify arrests of ethnic Poles across the USSR, with particular intensity in the Ukrainian and Belarusian borderlands, where Poles had lived for generations and where the Soviet-Polish border had been fixed after the war of 1919–1921. The accused included peasants, workers, railway staff, teachers, and priests, as well as Polish communists, whose party was dissolved by the Comintern in 1938. Because the operation relied on lists of names and categories, many victims had no connection with Poland beyond their descent or surname. The documented figures are 143,810 arrests, 139,835 convictions, and 111,091 death sentences.

The ethnic operations overlapped with, but were distinct from, deportations. In September and October 1937, roughly 170,000 Soviet Koreans were deported from the Far East to Kazakhstan and Uzbekistan, a measure discussed in Chapter 58. The national operations, by contrast, were campaigns of arrest and execution or imprisonment. They also targeted Soviet citizens of the relevant ethnicities more broadly, regardless of their loyalty or length of residence. The fate of such communities during the later war, including the deportations of 1941–1944, is covered in Chapters 59–60, with Chapter 58 reserved for the 1930s nationality-policy background. Historians have noted that the focus on "foreign" nationalities marked a shift from the class-based categories of the early revolution toward suspicion defined in part by ethnicity and kinship, though the language of class and of political conspiracy remained central.

Regional Context

Patterns of Repression Across the USSR

The terror had distinct regional patterns. In Soviet Ukraine, the republic's party leadership and many cultural and administrative figures were destroyed, and the Bykivnia burial site near Kyiv holds victims of the 1930s; Polish and German communities were targeted heavily. In Soviet Belarus, the Kurapaty forest near Minsk became one of the best-known execution sites. In Karelia, the Sandarmokh site contains the graves of prisoners from the Solovki camp shot in autumn 1937, along with others. In Kazakhstan, Central Asia, and the Caucasus, party and state leaders, among them members of the Uzbek leadership tried in the 1938 trial, were removed, and local elites were often accused of bourgeois nationalism. In the Far East, border and railway populations were prime targets.

Regional leaders were not only victims but participants: many requested higher quotas, and the pattern in a given region depended on the local NKVD, party leadership, and ethnic composition. Researchers also note that rural areas, towns, and industrial centers experienced the terror differently, and that quantitative data vary by region.

Section 57.12

Arrest, Interrogation, Confession, and Extrajudicial Violence

A typical case began with a name drawn from a list, a denunciation, a quota, or a confession by a previously arrested person. Arrests often took place at night. Interrogation could last days with relays of investigators, and detainees were pressed to sign protocols naming accomplices. In a telegram dated 10 January 1939, published from the Russian archives, Stalin informed regional party and NKVD leaders that the use of "physical pressure" in NKVD practice had been permitted since 1937 with the sanction of the Central Committee. The telegram confirms that coercion was authorized, not merely tolerated, and it was written at a moment when the leadership was blaming the "excesses" of the preceding period on enemies who had infiltrated the NKVD. Many interrogation records were drafted by investigators and signed under duress, and they include formulaic confessions of membership in imaginary organizations.

Sentencing followed one of several routes: the Military Collegium for senior figures, regional courts, the Special Council, the troikas, or the dvoikas. Execution was usually by a shot to the back of the head, often within days of sentencing, and bodies were buried secretly in mass graves. Those given camp terms entered the Gulag (§57.15). Relatives faced consequences too. Order No. 00486 of August 1937 provided for the arrest of wives of "traitors," and children were commonly placed in orphanages.

Individual cases bring the process into view. Evgenia Ginzburg, a Kazan historian and party member, was arrested in early 1937 after a colleague was accused of "Trotskyism" and she was alleged to have failed to expose him. Her memoir records interrogations by relays of investigators, the pressure to sign a protocol naming associates, and her own refusal to confess to crimes she had not committed, a refusal that resulted in a ten-year sentence rather than death. Her husband, a Kazan official, was also arrested and sentenced. Her account, like other memoirs, is a retrospective narrative by an educated party member and cannot stand for all victims, but it can be compared with case files and other testimony. In 1939, after the extraordinary operations had formally ended, the theater director Vsevolod Meyerhold was arrested; he later wrote that he had been beaten, and he was shot in early 1940. His case shows that violent interrogation continued after the mass operations had officially stopped.

For ordinary victims, the sources are less personal, consisting of questionnaires, short protocols, and lists. A typical peasant or worker arrested under Order No. 00447 might appear in the archive as a form with name, age, occupation, social origin, and a few lines accusing him of anti-Soviet agitation or membership in a counterrevolutionary group, followed by a troika's decision. The pattern of stages was fairly consistent: a warrant (sometimes signed after the arrest), a search of the home and confiscation of property, transfer to a city prison, interrogation, a short written sentence, and, for those sentenced to death, execution within days. Families commonly learned little. The phrase "ten years without the right of correspondence" was used to hide executions, and later, in the 1950s, relatives who inquired were often given death certificates that stated false dates and causes of death. Many relatives did not learn the truth until the Khrushchev period or later (Chapters 61 and 65).

Executions were carried out by NKVD personnel, with shifts of executioners and the use of vehicles to carry bodies to prepared pits. Some sites, such as Butovo, Kommunarka, Kurapaty, and Bykivnia, were near cities, while the Solovki prisoners shot at Sandarmokh were killed in a forest in Karelia. The scale required organization: orders specified procedures for secrecy, record-keeping, and disposal, and in some regions the NKVD complained that shortages of ammunition, vehicles, or personnel delayed implementation. Such details show that the terror depended not only on decisions at the center but on a technical apparatus whose members did the killing.

Arrest and Interrogation

NKVD investigators: Investigators worked under quotas and deadlines, and files show both ideological conviction and fear of being accused of leniency. Some took part in violence; some were later arrested themselves in the purge of the NKVD.
The accused: Detainees faced coercion, isolation, and threats to relatives. Some signed quickly, some resisted for long periods, and many were convinced that signing might spare their families. Survivors' accounts and case files record these diverse choices.
Relatives and neighbors: Families faced stigma, loss of housing and employment, and uncertainty about the fate of the arrested. Neighbors and colleagues sometimes distanced themselves, sometimes helped, and sometimes denounced.
Party and state officials: Officials had to choose between fulfilling instructions, protecting subordinates, and avoiding their own arrest. Many participated; some resisted and were removed.

These perspectives are reconstructed from case files, memoirs, and testimony, each with limits. The power relations among them were deeply unequal.

Section 57.13

The Purges of the Communist Party and Soviet Administration

The party and state apparatus lost much of its senior and middle leadership in 1937–1938. Old Bolsheviks who had joined before 1917 were disproportionately affected, as were regional party secretaries, Central Committee members, people's commissars, factory directors, and engineers. Many were accused of wrecking or of membership in "Trotskyite" or "nationalist" organizations. In the republics, whole leaderships were replaced. Executed figures included Stanislav Kosior and Vlas Chubar of Ukraine, and Rudolf Eikhe of western Siberia, who was arrested in 1938 and shot in 1940.

The purges changed the composition of power. A large cohort of younger officials, many promoted from technical and working-class backgrounds, moved into vacated positions (§57.19). The 18th Party Congress in March 1939 was dominated by delegates with short party histories compared with the 1934 congress. Historians disagree on the net effect on administrative competence: dismissal of experienced managers disrupted industry and administration, while the rapid promotion of newly trained personnel created a loyal but inexperienced elite. Both claims can be supported from different sectors and periods.

Purges also affected the economy. Accusations of wrecking led to the arrest of engineers and managers, discouraged initiative, and encouraged officials to avoid responsibility. At the same time, campaigns of vigilance sometimes served local rivalries and personal grievances. The 1937 census, whose results showed a smaller population than leaders had predicted, was suppressed and some of its organizers were arrested, a reminder that statistics themselves could be political.

The losses in the republics illustrate the breadth of the purge. In Armenia, the first secretary Aghasi Khanjian died in Beria's custody in 1936, in Georgia, party and state leaders such as Mamia Orakhelashvili were shot in 1937, and in Belarus the head of the republic's government was executed. In Uzbekistan, Ikramov and Khodzhaev were tried in Moscow in 1938 alongside Bukharin, and in Kazakhstan and the other Central Asian republics most senior officials were removed. Accusations commonly combined charges of "bourgeois nationalism" with those of espionage or Trotskyism, reflecting the regime's suspicion of local elites who had been promoted under the policy of indigenization (korenizatsiya, Chapter 53). Similar patterns affected the Ukrainian leadership, where the first secretary Kosior and the premier Chubar were executed, and where Pavel Postyshev, sent to Ukraine in 1933 to reinforce central control, was himself arrested in 1938 and shot in 1939.

Economic management suffered in particular. People's commissariats lost their leading personnel: the railways, heavy industry, defense industries, and the machine-building commissariats were purged repeatedly. Engineers and managers who had been trained under the old regime, as well as young communist managers, were accused of wrecking when production fell short or accidents occurred. The effect varied by sector. In some, such as the aviation and arms industries, arrests of designers and managers, including some who then worked in prison design bureaus, coexisted with continuing production. In others, the removal of experienced personnel and the climate of fear reduced initiative and led to cautious decision-making. Historians of the economy therefore emphasize both short-term disruption and the longer-term pattern in which managers learned to avoid risk.

By 1938 the leadership itself had begun to define the excesses as a problem. At the Eighteenth Congress in March 1939, Stalin stated that mass purges were no longer necessary, a claim that implicitly closed the phase of mass operations while insisting that the purges had achieved their goals. The new cohort, many of them born around the time of the revolution and trained in the 1930s, would constitute the core of the Soviet administrative elite for decades, including through the war and the postwar period.

Section 57.14

The Purges of the Red Army and Military Leadership

In May 1937, Marshal Mikhail Tukhachevsky, deputy commissar of defense and a major advocate of military modernization, was removed and arrested. In June he and seven other senior officers, including Iona Yakir and Ieronim Uborevich, were tried in a closed session of the Military Collegium on charges of treason and espionage and shot. The evidence was fabricated, and later Soviet leaders, beginning with Khrushchev, acknowledged that the charges were false. Of the five original marshals of the Soviet Union, only two, Voroshilov and Semyon Budyonny, survived the period; Vasily Blyukher died in custody in 1938 and Aleksandr Yegorov was shot in 1939.

The purge extended well beyond the top. Tens of thousands of officers were dismissed or arrested during 1937–1938, including most commanders of military districts, many corps and division commanders, and political officers. A portion of those dismissed were reinstated in 1939–1940, so precise figures depend on whether arrests, dismissals, executions, or net losses are counted. Quantitative research has measured the removal of large shares of senior officers and the disruption to command networks.

Historians debate the consequences for military readiness. Some argue that the loss of experienced commanders contributed to poor performance in the Winter War of 1939–1940 and the early defeats of 1941 (Chapter 59). Others point to the rapid expansion of the army, the promotion of younger officers who gained experience, doctrinal developments, and the role of other factors such as intelligence assessments and war planning. Most scholars regard the purges as seriously damaging, while differing on how decisive they were. Students should evaluate such claims against specific evidence instead of treating the purge as a simple explanation for later events.

The military purge was tied to the security services' surveillance of the army. In late May 1937, Yan Gamarnik, head of the army's political directorate, died by suicide when he learned that he was to be arrested. On 11 June, the closed trial of Tukhachevsky and seven others, Yakir, Uborevich, Kork, Eideman, Feldman, Primakov, and Putna, took place before a panel of senior officers, several of whom were later themselves arrested and shot. The defendants were accused of treasonous contacts with Germany and plotting a coup; the evidence was based on forced confessions and fabricated documents. The executions followed within hours. In May 1937 the dual command system was restored, with political commissars again given authority alongside commanders, which reduced commanders' autonomy and signaled distrust of the officer corps.

The purge extended to the navy, the air force, and the military districts. Admirals and fleet commanders were arrested, and commanders of nearly all military districts were removed. Figures from Soviet-era military personnel records, as presented by historians, suggest that approximately 34,000 officers were dismissed in 1937 and 1938, with a substantial share, about 11,600, subsequently reinstated by 1940. These figures should be treated as approximate, because they include different categories (dismissal, arrest, expulsion from the party) and because reinstatement did not always mean return to the previous rank. The losses were heaviest among senior commanders and political officers, and they left many formations in the hands of recently promoted officers.

The consequences are debated. Some scholars point to the disorganization visible in the Winter War of 1939–1940 and the catastrophic opening of the German invasion in 1941 as evidence of lasting damage (Chapter 59). Others note that the Red Army expanded rapidly in the late 1930s, that the officer shortage had many causes, and that younger commanders who rose after the purge, including future marshals, gained experience in war. The question also involves counterfactuals, which cannot be tested. A balanced assessment recognizes serious damage to command continuity, military thought, and the culture of initiative, while cautioning against attributing 1941 to the purge alone, since intelligence assessments, war planning, and the diplomatic situation after the pact of 1939 were also decisive (Chapters 58 and 59).

Section 57.15

The Origins, Expansion, and Administration of the Gulag

Soviet forced-labor camps date to the Civil War, and the Solovki camp in the White Sea region became a model in the 1920s. In 1929–1930 the government decided to use convict labor for developing remote regions and for major construction, and in 1930 a Main Administration of Camps, known by its acronym Gulag, was established under the OGPU. The camp system expanded rapidly with the arrests of the early 1930s and again in 1937–1938. According to official records, camps and labor colonies held roughly 1.6 to 1.7 million prisoners at the start of 1939.

The system had roots in practices that predated Stalin's dominance. The Solovki camp, set up in the former monastery in the White Sea in 1923, served as a laboratory for methods of exploiting prisoners' labor in a harsh climate. In 1929 a government decision directed that offenders sentenced to three years or more be sent to OGPU camps, and that these camps be used to develop remote resources. In 1930 the Gulag was created within the OGPU and expanded into a network of camp complexes. Matvei Berman headed it from 1932 to 1937, and Genrikh Yagoda, as head of the security police, supervised the whole. According to archival records, camps and labor colonies held on the order of half a million prisoners in 1934, a population that rose to about 1.67 million on 1 January 1939, after the arrests of 1937–1938.

The system comprised several types of institutions. Corrective-labor camps held prisoners serving longer sentences, usually in remote regions, and were organized as large complexes divided into numerous camp sections. Labor colonies held those with shorter sentences, often closer to population centers. Prisons held people during investigation, and transit camps handled transport. Prisoners were moved in long journeys by rail in overcrowded freight cars and then by foot or boat. The special settlements for deported peasants and, later, deported nationalities were separate, though administered within the same NKVD system for much of the period; their inhabitants were not formally imprisoned but were bound to their places of residence and obliged to work (Chapter 56).

The prisoner population was diverse. Convictions under Article 58 accounted for roughly one-third of camp inmates around 1939, and the share varied over time; the rest were convicted of theft, violence, economic offenses, violations of labor discipline, or other crimes. Many of the "politicals" were ordinary people caught in mass operations, not leaders or activists. Women were a minority, usually under ten percent, and children could be born in the camps. The camp administration relied on prisoners as well as guards: skilled inmates served as clerks, medical staff, technical specialists, and brigade leaders, and the hierarchy of roles shaped survival. Archival records of the Gulag, held in the State Archive of the Russian Federation, are the primary source for numbers, though they record what the camp administration chose to count.

Historical Vocabulary
Gulag

Definition: The acronym of the Main Administration of Camps, the NKVD agency that managed forced-labor camps and, in common usage, the whole Soviet system of camps and labor colonies.

Historical context: Established in 1930; expanded through the 1930s and 1940s; dismantled in stages after 1953. The system included large camps in remote regions and smaller labor colonies, as well as transit and prison facilities.

Why it matters: The Gulag was a central institution of Stalinism, and the term has entered global usage. It should be distinguished from the "special settlements" of deported peasants discussed in Chapter 56, from ordinary prisons, and from forced labor in other regimes.

Prisoners included people convicted under Article 58, referred to as "politicals," and also large numbers convicted of ordinary crimes or of offenses such as theft or labor-discipline violations. The proportion of political prisoners varied by year and camp. Administration was hierarchical: the Gulag directorate in Moscow supervised camp complexes headed by NKVD officers, with guards, internal staff, and designated prisoners in subordinate roles. Camps were located in the Far North, Siberia, the Far East, Kazakhstan, and other regions, often at great distance from population centers.

Section 57.16

Forced Labor, Industrial Projects, and the Camp Economy

The state used camp labor for projects in regions where free labor was scarce or unwilling to go. The White Sea–Baltic Canal (1931–1933) was an early showcase, built largely by hand under harsh conditions, and was publicized in propaganda as a site of "reforging." Other projects included the Moscow–Volga Canal, railways and roads, logging, coal mining at Vorkuta, nickel at Norilsk, and gold mining in the Kolyma region under the trust Dalstroi. Camp complexes were organized around such tasks, with production targets assigned to prisoner brigades and food rations tied to output.

Historians assess the camps' economic value critically. The state gained labor in remote areas and could mobilize prisoners quickly, but productivity was often low, mortality and illness reduced the workforce, supervision and transport were costly, and construction was frequently of poor quality. Camp economies also fostered corruption and falsified reporting. The long-running argument over whether the Gulag was mainly an economic or mainly a punitive institution has been largely resolved in favor of seeing both aims at work, with the balance varying across camps and years.

Specific projects reveal the system's logic. The White Sea–Baltic Canal, about 227 kilometers long, was built in under two years, from 1931 to 1933, by tens of thousands of prisoners with simple tools. Its construction cost many lives, with estimates of deaths ranging from thousands to tens of thousands depending on how deaths in transit and after release are counted, and it proved too shallow for much of the shipping planned for it. A volume celebrating the canal, produced under Maxim Gorky's editorship, presented it as a triumph of "reforging" criminals and class enemies through labor. Such propaganda shows how the regime justified forced labor ideologically, as rehabilitation, while the camps' administrative records speak mainly of plans and norms.

In the Far East, the trust Dalstroi, established in 1931 under Eduard Berzin, developed gold mining in the Kolyma region and used prisoners shipped through the port of Magadan. Berzin himself was arrested in 1937 and shot in 1938, after which the camp regime hardened. In the Arctic, the Norilsk nickel combine was built from 1935 with prison labor, and in the northern Urals, the Vorkuta coal fields and Ukhta region were developed with camps. Other prisoners built railways, roads, and timber operations. The Moscow–Volga Canal, completed in 1937, supplied water to the capital and relied on the labor of prisoners from the Dmitlag camp. The projects were located in places that free labor avoided because of climate and isolation, so that the camps served a policy of settling and exploiting frontier resources.

The mechanism of work combined norms, rations, and sentence reduction. Prisoners were assigned to brigades with daily output norms, and food rations depended on the percentage fulfilled, so that weakened prisoners who could not meet norms received less and weakened further. In the early 1930s, a system of crediting working days against sentences offered an incentive, though its application changed over time and was restricted for political prisoners in later years. The system was costly in practice. Guards, transport, and supply consumed resources, productivity was often below plan, and heavy mortality reduced the labor force. Camp managers faced pressure to meet production targets and often falsified reports. Historians such as Oleg Khlevniuk have documented the frequent inefficiency of the camps, while also noting that in remote regions they accomplished tasks the state could not have achieved otherwise. The system also employed specialists in "special design bureaus," where imprisoned engineers worked on aircraft and weapons, but these were exceptional.

Section 57.17

Life, Death, and Survival in the Gulag

Conditions varied by camp, season, and year. Prisoners lived in barracks that were often overcrowded and poorly heated, labored for long hours in cold climates, and received rations calibrated to work output. Illness, exhaustion, accidents, and malnutrition were common causes of death. Official records report roughly 1.5 million deaths in camps and colonies between 1930 and 1953, with the highest mortality in the early 1930s and during the Second World War; scholars note that this count excludes deaths in transit and deaths of prisoners released when close to death, so it should be treated as a minimum for recorded camp deaths rather than a total for the system.

Prisoners developed hierarchies and survival strategies. Those with technical skills or access to kitchens, clinics, or administrative work could improve their chances. Criminal inmates often dominated and preyed on political prisoners, though the pattern varied. Women constituted a minority of prisoners but faced distinct hazards, including sexual violence, and some gave birth in camps, where children were housed separately. Prisoners also formed friendships, shared information, practiced religion discreetly, and, in some cases, protested through hunger strikes or refusals to work.

Survivors' testimony, including the writings of Evgenia Ginzburg, Varlam Shalamov, and later Aleksandr Solzhenitsyn, shaped public understanding, and archival documents have since added administrative detail. Students should recognize that memoirs record individual experience, often of political prisoners with literacy, and should be compared with administrative records and other testimony to avoid generalizing from a single type of source.

The experience began with arrival after a long transport, followed by quarantine, assignment to a camp section, and a work brigade. Prisoners slept on plank bunks in crowded barracks heated poorly or not at all, wore state-issued clothing that was often inadequate, and worked ten or more hours a day outdoors in temperatures that could fall far below freezing. The ration system, with its link to output, produced the category known in camp slang as the "goner" (dokhodyaga), a prisoner who had reached terminal exhaustion. Medical staff, who were often prisoners themselves, could classify inmates as fit for lighter work and so save lives, but they also faced pressure from camp authorities to keep as many as possible at work. Scurvy, dysentery, tuberculosis, and frostbite were common, and epidemics occurred.

Recorded mortality varied sharply by year, and absolute deaths must be distinguished from mortality rates. The archival series on deaths in camps records 67,297 deaths in 1933, 31,056 in 1937, 108,654 in 1938, 352,560 in 1942, and 267,826 in 1943. The absolute number of recorded deaths in 1938 was therefore higher than in 1933, reflecting the sudden influx of hundreds of thousands of prisoners into overcrowded and inadequately supplied camps. Because the camp population in 1938 was several times larger than in 1933, the rate of mortality (deaths relative to the average population) was lower in 1938 than in the famine year, and the highest rates occurred in 1933 and again in 1942–1943, when wartime ration cuts struck an already weakened population. These series also exclude the mass executions of 1937–1938, which took place largely outside the camp system and appear in the execution statistics instead, though shootings also occurred inside camps, such as those at Kolyma and Solovki. The overall figure of roughly 1.5 to 1.7 million recorded deaths in camps and colonies between 1930 and 1953 is a minimum for deaths associated with the Gulag, since the records omit many who died in transit, in prisons, or after release when near death, and scholars use this range with care.

Differences among prisoners were large. Criminal prisoners, sometimes dominating barracks life, preyed on others, while officials often used them against the politicals. Skilled workers, doctors, and engineers could obtain relatively protected positions, while peasants, who lacked such skills, were often assigned to heavy labor. Women faced sexual coercion and exploitation, and some used relationships with guards or officials for protection. A camp near Akmolinsk in Kazakhstan, known by the acronym ALZhIR, held wives of men condemned as "traitors," and women prisoners with children lived apart from them in camp nurseries. Prisoners sometimes protested; in 1936–1937, political prisoners at Vorkuta undertook a long hunger strike, and refusals to work and petitions were recorded in other camps. Many also found ways to sustain religious practice, friendships, and mutual aid.

Testimony shapes understanding. Evgenia Ginzburg's Journey into the Whirlwind, Varlam Shalamov's Kolyma Tales, and Aleksandr Solzhenitsyn's The Gulag Archipelago are among the best known works, and Memorial has collected many accounts of other prisoners. Shalamov's stories, for instance, are literary reconstructions of Kolyma that stress degradation and moral destruction, while other memoirists emphasize solidarity and moral resilience; both stress the importance of the individual's position within the camp. The reader should compare such sources with the administrative records, noting that the first often come from literate political prisoners and the second reflect administrators' priorities.

Section 57.18

The Stalin Constitution of 1936: Rights, Citizenship, and Political Reality

The new Soviet Constitution, adopted by the Extraordinary Eighth Congress of Soviets on 5 December 1936 and widely called the Stalin Constitution, replaced the 1924 constitution. It declared the USSR a socialist state, established a bicameral Supreme Soviet of the USSR with a Soviet of the Union and a Soviet of Nationalities, and introduced universal, equal, direct, and secret-ballot elections. It abolished the voting restrictions that had disenfranchised "former people" and other groups. It guaranteed rights to work, rest, education, social security, and freedoms of speech, assembly, and religion, while Article 126 designated the Communist Party as the leading core of all organizations. The Transcaucasian federation was dissolved, and eleven union republics were listed.

Historians read the document in different ways. Some stress its propagandistic purpose, aimed at domestic and international audiences, and its coexistence with the Great Terror. Others note its function as a statement of the regime's self-image and of citizenship as a social entitlement. In practice, elections in December 1937 offered a single candidate per seat, no meaningful choice, and no independent institutions to enforce rights. Rights were framed as dependent on the socialist order, and political dissent could be prosecuted under Article 58. The contrast is instructive: the constitution defined the rhetoric of legitimacy while the security police defined the limits of action.

The constitution's drafting is itself instructive. A commission chaired by Stalin worked in 1935–1936, and drafts were prepared by a group that included Bukharin and Radek, both of whom were later accused and executed. The draft was published in June 1936 and discussed at meetings across the country, with millions of participants and many thousands of proposed amendments, some of which were incorporated. Stalin's report to the Congress in November 1936 declared that the exploiting classes had been eliminated and that the Soviet Union had become a socialist society, which meant that social categories used for discrimination, such as "disenfranchised persons," could be dropped. Foreign audiences, particularly in the context of the antifascist Popular Front, were also addressed: the document could be presented as the world's most democratic constitution.

The tension with practice is documented in the text itself. Article 127 provided that no citizen could be arrested except by a court's decision or with the sanction of a procurator, while in 1937 hundreds of thousands were arrested by the NKVD and sentenced by troikas. Article 126 recognized the party as the leading core of all organizations, and political competition outside the party was not permitted. The right of union republics to secede, written into the constitution, was a formality, since the party structure ruled out its exercise. Early discussions of elections with multiple candidates for each seat were dropped in 1937 after the leadership's warnings about hidden enemies; when elections to the Supreme Soviet took place in December 1937, there was a single candidate for each seat, and the authorities reported very high turnout and an overwhelming vote for the official candidates.

Historians nevertheless caution against dismissing the constitution as meaningless. It formalized the regime's claims about citizenship, including social rights to work, education, rest, and social security, which many citizens valued and which were in part implemented, if unevenly. It also shaped the language in which later dissidents and reformers demanded that the regime obey its own constitution (Chapter 64), and the contrast between the text and the practice has been central to later memory of the period. The case is therefore an example of how a document can serve both as propaganda and as a standard by which regimes are later judged.

Section 57.19

Social Mobility, New Soviet Elites, and the Beneficiaries of Stalinism

The 1930s offered unusual opportunities to many people. Expanding schools, technical institutes, factories, and bureaucracies created posts that needed filling, and the regime promoted workers and peasants' children through vydvizhenie, or promotion. Historians such as Sheila Fitzpatrick have shown that a generation of young engineers, administrators, and officers rose rapidly, among them later leaders such as Leonid Brezhnev and Aleksei Kosygin. The Stakhanovite movement, launched in 1935, rewarded shock workers with recognition and privileges, though it also provoked tension with coworkers and managers.

The relationship between mobility and terror was close. The removal of older officials opened positions for younger people, and some of the newly promoted took part in denunciation campaigns. But it would be incorrect to say the terror was designed to create social mobility: the arrest of workers, peasants, and specialists also shattered careers and families, and promoted individuals were themselves at risk. The regime also privileged certain groups: party officials, managers, technical specialists, and cultural figures received better housing, consumer goods, and status. The picture is of a society in which opportunity and danger were intertwined, and in which many people who benefited could also feel fear.

Education expanded sharply. The number of students in technical schools and higher education grew rapidly through the 1930s, and the regime prioritized training of engineers, technicians, and teachers. Workers' faculties had prepared adults for higher education in the 1920s; in the 1930s the emphasis shifted to a more regular system of schooling and entrance examinations, which favored children of the educated as well as those who qualified through promotion programs. The result was a generation that Sheila Fitzpatrick and others have called the "Stalin generation," including many who entered leading posts at young ages during or just after the purges. Leonid Brezhnev, born in 1906, who trained as an engineer and rose in the party apparatus in Dnipropetrovsk, and Aleksei Kosygin, a textile manager from Leningrad who became a People's Commissar in 1939, are often cited as examples.

The Stakhanovite movement illustrates the mixture of incentives and pressure. In August 1935, the Donbas miner Aleksei Stakhanov was credited with cutting a record quantity of coal in a single shift, and the party turned his feat into a movement that urged workers to exceed output norms. Stakhanovites received public recognition, bonuses, and better housing, and they were presented in the press as heroes of the new society; a number of women, such as the sugar-beet grower Maria Demchenko in Ukraine, were also celebrated. But the movement provoked resentment among ordinary workers whose norms were raised, and managers sometimes staged record-setting with extra support. Stakhanovites were occasionally attacked by colleagues, and in 1937–1938 some managers who were seen as obstructing the movement were accused of wrecking.

The privileged groups were numerous and varied. They included party officials and managers, who received better apartments and access to special stores, and military officers, scientists, engineers, and prominent writers and performers. Their status depended on political loyalty, and it could be lost with arrest. Social mobility also had geographic and ethnic dimensions: rapid promotion was available in industrial cities and in the republics' expanding administrations, where local cadres replaced those removed, but those with "wrong" social origins or foreign ties faced exclusion. The result was a stratified society in which the same events produced advantage for some and disaster for others, and in which many citizens belonged to both groups at different times or within the same family.

Section 57.20

Fear, Denunciation, Participation, and Everyday Soviet Life

Everyday life in the late 1930s combined work, schooling, family routines, and public ritual with the knowledge that arrest could come without warning. In cities, many families lived in communal apartments (kommunalki), where several households shared kitchens and corridors and neighbors could observe one another closely. Arrests at night were frequent enough that people packed small bags or avoided certain conversations. Families of the arrested faced loss of jobs, housing, and social standing; relatives sent parcels to camps when allowed, and waited at prison offices for information.

Everyday Life and Social History

Family and Neighborhood Under Stalinism

Children of arrested parents might be sent to relatives or orphanages and sometimes were pressed to denounce or disown their parents in school. Other families sheltered the children of neighbors. The phrase "enemy of the people" stigmatized not only the accused but those linked to them. Women often became sole supporters and traveled to prisons to seek news, and wives of the arrested faced arrest themselves under Order No. 00486. Anna Akhmatova's poem cycle Requiem and Lydia Chukovskaya's writings record the experience of waiting outside prisons, though such literary sources reflect educated Leningrad and Moscow milieus.

Ordinary people also participated in other ways. Denunciations, whether motivated by ideology, fear, opportunism, or personal quarrels, supplied the police with material, and historians such as Sheila Fitzpatrick and Robert Gellately have examined how citizens used the system. Diaries analyzed by Jochen Hellbeck show people who sincerely tried to understand themselves as Soviet subjects and to align their lives with the regime's aims. Regional and social differences were wide: the experience of a Moscow official, a collective-farm worker, a Kazakh herder, and a Gulag prisoner's family differed profoundly.

Fear did not eliminate other emotions. Many citizens took pride in industrial achievements, valued access to education and employment, and supported the regime's goals. Others privately doubted official claims. Evidence for private opinion is limited because speaking candidly was dangerous, and NKVD summaries of public moods reflect both what people said and what police wished to record. Students should therefore avoid both the claim that all citizens were terrorized into silence and the claim that popular support made terror irrelevant.

Daily routines continued within these constraints. Rationing of bread ended in 1935, and, for a time, conditions in the cities improved compared with the famine years, a change publicized by Stalin's remark in late 1935 that life had become better and more cheerful. Yet shortages of housing, clothing, and consumer goods persisted, queues were common, and workers lived in barracks or crowded apartments. Women, in addition to wage labor, bore the burden of domestic work and queuing. Schools, the Pioneer youth organization, and the Komsomol offered education and activities and also instilled loyalty. The story of Pavlik Morozov, a boy said to have been killed in 1932 after denouncing his father, was promoted as a model; historians have questioned much of the official account, but the cult shows the regime's ideal of loyalty to the state above kinship.

Historians disagree about the role of denunciation. Some, including Sheila Fitzpatrick and Robert Gellately, have shown that citizens wrote large numbers of letters to authorities, motivated by ideological commitment, personal conflict, career ambitions, or fear, and that such letters sometimes triggered investigations. Others, including Wendy Goldman in her study of workplace denunciation, stress how the leadership's campaigns encouraged denunciations and how those in danger tried to protect themselves by accusing others. Many denunciations led to no action, and the mass operations relied more on NKVD lists and quotas than on spontaneous reports. The scholarly consensus is therefore that denunciations were an important element but not the sole engine of repression.

Belief and doubt coexisted. Jochen Hellbeck's study of diaries kept in the 1930s shows people trying to understand their own lives in terms of the Soviet project, while Stephen Kotkin's study of the industrial city of Magnitogorsk showed how people learned to "speak Bolshevik," using the regime's vocabulary to navigate its institutions. Private religious belief persisted: the 1937 census, whose results were suppressed, recorded that a majority of adults declared themselves believers, and the organizers were arrested for producing unwelcome results. Clergy and active believers were among the victims of 1937–1938. In Central Asia, the Caucasus, and the Buryat region, Muslim and Buddhist communities faced the closure of mosques and monasteries and the arrest of religious figures. Such evidence reveals a society whose members held diverse and sometimes contradictory attitudes, and it warns against a single portrait of "the Soviet people."

Section 57.21

The End of the Great Terror and Its Continuing Consequences, 1938–1939

By mid-1938, complaints about excesses and disorder, the disruption of the economy and army, and signs that the apparatus itself was out of control prompted the leadership to change course. Beria was appointed first deputy People's Commissar of Internal Affairs on 22 August 1938. On 17 November 1938 a resolution of the government and the party condemned "mistakes" in the conduct of arrests, ordered an end to the mass operations, and abolished the troikas. Yezhov resigned within days, was arrested in April 1939, and was shot in 1940. Beria was formally appointed head of the NKVD on 25 November 1938, and a purge of NKVD personnel followed, with some officers prosecuted for "violations of legality." Some prisoners were released, and a limited review of cases occurred in 1939–1940.

The change was not a return to legality. Arrests, sentences, and Gulag expansion continued, and the security police retained broad powers. The leadership attributed the excesses to enemies who had infiltrated the NKVD, not to the system itself, a narrative that protected the central leadership and left the underlying practices intact. Official acknowledgment of responsibility came only under Khrushchev (Chapter 61), and a full public accounting began in the Gorbachev period (Chapter 65).

The course reversal developed in stages. In April 1938, Yezhov was named People's Commissar of Water Transport while remaining head of the NKVD, a sign that his position was changing. In August, Beria, who had led the Georgian party organization, arrived in Moscow as his first deputy. The resolution of 17 November 1938, "On Arrests, Procuratorial Supervision, and the Conduct of Investigations," criticized the NKVD for simplified procedures, mass arrests without adequate evidence, and the falsification of cases, and it ordered an end to the mass operations and the abolition of the troikas and dvoikas. Yezhov's resignation was accepted within days, and Beria's appointment followed on 25 November. Yezhov was arrested in April 1939 on charges that included conspiracy and espionage, and was executed in early February 1940.

The leadership presented the reversal as correcting the work of "enemies" who had infiltrated the NKVD, which allowed it to avoid acknowledging its own role. A purge of the security services followed, with thousands of NKVD officers arrested, some for specific abuses and others as part of the settling of accounts with Yezhov's circle. Prisoners whose cases were reviewed were released in the tens of thousands in 1939 and 1940, a small share of the total, and some released prisoners reported on what they had endured. Beria's NKVD nevertheless remained a powerful institution, and the Stalin telegram of 10 January 1939 shows that the leadership regarded physical coercion as still permissible against "obvious enemies." The review process gave little redress to the executed, to those still in camps, or to the families of victims.

Repression continued after 1938 in other forms. Arrests for political offenses persisted at lower levels, the camp system continued to grow, new targets emerged in the territories annexed in 1939–1940 (Chapter 59), and the leadership's habits of fabricated charges and extrajudicial punishment remained. The consequences for society included a population in which many families bore the stigma of arrest, a party and army with younger and more dependent personnel, and a leadership with less internal opposition. Whether the army, the economy, and the party emerged stronger or weaker is a matter of ongoing historical assessment (§§57.13–57.14). The first official acknowledgment of the terror's illegality came only in 1956 (Chapter 61), and mass rehabilitations followed in two later periods (Chapters 61 and 65).

Continuity and Change Over Time

After the Mass Operations

What Changed

  • The extraordinary quota-driven mass operations ended, and troikas were abolished.
  • The party, army, and security apparatus were composed largely of new, younger personnel.
  • The cult of Stalin and the leader's authority were reinforced; potential rivals had been eliminated.

What Continued

  • Political arrests, administrative sentences, and the Gulag remained, and the camp population kept growing.
  • The NKVD retained its broad powers under Beria.
  • Families of victims continued to face stigma and secrecy about the fate of the executed.

Why the pattern matters: The end of the Great Terror was a change in method and scale, not an end to repression, and it left a transformed elite entering the Second World War (Chapter 59).

Section 57.22

Historical Debates: Responsibility, Motives, Scale, and Evidence

Interpretations of the Great Terror have shifted with the evidence. In the Cold War, Western scholars often described the terror in "totalitarian" terms as a deliberate project directed by Stalin, a view associated with Robert Conquest. In the 1980s, "revisionist" historians such as J. Arch Getty emphasized the role of institutional conflict, central-local tensions, and social pressures, and questioned whether a master plan existed. The opening of archives after 1991 shaped a more complex synthesis: documents such as those published by Getty and Naumov in The Road to Terror and studies by Oleg Khlevniuk show Stalin's central role in approving and directing repression, while research by Nicolas Werth and others on the mass operations demonstrates how quotas, local initiative, and bureaucratic dynamics shaped outcomes.

The historiography developed in stages, and each stage reflected the evidence then available. Robert Conquest's The Great Terror (first published in 1968) was based on published Soviet sources, émigré and defector accounts, and the memoirs of survivors, and it presented the terror as a purposeful campaign directed by Stalin. It brought the subject to wide attention and stood up on many points, though some of its numerical estimates for executions were later superseded by archival figures. Conquest's interpretation was criticized by historians such as J. Arch Getty in Origins of the Great Purges (1985), who, using the Smolensk party archive and Soviet press, argued that the leadership's policies were often improvised, that central-local conflict and institutional rivalries drove events, and that the idea of a master plan was unsupported. Critics of Getty responded that he underestimated Stalin's agency and the scale of violence; Getty himself later acknowledged the evidence of central direction.

The opening of the archives in the 1990s allowed more precise analysis. The documents published by Getty and Oleg Naumov in The Road to Terror (1999) included Politburo decisions, correspondence, and interrogation records, and showed Stalin's close involvement, while Oleg Khlevniuk's studies, including Master of the House and his later biography of Stalin, used the leadership's correspondence and records of meetings to reconstruct the decision-making of the inner circle and to argue that Stalin's role was central and that the terror was in large part a preemptive strike against perceived potential enemies in advance of an expected war. Nicolas Werth's work on the mass operations drew on NKVD files to demonstrate the centrally directed, quota-based character of Order No. 00447 and the national operations, and Paul Gregory's Terror by Quota emphasized the planning of the process. Wendy Goldman, Sheila Fitzpatrick, and others added studies of social participation, denunciation, and workplaces.

Regional and national historiographies have added further perspectives. Ukrainian and Belarusian historians have documented the repression of national elites and the sites of execution in their republics, and the 1988 revelation by the Belarusian archaeologist Zianon Pazniak of mass graves at Kurapaty became a catalyst for national movements in Belarus. In Russia, Memorial's compilation of victim lists and its research into killing sites and perpetrators developed an alternative to official narratives, while the position of official history in the Russian Federation has shifted over time (Chapter 72). In Kazakhstan, Georgia, and the Baltic states, historians have emphasized the experience of their peoples under Soviet repression, sometimes placing it within wider histories of colonialism or occupation, claims that are debated.

What did the archives resolve, and what did they leave open? They established the scale of the mass operations, the existence of the orders and quotas, the involvement of the leadership in approving lists, the fabricated nature of the charges, and the use of torture. They discredited both extreme numerical claims, such as those that reached tens of millions of executions in 1937–1938, and attempts to minimize the number of victims. They did not settle why Stalin chose 1937, whether the terror was mainly a response to a real fear of war, a means of consolidating personal power, a way of resolving long-standing tensions in the party and society, or some combination; they did not answer whether the Kirov killing was organized by the leadership; and they leave the regional and social dynamics, and the totals of indirect victims, as subjects of continuing research. Students should therefore distinguish among consensus findings, interpretive disagreements, and unanswered questions, and should not treat the existence of disagreement about motives as casting doubt on the documented facts.

Historical Debate

Responsibility and Motives in the Great Terror

The question: To what extent was the Great Terror directed by Stalin and the central leadership, and to what extent did it arise from institutional dynamics, local initiative, and social tensions?

Interpretation A — central direction: Documents show that Stalin approved execution lists, shaped the 1937 plenum, signed off on mass operations, and later endorsed the use of physical coercion. On this view the terror was a leadership policy aimed at eliminating perceived threats and consolidating personal power, whether or not a detailed plan existed in advance.
Interpretation B — institutional and social dynamics: Other historians stress the roles of quotas, local officials' competition and fear, the NKVD's institutional interests, social resentments, and denunciation. Without these mechanisms, they argue, central directives would not have produced the scale or form of the terror.

Evidence: Politburo decisions and execution lists; NKVD orders and statistical reports; correspondence among leaders; regional archives; interrogation and case files; trial transcripts; memoirs and testimony.

Points of agreement: Most historians agree that the terror was carried out by the regime's institutions, that Stalin was centrally involved, that the accusations were fabricated, and that archival evidence establishes mass killing on a scale of hundreds of thousands.

Points of disagreement: Scholars differ on the relative weight of Stalin's personal initiative versus structural factors, on his motives (security fears, consolidation of power, preparing for war), on how much was planned, and on some numerical details.

Why it matters: The question bears on the nature of the Soviet system and on responsibility, including that of mid-level officials and participants.

Student task: Choose two pieces of evidence discussed in this chapter and explain which interpretation each supports most directly, noting its limits.

Other questions are more settled. The fabrication of the trial charges, the existence of the NKVD orders and quotas, and the order of magnitude of arrests and executions are well documented. Questions that remain open include the exact circumstances of Kirov's death, the weighting of motives, and the total of deaths when indirect effects are counted. Students should state clearly which of these categories a claim falls into.

Section 57.23

Chapter Review and Historical Thinking Checkpoint

This chapter has argued that the Great Terror consisted of several related but distinct processes: the staged Moscow Trials of prominent figures, the secret mass operations against categories of ordinary people, the purge of party, administrative, and military elites, and the expansion of the Gulag. These unfolded within a political system in which the party controlled appointments, the NKVD wielded extrajudicial power, and a cult of the leader supplied legitimacy. They coexisted with the 1936 Constitution, expanded education, and rapid social mobility.

The chapter's central problem was how these features were bound together. The answer offered here combines central decisions, institutional mechanisms (quotas, troikas, files, denunciation), ideological categories of enemy, and fears of war and conspiracy, with the participation of officials and citizens at many levels. No single factor suffices, and historians weigh them differently.

Source criticism. Four kinds of evidence deserve particular care. Archival statistics are produced by the agency that carried out the repression and may be incomplete or inflated by reporting pressures, though they are the best baseline. Trial transcripts record staged performances and cannot be read as accounts of actual conduct. Interrogation files contain coerced statements and must be read against the grain. Memoirs and testimony are invaluable for experience but represent particular, often educated, voices and are shaped by memory and later events.

Checkpoint problems. (1) Explain the difference between the Moscow Trials and the mass operations, with evidence. (2) Identify one claim about the terror that is well established and one that is disputed, and explain the difference. (3) Explain how the Gulag fit with the repression and the economy. (4) Explain how social mobility and repression could coexist. The chapter's review apparatus follows.

Chapter Summary

This chapter examined how the Stalinist political system operated and how it carried out the Great Terror of 1934–1939. Its central problem was how a state promising equality, participation, and advancement also created a system of dictatorship, mass imprisonment, executions, and pervasive fear.

The political system rested on the party-state. The Politburo, Secretariat, and nomenklatura gave the leadership control of appointments across the administration, while Stalin's personal authority depended on networks of colleagues and clients who were themselves vulnerable. The system's concentration of power was real but limited by distance, shortages of personnel, and the behavior of local officials.

Legal and security institutions provided the means of repression. Article 58, the Procuracy, the Military Collegium, the Special Council, and later the troikas formed a structure in which formal law coexisted with extrajudicial sentencing. The NKVD, created in 1934, combined state security, police, and the camp administration, and built a network of informants and files.

The assassination of Kirov in December 1934 triggered emergency legislation and a campaign against former oppositionists. Historians agree that the regime exploited the killing; they do not agree that Stalin organized it, and documentary proof of that claim has not been found. Party verification and card exchange in 1935–1936 produced expulsions and files that later fed arrests.

The Moscow Trials of 1936, 1937, and 1938 were staged public proceedings based on fabricated charges and coerced confessions. They had domestic and international audiences and drew divided foreign reactions. They were only the visible part of the terror. The secret mass operations of 1937–1938, beginning with Order No. 00447 and followed by the national operations, targeted hundreds of thousands of ordinary people by category and nationality, using quotas and troikas. The NKVD statistical series records 1,575,259 arrests in 1937–1938 (about 87 percent classified as political) and 681,692 death sentences; Order No. 00447 accounts for 767,397 sentenced, 386,798 of them to death.

The terror also reshaped institutions. Party and state officials, economic managers, and the Red Army command suffered heavy losses, and a younger cohort moved into leading posts. Historians differ about the impact on military readiness but agree on severe disruption. The Gulag expanded as an administrative institution, a forced-labor economy, and a site of mass suffering, with varied conditions and high mortality.

Society experienced the period in contradictory ways. The 1936 Constitution proclaimed rights while the regime restricted political freedom. Social mobility created beneficiaries, and many citizens believed in the system's aims, while fear, stigma, and denunciation shaped daily life. Regional experience varied, with severe patterns in Ukraine, Belarus, Karelia, Kazakhstan, the Far East, and elsewhere.

The extraordinary operations ended in November 1938 with the abolition of the troikas, Yezhov's replacement by Beria, and a purge of the NKVD. Repression continued in other forms, and the transformed party, army, and security services entered the war years. Interpretations have moved from totalitarian models through revisionist critiques to a synthesis that combines central direction with institutional dynamics.

The chapter also emphasized source criticism: archival statistics, trial transcripts, interrogation files, and memoirs each have distinct strengths and limits. It connects to Chapter 58's treatment of culture, nationalities, and foreign policy, to Chapter 59's account of the war, and to Chapters 61, 65, and 72, which treat rehabilitation, glasnost, and the memory of the repressions.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because the Great Terror is central to understanding the Soviet Union's political system, its society, and its memory. It showed how dictatorship, bureaucracy, ideology, and fear interacted, and how repression struck leaders and ordinary people alike. It also showed that the same decade included real social mobility and sincere commitment, a combination that challenges simple narratives.

The consequences shaped the years that followed. A transformed elite, a reinforced cult of the leader, an enlarged Gulag, and a security apparatus under new leadership entered the period of Chapter 58's foreign policy and cultural developments and the war examined in Chapters 59 and 60. After Stalin's death, the exposure of the repressions began with Khrushchev's Secret Speech (Chapter 61) and expanded under glasnost (Chapter 65).

The chapter also reveals larger themes of the textbook: the relationship between center and region, the role of institutions in shaping violence, the experience of different nationalities, and the uses of history in later politics. Questions remain, including the exact weights of motives and the full human toll. These should be stated as open problems, not resolved by assumption, and Chapter 72 returns to the politics of memory in the Russian Federation and beyond.
