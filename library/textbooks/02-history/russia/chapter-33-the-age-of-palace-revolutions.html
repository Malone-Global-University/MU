Part VI — Peter the Great and the Eighteenth-Century Empire
33

The Age of Palace Revolutions

A History of Russia: From the Eurasian World to the Russian Federation

Chapter Opening

On the morning of January 28, 1725 (Old Style; February 8, 1725, New Style), Peter I, who had spent nearly three decades reorganizing the government, army, church, and elite service obligations of the Russian state he ruled, died in the Winter Palace in St. Petersburg without having clearly and securely designated who should succeed him.

The man who had built the Senate, the colleges, a new capital, a new navy, a reorganized church administration, and a service nobility bound to lifelong obligation to the state left behind no unambiguous heir. His only surviving son by his first wife, Tsarevich Alexei, had died in 1718 while in his father's custody, after Peter had come to suspect him of disloyalty and had him investigated and condemned; that episode is examined more fully in Section 33.1 below, since it belongs directly to this chapter's account of the succession crisis it helped produce. In 1722 Peter had issued a statute formalizing and broadening the sovereign's long-recognized right to designate a successor of his own choosing, a right Muscovite and early imperial practice had already exercised in less codified form. Yet Peter himself, whether from illness, indecision, or the simple difficulty of choosing among competing candidates, never exercised that right in any way that produced an effective, uncontested designation before his death. The empire that Peter had built around an unusually forceful, personally engaged monarch now faced a question his reforms had done nothing to resolve: who would rule next, and by what right?

What followed, across the thirty-seven years between Peter's death in 1725 and the accession of Catherine II in 1762, was a sequence of six reigns, several of them brief, most of them touched in some way by court intrigue, guards regiments, and competing noble factions. Catherine I, Peter's second wife, reigned for little more than two years before her own death. Peter II, Alexei's son and Peter the Great's grandson, died of smallpox at fourteen. Anna Ivanovna, a niece of Peter the Great, briefly confronted an attempt by a small group of high officials to bind her authority with written conditions before rejecting those conditions and ruling as an unrestricted autocrat for a decade. Ivan VI, an infant great-grandnephew of Peter the Great, held the throne for little more than a year before being overthrown in a coup carried out by a company of the Imperial Guard. Elizabeth Petrovna, Peter the Great's surviving daughter, then reigned for two decades, presiding over sustained diplomatic and military activity, including major Russian involvement in the Seven Years' War. Her nephew and successor, Peter III, held the throne for only six months before being deposed, in turn, by his own wife.

Later historians gave this sequence of reigns a memorable but potentially misleading label: the Age of Palace Revolutions. The phrase captures something real about the period's politics — the throne changed hands repeatedly, often through court intrigue, occasionally through outright coups carried out by armed guardsmen rather than through orderly hereditary succession. But the phrase can also mislead students into imagining that Russia spent nearly four decades in a state of governmental chaos, its administration paralyzed, its army disorganized, its international standing collapsed. That impression would be inaccurate. Beneath the unstable politics of the court, the institutional apparatus Peter had built — the Senate, the administrative colleges, the standing army, the Table of Ranks, the provincial administrative structure, and Russia's expanding diplomatic and military role in European affairs — continued to function and, in important respects, to develop further. Russian armies fought successfully deep inside Central Europe during the Seven Years' War, a conflict examined later in this chapter, at a moment when the throne in St. Petersburg had already passed through repeated changes of monarch since Peter's death.

This chapter's central task is therefore to hold two observations together without collapsing one into the other. The throne was unstable: succession depended increasingly on court coalitions, the backing of Guards regiments stationed in or near the capital, and shifting alliances among noble families, rather than on any secure, universally accepted principle of hereditary right. The state, by contrast, showed considerable institutional continuity: the machinery of government that Peter had constructed did not require his personal presence, or even a particularly capable monarch, to continue operating. Explaining why the first was true, and why the second was nonetheless also true, is this chapter's guiding problem.

A further theme runs through this period and becomes especially important in the chapter's final section: the changing relationship between the monarchy and the service nobility. Chapter 32 explained how Peter had imposed an unusually demanding regime of lifelong state service on Russia's noble landholding class, a class whose privileged social and economic position depended, under Peter's system, on continuous service to the state in the army, navy, or civil administration. In the decades after Peter's death, the monarchy's dependence on noble cooperation during repeated succession crises, combined with noble pressure for relief from the burdens Peter had imposed, gradually reshaped this relationship. By the end of this chapter, in the final months of Peter III's brief reign, the compulsory service obligation that had defined noble status since Peter's time would be formally lifted — a development this chapter treats not as evidence of state weakness but as evidence of a genuine renegotiation between crown and elite.

Finally, this chapter connects directly backward to Chapters 31 and 32 and directly forward to Chapter 34. The military-fiscal state, the reformed government institutions, and the internationally engaged Russia examined in this chapter are the direct products of the Petrine transformation; understanding how that inheritance survived — and was modified — across six reigns prepares students to understand how Catherine II, arriving on the throne through the last and most consequential of this period's palace coups, would build upon a state whose basic structure had already proven durable.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why Peter I's 1722 succession law and his death without a secure designated heir created an unstable dynastic environment.
  2. Trace the sequence of post-Petrine rulers from Catherine I through Peter III, including the dynastic relationship of each to Peter the Great.
  3. Analyze the political role of the Imperial Guard regiments in determining succession outcomes between 1725 and 1762.
  4. Evaluate the Conditions of 1730 as evidence of elite political ambition and of the limits of noble political unity.
  5. Distinguish the instability of the throne from the institutional continuity of Petrine government structures during this period.
  6. Assess Russia's participation in the Seven Years' War as evidence for the durability of the Petrine military-fiscal state.
  7. Describe the gradual change in the relationship between the monarchy and the service nobility after Peter's death.
  8. Critique the traditional historical label "Bironovshchina" as an interpretation shaped by later political memory rather than a neutral description.
  9. Compare the reigns of Catherine I, Anna Ivanovna, and Elizabeth Petrovna as distinct political systems rather than as a single undifferentiated pattern of "weak rulers controlled by favorites."
  10. Connect Peter III's vulnerable political position at the end of this chapter to the coup of 1762 examined in Chapter 34.
  11. Interpret court politics, patronage, and factional alliance as a coherent political system rather than as mere chaos or scandal.
  12. Explain how the same succession crisis could appear differently depending on a historical actor's social position, geographic location, and relationship to the court.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How could an autocratic monarchy possess enormous formal power over its subjects yet lack a reliable mechanism for transferring that power from one ruler to the next?
  2. Why did the Imperial Guard regiments become such consequential political actors in the decades after Peter's death?
  3. What do the Conditions of 1730 reveal about the ambitions, and the limitations, of Russia's highest-ranking nobility?
  4. Why is it misleading to describe this period as one of governmental chaos, given the continued operation of the Senate, the colleges, and the army?
  5. What does Russia's sustained military performance in the Seven Years' War suggest about the durability of Peter's institutional reforms?
  6. How and why did the nobility's relationship to compulsory state service change between 1725 and 1762?
  7. Why should historians treat the term "Bironovshchina" with caution rather than accepting it as a neutral historical description?
  8. In what ways did a palace revolution in St. Petersburg affect, or fail to affect, the everyday obligations of provincial officials, soldiers, and taxpayers elsewhere in the empire?

Historical Context

A note on dates: Russia used the Julian calendar, or Old Style (O.S.), for civil purposes throughout the period this chapter examines, while most of Western Europe had already adopted the Gregorian, or New Style (N.S.), calendar. In the eighteenth century, New Style dates ran eleven days ahead of Old Style. This chapter gives dates in Old Style, as contemporary Russian sources did, except where a date's New Style equivalent is identified explicitly.

Chapters 31 and 32 examined Peter I's transformation of the Russian state during his own lifetime: victory over Sweden in the Great Northern War, the founding of St. Petersburg, the creation of the Senate and administrative colleges, the Table of Ranks, a reorganized provincial administration, a subordinated church administration under the Holy Synod, and a demanding new regime of lifelong service obligation imposed on the landholding nobility. These reforms depended, in considerable part, on Peter's own extraordinary personal energy, his direct supervision of government business, and his willingness to override established custom when it obstructed his goals. A central question this chapter must address is what happened to such a system once its founder was no longer present to drive it.

The succession problem facing Russia in 1725 had two intertwined sources. The first was dynastic: Peter's only surviving son by his first marriage, Tsarevich Alexei, had died in 1718 following his father's investigation into an alleged conspiracy against him, an episode examined more fully in Section 33.1. Peter's children by his second wife, Catherine, included several daughters, of whom Elizabeth and Anna Petrovna survived into adulthood, but Peter had no surviving son by Catherine. The clearest male-line claimant to the throne by traditional principles of descent was Alexei's son, the young Grand Duke Peter Alekseevich, a small child at the time of Peter's death.

The second source of the problem was legal and institutional. Muscovite and early imperial Russian succession had never rested on a single, fixed, automatic rule of primogeniture in the manner of some Western European monarchies; in practice it had long depended substantially on public designation by the reigning sovereign, sometimes confirmed after the fact by assemblies of the elite. Peter's 1722 Law of Succession did not overturn an older automatic system so much as it formally codified and considerably broadened this existing principle of sovereign designation, explicitly asserting that the reigning monarch could name any successor he judged fit, of whatever relationship or rank, rather than being bound even by the loose customary expectations that had previously guided the choice. This law reflected Peter's broader inclination to treat the state, its offices, and even the succession as instruments to be organized rationally according to the sovereign's judgment rather than according to inherited custom. The vulnerability this law created was therefore not the abolition of an older rule, but the concentration of an already-important power of designation entirely in the hands of a single individual, with no fallback procedure should that individual fail to exercise it. When Peter died without having made an effective, uncontested designation, Russia's political elite faced a genuine institutional gap: the broadened right of designation existed in law, but no one had yet exercised it in a way all parties were prepared to accept.

Into this vacuum stepped several kinds of political actors whose roles this chapter examines in detail. High-ranking officials and courtiers, some of them Peter's own closest associates, sought to influence the succession in directions favorable to their own continued power. The Imperial Guard regiments — the Preobrazhensky and Semyonovsky Guards, elite units originally organized under Peter and stationed in or near the capital — possessed both symbolic prestige as guardians of the sovereign and, more practically, the organized armed force necessary to make a succession claim effective. And the empire's noble elite more broadly, though it did not act as a single unified political body, exercised influence through its collective attitudes toward any given claimant or arrangement, as the events surrounding Anna Ivanovna's accession in 1730 would demonstrate with particular clarity.

It is essential that students not conclude from this instability that the Russian state itself was fragile or that its administrative and military capacities collapsed. The Senate, the colleges, the provincial administrative structure, and the army and navy Peter had created all persisted across this period as functioning institutions, and Russia's diplomatic engagement with the other major European powers, rather than diminishing, deepened across these decades. Continuity of this kind, however, did not mean an unchanged institutional hierarchy. The Senate's own position relative to newer coordinating bodies shifted considerably from reign to reign: the Supreme Privy Council created in 1726 substantially reduced the Senate's practical authority, a powerful Cabinet occupied a comparable position above it under Anna Ivanovna, and Elizabeth Petrovna, on taking power in 1741, deliberately restored much of the Senate's earlier standing. This chapter's central interpretive framework therefore asks students to separate two distinct questions that are easy to conflate: whether the mechanism for choosing a ruler was stable, and whether the underlying machinery of government persisted and adapted. The evidence of this period answers the first question negatively, while showing that the second was met not by an unchanged hierarchy but by durable institutions whose relative position continued to be renegotiated by each new administration.

Section 33.1

The Succession Problem

To understand the political history of Russia between 1725 and 1762, students must first understand the specific, self-created problem that Peter I bequeathed to his successors. Peter had spent much of his reign asserting the sovereign's authority to reorganize inherited institutions according to rational calculation rather than established custom, and the 1722 Law of Succession applied this same logic to the monarchy itself. Muscovite and early imperial succession had already rested substantially on public designation by the reigning sovereign rather than on a single fixed rule of automatic primogeniture, but that older practice of designation was informal, and expectation still favored recognized dynastic seniority in the absence of a clear alternative. Peter's 1722 statute formalized the sovereign's right to designate any successor of whatever relationship or rank he judged fit, removing even the loose customary expectations that had previously guided the choice. In principle, this reform might have allowed a capable and far-sighted monarch to select the most suitable successor regardless of birth order. In practice, it concentrated the entire burden of resolving the succession in a single individual's timely action, with no fallback procedure should that individual, like Peter himself, fail to exercise the right effectively.

Peter did not exercise this right in any way that produced a clear or uncontested outcome. His only son by his first wife, Evdokia Lopukhina, was Tsarevich Alexei, who had died in 1718 in his father's custody, after Peter suspected him of disloyalty and conspiracy, had him investigated, and secured his condemnation. Alexei left a young son, Grand Duke Peter Alekseevich, who by the older logic of hereditary succession represented the most direct male-line claimant to the throne. Peter I also had several children by his second wife, Catherine, of whom two daughters, Anna and Elizabeth, survived to adulthood, but no surviving son. According to some accounts, Peter intended, in his final illness, to name a successor but died, in January 1725, before completing or clearly communicating that intention. Whatever the precise truth of his final wishes — a matter historians cannot resolve with certainty given the ambiguous and sometimes contradictory later accounts — the practical result was the same: Russia's highest political and military figures found themselves, in the hours after Peter's death, with no unambiguous answer to the question of who should rule next.

This chapter's guiding analytical problem follows directly from this situation. Peter had built an unusually centralized and formally powerful monarchy, resting on a reorganized Senate, specialized administrative colleges, a standing army and navy, a service nobility bound by the Table of Ranks, and a subordinated church administration. Yet this same monarchy possessed no reliable, broadly accepted mechanism for transferring its own supreme authority from one holder to the next. Students should hold this apparent paradox in mind throughout the chapter: how could a state organized around concentrated, formally unlimited monarchical power simultaneously lack a stable procedure for determining who would exercise that power?

The dynastic field that resulted, and that this chapter traces across six reigns, can be summarized briefly before the detailed sections that follow. Catherine I, Peter's widow, took the throne in 1725 with Guards and elite backing. Peter II, Alexei's son and Peter the Great's grandson, succeeded her as a child in 1727 and died in 1730, extinguishing the direct male Romanov line descended from Peter the Great. Anna Ivanovna, daughter of Peter the Great's half-brother and co-tsar Ivan V, then acceded to the throne in 1730 under contested conditions and ruled for a decade. Her chosen infant successor, Ivan VI, held the throne only briefly under regency before being overthrown in 1741 by Elizabeth Petrovna, Peter the Great's surviving daughter, who reigned for two decades. Elizabeth's chosen heir and nephew, Peter III, briefly succeeded her in 1761 before being deposed in 1762 by his own wife, who became Catherine II. Each of these transitions, examined in the sections that follow, involved some combination of dynastic claim, elite alliance, court intrigue, and, in several instances, direct Guards intervention.

Section 33.2

Catherine I

Catherine, Peter I's second wife, acceded to the throne within hours of Peter's death in January 1725, in a sequence of events that already displayed the essential features of this chapter's political pattern. Catherine's own background gave her no traditional claim to rule in the manner of a hereditary successor: born to a peasant or lower-status family in what is now Latvia or Estonia, she had entered Peter's household as a servant or camp companion before becoming his mistress and, eventually, his wife, crowned empress-consort in 1724. Her accession as reigning sovereign therefore depended almost entirely on the alliance of powerful figures at court, rather than on an uncontested dynastic claim of the kind more traditional successors might have possessed.

The decisive factor in Catherine's accession was the support of the Guards regiments, summoned to the palace by her leading supporters at the moment of Peter's death, and the influence of Aleksandr Menshikov, Peter's longtime close associate, a man of relatively humble origins himself who had risen to become one of the wealthiest and most powerful figures in the empire through his personal relationship with Peter. Menshikov organized support among the Guards officers and other court figures for Catherine's succession, presenting the assembled Guards with the choice between Catherine, Peter's widow and a figure closely associated with his reforms, and the young Grand Duke Peter Alekseevich, whose accession would likely have empowered a different set of noble families associated with more traditional court factions. The Guards declared for Catherine, and the Senate, presented with this fait accompli, confirmed her accession.

This episode should not be read simply as a story of Menshikov manipulating a passive empress. Catherine's reign — brief as it was, lasting only a little over two years until her own death in 1727 — illustrates two important and lasting features of the post-Petrine political order. First, it demonstrates decisively that court alliances and Guards backing had become central mechanisms of succession politics, displacing any simple, automatic application of hereditary right. Second, and equally important for this chapter's larger argument, Catherine's reign shows that the disappearance of Peter's personal, hands-on style of governance did not mean the disappearance of the governmental apparatus he had built. The Senate continued to meet and to administer central government business; the colleges continued their specialized functions in finance, war, foreign affairs, and other areas; provincial administration continued to operate; and the army and navy Peter had created continued to exist as functioning institutions, even though the monarch nominally at their head possessed neither Peter's administrative experience nor, evidently, a strong personal inclination to govern in detail.

One important institutional development of Catherine's reign further illustrates this point. In 1726, the Supreme Privy Council was established, an elite body composed of a small number of the highest-ranking officials, including Menshikov, intended to coordinate major governmental decisions at a level above the Senate. The council's creation can be read as an elite attempt to supply the coordinated executive judgment that Peter's personal engagement had previously provided, now that the reigning monarch was not positioned, or perhaps not inclined, to provide it herself. The Supreme Privy Council would prove to be a body of considerable future significance, reappearing at the center of the constitutional crisis surrounding Anna Ivanovna's accession in 1730, examined in Section 33.4.

Section 33.3

Peter II

Catherine I's death in 1727 again raised the succession question, and this time it was resolved, after negotiation among competing court factions, in favor of Grand Duke Peter Alekseevich, Tsarevich Alexei's son and Peter the Great's only surviving grandson through the male line. Peter II acceded to the throne at eleven years of age, a circumstance that necessarily placed real governing authority in the hands of regents, advisors, and competing noble families rather than in the hands of the nominal sovereign himself.

The most immediately significant political development of Peter II's short reign was the rapid fall of Aleksandr Menshikov, the very figure who had done the most to secure Catherine I's accession and who initially attempted to extend his dominant influence into the new reign, in part by betrothing his own daughter to the young emperor. Rival noble families, chiefly the Dolgorukov and Golitsyn families, maneuvered successfully against Menshikov, securing his arrest, the confiscation of his enormous fortune, and his exile to Siberia in 1727, where he died two years later. This episode is instructive for this chapter's larger argument: it shows that political power under the post-Petrine system depended heavily on proximity to the reigning sovereign and control over patronage, and that such power, however great, could be lost quickly once a rival faction gained better access to the monarch or greater influence over those around him.

With Menshikov removed, influence over the young emperor passed chiefly to the Dolgorukov family, who moved the court from St. Petersburg back to Moscow and arranged a betrothal between Peter II and a Dolgorukov daughter, seeking to secure their own family's dominant position for the future. This return of the court to Moscow, even if temporary, is worth noting as a small but telling sign that Peter the Great's specific institutional and cultural creations, including his new capital, depended in some measure on continued conscious commitment by those in power, rather than being permanently fixed the moment Peter completed them.

Peter II's reign ended abruptly with his death from smallpox in January 1730, only days before his planned wedding to the Dolgorukov daughter. His death carried a consequence of first-rank historical importance: it extinguished the direct male line of the Romanov dynasty descended from Peter the Great himself. Whatever plans the Dolgorukov family may have had for securing their own long-term influence collapsed instantly, and Russia's political elite faced, for the second time in five years, an open and unresolved succession question. The resolution of that crisis, and the remarkable constitutional episode that accompanied it, is the subject of the following section.

Section 33.4

Anna Ivanovna

The death of Peter II in January 1730 confronted the Supreme Privy Council, by this point dominated by the Golitsyn and Dolgorukov families following Menshikov's fall, with the task of selecting a new sovereign from among the surviving members of the extended Romanov family. Their choice fell on Anna Ivanovna, daughter of Peter the Great's half-brother and co-tsar Ivan V, and by this point a widow living in the Duchy of Courland, on the Baltic coast, with no strong independent power base of her own within Russia. This last feature was almost certainly central to her selection: a small group of high officials calculated that a candidate without her own established network of court supporters would be more likely to accept limits on her authority in exchange for the throne.

Those limits took concrete form in a document historians call the Conditions of 1730, presented to Anna as a precondition of her accession. The Conditions required Anna to govern in consultation with the Supreme Privy Council on major matters including making war, concluding peace, imposing new taxes, promoting officials above a certain rank, and disposing of significant state property, effectively transferring substantial elements of sovereign authority from the monarch to the small oligarchic council. Anna initially accepted these Conditions and traveled to Russia to take up the throne on that basis.

Once in Moscow, however, Anna found support among a broader circle of nobles, including Guards officers and members of noble families excluded from the narrow oligarchy of the Supreme Privy Council, who resented the prospect of rule by a small self-selected group of great families rather than by the sovereign herself. With this broader backing, Anna publicly tore up the Conditions in February 1730, dissolved the Supreme Privy Council, and restored, in her own person, the unrestricted autocratic authority that Peter I's system had assumed. In its place she established, in 1731, a new coordinating body of her own, the Cabinet of Ministers, which occupied a position of considerable authority above the Senate for the remainder of her reign — a further illustration that the relative standing of Peter's central institutions continued to be renegotiated by each new administration even as the institutions themselves persisted. This episode deserves careful, precise interpretation rather than a simple headline description. It would be too strong to describe this moment as "Russia nearly became a constitutional monarchy," since the Conditions aimed not at broadly representative or constitutional government but at concentrating power in the hands of a narrow group of aristocratic families, a project that many other nobles actively opposed as a form of narrow oligarchy rather than as a step toward broader rule of law. The episode instead reveals something more specific and, in its way, more analytically interesting: that Russia's highest elite was not a single unified political body with one coherent program. Ambitions for constraining monarchical power were genuinely widespread in early 1730, and surviving noble petitions and political projects from that winter show several groups proposing their own alternative arrangements — broader consultative councils, wider noble participation in government, and different procedures for restraining arbitrary rule — rather than uniformly defending unrestricted autocracy on principle. What united the coalition that ultimately prevailed was narrower and more specific: a preference for restoring Anna's unrestricted authority over continued domination by the existing Supreme Privy Council, whose eight members represented only a handful of great families rather than the nobility as a whole. Many of the same nobles who helped defeat the Conditions also held real grievances against the compulsory service system Peter had imposed on them, grievances examined further in Section 33.9.

Anna's decade-long reign that followed, from 1730 to 1740, is often remembered through the label "Bironovshchina," a term derived from the name of her close favorite, Ernst Johann von Biron, a nobleman of Baltic German background who held enormous influence at Anna's court and who, according to a long tradition of Russian historical writing, presided over a period of harsh, corrupt, and specifically foreign-dominated government widely resented by the native Russian nobility. Students should approach this traditional interpretation with real caution. It is certainly true that foreign-born officials, including a number of Baltic German nobles with administrative and military experience, held prominent positions under Anna, a pattern that had precedent under Peter I himself and reflected the broader eighteenth-century practice of recruiting skilled administrators and officers from across Russia's multiethnic empire and from foreign states more generally. It is also true that later generations, and later nationalist historiography in particular, seized upon "Bironovshchina" as a convenient explanation for a period they wished to portray critically, sometimes for reasons connected to nineteenth- and twentieth-century political and national considerations rather than to careful contemporary evidence. Modern historians generally treat the term as a piece of later historical memory and political rhetoric rather than as an accurate, balanced description of Anna's government, and caution against substituting ethnic-national caricature — the idea of oppressive foreigners ruling over resentful native Russians — for the more complex realities of patronage, court faction, and administrative competence that actually shaped appointments and policy during her reign.

Beyond the court intrigue surrounding Biron, Anna's reign also began to show, in modest form, a development that would grow more significant across the remainder of this chapter: initial noble pressure for relief from the demanding lifetime service obligations Peter I had imposed. In 1736, Anna's government reduced the term of compulsory noble military service from an unlimited lifetime obligation to twenty-five years, and permitted a family with multiple sons to keep one son at home to manage the family estate. These were modest concessions by later standards, but they mark the beginning of a trajectory this chapter traces fully in Section 33.9, running from Peter's uncompromising service-state model toward a substantially freer relationship between the nobility and the crown by 1762.

Historical Vocabulary
Supreme Privy Council

Definition: An elite governing body of a small number of the highest-ranking officials, established in 1726 during the reign of Catherine I to coordinate major decisions of state above the level of the Senate.

Historical context: The council's creation reflected the political vacuum left by Peter I's absence: no reigning monarch after Peter possessed his combination of personal authority, administrative experience, and appetite for direct governance, and the council represented one elite solution to that gap. The council reached the height of its political ambition in 1730, when its leading members attempted to bind Anna Ivanovna's authority through the Conditions, examined above and in this chapter's Primary Source Window.

Why it matters: The council's rise and abrupt dissolution in 1730 illustrates both the real influence that a small circle of great noble families could exercise during a succession crisis and the sharp limits on that influence once a broader coalition of nobles, backed by the Guards, chose to oppose it.

Historical Vocabulary
Conditions of 1730

Definition: A set of written restrictions on monarchical authority that the Supreme Privy Council required Anna Ivanovna to accept as a precondition of her accession to the throne, later rejected and destroyed by Anna once she secured broader noble and Guards support.

Historical context: The Conditions would have required the monarch to consult the Supreme Privy Council before declaring war, making peace, levying new taxes, promoting officials above a certain rank, granting or confiscating estates, or marrying — effectively subordinating the crown to a small oligarchic council of great families.

Why it matters: The episode is frequently misdescribed as a near-miss at constitutional monarchy. It is more accurately understood as evidence of a narrow aristocratic bid for oligarchic power, opposed by a broader coalition of nobles who preferred restoring Anna's unrestricted authority to continued domination by the existing Supreme Privy Council specifically — even though several of those same noble groups had their own, different proposals for restraining arbitrary rule. The episode therefore shows that Russia's elite held no single unified political program regarding the future of autocratic government, and that opposition to the Council's narrow oligarchy did not amount to unanimous support for unrestricted autocracy on principle.

Section 33.5

Court Politics and Guards Regiments

This section stands at the analytical center of the chapter, because the Guards regiments and the court politics surrounding them provide the mechanism that connects nearly every succession episode this chapter examines. The Preobrazhensky and Semyonovsky Guards, elite infantry regiments originally organized by Peter I in his youth and stationed in or near the capital, occupied a unique position within the Russian military and political system. Their officers were drawn overwhelmingly from noble families, often the same great families active at court; their proximity to the capital and to the imperial residences gave them a practical ability to intervene physically and immediately in a succession dispute in a way that army units stationed on distant frontiers could not; and their historical association with Peter the Great himself gave their support a particular symbolic legitimacy, since a claimant backed by "Peter's own Guards" could present that backing as a form of continuity with the founder of the modern Russian state.

Students should resist a temptation to conclude from this that the Guards simply "chose the tsar" in some crude, arbitrary sense, as though Russian succession had become nothing more than a barracks mutiny repeated every few years. Dynastic legitimacy continued to matter enormously throughout this period. Every successful claimant examined in this chapter possessed some genuine, if sometimes contested, connection to the Romanov dynasty: Catherine I as Peter's widow, Peter II as his direct grandson, Anna Ivanovna as his niece, Elizabeth Petrovna as his own daughter, and Peter III as his grandson through Anna Petrovna. Guards support functioned not as an independent, arbitrary source of legitimacy in its own right, but as the decisive practical instrument by which one plausible dynastic claim prevailed over a competing one, or by which a plausible claimant overcame the objections of a rival court faction.

The pattern that recurs across this chapter can therefore be stated precisely: successful claimants to the Russian throne during this period typically combined four elements — a plausible dynastic claim, sustained court support from an influential faction of nobles, alliances among competing elite families, and, at the decisive moment, armed backing from Guards officers willing to declare for the claimant and to secure the practical mechanics of accession, including, in several instances, the physical arrest or removal of a rival. Where any of these four elements was weak or absent, a claim to the throne proved more vulnerable, as the fates of the infant Ivan VI in 1741 and, ultimately, Peter III in 1762 would demonstrate.

This pattern should be understood as a genuine political system, however irregular by the standards of orderly hereditary succession, rather than as mere chaos. It reflects the practical consequences of Peter's 1722 Law of Succession, discussed in Section 33.1: having concentrated the power to designate an heir in the person of the reigning sovereign, and having left no fallback procedure for the recurring circumstance in which that power went unexercised or was contested, Russia's political elite developed an alternative, informal mechanism for resolving succession disputes, one that relied on coalition-building among courtiers and officers rather than on an established constitutional procedure. The palace coups of this period were not random military mutinies erupting without pattern or purpose; they were the recurring, predictable product of an unresolved structural problem at the heart of the post-Petrine monarchy.

Cause and Effect

Guards Politics and the Palace Revolutions

Long-Term Conditions

Peter I's construction of an unusually centralized autocracy resting on the personal authority of the sovereign, combined with the creation of elite Guards regiments closely tied to the ruling dynasty and to noble society.

Intermediate Developments

The 1722 Law of Succession concentrating the power to designate an heir entirely in the reigning sovereign, with no fallback procedure; the death of Tsarevich Alexei; Peter's death without an effective, uncontested designation; the emergence of competing noble families and court factions after 1725.

Short-Term Triggers

The death of a reigning monarch (1725, 1730, 1740, 1761) without an uncontested, universally accepted successor already in secure possession of the throne.

Effects

A recurring pattern of court coalition-building, elite bargaining, and, at decisive moments, Guards intervention to secure a chosen claimant's accession or to remove a sitting but vulnerable monarch, most dramatically in 1741 and 1762.

Historical complexity: No single cause — not the Guards' ambition alone, not noble factionalism alone, not the flawed succession law alone — fully explains the palace revolutions; the specific 1722 legal change interacted with pre-existing institutions (the Guards, noble court society) to produce a recurring, structurally predictable pattern rather than a series of unrelated accidents.

Section 33.6

Elizabeth Petrovna

Anna Ivanovna's death in 1740 produced yet another unstable transition. Anna had designated as her successor her infant great-nephew, who became Ivan VI, with a series of regents, first Biron and then the infant's mother, exercising actual power. This arrangement proved highly unstable and deeply unpopular among the Guards and much of the court, who resented continued domination by favorites associated with Anna's reign and who increasingly looked toward Elizabeth Petrovna, Peter the Great's surviving daughter by Catherine I, as a more legitimate and appealing alternative. In November 1741, Elizabeth, with the direct support of Preobrazhensky Guards officers, carried out a nighttime coup, personally leading a detachment of guardsmen to the imperial palace, arresting the infant Ivan VI and his regents, and proclaiming herself empress.

This episode illustrates the Guards-politics pattern examined in Section 33.5 in unusually vivid and well-documented form: a direct daughter of Peter the Great, possessing strong dynastic legitimacy but excluded from power for more than a decade, secured the throne through the direct, personal, physical intervention of Guards officers acting on her behalf. Elizabeth rewarded the guardsmen who supported her with promotions, land, and honors, reinforcing for the future the practical lesson that Guards loyalty could be a decisive and richly rewarded political asset.

It would be a serious error, however, to let the drama of Elizabeth's coup overshadow the substance of her subsequent reign, which lasted two decades, from 1741 until her death in 1761 — far longer than any other reign examined in this chapter apart from that of Peter the Great himself. This longevity alone should prompt students to reconsider any assumption that the entire post-Petrine era was one of permanent, unrelenting instability. Elizabeth's government, once established, showed considerable institutional consolidation. Upon taking power she deliberately abolished Anna's Cabinet of Ministers and restored much of the Senate's earlier standing and authority, reversing the diminished position it had occupied under the Supreme Privy Council and the Cabinet in turn — a further instance of the pattern this chapter traces, in which durable institutions persisted while their relative position within the government continued to be renegotiated by each new administration. Beyond this restoration, her reign showed continued operation and gradual refinement of the Senate and colleges; sustained development of Russia's diplomatic corps and its engagement with the other major European powers; continued expansion of educational institutions, including the founding of Moscow University in 1755 under the sponsorship of her court; and, as the following section examines in detail, sustained and materially significant military engagement in the Seven Years' War.

Students should note that this chapter deliberately limits its treatment of the social, cultural, and everyday-life dimensions of Elizabeth's court and of eighteenth-century Russian society more broadly, since Chapter 35 addresses estate society, noble culture and provincial life, peasant economy, women and family, and the diversity of the empire's peoples in dedicated sections of its own. This chapter's task with respect to Elizabeth's reign is narrower and more specific: to show that a monarch who reached the throne through a palace coup could nonetheless preside over a functioning, internationally engaged imperial government for two full decades, thereby reinforcing this chapter's central distinction between the instability of the throne and the continuity of the state.

Section 33.7

Russia and the Seven Years' War

The chapter's purpose explicitly requires attention to Russia's continued European role during this period, and no episode illustrates that role more forcefully than Russian participation in the Seven Years' War, a major continental and colonial conflict fought roughly between 1756 and 1763 and involving most of the leading powers of Europe in shifting coalitions. Russia entered the war as part of a coalition that included Austria and France, aligned against Prussia, ruled by King Frederick II, and Prussia's ally Great Britain. Russian forces engaged Prussian armies directly in a series of major campaigns fought chiefly in East Prussia and the wider region of Central Europe between 1757 and 1762.

The point of this section is not merely to narrate individual battles, though students should be aware of major engagements such as the Battle of Gross-Jägersdorf in 1757 and the Battle of Kunersdorf in 1759, in which Russian and allied forces inflicted a severe defeat on Frederick's army. The more important analytical point concerns what Russia's participation in this war reveals about the durability of the institutional and military system Peter I had constructed. By the late 1750s, a Russian army was capable of sustained campaigning deep inside Central Europe, maintaining supply lines across considerable distances, coordinating with allied powers, and inflicting serious damage on one of contemporary Europe's most professionally regarded military establishments. Russian forces occupied and administered East Prussia from 1758 until 1762, incorporating the territory into the Russian imperial administrative system for the duration of the war; in 1760, Russian forces, together with allied Austrian troops, briefly occupied Berlin itself, the Prussian capital, before withdrawing.

This military performance should be read as direct evidence bearing on the question posed throughout this chapter. The army that fought this war, the fiscal system that financed it, the administrative colleges that supplied and organized it, and the diplomatic corps that negotiated Russia's coalition commitments were all, in their basic structure, the products of Peter I's reforms examined in Chapters 31 and 32. That this system could sustain a demanding, multi-year continental war more than three decades after Peter's death, and after several changes of monarch achieved through court intrigue and Guards intervention, provides strong evidence for this chapter's central argument regarding institutional continuity beneath political instability at the level of the throne.

The war's outcome for Russia, however, illustrates a further important point examined more fully in the following section: military and diplomatic achievements, however substantial, remained vulnerable to sudden reversal by the personal decisions of a new monarch. When Elizabeth Petrovna died on December 25, 1761, Old Style (January 5, 1762, New Style), and her nephew succeeded her as Peter III, the new emperor, an enthusiastic admirer of Frederick II, abruptly withdrew Russia from the war against Prussia, returned Russia's hard-won conquests in East Prussia without compensation, and moved toward an alliance with the power his predecessor's government had spent years fighting. This dramatic reversal, examined in Section 33.8, shows that while institutions could provide continuity, the specific direction of foreign policy remained, under Russia's autocratic system, subject to the judgment, or misjudgment, of whichever individual happened to occupy the throne.

Russia and the World

Russia, Prussia, and the European Balance of Power

The Seven Years' War is sometimes treated in general European histories chiefly as a Franco-British and Anglo-Prussian conflict, with Russia appearing as a secondary participant. From the perspective of this textbook's larger argument about Russia's evolving relationship with Europe, established in Chapters 31 and 32 through the Great Northern War and Peter's diplomatic and cultural reorientation toward Europe, Russian participation in the Seven Years' War deserves fuller emphasis. By the 1750s, Russia's alliance choices, military capacity, and battlefield performance materially affected calculations among the other major European powers regarding the continental balance of power. Frederick II himself, according to later testimony, regarded the change of Russian policy after Peter III's accession as a decisive stroke of fortune that rescued Prussia from a potentially disastrous strategic position — sometimes described in later historical writing as the "Miracle of the House of Brandenburg." Whatever the precise accuracy of Frederick's own later characterization, the episode illustrates that Russian policy, by the mid-eighteenth century, had become a variable that other European powers had to weigh seriously in their own strategic calculations, a marked change from Russia's more peripheral position in European great-power politics before Peter's reign.

Section 33.8

Peter III

Peter III, born Karl Peter Ulrich of Holstein-Gottorp, was the son of Anna Petrovna, Peter the Great's daughter, and a German duke, making him Peter the Great's grandson through the female line. Elizabeth Petrovna, lacking children of her own, brought her nephew to Russia and designated him her heir, and he acceded to the throne upon her death on December 25, 1761, Old Style (January 5, 1762, New Style).

Peter III's reign, lasting only about six months, requires careful and non-caricatured treatment, since textbook and popular accounts have often reduced him to a foolish, unpopular figure whose removal simply cleared the way for Catherine II's more celebrated reign. A more precise account should note both his substantive policy actions and the specific political dynamics that made his position vulnerable. Raised partly in the Holstein court in Germany, Peter III retained throughout his life a strong personal attachment to German, and particularly Prussian, culture and to King Frederick II specifically, whom he had long admired. Upon acceding to the throne in the midst of the Seven Years' War, examined in Section 33.7, Peter III abruptly reversed Elizabeth's anti-Prussian policy, withdrew Russian forces from the war, returned Russian conquests in East Prussia without compensation, and moved toward a military and diplomatic alliance with Frederick II, even preparing Russian forces, with Prussian support, for a possible war against Denmark-Norway over his own Holstein-Gottorp claims in Schleswig.

This reversal certainly alienated important constituencies within the Russian military and court, including officers who had fought and in some cases died in the war against Prussia and who now saw its hard-won gains discarded without evident compensating benefit to Russia. But students should resist the common oversimplification that Peter III's fall resulted simply from personal unpopularity over his pro-Prussian sympathies. His domestic actions during his brief reign were, in several respects, substantively significant and not obviously unpopular in themselves: the Manifesto on the Freedom of the Nobility, examined fully in Section 33.9, freed the nobility from compulsory state service and was received favorably by much of the noble elite. His reign also included measures affecting church landholding, secularizing some monastery estates, which alienated ecclesiastical interests even as it may have pleased others.

The forces that combined to end Peter III's reign should be understood, consistent with the analytical framework developed in Section 33.5, as a combination of factors rather than a single grievance. Court politics played a central role: Peter III's own wife, the future Catherine II, had cultivated her own network of supporters at court and among Guards officers over the preceding years, developing personal relationships and political alliances largely independent of, and in some respects opposed to, her husband's own circle. Guards loyalty proved decisive once again: officers sympathetic to Catherine, resentful of Peter III's foreign-policy reversal and his apparent preference for Holstein military customs and personnel, proved willing to back her claim. Dynastic considerations mattered as well, since Catherine, though not herself a blood descendant of the Romanov line, could present herself as acting to protect the interests of her young son, Grand Duke Paul, a great-grandson of Peter the Great through his father.

This chapter deliberately stops short of narrating the coup of 1762 itself in full. That specific event — Catherine's seizure of power, Peter III's arrest, and his subsequent death in custody under circumstances that remain historically debated — belongs, according to the outline governing this textbook, to the opening section of the following chapter, titled explicitly "The Coup of 1762." This chapter's task with respect to Peter III is to establish clearly why his political position had become precarious by the middle of 1762: a foreign policy reversal that alienated important military and court constituencies, a personal style that some contemporaries found alien to Russian court custom, and, most decisively, his wife's independent cultivation of exactly the kind of court and Guards support that this chapter has shown, again and again, to be the essential ingredient of a successful bid for the Russian throne.

Section 33.9

Nobility and State After Peter the Great

This final numbered section before the chapter's Review Checkpoint draws together a theme that has appeared, in more limited form, in several earlier sections: the gradually changing relationship between the Russian monarchy and its service nobility across the decades examined in this chapter. Chapter 32 explained how Peter I had imposed an unusually demanding regime of lifelong, compulsory state service on the landholding nobility, whose privileged legal and social status, under Peter's system, depended directly on continuous service to the state in the army, navy, or civil administration, organized and tracked through the Table of Ranks. This arrangement reflected Peter's broader conception of the nobility not as a class possessing inherent, unconditional privilege by birth alone, but as a service estate whose privileges were, in principle, earned and maintained through ongoing obligation to the sovereign.

The decades following Peter's death, examined across this chapter, show a gradual but unmistakable shift away from this uncompromising model, driven by several interacting forces. The monarchy's repeated dependence on noble and Guards cooperation during the successive succession crises examined in Sections 33.2 through 33.8 gave the nobility, and especially its most influential and best-connected members, increasing practical leverage over the crown, even in the absence of any formal constitutional change in their favor. At the same time, sustained noble pressure sought relief from the specific burdens Peter had imposed, particularly the requirement of lifelong service beginning in early adulthood, which many noble families found economically and personally onerous, since it removed sons from the management of family estates for decades at a time.

This pressure produced incremental concessions across the reigns examined in this chapter. As noted in Section 33.4, Anna Ivanovna's government in 1736 reduced compulsory military service from an unlimited lifetime term to twenty-five years and permitted families with multiple sons to retain one at home. Educational and career structures established under Peter, including cadet corps intended to prepare young nobles for officer service, continued to develop and, in some respects, to offer noble families greater ability to manage the terms and timing of their sons' entry into state service. The Table of Ranks itself continued to operate throughout this period as the formal structure organizing military, civil, and court service and determining the path by which service could confer or reinforce noble status, but its operation increasingly coexisted with a nobility that, as a whole, had grown more confident in asserting its own collective interests vis-à-vis the crown.

This gradual trajectory culminated, at the very end of the period this chapter examines, in the Manifesto on the Freedom of the Nobility, issued by Peter III in February 1762, only months before his own deposition. This legislation formally ended the requirement of compulsory state service for the nobility, permitting noble landholders to serve the state if they chose but no longer compelling them to do so as a condition of retaining their landed estates, serfs, and social privileges. The measure was received favorably by much of the noble elite and would be substantially reaffirmed and elaborated under Catherine II in her own Charter to the Nobility, examined in Chapter 34.

The important interpretive conclusion to draw from this trajectory is not that the nobility somehow "defeated" or overpowered an otherwise unchanged autocratic state. Russia's monarchy remained, throughout this period and well beyond it, formally autocratic, and the crown retained enormous authority over noble families' fortunes, offices, and even personal freedom. Rather, the more precise conclusion is that the post-Petrine monarchy, dependent as it had become on elite and Guards cooperation to manage a chronically unstable succession system, found itself increasingly obliged to renegotiate the specific terms of its relationship with the privileged service elite whose support it needed. Peter I had built a demanding, uncompromising service-state model resting on his own extraordinary personal authority and energy; his less commanding successors, needing the sustained cooperation of the very class Peter had bound so tightly to the state, gradually loosened those bonds, even as the nobility's overall privileged position, wealth, and social dominance, resting substantially on the labor of the enserfed peasantry examined in Chapter 35, remained fully intact and would, if anything, expand further under Catherine II.

The Conditions of 1730

Creator: The Supreme Privy Council, led by members of the Golitsyn and Dolgorukov families · Date: January–February 1730 · Type: Political-constitutional document

Context: Following the death of Peter II without an heir, the Supreme Privy Council selected Anna Ivanovna as the new sovereign and, before confirming her accession, presented her with a written set of conditions restricting her authority. Anna initially signed and accepted the Conditions in Courland before traveling to Russia.

Brief paraphrase of the document's substance (not a verbatim translated quotation):

The Conditions required the sovereign to govern jointly with the Supreme Privy Council, obligating her not to declare war, conclude peace, impose new taxes, spend state revenue on major expenditures, promote officials above the rank of colonel, confer or confiscate noble estates, or marry or designate a successor without the Council's consent. The document effectively transferred a substantial share of the monarch's traditional prerogatives to the eight-member Council.

Historical Debate

Were the Palace Revolutions Evidence of a Weak State, or Specifically of a Weak Succession Mechanism?

The question: Should the repeated palace coups of 1725–1762 be read as evidence that the Russian state itself was fundamentally fragile in this period, or as evidence of a narrower, specific institutional flaw in how Russia transferred supreme political authority?

The "weak state" reading: Some accounts, particularly older or more popular narratives, treat the frequency of coups, the prominence of court favorites, and episodes such as the "Bironovshchina" as evidence that Russian government in this period was generally disorganized, corrupt, or ineffective, a mere interlude of decline between the strong reigns of Peter I and Catherine II.
The "weak succession mechanism" reading: Other historians, examining the continued operation of the Senate and colleges, the sustained development of the army and navy, and above all Russia's substantial military performance in the Seven Years' War, argue that the instability of this period was narrowly located in the mechanism for transferring supreme authority, rather than reflecting any general collapse of governmental or military capacity. On this reading, Peter's 1722 Law of Succession created a specific, identifiable structural flaw, while leaving the rest of his institutional creation largely intact and functioning.

Evidence: Historians weigh the continued operation of central administrative institutions, fiscal and military records demonstrating sustained state capacity, the outcome of the Seven Years' War, and the specific, narrow focus of succession disputes on the person of the monarch rather than on the structure of government more broadly.

Points of agreement: Most historians agree that succession politics during this period were genuinely unstable and that court favorites, including Biron, wielded considerable influence.

Points of disagreement: Historians differ over how much weight to give to court instability as an indicator of the health of the Russian state more broadly, and over how directly to connect specific policy failures, such as the loss of Prussian conquests under Peter III, to the underlying instability of the succession system rather than to individual misjudgment.

Student task: Using evidence from Sections 33.2 through 33.7, identify one piece of evidence supporting the "weak state" reading and one piece of evidence supporting the "weak succession mechanism" reading, and explain which reading the balance of evidence in this chapter better supports.

Multiple Perspectives

A Succession Crisis From Five Vantage Points

A Guards officer in St. Petersburg: For an officer of the Preobrazhensky or Semyonovsky Guards, a succession crisis represented both danger and opportunity: danger, because backing the losing claimant could mean disgrace, exile, or worse, and opportunity, because backing the winning claimant could bring rapid promotion, land grants, and lasting court influence, as it did for many guardsmen who supported Elizabeth Petrovna in 1741.
A senior noble at court, such as a member of the Golitsyn or Dolgorukov family: For a great noble family with a long tradition of court service, a succession crisis offered a chance to reassert family influence against rivals, whether through participation in a body such as the Supreme Privy Council or through direct alliance with a promising claimant, though, as the fate of the Conditions of 1730 demonstrated, overreaching ambition could provoke a broader coalition of nobles to act against a narrow oligarchic project.
A provincial official administering a district far from the capital: For an official responsible for tax collection, local justice, or military recruitment in a province far from St. Petersburg, news of a change of monarch might arrive weeks after the event and would typically bring little immediate change to daily administrative routine, since the underlying structure of provincial government, tax obligation, and recruitment quotas continued largely unaffected by which individual occupied the throne.
A foreign diplomat resident in St. Petersburg: For an ambassador representing a foreign power, each change of monarch required careful reassessment of Russia's likely foreign-policy direction, since, as Peter III's dramatic reversal of policy toward Prussia demonstrated, the personal inclinations of a new sovereign could produce sudden and consequential shifts in alliance commitments, regardless of the underlying continuity of Russia's military and administrative capacity.
An enserfed peasant on a noble estate: For the great majority of the empire's population, bound in varying degrees of dependency to noble landlords or to the state, a change of ruler in the capital had little direct or immediate bearing on daily obligations of labor, rent, or military recruitment, though the gradual changes to noble service obligations examined in Section 33.9 would, over time, have complex effects on the relationship between noble landlords and their dependent peasant populations, effects examined further in Chapter 35.

These perspectives illustrate a central lesson of this chapter: the political drama of a palace revolution in St. Petersburg could be simultaneously momentous for those directly involved at court and largely invisible in its immediate effects to much of the empire's population and administrative apparatus.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Under Peter and After Peter

Under Peter I

  • Personally driven, direct monarchical governance, with Peter closely supervising military, administrative, and religious reform.
  • A demanding, largely uncompromising regime of lifelong compulsory service imposed on the nobility.
  • A newly asserted, but institutionally untested, principle allowing the sovereign to name any successor.

After Peter I (1725–1762)

  • Continued operation of the Senate, colleges, army, navy, and provincial administration largely independent of any particular monarch's personal engagement.
  • A weaker, less commanding monarchy in several reigns, increasingly dependent on court coalitions and Guards backing to secure and retain the throne.
  • Gradual, incremental relief of noble service obligations, culminating in the 1762 Manifesto on the Freedom of the Nobility.
  • Continued, and in some periods intensified, Russian engagement in European diplomacy and warfare, demonstrated decisively by participation in the Seven Years' War.

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing this pattern equips students to see the Petrine state as an institutional structure capable of outliving its founder's personal engagement, even while the political mechanism for choosing who would head that structure remained genuinely unstable for decades.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Provincial Official Amid Distant Coups

For most of the empire's population, and even for much of its lower and middle-ranking officialdom, the succession of Catherine I, Peter II, Anna Ivanovna, Ivan VI, Elizabeth Petrovna, and Peter III unfolded as a series of distant events, announced through official proclamations that took time to travel across the empire's vast distances, and confirmed through the continued, largely unaltered operation of the same provincial governors, tax collectors, garrison commanders, and church officials from one reign to the next. A district official responsible for recruit levies or grain-tax collection under Catherine I generally remained responsible for the same duties, under the same general administrative rules established under Peter I, through the reigns of Peter II, Anna, and beyond, since the colleges and provincial administrative structure Peter had created did not depend for their basic operation on which individual occupied the throne.

This is not to say that changes of ruler carried no local consequences whatsoever. A new monarch's favor or disfavor toward a particular noble family could affect that family's fortunes and, indirectly, the fortunes of officials connected to it; shifts in foreign policy, such as Peter III's abrupt reversal of the war against Prussia, could bring soldiers home from distant campaigns or send them on new ones; and the gradual relaxation of noble service obligations examined in Section 33.9 did, over time, alter the rhythms of noble family life, allowing more sons to remain on family estates rather than departing for lifelong service in distant garrisons. But the basic administrative and fiscal machinery of the state, examined in this feature and throughout this chapter, proved considerably more durable than the individuals who occupied its highest office.

Chapter Summary

This chapter examined the thirty-seven years between the death of Peter I in 1725 and the deposition of Peter III in 1762, a period traditionally, and somewhat misleadingly, known as the Age of Palace Revolutions. The chapter's central argument holds that this era combined genuine dynastic and political instability at the level of the throne with substantial continuity in the institutional apparatus of Petrine government, and that these two observations must be held together rather than allowed to collapse into a single simplified narrative of either chaos or seamless continuity.

The chapter began by tracing the specific origin of this instability to Peter I's own 1722 Law of Succession, which formalized and broadened the sovereign's existing right to designate a successor while concentrating that power entirely in a single individual with no fallback procedure, combined with Peter's death without an effective, uncontested designation. This structural problem produced six successive reigns: Catherine I, whose accession demonstrated the new centrality of Guards and court backing; Peter II, whose brief reign illustrated the fragility of even dominant court favorites such as Menshikov; Anna Ivanovna, whose accession under the Conditions of 1730, and her subsequent rejection of those Conditions, revealed the ambitions and the limits of Russia's highest aristocracy; the brief regency of the infant Ivan VI; Elizabeth Petrovna, whose Guards-backed coup in 1741 opened two decades of comparatively sustained government; and Peter III, whose brief reign combined a consequential domestic reform with a foreign-policy reversal and personal alliances that left him vulnerable to the same Guards-and-court dynamic that had shaped every earlier transition examined in this chapter.

The chapter gave sustained attention to the specific mechanics of succession politics during this period, identifying a recurring combination of dynastic claim, court faction, elite alliance, and Guards backing as the effective, if informal, system by which Russia's political elite resolved repeated crises of legitimate authority. This system, however irregular by the standards of orderly hereditary succession, functioned as a genuine and predictable political pattern rather than as mere chaos.

At the same time, the chapter emphasized substantial continuity beneath this political instability. The Senate, the administrative colleges, the provincial administration, and the army and navy that Peter I had constructed continued to operate across all six reigns examined in this chapter, largely independent of the particular qualities or engagement of whichever individual occupied the throne. Russia's sustained and materially significant participation in the Seven Years' War, examined in detail, provided the chapter's clearest single piece of evidence for this institutional durability, demonstrating that the Petrine military-fiscal state could sustain demanding continental warfare more than three decades after its founder's death.

The chapter also traced a significant social and political development running throughout this period: the gradual relaxation of the demanding compulsory service obligations that Peter I had imposed on the landholding nobility, a trajectory running from modest concessions under Anna Ivanovna in 1736 to the comprehensive Manifesto on the Freedom of the Nobility issued by Peter III in 1762. This chapter interpreted this development not as evidence that the nobility had defeated or overpowered the state, but as evidence that the post-Petrine monarchy, dependent on sustained elite and Guards cooperation to manage its unstable succession system, found itself obliged to renegotiate the specific terms of its relationship with the privileged service class whose support it needed.

The chapter addressed the traditional historical label "Bironovshchina," cautioning students against accepting later, sometimes nationalist-inflected historical memory as a neutral, balanced description of Anna Ivanovna's government, and encouraging more careful attention to the actual mechanisms of patronage, court faction, and administrative appointment that shaped her reign.

Finally, the chapter positioned Peter III's brief and vulnerable reign as the direct prelude to Chapter 34's account of the Coup of 1762, deliberately withholding a full narrative of that coup itself in order to preserve the proper chapter boundary established by the textbook's authoritative outline.

These lessons — distinguishing political instability from institutional continuity, reading constitutional episodes with precision rather than through simplified labels, tracing the specific mechanics of Guards-and-court succession politics, and following the gradual renegotiation of the crown's relationship with its service nobility — prepare students directly for Chapter 34's examination of Catherine II's rise to power and her subsequent combination of enlightened political language, autocratic government, and imperial expansion.

Key Terms

Succession crisis
A situation in which no clear, universally accepted rule determines who should next hold supreme political authority, the recurring condition examined throughout this chapter.
Law of Succession (1722)
Peter I's statute formalizing and broadening the sovereign's existing right to designate any successor, concentrating that power in one individual and creating the structural vulnerability examined in Section 33.1.
Supreme Privy Council
An elite governing body established in 1726 to coordinate major state decisions above the Senate, later central to the Conditions of 1730.
Conditions of 1730
Written restrictions on monarchical authority presented to Anna Ivanovna by the Supreme Privy Council and later rejected by her with broader noble and Guards support.
Guards regiments
Elite military units, chiefly the Preobrazhensky and Semyonovsky Guards, whose backing repeatedly proved decisive in succession disputes examined in this chapter.
Palace revolution
A change of ruler accomplished through court intrigue and, often, direct military intervention by Guards units rather than through orderly hereditary succession.
Bironovshchina
A traditional label for Anna Ivanovna's reign, associated with her favorite Ernst Johann von Biron, requiring critical evaluation as a product of later historical memory rather than neutral description.
Favorite
A courtier who gains disproportionate personal influence over a reigning monarch, exemplified in this chapter by Menshikov and Biron.
Court faction
A coalition of nobles and officials organized around shared family, patronage, or political interests, competing with rival factions for influence over the throne.
Regency
A period in which actual governing authority is exercised by advisors or relatives on behalf of a monarch too young or otherwise unable to rule directly, as under the infant Ivan VI.
Table of Ranks
Peter I's hierarchical system of military, civil, and court ranks, introduced in Chapter 32 and continuing to structure noble service and status throughout this chapter's period.
Service nobility
The Russian landholding elite whose privileged status, under Peter's system, depended on continuous state service, gradually relaxed across this chapter's period.
Compulsory service
The obligation imposed on nobles under Peter I to serve the state, typically for life, in the military or civil administration.
Manifesto on the Freedom of the Nobility (1762)
Legislation issued by Peter III ending compulsory state service for the nobility, the culmination of the trend examined in Section 33.9.
Military-fiscal state
A state organized substantially around the extraction of revenue and manpower to sustain a large standing military establishment, a useful description of the Petrine system examined across this chapter.
Seven Years' War
A major mid-eighteenth-century European and colonial conflict in which Russia participated against Prussia from 1757 until Peter III's withdrawal in 1762.
Balance of power
The distribution of military and diplomatic strength among European states, which Russian participation in the Seven Years' War materially affected.
Dynastic legitimacy
A claimant's genealogical connection to the ruling dynasty, a necessary but not sufficient condition for successfully securing the Russian throne in this period.
Patronage
The exchange of favor, office, or resources for loyalty and service, a central mechanism of court politics examined throughout this chapter.
Institutional continuity
The persistence of governmental structures and functions despite changes in the individual holding supreme political office, this chapter's central analytical theme.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has carried students through six reigns and thirty-seven years of Russian history in which the throne changed hands repeatedly, often through court intrigue and Guards intervention, while the institutional structure Peter I had built proved considerably more durable than any single occupant of that throne. The Age of Palace Revolutions, properly understood, was neither a period of seamless continuity with Peter's own reign nor a period of governmental collapse; it was a distinct historical phase in which an unresolved structural problem in the mechanism of succession coexisted with substantial institutional and military continuity.

This chapter's central methodological lesson — that political instability at the highest level of a state does not automatically indicate instability throughout that state's institutions — will recur throughout this textbook, and students should carry it forward as a general analytical tool rather than treating it as a claim specific only to eighteenth-century Russia. The chapter's attention to the gradual renegotiation between crown and nobility likewise establishes a pattern of incremental institutional change that recurs, in different forms, across later chapters of this textbook.

As the textbook proceeds to Chapter 34's account of the Coup of 1762 and the subsequent reign of Catherine II, students should carry forward this chapter's careful distinction between the instability of the throne and the durability of the state, its critical approach to traditional but potentially misleading historical labels such as "Bironovshchina," and its attention to the specific mechanics — dynastic claim, court alliance, and Guards backing — by which the Russian throne changed hands during this formative and often underestimated period of the eighteenth-century empire.
