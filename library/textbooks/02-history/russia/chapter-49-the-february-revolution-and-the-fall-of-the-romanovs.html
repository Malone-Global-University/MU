The February Revolution and the Fall of the Romanovs

A History of Russia: From the Eurasian World to the Russian Federation

Chapter Opening

The sense of Nicholas II's diary entry for 2 March 1917 (Old Style), written on a stalled railway line in Pskov on the day he renounced the throne: the emperor recorded that he had signed the act of abdication and that, as he saw it, treason, cowardice, and deceit surrounded him. (A paraphrase of a widely published entry; students should consult a verified edition of the diary for exact wording.)

On the morning of 23 February 1917 (Old Style; 8 March in the Gregorian calendar), women who worked in the textile mills of Petrograd's Vyborg district walked out of their factories and into the streets. The date was International Women's Day, an occasion marked by socialist women's organizations, and the day's demands were not obviously revolutionary: bread, and an end to the long queues outside bakeries in a city whose population had swollen with war workers, refugees, and soldiers. Eight days later, on the afternoon of 2 March (15 March), the Emperor Nicholas II, ruler of an empire that stretched from the Baltic to the Pacific and that had been governed by the Romanov dynasty since 1613, signed a document of abdication in a railway carriage at the station of Pskov, some 250 kilometers from the capital he could no longer reach. The next day his brother, the Grand Duke Michael Aleksandrovich, deferred assuming supreme power unless and until a Constituent Assembly should confirm it. A dynasty that had outlasted the Time of Troubles, Peter the Great's reforms, the Napoleonic invasion, and the revolution of 1905 had ended in less than two weeks.

This chapter asks how that happened. Chapter 48 explained why the Russian Empire entered the winter of 1916–1917 in a condition of acute strain: military defeats and the Great Retreat, inflation and transport breakdown, the loss of public confidence in the court and ministers, and the emperor's decision to take personal command of the army. Those conditions made a political crisis likely. They did not make this particular revolution, on this particular timetable, certain. Many societies have endured worse shortages and heavier losses without the sudden disappearance of the state. The purpose of this chapter is therefore narrower and more chronological than that of Chapter 48: to follow, day by day, how a series of demonstrations became a revolution, and how a government that possessed an army of millions, a large police apparatus, and a centuries-old tradition of sacred monarchy lost the capacity to give effective orders in the capital within about four days.

The answer developed here is one of interaction. The February Revolution was not simply a hunger riot that grew by its own momentum, nor the work of any political party, nor a plot by generals and parliamentarians to remove an unpopular emperor. It began with women and male workers striking and demonstrating in numbers the authorities had not anticipated. It became a revolution when soldiers of the Petrograd garrison, reluctant to fire on crowds that included their own families' neighbors, refused orders and then turned their weapons against their officers and the police. It became irreversible when the emperor, isolated at military headquarters and then on his own train, found that the political leaders in the Duma and the senior commanders of his army, none of them revolutionaries, concluded that he could no longer be the center of a government capable of waging the war. Street protest, labor action, military mutiny, improvised institutions, and elite abandonment each played a part, and none was sufficient alone.

The people who experienced these days were many and their experiences differed sharply. Women textile workers and metalworkers, the soldiers of reserve battalions who were quartered in cramped barracks and dreaded dispatch to the front, policemen who found themselves the targets of crowds, ministers who met in the Mariinsky Palace with a diminishing number of reliable subordinates, Duma deputies who had to decide whether to defy an imperial decree, socialist intellectuals who hurried to the Tauride Palace to organize a council of workers and soldiers, generals at the front who feared that civil disorder would ruin the war effort, and an emperor who, in the sense of his own diary, felt himself betrayed: all appear in the following sections. So, in the Regional Context feature, do the sailors of Kronstadt, the garrison of Moscow, Ukrainian and Finnish political leaders, and the populations of the empire's borderlands, who learned of events in the capital by telegram and rumor.

The evidence for these events is abundant but demands careful handling. It includes police and factory-inspector reports, the telegrams exchanged between the capital, military headquarters, and the emperor's train, the diaries of Nicholas II and of numerous witnesses, the records of the Extraordinary Commission of Inquiry that the Provisional Government established to investigate the old regime's officials, and a large body of memoirs written afterward by participants such as the Duma monarchist Vasily Shulgin and the socialist journalist Nikolai Sukhanov. Memoirs of this kind are vivid but self-justifying: those who had hesitated, miscalculated, or acted against their own earlier principles had strong reasons to shape their accounts. The chapter therefore distinguishes, wherever possible, between contemporary documents and later recollection, and it identifies the historiographical disagreements, examined in the Historical Debate feature, over how much of the revolution was spontaneous and how much owed to organized political actors.

This chapter also introduces a dating problem that the textbook has flagged since Chapter 1. Until 1918 the Russian Empire used the Julian calendar, which in the twentieth century ran thirteen days behind the Gregorian calendar used in Western Europe. The event known as the February Revolution took place largely in March by the Gregorian reckoning. This chapter gives dates in Old Style with the New Style date in square brackets, for example 23 February [8 March], and explains where the distinction matters. Readers should also note that the capital, called St. Petersburg until 1914, had been renamed Petrograd soon after the outbreak of war with Germany, as discussed in Chapter 48.

The chapter's scope is deliberately limited. It ends with the disappearance of the monarchy and with the appearance, in the Petrograd Soviet and the committee of the State Duma, of two competing centers of authority. The long, contested relationship between those centers, the Provisional Government's decisions about war, land, and nationalities, and the rise of the Bolsheviks belong to Chapters 50 and 51. Students should resist reading the events of February backward from October. In the first days of March 1917 almost no one expected a Bolshevik government, a civil war, or a Soviet Union (Chapters 52 and 53); what contemporaries saw was the sudden end of an ancient regime and a very uncertain question about what would replace it.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Describe the conditions in Petrograd in February 1917 that made mass protest possible, distinguishing them from the longer wartime crisis analyzed in Chapter 48.
  2. Explain the role of women workers and the International Women's Day demonstrations in the outbreak of the revolution.
  3. Trace the sequence of events from 23 February [8 March] to 3 March [16 March] 1917, using Old Style and New Style dates correctly.
  4. Analyze why the government's attempt at repression failed, with particular attention to the behavior of Cossacks, police, and reserve soldiers.
  5. Evaluate the mutiny of the Petrograd garrison as the decisive turning point separating a protest movement from a revolution.
  6. Compare the origins, composition, and aims of the Petrograd Soviet and the Temporary Committee of the State Duma as competing centers of authority.
  7. Assess the choices of Nicholas II, his senior commanders, and Duma leaders in the decision for abdication, distinguishing coercion, persuasion, and miscalculation.
  8. Interpret the abdication manifesto and the Grand Duke Michael's decision as documents and as legal and political acts.
  9. Explain why the monarchy collapsed so rapidly by weighing long-term, wartime, political, popular, military, and elite causes together.
  10. Distinguish contemporary evidence from later memoir and from political interpretation when reconstructing the February events.
  11. Contextualize the revolution in the empire's provinces, borderlands, and armies, and in the wider wartime international system.
  12. Connect the end of the monarchy to the problems of authority, legitimacy, and war that Chapters 50 and 51 take up.

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  2. What role did women play in beginning the revolution, and why have historians sometimes treated them as background to the story?
  3. Why was the mutiny of the Petrograd garrison decisive, and what conditions in the barracks made it possible?
  4. How did the government's own decisions, including the Duma's prorogation and the reliance on improvised military force, contribute to its collapse?

  6. Why did senior generals who were not revolutionaries conclude that the emperor should abdicate?
  7. What was the political and legal significance of Michael's conditional deferral of supreme power?
  8. Can the February Revolution be described as spontaneous, as planned, or neither, and what evidence supports each description?
  9. How did the monarchy's collapse look from the front, the provinces, the borderlands, and abroad?

Historical Context

The Romanov monarchy of 1917 was a reconstructed autocracy. After the revolution of 1905 (Chapter 46), Nicholas II had issued the October Manifesto and, in 1906, the Fundamental Laws, which created a legislative State Duma and a State Council but retained for the emperor the title of autocrat, control over ministers, command of the armed forces, and the right to dissolve the Duma (Chapter 47). The Duma monarchy that resulted was neither a parliamentary system nor an unreformed autocracy. Its electoral law was weighted toward propertied voters after 1907, its ministers answered to the emperor and not to the legislature, and its relations with the court deteriorated steadily after 1912. Yet it had created a national political arena in which liberal, conservative, and moderate socialist politicians could speak, organize public committees, and claim to represent society. That arena would matter in February 1917, because when the emperor's authority failed in the streets there existed a recognized body of elected, if unrepresentative, politicians to whom many people turned.

The First World War imposed demands the political system could not meet (Chapter 48). Mobilization removed millions of men from villages and workshops. Military reverses in 1915 led the Duma and public organizations such as the Zemgor, the joint union of provincial and municipal governments, to take over parts of supply and relief; they also led Nicholas to assume the supreme command in September 1915 and move to the army's headquarters, the Stavka, at Mogilev in present-day Belarus. His absence left the capital's government to ministers who were frequently replaced and whose reputations suffered from the influence of Empress Alexandra and Grigory Rasputin. By late 1916 inflation, uneven food distribution, and rail congestion had produced long queues in Petrograd, and a widespread belief that the authorities were either incompetent or indifferent. These conditions are antecedents for this chapter; the chapter does not retell them. It begins when the question changes from why discontent was widespread to why and how it overthrew the government.

Petrograd itself shaped the outcome. In 1917 it was a city of roughly two and a half million people, whose industrial workforce had grown rapidly in wartime. Its great metalworking and armaments plants, including the Putilov works, lay on the outskirts, as did its textile mills; the Vyborg side, across the Neva from the center, was a densely worker-populated district with strong traditions of strikes. The center of the city, with the Nevsky Prospekt, the Winter Palace, the Admiralty, the ministries, and the Mariinsky Palace where the Council of Ministers met, was separated from the industrial suburbs by the river and its bridges, which the police could try to raise or guard. Dozens of reserve and training battalions were quartered in barracks within the city, packed with recruits, many of them older conscripts and peasants awaiting dispatch to the front. The size of the garrison is difficult to establish: estimates range from about 150,000 to well over 300,000 depending on what units are counted. What matters is that it was large, young or recently mobilized, poorly trained, led by newly commissioned and often inexperienced officers, and not a body of reliable guards.

The political landscape was also fragmented. Liberals and moderate conservatives in the Duma, grouped since 1915 in the Progressive Bloc, wanted a government that enjoyed public confidence, usually described as a "ministry of confidence" or a responsible ministry, but many feared that mass unrest would destroy the war effort and their own social position. The socialist parties, the Mensheviks, the Socialist Revolutionaries, and the Bolsheviks, were weakened by wartime arrests, divided over whether to support the war, and unsure how far workers' protests could be led. The Bolsheviks in Petrograd were a small underground organization, with a Russian Bureau of the Central Committee led by Aleksandr Shlyapnikov and a strong district organization in Vyborg; most of their prominent leaders were in prison, Siberian exile, or emigration. No party commanded the movement that began in February. Finally, the empire's senior military leaders were professional officers who regarded the preservation of the army and the continuation of the war as their primary duty. How they interpreted that duty in the last days of February is central to the story.

The dating convention is explained more fully in the Historical Vocabulary box in Section 49.1. Throughout this chapter the term "February Revolution" refers to the events of 23 February–3 March (Old Style), which ended the monarchy. It should be distinguished from the Provisional Government period, the soviet movement, the Bolshevik rise, and the October seizure of power, each of which is a separate subject in the chapters that follow.

Section 49.1

Petrograd in Crisis

By the middle of February 1917 Petrograd was a city under strain but not yet a city in revolt. Winter had been severe, and fuel and food deliveries by rail had been disrupted by snow, cold, and a transport system overloaded by military traffic. The supply of flour to the capital was uneven rather than absent: historians have noted that stocks existed, but that distribution was erratic, that prices rose faster than wages, and that rumors of impending rationing led residents to queue early and to buy more than they needed. The queues, called by contemporaries "tails," became the daily experience of the city's women, who spent hours in the cold waiting for bread, kerosene, and other goods, and who connected their frustrations to a broader sense that the authorities could not or would not manage the war economy.

Labor unrest was already substantial. Strikes had increased through 1916, and in January 1917 the anniversary of Bloody Sunday of 1905 (Chapter 46) brought demonstrations and walkouts by tens of thousands of workers. In mid-February, workers at the Putilov works, one of the largest metal and armaments plants in the city, struck over wages and conditions; on 22 February [7 March] the management announced a lockout, which put thousands of additional workers out of work and into the streets. Factory inspectors and the Okhrana, the security police, reported the mood among workers as bitter, politically hostile to the government, and increasingly unwilling to accept the war's burdens. Their reports warned of disorder, but they also tended to assume that the police and the garrison could contain it, as had been the case in earlier disturbances.

That assumption rested on a weakening foundation. The regular guards regiments that had defended the capital in 1905 had been largely destroyed or dispersed in the early campaigns of the war. Their place had been taken by reserve and training battalions that held men awaiting assignment to the front, many of them older reservists recently called up, peasants with little loyalty to the officers over them, and recruits from the same social milieu as the factory workers around them. The officers who commanded them were often wartime graduates of short courses. The police force, meanwhile, had lost experienced personnel to the army and numbered only a few thousand in a city of some two and a half million; it could manage routine demonstrations but not a sustained mass movement unless backed by dependable troops.

The political situation had also deteriorated. The Duma's liberal and moderate members, who had spent the autumn of 1916 denouncing the government's incompetence, found themselves ignored by an emperor who spent most of his time at the Stavka. Cabinet ministers changed frequently; the interior minister, Aleksandr Protopopov, assured the court that the situation in the capital was under control. The Allied delegations that had visited Petrograd earlier that winter had left with unease about Russia's ability to continue the war, but also with the expectation of a spring offensive. Neither the authorities nor the oppositional politicians anticipated that the crisis would take the form of a mass uprising in the streets within the following week. Students should note the paradox: nearly everyone expected trouble, and almost no one expected that the state's coercive capacity could simply give way.

This section serves as a handoff from Chapter 48 and not as a repeat of its analysis. The conditions summarized here, queues, inflation, strikes, a garrison of uncertain loyalty, a distrusted government, a distant emperor, and a divided opposition, are best understood as a set of vulnerabilities. They explain why a protest was possible and why the state's response might falter. They do not by themselves explain why the protest of 23 February [8 March] became a revolution. For that, the chapter turns to the events of the next eight days.

Historical Vocabulary
Old Style (O.S.) and New Style (N.S.)

Definition: Old Style dates follow the Julian calendar, used for civil purposes in the Russian Empire until 1918; New Style dates follow the Gregorian calendar, adopted in most of Europe from the sixteenth to eighteenth centuries. In the twentieth century the Julian calendar ran thirteen days behind the Gregorian.

Historical context: The "February Revolution" began on 23 February 1917 (O.S.), which was 8 March (N.S.). The Russian Orthodox Church continued to use the Julian calendar for its liturgical year. Soviet authorities adopted the Gregorian calendar in early 1918, which is why the "October Revolution" is commemorated in November.

Why it matters: Contemporary Russian sources, including telegrams, diaries, and manifestos, use Old Style dates; Western newspapers and diplomatic records use New Style. Mixing the two systems silently produces false chronologies. This chapter uses Old Style with New Style in brackets, and students should do the same when citing sources, identifying which system a source uses. International Women's Day, 8 March in the Gregorian calendar, was observed in Russia on 23 February.

Section 49.2

Women, International Women's Day, and the Bread Demonstrations

International Women's Day had been observed by socialist women's organizations since 1911, and in Russia since 1913, usually with meetings and lectures. In 1917 the day fell on Thursday, 23 February [8 March]. Some socialist groups had planned speeches and gatherings, but there was no plan for a strike. The Bolshevik district committee in Vyborg, according to the later recollection of one of its activists, Vasily Kayurov, had advised women workers to hold back from a strike, in the belief that it was premature; women in the textile mills went out anyway. This testimony comes from a memoir written afterward, and historians treat it with the usual caution, but it is consistent with other evidence that the initiative came from women workers rather than from party organizations.

The women involved were primarily textile workers, who had few ties to the better-organized metal-working workforce. Women constituted a much larger share of the industrial labor force than before the war, having replaced men called to the army, and a large proportion of textile employees were women, many of them with husbands or sons at the front. On the morning of the 23rd the women of several Vyborg mills left their machines, went from factory to factory calling on male workers to join them, and gathered in the streets. Contemporary and police estimates of the number of strikers on the first day vary widely, from under 100,000 to well over that figure, reflecting both the difficulty of counting and differing definitions of participation. What is clear is that a very large fraction of the city's industrial workforce was involved by evening.

The demonstrators' slogans on the first day centered on bread, and on the return of husbands and sons from the front. Over the course of the day, crowds moved from the industrial Vyborg district toward the center of the city, cheering as they went, and bringing out additional workers. Observers noted that the mood was more festive than violent. Cossacks and police were deployed to prevent the crowds from crossing the bridges and from entering the Nevsky Prospekt, the main avenue, but at several points the Cossacks, who were not eager to attack women, allowed the crowds to pass or to slip beneath their horses. This apparent reluctance was noted by participants and by officials at the time; its significance would become clear in the following days.

Historians have asked why the women's action mattered. One reason is that the disturbance began without the direction of the underground parties, which meant that the authorities' intelligence about party plans offered little warning. A second is that bread was an issue that crossed class and occupational lines: queueing women of the lower middle class, soldiers' wives, and workers shared a grievance, and the government had little with which to answer it other than promises. A third is the symbolic and practical effect of women appealing to soldiers as sons, brothers, and husbands, a pattern reported on several of the following days. Treating women's role as simple "background" to a revolution led by men misreads the evidence. The Revolution began as a women's protest, and its early dynamics, including the relative gentleness of the crowds and the soldiers' reluctance to confront them, owed a good deal to that fact.

None of this means that the outbreak was solely about women or only about bread. Contemporary police reports describe political slogans, including calls of "Down with the war" and "Down with the autocracy," appearing within a day or two. The best-supported interpretation is that economic grievance, anger at the war, distrust of the government, and long-standing political radicalism in the factories were intertwined, and that the first day's events lowered the barrier to action for those who had been waiting for a signal. By nightfall on 23 February [8 March], the authorities regarded the day's demonstrations as serious but manageable. They were mistaken.

Section 49.3

From Protest to General Strike

On Friday, 24 February [9 March], the movement doubled in size. Strikes spread through most of the Vyborg district and into the metal plants of other parts of the city, and estimates of strikers range around 200,000, roughly half of the city's industrial workers by some counts. Workers marched in columns along streets and across the frozen Neva toward the center; police tried to block the bridges, but crowds crossed the ice. By afternoon thousands were on the Nevsky Prospekt, where students, clerks, and other townspeople mingled with workers. Some demonstrators carried red banners; others sang the Marseillaise or shouted for bread. Shop windows were broken in places, and tramcars were stopped, though looting remained limited. Cossack units again behaved ambiguously, avoiding direct attacks on the crowds and sometimes responding to their cheers.

The slogans began to change. Alongside "Bread," marchers called for an end to the war and an end to the monarchy, and the authorities began to describe the movement as politically subversive and not merely a food riot. This transformation is characteristic of mass movements under authoritarian governments: when a government cannot or will not address a specific grievance, and when it is widely distrusted, the grievance becomes a political demand. Socialist activists played a role at this stage, as organizers in factories and neighborhoods, as speakers at meetings, and as distributors of leaflets, though they did not control the movement. The Bolshevik Russian Bureau, the Mensheviks, the Socialist Revolutionaries, and the small Inter-District group (the Mezhraiontsy) all sought to give direction to the crowds, and in some districts local party members were prominent. Historians disagree about how much weight to assign their efforts, an issue taken up in the Historical Debate feature.

On Saturday, 25 February [10 March], the strike became a general one. Estimates suggest that a majority of Petrograd's industrial workers and many other employees, including some students and shop workers, took part. Most factories were closed; newspapers did not appear; trams ceased running in places. Crowds again gathered in the city center, and by this point a pattern had emerged: large demonstrations would form in the industrial districts, move toward the Nevsky Prospekt and Znamenskaya Square near the Nikolaevsky railway station, hold rallies, and disperse under pressure from police or troops, only to re-form elsewhere. The authorities, still operating on the assumption that firm action would restore order, posted warnings that crowds would be fired upon, and the military district commander, General Khabalov, prepared to deploy troops at key points. On that day demonstrators and police clashed; at least one police official was killed by a Cossack in an incident on Znamenskaya Square, an event often cited because it suggested that soldiers would not necessarily obey the police.

It is worth pausing on what made the strike general. Factory discipline and the cooperation among workers in different industries, built over a decade of strikes, meant that news of walkouts traveled fast. The geography of the city mattered: workers in the Vyborg district and other outlying areas could walk to the center; the Neva was frozen and passable on foot; and the police, too few to patrol every bridge and street, could not prevent crowds from converging. The social composition mattered too. Skilled male metalworkers, whose organizational experience was greatest, were joined by textile women, by white-collar employees, and by students. The movement's breadth made it difficult to suppress by arresting a few leaders, as had worked in the past. By the evening of 25 February [10 March], the authorities had lost the ability to restore normality through the ordinary instruments of policing. The question was whether the army would supply what the police could not.

Section 49.4

The Government Attempts Repression

The emperor was at the Stavka in Mogilev, having left Tsarskoe Selo on 22 February [7 March]. He received reports from the interior minister and from General Khabalov that tended to understate the seriousness of events, as well as letters from the empress, who took a dismissive view of the disorders. On the evening of 25 February [10 March], after receiving news of the day's demonstrations, Nicholas sent a telegram to Khabalov ordering him to put an end to the disorders in the capital the following day. Historians generally read this telegram as an order to use force if necessary. It reflected a belief that determined action would, as in 1905 and in other episodes, discourage the crowds. It also committed the emperor to a course from which retreat would be difficult.

Sunday, 26 February [11 March], was the day of the largest use of force. Soldiers, including training detachments and units of the Pavlovsky, Volynsky, and other regiments, were deployed in the center, and orders to fire on crowds were given at several locations. At Znamenskaya Square and on the Nevsky Prospekt troops fired, killing dozens of demonstrators and wounding many more; the best-known incident involved the training detachment of the Volynsky Regiment. The shootings dispersed the crowds, and in the evening the authorities felt that order had been temporarily restored. But the effect on the soldiers was different from what the authorities intended. Many of the men who had fired on the crowds were shaken, and others, in barracks across the city, heard of the shootings and discussed them. A company of the Pavlovsky Regiment, according to accounts of the evening, fired on mounted police in anger at the killings. Discipline was fraying even as the commanders believed they were restoring it.

The same day brought a political rupture. The government, with the emperor's approval, issued a decree suspending the sessions of the State Duma, a move designed to prevent the legislature from becoming a rallying point. The decision to prorogue the Duma had two consequences. First, it deprived the government of its principal channel to moderate opinion at a moment when Duma leaders, including Rodzianko, were urging the emperor to appoint a government with public confidence. Rodzianko sent the emperor telegrams on 26 and 27 February [11 and 12 March] warning that the situation in the capital was grave, that the government was paralyzed, that troops were firing on one another in places, and that a person enjoying the country's confidence should be charged with forming a government. Accounts differ in detail on how Nicholas received these messages, and some later memoirs attribute dismissive remarks to him; but the documentary record shows that the emperor did not change course in response. Second, the prorogation forced Duma members to decide whether to disperse, as the decree required, or to remain in the capital as a force capable of acting in the crisis.

Historical Vocabulary
Prorogation

Definition: The suspension of a legislature's sessions by the executive, without dissolving the legislature or calling new elections. Under the Fundamental Laws of 1906 the emperor could dissolve the State Duma and call new elections, and could determine the length and timing of its sessions.

Historical context: The decree of 26 February [11 March] 1917 suspended the Duma's sessions. It did not formally dissolve the body, a distinction that mattered because the deputies could argue that they had not been abolished, only told to stop meeting formally.

Why it matters: Deputies chose to meet not in formal session but in a "private conference," which allowed them to obey the letter of the decree while remaining together in the Tauride Palace. The prorogation thus both removed the monarchy's remaining link to moderate politicians and gave the deputies a legal form in which to organize a response (Section 49.8).

The most important development of the evening and night of 26 February [11 March], however, took place in the barracks. The government's use of the army had been predicated on the assumption that soldiers would obey. Instead, the troops who had been ordered to shoot at civilians found themselves in a moral and practical dilemma. Many were reservists and peasants in uniform, with little reason to defend a government they distrusted against crowds of workers, women, and townspeople who might include their relatives. They also feared that if the crowds prevailed, they would be the ones punished. Whether the army would remain a reliable instrument of repression had become, by the end of the day, the central question of the revolution. Section 49.5 shows how it was answered.

Section 49.5

Mutiny of the Petrograd Garrison

The mutiny began early on Monday, 27 February [12 March], in the training detachment of the Volynsky Regiment, a unit that had taken part in the shootings the previous day. According to the usual account, drawn from participants' testimony and later reconstructed by historians, the soldiers, who had been made uneasy by what they had done, discussed in the barracks whether to fire on the crowds again. When their commanding officer, Staff Captain Lashkevich, arrived to give orders, he was shot. A non-commissioned officer, Timofei Kirpichnikov, emerged as a leader of the soldiers, who then marched out of their barracks and called on neighboring units to join them. The Litovsky and Preobrazhensky regiments, the latter among the oldest and most prestigious of the imperial guards, were among those that followed. Accounts differ on the exact sequence and on how much was planned; the best-supported interpretation is that the initial act was a spontaneous reaction to an immediate dilemma, and that its spread was made possible by the conditions in the garrison.

Those conditions deserve emphasis. The garrison's reserve battalions were overcrowded, with thousands of men living in barracks built for far fewer; their training was rudimentary; their officers were often inexperienced; and the men themselves feared transfer to the front. They were also connected to the city around them. Many were of working-class or peasant background; they lived in a community that shared the shortages and the resentments of the queues; and during the previous days the demonstrators had appealed directly to them, in some cases handing them bread and cheering them. Military discipline in the Russian army depended on a combination of habit, hierarchy, and fear of punishment. Once a unit in the capital refused to obey and killed an officer without being suppressed, other soldiers calculated that the risks of obedience had become higher than the risks of joining. Mutiny thus acted as a cascade: each unit that joined made it safer for the next.

During the day the mutineers took up arms, seized the arsenal on the Liteiny Prospekt and distributed rifles and ammunition to soldiers and workers, and moved to take control of key points. They freed prisoners from the Kresty prison and other jails, including political prisoners and, in the confusion, many criminal ones. Crowds attacked police stations, burned records, and in some cases killed policemen and gendarmes who had become the targets of general hatred. The building of the district court was set on fire, as was the headquarters of the Okhrana, whose files were destroyed. Officers who tried to keep order were in some cases killed, in others disarmed; many simply stayed away from their units. By the evening, thousands of soldiers had joined the movement, and in the following days the numbers rose to well over 100,000, according to estimates that vary with the source. The rank-and-file soldiers who had joined, together with armed workers, were now a new and unprecedented presence in the streets of the capital.

The mutiny marked the turning point because it changed the question from whether the demonstrators could be dispersed to whether the government retained any force at all. Previously, the regime had faced crowds that were large but unarmed. Now it faced armed soldiers in its own capital, and the troops that remained loyal were fewer and less certain every hour. Historians of revolutions have long noted that regimes typically fall not when crowds appear, but when the armed forces refuse to suppress them or join them. The February Revolution fits that pattern closely. The point should not be pushed too far: the soldiers did not act in a vacuum, and their decision was shaped by what had happened in the preceding days, including the demonstrations, the strikes, and the killings of 26 February. The mutiny was the decisive event, but it presupposed the movement that had preceded it.

Section 49.6

The Collapse of Imperial Authority in Petrograd

What the mutiny did to state power can be understood by asking what a government needs in order to rule. It requires officials who carry out decisions, police who enforce them, communications by which orders are transmitted, and armed forces that will support the rest if necessary. By the night of 27 February [12 March] the imperial government in Petrograd had lost most of these. The police had effectively disappeared from the streets, many of them in hiding or dead; the Okhrana's records were burned. The military district headquarters could no longer rely on most units in the city. Khabalov, with a small force of remaining loyal troops, gathered at the Admiralty and then the Winter Palace, but the men were hungry, uncertain, and increasingly disinclined to fight; on 28 February [13 March] they dispersed or surrendered.

The Council of Ministers, the cabinet, met on the evening of 27 February [12 March] in the Mariinsky Palace. Prime Minister Prince Nikolai Golitsyn, who had earlier tried to resign and had been refused, presided over a body whose members had little to offer by way of practical measures. According to accounts of the meeting, the ministers concluded that the situation required either a military dictator or a government with public confidence; they telegraphed the emperor to say so, suggesting among other things that they would be willing to resign. Nicholas, instead, appointed General Nikolai Ivanov, a veteran of the Southwestern Front, to take command of the capital with dictatorial powers, and ordered him to move troops from the front. The ministers dispersed. In the following two days many were arrested by revolutionary crowds or by detachments acting under the authority of the Duma committee; Protopopov, the interior minister, presented himself at the Tauride Palace and was taken into custody.

Revolutionary crowds and armed soldiers took over government buildings and strategic points, among them the Peter and Paul Fortress, the telegraph and telephone offices, and railway stations. The Winter Palace and the Admiralty were occupied. Prisoners were released across the city. Within a day or two, the symbols of the old regime, from double-headed eagles on public buildings to portraits of the emperor, were being removed or defaced in many places, while red flags and ribbons appeared on public buildings and on the clothing of many ordinary people. These changes in the public sphere were not trivial. A government's authority depends partly on habit and ritual; once the ritual is broken, a regime's claim to obedience becomes harder to restore.

The central lesson of this section is that protest became revolution when the government lost the instruments by which it could enforce its commands. This is a different claim from saying that the regime was unpopular, though it was, or that it had made mistakes, though it had. It is a claim about power in the strict sense: in the capital, on 27 and 28 February, the emperor's government could no longer arrest, shoot, or even reliably communicate with the people it was supposed to govern. That condition could not be repaired by a decree, a telegram, or a change in ministers. The emperor was far away, and his appointment of a military commander with expeditionary troops depended on units that might themselves refuse to obey. The state had not been overthrown in battle; it had come apart in the capital, and a vacuum of authority had opened that others would try to fill.

Not all of the violence of these days was directed at the old regime's officials. Casualty figures for the revolution in Petrograd are uncertain, with commonly cited estimates of killed and wounded in the range of a thousand to some thousands over the days of fighting, including soldiers, workers, police, officers, and bystanders. Historians have also stressed the improvised discipline that appeared in the capital: volunteer militias, formed by workers and students, helped to guard buildings and protect property, and public meetings debated the next steps. The revolution was marked by violence against the police and some officers, by acts of crowd justice, and by an unusual degree of public organization and celebration. All of these features were part of the same event.

Section 49.7

Formation of the Petrograd Soviet

The Petrograd Soviet of Workers' and Soldiers' Deputies was formed on 27 February [12 March], on the same day as the garrison mutiny, in the Tauride Palace. Its immediate precedent was the St. Petersburg Soviet of Workers' Deputies of 1905, which had arisen during the general strike of that year and in which Leon Trotsky and others had played leading roles (Chapter 46, §46.6). That body had been suppressed after the revolution's defeat, but its example remained alive in the memory of the labor movement and the socialist parties. In 1917, a group of socialist activists, including members of the workers' group of the Central War Industries Committee, who had been freed from the Kresty prison that day, and Menshevik and other left-wing deputies of the Duma, issued an appeal calling on factories and military units to elect representatives and send them to the Tauride Palace.

The Soviet's organizers acted quickly. A provisional executive committee was formed under the chairmanship of the Menshevik Duma deputy Nikolai Chkheidze, with Matvei Skobelev and the Trudovik deputy Aleksandr Kerensky among its early leaders; it also included socialists from outside the Duma such as Nikolai Sukhanov, whose memoir later became one of the most important, and most debated, accounts of the period, and a smaller number of Bolsheviks, including Shlyapnikov. A first meeting that evening gathered workers' delegates and the first soldiers, and in the following days elections multiplied the number of deputies. Workers were elected on the basis of factories, roughly in proportion to their numbers; soldiers' deputies were elected by companies and other units, in ways that were often informal. By 1 March [14 March] the workers' and soldiers' sections had merged into a single body, which became known as the Petrograd Soviet of Workers' and Soldiers' Deputies.

The inclusion of soldiers was of great importance. The Soviet's claim to speak for armed units, many of which looked to it for guidance rather than to their officers or to the Duma, gave it a power in the capital that the 1905 soviet had never had. Soldiers' deputies came to outnumber workers' deputies, and soldiers' demands, concerning officers' behavior, discipline, and the army's status, shaped the body's early decisions. The best known of those decisions, Order No. 1, issued on 1 March [14 March], is treated in Chapter 50; it should be mentioned here only to note that the Soviet's authority over the troops of the garrison grew directly from its position as the representative of their interests in the first days of the revolution.

Historical Vocabulary
Soviet

Definition: The Russian word for "council." In 1905 and 1917 it referred to elected councils of workers, soldiers, and, later, peasants' deputies, which claimed to represent those groups and which in some circumstances exercised practical authority over their areas.

Historical context: The first soviets emerged in 1905, notably in St. Petersburg and Ivanovo-Voznesensk. In February–March 1917 soviets appeared in Petrograd, Moscow, and hundreds of other cities, towns, and army units. The Petrograd Soviet was the most influential, but local soviets differed in composition and politics. After the Bolsheviks took power in October 1917 the term was adopted for the organs of the new state, which is why "Soviet" later became the name of the country.

Why it matters: Students should not read the later state back into the early soviets. In February 1917 the Petrograd Soviet was dominated by Mensheviks and Socialist Revolutionaries, not Bolsheviks; many of its leaders did not wish to take governmental power themselves and believed that a government drawn from the propertied classes should be formed. The Soviet should be understood as a representative body that exercised influence, not as a government in waiting.

It is equally important to specify what the Soviet was not. It was not at this stage a government with a program of taking power, and most of its leaders, who regarded the revolution as a "bourgeois" one that should lead to a government of the propertied classes and liberal politicians, were reluctant to assume governmental responsibility. This stance made the Soviet's leaders partners in the creation of a government with the Duma's leaders, though also supervisors of it, a relationship that would shape the next months and that is treated in Chapter 50 under the heading of "dual power." This chapter stops at the point where the Soviet appeared, claimed to represent workers and soldiers, and acquired the loyalty of the garrison.

Section 49.8

The Duma Committee and the Search for Government

The deputies of the State Duma, having received the prorogation decree on 26 February [11 March], gathered on the 27th in the Tauride Palace in what they called a private conference, not a formal session. Their position was uncomfortable. Most were not revolutionaries and had little desire to see the monarchy overthrown by a street uprising; many feared that the mob violence and soldiers' mutiny would destroy the army and the war effort, and perhaps their own social position. At the same time they were aware that the government they had criticized for months had lost control of the capital. The question was whether to disperse, obeying the decree, or to take action to restore order and to fill the vacuum.

On the evening of 27 February [12 March] the conference created the Provisional Committee of Members of the State Duma for the Restoration of Order in the Capital and for Relations with Persons and Institutions, known as the Temporary Committee. Its chairman was Mikhail Rodzianko, the Duma's president. Its members included liberal figures such as Pavel Milyukov, the leader of the Constitutional Democrats (Kadets), and Octobrist and other centrist deputies, the monarchist Vasily Shulgin, and, significantly, the Menshevik Chkheidze and the Trudovik Kerensky, who linked the committee to the emerging Soviet. The committee's title was cautious: it claimed to be restoring order and communicating with others, not to be a government. Rodzianko, according to later accounts, hesitated before agreeing to serve, aware that he was acting in tension with the emperor's orders. Historians generally interpret the decision as a response to events: the deputies stepped into a vacuum because no one else was available.

On 28 February [13 March] the committee began acting as an authority. It sent commissioners to take charge of ministries and government offices, appointed a commandant of the Petrograd garrison, and sought to bring the mutinous soldiers under some form of officer leadership; it also moved to take control of the railways through one of its members, Aleksandr Bublikov, who used the railway telegraph network to announce that the Duma Committee had assumed authority and, in a separate order, restricted the movement of military trains toward Petrograd without the Committee's authorization. This step mattered a great deal: the railways were the arteries by which the emperor and loyal troops might return to the capital, and by controlling them, the committee obstructed any counterattack. The committee also appealed to officers and soldiers for discipline and cooperation, and tried to ensure the provision of food in the capital.

The committee's ambiguity is the key to understanding the next days. It was not a revolutionary body: its leaders wished to avoid a collapse of the war effort and, many of them, to preserve a monarchy, perhaps in reformed form, with a government responsible to the Duma. But it also could not be a purely conservative body, because its authority depended on the revolution that had made the old government powerless. Its leaders therefore began to negotiate, on the one hand with the Petrograd Soviet, whose cooperation they needed to restrain the soldiers and workers, and, on the other, with the emperor, whose abdication they would soon decide to seek. On 1–2 March [14–15 March] they negotiated with the Soviet's executive committee over the composition and program of a new government. A Provisional Government, headed by Prince Georgy Lvov with Milyukov as foreign minister, Aleksandr Guchkov as war minister, and Kerensky as minister of justice, was formed on 2 March [15 March] and publicly proclaimed on 3 March [16 March]. Its relationship to the Soviet is the central subject of Chapter 50.

Sections 49.7 and 49.8 together show the emergence of competing centers of revolutionary authority. The Soviet drew its legitimacy from workers and soldiers who elected its members, and its practical power from the garrison's loyalty. The Duma Committee drew its legitimacy from the Duma's elected status, however limited by the franchise, and its practical power from its ability to speak for the educated and propertied public, to claim a legal continuity with the previous order, and to deal with the army's officers and the Allies. Neither could govern without the other, and the relations between them were uneasy from the start. Neither was predetermined to prevail, and in the first days of March their leaders were still deciding what they wanted to do about the monarchy.

Section 49.9

Nicholas II at Stavka and the Failed Restoration of Control

Throughout these days the emperor was at Mogilev, where the Stavka coordinated the war on the Eastern Front. His information about Petrograd arrived through telegrams from Khabalov, from the ministers, and from Rodzianko, and in letters and telegrams from the empress at Tsarskoe Selo, where several of their children were ill with measles. The information was incomplete, delayed, and often contradictory. Khabalov and Protopopov in the first days tended to minimize the disorders, while Rodzianko's warnings of 26 and 27 February [11 and 12 March] described a collapse of government. Nicholas, faced with these differing accounts, relied on the first set until events made it impossible. When he learned on 27 February [12 March] that the garrison had mutinied and that the ministers had lost control, he reacted by sending troops. General Ivanov, appointed to command in the capital, was to take a force from the front, including a battalion of St. George's Cross holders, and restore order by force.

The expedition never became a military force. Ivanov's troops were small in number, they were delayed by railway congestion, and many of those units were infiltrated by news and rumor of the revolution. By 1 March [14 March] Ivanov had reached the neighborhood of Tsarskoe Selo but could neither enter Petrograd nor persuade his soldiers to fight. He halted at the emperor's instruction after Nicholas, under pressure from his generals, agreed to political concessions. The military option, which had seemed to the emperor and the ministers on 27 February [12 March] to be the way to restore control, had dissolved. The point is a general one: a restoration of control by force would have required not only a commander but also dependable troops and open communications, and in the last days of February none of these existed.

Nicholas decided to go himself. In the early hours of 28 February [13 March] his train left Mogilev for Tsarskoe Selo, where his family was. The route was blocked: the Duma committee's control of the railway system, the disruption of traffic, and the rumors that stations ahead were in revolutionary hands led to a series of delays and detours. The train was stopped near Malaya Vishera, at a point from which the direct route to the capital passed through territory that could not safely be traversed, and was rerouted by way of Bologoe and Dno toward Pskov. It reached Pskov, the headquarters of General Ruzsky's Northern Front, on the evening of 1 March [14 March]. For about two days the emperor, in effect, was out of contact with the capital except through telegraph and telephone lines controlled by others, and his movements were determined by the railway system on which his government had stopped being able to rely.

At Pskov, Ruzsky, an experienced general with liberal sympathies, urged the emperor to concede a responsible ministry, that is, a government answerable to the Duma, headed by Rodzianko. Nicholas agreed on the evening of 1 March [14 March], after long hesitation, and the offer was transmitted. It was too late. When Ruzsky spoke with Rodzianko by direct telegraph apparatus during the night of 1–2 March [14–15 March], Rodzianko replied that the situation had moved beyond the point at which a concession of this kind could be effective: the revolutionary movement in the capital, the army, and the Duma was demanding the emperor's abdication, and the question had become whether the dynasty could be saved. The record of this telegraph conversation, preserved and later published, is among the most important documents of the revolution. It shows the speed at which political demands were changing: a ministry responsible to the Duma, which liberals had sought for years, had become irrelevant in a matter of days.

The sequence illustrates a theme that recurs in the chapter. Nicholas II's decisions during the crisis, to leave for the Stavka, to prorogue the Duma, to order force, to refuse a political compromise until too late, and to travel by train into a region where his authority was slipping, each seemed defensible to him at the time, and each made the situation worse. But they should not be treated as the sole cause. A different ruler might have conceded earlier, and an earlier concession might have changed the dynamics, but it is equally plausible, as many historians argue, that the structural weakness of the state and the speed of the garrison's defection would have overwhelmed any decision. The point of the narrative is to show how choices operated inside constraints, not to assign blame to one man.

Section 49.10

The Generals, the Political Elite, and the Decision for Abdication

The decision for abdication was not made by the revolutionary crowds or by the Soviet. It was made, in effect, by a convergence of two elite groups: the leaders of the Duma and the senior officers of the army. General Mikhail Alekseev, chief of staff at the Stavka and in practice the manager of the army's day-to-day operations, was a conservative professional soldier whose overriding concern was the war. In the first days of the revolution he shared the emperor's wish to restore order, but his information from Petrograd, together with Rodzianko's reports and the news of the spread of disorder to Moscow and the Baltic Fleet, convinced him that force would not work and that a continuation of the crisis threatened the army itself. By 1–2 March [14–15 March] he had concluded that a political solution requiring the emperor's departure was necessary to prevent a disintegration of the front.

After the Rodzianko–Ruzsky telegraph conversation, Alekseev sent a message on the morning of 2 March [15 March] to the commanders of the fronts and fleets asking for their opinion on the advisability of the emperor's abdication in favor of his son. The replies, which arrived within hours, were nearly unanimous. Grand Duke Nikolai Nikolaevich, the emperor's uncle and commander on the Caucasus front, urged the step in strong terms; Generals Aleksei Brusilov of the Southwestern Front, Aleksei Evert of the Western Front, and Vladimir Sakharov of the Romanian Front supported it, as did Ruzsky; and the commander of the Baltic Fleet, Admiral Adrian Nepenin, responded in the same sense. The Black Sea Fleet commander Admiral Aleksandr Kolchak is generally described as having not joined the appeal in the same manner. Historians stress that these were not revolutionaries but men who feared that unrest at the rear would undermine the army and that only the emperor's departure could restore political order and permit the war to continue.

The Duma leaders' motives overlapped with those of the generals but were not identical. Rodzianko, Milyukov, Guchkov, and others had hoped to preserve the monarchy as an institution, perhaps through the accession of a young Alexei with a regent, and they feared that continued resistance by Nicholas would lead to a radicalization that could sweep away the dynasty entirely. They were also aware that the soldiers and workers in the streets, and the Soviet, would not accept Nicholas. Guchkov, a former Duma chairman and a figure with strong ties to the army and to industrial circles, and Shulgin, a right-wing monarchist, were chosen to go to Pskov to receive the abdication and to ensure it was in a form that would help preserve a constitutional monarchy. That the monarchist right would be carrying the abdication illustrates the extent to which even supporters of the old order concluded that the emperor was an obstacle.

Students should be cautious about two oversimplifications. One is that the generals "overthrew" the emperor in a coup. The documents show military leaders advising, urging, and pressing the emperor, but they did not arrest him or order his removal; rather, they withdrew the support on which his authority depended and made clear that they would not use the army to defend him. The other is that the generals were merely responding to a revolution made by others. They acted on their own judgment about the war and about what was necessary to preserve the army, and their decision decisively shaped the form the abdication took. The political elite and the military command abandoned Nicholas II, each for its own reasons, at the moment when the streets had already taken the capital. Without that abandonment the revolution might have been prolonged and its outcome different, perhaps with a violent attempt at restoration; without the street movement and the mutiny it would not have occurred at all.

Section 49.11

Abdication of Nicholas II and the End of Romanov Rule

On 2 March [15 March], at Pskov, Nicholas II received the replies of the generals. According to the accounts of those who were present and to the emperor's own diary, he concluded within hours that he had no alternative. His first decision was to abdicate in favor of his thirteen-year-old son, the Tsarevich Alexei, with his brother Michael as regent, a solution consistent with the proposals of the Duma leaders and the generals. He drew up telegrams to that effect for Rodzianko and Alekseev. The act seemed to preserve the dynasty and the constitutional monarchy as institutions, and it matched what many of his advisers expected.

Nicholas then changed his mind. In the course of the day he consulted the court physician, Sergei Fedorov, about Alexei's health. The Tsarevich suffered from hemophilia, a hereditary blood disorder, and the doctor, according to later accounts, told the emperor that the disease was incurable and that the boy's life expectancy was uncertain. Nicholas, who could not bear the prospect of being separated from his son and who feared for the boy's safety if he remained in Russia under a regency, decided to abdicate for both himself and Alexei in favor of Michael. When Guchkov and Shulgin arrived at Pskov late that evening, they found that the emperor had already decided on this alternative. They raised legal questions, since the succession law did not obviously allow an emperor to renounce the rights of a minor heir, but they did not press them. The manifesto, which had been drafted with the participation of staff at the Stavka and revised at Pskov, was dated about 3 p.m. on 2 March [15 March], although the final signing took place later in the evening, according to most accounts.

The document, whose content is discussed in the Primary Source Window, announced Nicholas's abdication on behalf of himself and his son, named Michael as successor, and called on Michael to rule in unity with the representatives of the people in the legislative institutions, on the principles to be established by them, and to take an oath to that effect. It presented the abdication as a patriotic sacrifice demanded by the needs of the war and the unity of the country. It thereby framed the end of the dynasty as a voluntary act undertaken for the good of Russia, a formulation consistent with Nicholas's own self-understanding but one that obscured the pressure of generals, politicians, and events.

How did Nicholas himself experience these events? His diary, brief and self-controlled in style, records the abdication with few words, saying that he had signed the act after being persuaded by the reports of the generals, and noting his sense of being surrounded by betrayal. It records, too, his desire to see his family. The self-presentation, calm and fatalistic, has been interpreted differently by historians: some see it as evidence of his detachment and of his inability to grasp the political situation; others see it as the expression of a man who believed in the sacred character of the monarchy and who felt that he had been abandoned by those whose duty was to support it. The diary is a valuable contemporary source on his state of mind, but a limited one, because it is terse and was not written for public view.

The abdication ended the reign of Nicholas II, which had begun in 1894, and with it the effective power of the House of Romanov as the autocratic government of Russia. The legal and constitutional status of the abdication was uncertain, and some contemporaries and later scholars have questioned whether Nicholas had the right to abdicate for his son. But the question was soon overtaken by events, and it is better understood as an indication of the improvised character of the entire process. Nicholas returned to Mogilev on 3 March [16 March], took leave of the Stavka staff a few days later, and was placed under arrest by representatives of the Provisional Government; he and his family were detained at Tsarskoe Selo. Their fate in 1918 belongs to Chapter 52. Michael's decision, the last stage in the end of the monarchy, took place the next day.

Section 49.12

Grand Duke Michael and Why the Monarchy Collapsed

The Grand Duke Michael Aleksandrovich learned of his brother's abdication in Petrograd, where he had come from Gatchina, and the situation he faced on 3 March [16 March] was extraordinary. He had not sought the throne, had been told nothing of the plan, and was named heir only by a document he had not seen. In the morning, Duma leaders and members of the new Provisional Government came to meet him at the apartment of Prince Putyatin on Millionnaya Street. They were divided. Milyukov and Guchkov argued that the monarchy was needed as a symbol of continuity and that Michael should accept the crown, at least provisionally, as a constitutional monarch. Kerensky and Rodzianko, among others, argued that the mood of the soldiers and workers in the capital made his acceptance dangerous, and that they could not guarantee his safety or the public order if he did. After conferring privately with Rodzianko, Michael decided not to assume supreme power at that time.

His act, published on 4 March [17 March], stated that he would assume supreme power only if that was the will of the great people, expressed through a Constituent Assembly to be elected by universal suffrage, and it called on Russian citizens to submit to the Provisional Government until such an assembly could establish a form of government. Michael thus neither abdicated nor reigned: he made the throne conditional on a future popular decision. In practice, the effect was to end the monarchy, since there was now no monarch and no mechanism by which one could be confirmed until a Constituent Assembly met. The formal declaration of a republic did not come until September 1917, and the Constituent Assembly's fate is part of Chapters 50 and 52. But the monarchy that Michael Romanov, the first of the dynasty, had founded in 1613 (Chapter 25, §25.9, and Chapter 26), effectively ended with another Michael Romanov's conditional deferral of power three centuries later, in a coincidence of names that contemporaries and later writers did not fail to notice.

Why did the monarchy collapse so rapidly? The question has no single answer, and students should resist both of the simplest ones. It is not adequate to say that the masses overthrew the tsar: the demonstrations and strikes were essential but would not have succeeded had the garrison not mutinied, as in 1905, when the army remained loyal and the government survived. Nor is it adequate to say that the generals or the political elite removed him: they acted only after the capital had been lost, and in response to a revolution they had not planned. The explanation lies in the combination of several sets of factors, which can be separated for analysis but which operated together.

First are long-term institutional weaknesses. The Duma monarchy had left a legitimacy gap: it gave society a legislature and a public voice but withheld responsible government, and the emperor's control of ministers, the court's reputation, and the weakness of the bureaucracy left the system without the flexibility to absorb shocks. Second are the wartime pressures analyzed in Chapter 48, which strained the economy, drained the army, politicized the population, and eroded the loyalty of the elite. Third are Nicholas II's own decisions, including his assumption of the supreme command, his absence from the capital, his reliance on the court circle, his refusal of compromise, and his orders to use force. Fourth is popular mobilization: the women, workers, and others whose action in the streets created the crisis. Fifth is the decisive defection of the soldiers, which removed the state's coercive power in the capital. Sixth is elite abandonment: the Duma leaders and generals who withdrew their support from the emperor and thereby foreclosed a violent restoration. Seventh is contingency and timing: the winter's weather, the emperor's absence, the rumor of rationing, the accident that the Volynsky detachment was the first to refuse, and the speed of the garrison's cascade all shaped the outcome.

Comparison sharpens the point. In 1825 (Chapter 37) officers led soldiers into Senate Square, but the regime held, because most of the army and the capital's administration stayed loyal. In 1905 (Chapter 46) the regime survived mass strikes and soviets, largely because the army remained obedient. In the eighteenth-century palace revolutions (Chapter 33) the guards deposed rulers in the name of competing elite factions, but without mass uprisings. February 1917 combined elements of all these: a mass movement, a military defection, and elite withdrawal of support. Only the combination was fatal. And the outcome was far from settled by it. The monarchy had ended, but what would take its place, a liberal government, a council of workers and soldiers, a republic, a continuation or end of the war, remained entirely open. The two centers of authority that had emerged in the Tauride Palace were now compelled to find a way of living together, which is the subject of Chapter 50.

Primary Source Window

The Abdication Manifesto of Nicholas II, 2 March 1917 O.S. [15 March N.S.]

Creator: Nicholas II, with the assistance of staff at the Stavka and Northern Front headquarters · Date: 2 March 1917 O.S. [15 March N.S.], dated about 3 p.m. · Type: Imperial manifesto (official state document)

Context: The manifesto was prepared at the Stavka and revised at Pskov, where the emperor's train was standing, after the front commanders had urged abdication. It was signed in the presence of General Ruzsky and other officers, and delivered in typed form to Guchkov and Shulgin, who took it to Petrograd. It was published on 4 March [17 March]. Students should consult a reliable published translation for the exact text.

Brief paraphrase of the content (not a verbatim translated quotation):

The manifesto states that Russia is engaged in a struggle with a foreign enemy that has lasted nearly three years, that internal disturbances threaten the conduct of the war, and that the nation's fate and the honor of the army require unity. The emperor therefore declares, in agreement with the State Duma, that he is renouncing the throne for himself and his son, and names his brother Michael as successor. He asks Michael to rule in complete unity with the representatives of the people in the legislative bodies and to swear an oath to the country, and he calls on all sons of the fatherland to obey the new sovereign.

Analysis: The manifesto is a primary source for how the monarchy chose to present its own end. It represents abdication as voluntary, patriotic, and consistent with the interests of the war, and it speaks the language of sacred duty and national unity. It reveals the monarchy's priorities, which are the continuation of the war and the preservation of the dynasty through the named successor, and its claim to continuity through the phrase "in agreement with the State Duma." It does not mention the strikes, the mutiny, the generals' telegrams, or the revolution. Its limitations are therefore considerable: it is the product of an emperor under pressure, edited by others, and designed to shape public understanding. It is silent about the perspectives of the workers, soldiers, and women whose actions created the crisis, and about the generals' role. It can answer questions about how the abdication was officially framed and what legal basis it claimed; it cannot alone explain why Nicholas abdicated or how the revolution happened. Historians read it with the emperor's diary, the Rodzianko–Ruzsky telegraph record, the generals' replies to Alekseev, and the memoirs of Guchkov and Shulgin.

Historical Debate

Who Made the February Revolution?

The question: Was the February Revolution a spontaneous popular uprising, the product of organized political leadership, an elite maneuver against Nicholas II, or some combination?

Interpretation A — Spontaneity from below: Many participants and scholars have emphasized that no party directed the events. The women's strike began against the advice of local Bolshevik leaders, the garrison mutiny arose from an immediate dilemma in a single barracks, and the socialist leaders were surprised by it. On this reading the revolution grew out of hunger, war weariness, and a collapse of trust in the government, and the parties struggled to catch up.
Interpretation B — Organized leadership: Soviet-era historiography long stressed the leadership of the working class under the guidance of the Bolshevik party. Later Soviet scholars, among them Eduard Burdzhalov in a study published in the 1960s, argued for a more complex picture that recognized the spontaneity of the outbreak while still crediting activists in factories and districts. Other historians have stressed the role of mid-level socialist organizers, of all parties, in spreading strikes and giving slogans to the crowds.
Interpretation C — Elite action and conspiracy: Some contemporaries and later writers, particularly on the monarchist right, claimed that the revolution was engineered by liberal politicians, generals, or foreign powers. Historians have found evidence that elites discussed removing Nicholas II or replacing his ministers in the winter of 1916–1917, but no plan that explains the strikes or the mutiny, and claims of Allied or masonic engineering of the revolution are not supported by reliable evidence.
Interpretation D — Interaction: Detailed studies of the Petrograd events, including Tsuyoshi Hasegawa's work, emphasize the interplay of popular protest, soldier defection, political improvisation, and elite abandonment. This view is the one on which this chapter is chiefly built.

Evidence: Police and factory-inspector reports, party documents and memoirs, soldiers' testimony, telegrams and diaries of elites, and the Extraordinary Commission's records. Each type has biases: memoirists wrote with hindsight and often to defend their conduct.

Points of agreement: Many historians agree that no major political party planned or directed the initial outbreak: women workers initiated the demonstrations, while socialist activists subsequently helped spread and politicize the movement. They also agree that the garrison's mutiny was decisive, that the Duma Committee and the Soviet were improvised, and that the generals and Duma leaders acted after the capital was already lost.

Points of disagreement: The relative weight of economic and political motives, the importance of local socialist organizers, the generals' independent agency, and how far the outcome could have been different.

Why it matters: How historians explain February shapes how they explain October: a revolution without clear leadership suggests that later seizures of power exploited a vacuum, whereas a revolution directed by a party suggests continuity of design.

Multiple Perspectives

Eight Days in Petrograd

Women workers and queuers: For textile workers and soldiers' wives, the revolution began as a protest over daily survival: long hours at the mill, hours more in bread queues, and the absence of husbands at the front. Their appeal to soldiers as relatives and neighbors helped transform the movement. For them the end of the monarchy was neither the first aim nor, at the start, an expected result.
Reserve soldiers: Recruits and reservists in crowded barracks faced the order to fire on crowds that included people like their own families, under inexperienced officers, with the threat of the front over them. Their mutiny was both a refusal and a calculation of safety, and it gave them a new status as armed participants in political life.
Police and officials: Policemen, gendarmes, and ministers were the immediate targets of crowd anger. Understaffed and abandoned by the army, many fled or were killed; the evidence of officials' own testimony before the Extraordinary Commission shows disbelief and confusion at the speed with which authority evaporated.
Duma politicians and generals: Liberals, moderates, and conservative monarchists feared both the old regime's incompetence and the revolution's radicalism. They intervened to limit the damage, to keep the war effort alive, and, many hoped, to preserve a monarchy in some form.

These perspectives reflect different levels of power and risk: workers and soldiers faced shooting, while elites faced loss of status. The revolution's success depended on the interaction of these groups.

Cause and Effect

The Collapse of the Monarchy, February–March 1917

Long-Term Conditions

A reconstructed autocracy lacking responsible government; the weak legitimacy of the Duma monarchy; a rapidly growing industrial working class with a strike tradition; a court discredited by scandal.

Intermediate Developments

Wartime losses and mobilization; inflation, queues, and transport disruption (Chapter 48); the emperor's assumption of command and absence from the capital; a garrison of reservists in overcrowded barracks.

Short-Term Triggers

The Putilov lockout; rumors of rationing; the women's strike of 23 February [8 March]; the shootings of 26 February [11 March]; the prorogation of the Duma.

Event or Transformation

The mutiny of the garrison on 27 February [12 March], the loss of the state's coercive capacity in the capital, and the withdrawal of elite support from the emperor.

Immediate Consequences

Formation of the Petrograd Soviet and the Duma Committee; arrest of ministers; the emperor's isolation; abdication on 2 March [15 March]; Michael's deferral of supreme power on 3 March [16 March].

Long-Term Consequences

A state without a sovereign; competing centers of authority; unresolved questions of war, land, and nationalities (Chapter 50); the opening for later revolutionary change (Chapter 51).

Historical complexity: No single cause is sufficient. Hunger without mutiny, mutiny without elite abandonment, or elite discontent without mass protest would each have produced a different outcome. The collapse required the combination, and its timing depended on contingent events.

Continuity and Change Over Time

What the Revolution Altered

What Changed?

  • The Romanov monarchy and the emperor's claim to autocratic power ended within eight days.
  • The police apparatus was dismantled in the capital, and political prisoners were freed.
  • Soldiers became political actors, and soviets and committees appeared as new institutions.
  • Public language and symbols shifted: red ribbons, "citizen," and open political meetings.

What Remained?

  • The war against Germany and its demands on the economy, the army, and the food supply continued.
  • Much of the bureaucracy, local administration, and the army's command structure persisted, at least nominally.
  • The shortages and transport problems that triggered the strikes were not resolved.
  • The empire's diversity and unresolved land question remained.

When and who: Change in the capital was rapid, occurring within days; in the provinces it spread over weeks. Soldiers, workers, and police experienced the greatest immediate change; peasants in distant villages experienced more continuity at first. Why the pattern matters: The sudden fall of the monarchy did not by itself transform the social and economic conditions that had produced it, which helps explain why 1917 did not end in February.

Everyday Life and Social History

The Bread Queue and the Barracks

For a woman worker in a Vyborg textile mill in the winter of 1917, a working day of ten or more hours was followed by a search for food. Queues for bread formed before dawn and continued into the evening, in freezing weather; a woman might lose hours of sleep and part of her wages by standing in line. Many of these women were raising children while husbands served at the front, and rising prices outpaced wages. Housing in the industrial districts was crowded, and fuel for heating was scarce. The queue was not only a site of frustration but a place of conversation, where rumors circulated about rationing, hoarding, and officials' incompetence. These everyday conditions were the social setting in which a strike could spread by word of mouth.

In the barracks, the experience differed. Recruits and reservists slept in crowded halls, with insufficient training or equipment, under officers who were often young and inexperienced. They drilled, ate poorly, and awaited orders that might send them to the front. Many had left villages where families depended on their labor. When the demonstrators in the streets appealed to them as brothers, husbands, and sons, they heard it in the context of shared hardship. Evidence about everyday experience comes largely from memoirs, police reports, and later oral and written testimony, which mostly represent literate and urban participants; the experiences of rural women and of soldiers in distant garrisons are less well documented and should not be assumed to be identical.

Regional Context

Beyond Petrograd

News of the revolution spread by telegraph and railway, and different regions responded in different ways. In Moscow, the garrison and workers joined the movement within days, in a largely peaceful transfer of authority to a committee of public organizations and a soviet. At the naval base of Kronstadt and in the Baltic Fleet, sailors rose against their officers, and a number of officers, including Admiral Robert Viren at Kronstadt and, a few days later, Admiral Nepenin at Helsingfors, were killed; the numbers are uncertain and reported differently in different sources. At the front, soldiers' committees began to form, while commanders tried to keep the army cohesive.

In the empire's borderlands the revolution raised questions of national self-government. In Kyiv, Ukrainian civic and political leaders formed the Central Rada in March, beginning a process that Chapter 50 follows. In Finland, which had a distinct constitutional status as a Grand Duchy, the new government moved within weeks to restore the guarantees that had been weakened under the old regime. Poles, Baltic peoples, Jews, Muslims of the Volga and Central Asia, and peoples of the Caucasus were affected by the removal of restrictions and by the new possibilities of political organization. In Central Asia the memory of the revolt of 1916 and its suppression shaped local reactions. Students should avoid treating these regions as passive recipients of news from Petrograd: they had their own institutions, interests, and trajectories, which Chapters 50 and 52 explore.

Russia and the World

Allies, Enemies, and the News

The revolution occurred in the third year of a world war, and foreign governments observed it as a wartime event. Britain and France, Russia's allies, had been concerned about the court's influence and had sent delegations to Petrograd earlier in the winter; the news of the monarchy's fall prompted both hope that a more efficient, popular government would fight harder and fear that disorder would weaken the Eastern Front. The Allied governments recognized the Provisional Government quickly, with the United States, not yet at war with Germany, the first to do so, on 9 March [22 March], and others following within days. Western newspapers described the events as a victory for liberalism over autocracy. Germany and Austria-Hungary, for their part, watched for signs that Russian military capacity would decline and that a separate peace might become possible, though no one yet knew how the new authorities would approach the war. The revolution also drew attention from the international socialist movement, which saw in it a vindication of hopes first raised in 1905. Russia's revolution was thus never purely domestic: its causes lay partly in the demands of an international war, and its consequences would depend on the international system in which it occurred. Note that Russia's partners did not simply react; many Russian politicians sought Allied recognition as a source of legitimacy, and Allied expectations constrained the new government's choices.

Section 49.13

Chapter Review and Historical Thinking Checkpoint

This chapter has argued that the February Revolution was a rapid and contingent collapse of state authority, not an automatic consequence of the wartime crisis described in Chapter 48. The transformation occurred when demonstrations and strikes begun by women and extended by the city's workers converged with the mutiny of the Petrograd garrison, depriving the government of coercive control, while political and military elites, concluding that the emperor could no longer serve the war effort, withdrew their support and helped arrange his departure. New institutions, the Petrograd Soviet and the Duma's Temporary Committee, appeared at once and began to compete and cooperate for authority.

Several continuities and changes should be kept in mind. The monarchy ended, but the war, the shortages, the bureaucratic and military structures, and the unresolved questions of land and nationality continued. Causation was multiple: long-term institutional weakness, wartime strain, the emperor's decisions, popular mobilization, military defection, elite abandonment, and contingency each played a part. The evidence is rich but uneven, drawn from police reports, telegrams, diaries, official documents, and memoirs, each with its own perspective and limitations. Historians generally agree that no major party planned or directed the initial outbreak, though socialist activists later helped spread and politicize it, and that the mutiny was decisive; they differ over the weight of organized activists, the generals' agency, and how far the outcome was avoidable. The chapter's broader significance is that it leaves the question of what would replace the monarchy open. Chapter Summary

This chapter examined how the wartime crisis of the Russian Empire became a revolution that ended the Romanov monarchy within eight days. Its central problem was not why discontent existed, which Chapter 48 explained, but why in late February 1917 discontent produced the sudden loss of state authority in the capital and the disappearance of a centuries-old dynasty.

The conditions in Petrograd, queues, inflation, strikes, a garrison of reservists, a discredited court, and an emperor at distant headquarters, created vulnerabilities rather than inevitabilities. On 23 February [8 March], International Women's Day, women textile workers walked out and appealed to male workers to join them. Their action, which began without direction from the political parties, drew on a grievance over bread that cut across social lines and quickly acquired political slogans.

Over the next two days the strike expanded into a general strike, with perhaps 200,000 or more workers in the streets, crowds crossing the frozen Neva, and demonstrators reaching the center of the city. The police could not contain them. The emperor ordered the commander of the military district to end the disorders, and on 26 February [11 March] troops fired on crowds, killing dozens. The government also prorogued the Duma, severing its link to moderate opinion.

The use of force proved self-defeating. Soldiers in the reserve battalions, many of them recent conscripts living in crowded barracks, were reluctant to fire on civilians. On 27 February [12 March] the Volynsky training detachment mutinied, killing an officer, and the mutiny spread through the garrison. Soldiers seized the arsenal, freed prisoners, and attacked the police. This was the decisive turning point, because it removed the state's coercive power in the capital and turned a protest movement into a revolution.

The collapse of the old government was swift. Ministers lost control, the Council of Ministers dispersed, loyal troops surrendered, and officials were arrested. In the vacuum two new centers of authority appeared in the Tauride Palace: the Petrograd Soviet of Workers' and Soldiers' Deputies, modeled on the soviet of 1905, which drew its strength from workers and the garrison, and the Temporary Committee of the State Duma, which drew its legitimacy from the elected Duma and claimed to restore order. Their uneasy relationship would shape the months that followed.

The emperor, at Mogilev and then on his train, found himself isolated. His attempt to send troops under General Ivanov failed, his journey toward the capital was blocked, and his concession of a responsible ministry at Pskov on 1 March [14 March] came too late. When the front commanders, polled by General Alekseev, advised abdication, and the Duma leaders concurred, Nicholas abdicated on 2 March [15 March], first intending to do so in favor of his son and then, after consulting his physician about Alexei's hemophilia, in favor of his brother Michael.

On 3 March [16 March] Michael deferred assuming supreme power unless and until a Constituent Assembly confirmed it; in practical political terms this ended the monarchy. The chapter's concluding analysis argued that the collapse should be understood through the interaction of long-term institutional weakness, wartime strain, the emperor's decisions, popular mobilization, military defection, elite abandonment, and contingency, and that neither "the masses overthrew the tsar" nor "the generals overthrew the tsar" is an adequate account.

Historians generally agree that no major party planned or directed the initial outbreak, though socialist activists later helped spread and politicize it, and that the garrison's mutiny was decisive; they differ over the role of organized activists, the generals' independent agency, and the weight of economic versus political motives. The evidence, from police reports to telegrams, diaries, manifestos, and memoirs, requires careful distinction between contemporary documents and later recollection. Regionally, the revolution spread to Moscow, the navy, the front, and the borderlands in different ways, and internationally it was received as a wartime event by allies and enemies alike.

The chapter ended with a monarchy gone and its replacement undecided. Chapter 50 examines the Provisional Government, the Soviet, and the problem of dual power; Chapter 51 follows the Bolsheviks' rise. Students should not assume that October was the inevitable result of February.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it explains how one of the most durable political institutions in Eurasia ended almost without a defender. The Romanov dynasty had survived invasion, civil war, palace coups, and the revolution of 1905; in 1917 it fell in days. The explanation offered here emphasizes interaction: popular protest, military mutiny, and elite withdrawal of support combined, and the speed of events owed much to contingency. It also shows how a state's authority depends on instruments, police, troops, communications, and the cooperation of elites, that can fail abruptly.

The chapter changed the terms of Russian politics. The old question of how to reform an autocracy gave way to the question of how to build a new authority from competing claims. The Petrograd Soviet and the Duma Committee embodied two answers, and the relations between them, together with the war, the land question, and the nationalities, would define the following months. What continued was the war, the shortages, and much of the administrative and military apparatus; what ended was the sacred and legal foundation of the old order.

The February Revolution also shows major themes of this textbook: the interaction of environment, economy, and war with political institutions; the importance of social groups often absent from older narratives, here especially women and soldiers; the role of historical memory, since the revolution has been remembered variously as a liberal triumph, a popular uprising, a catastrophe, and a prelude to October; and the need to resist teleology. Nothing in February made October inevitable.

Questions remain open: how much weight to give organized activists, how far the generals shaped the outcome, whether earlier concession might have saved a constitutional monarchy, and how different regions and peoples understood the change. Chapter 50 takes up the immediate problem the revolution bequeathed, the attempt of the Provisional Government and the Soviet to govern a country at war without a monarch.
