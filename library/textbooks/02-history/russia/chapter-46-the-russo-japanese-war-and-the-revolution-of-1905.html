Part VIII — The Late Imperial Crisis

The Russo-Japanese War and the Revolution of 1905

A History of Russia: From the Eurasian World to the Russian Federation

Chapter Opening

On a Sunday in January 1905, tens of thousands of men, women, and children set out toward the center of the imperial capital carrying icons, portraits of the tsar, and a petition. Most of them expected to be received as subjects appealing to a sovereign. Many did not return as subjects who believed in one.

On 9 January 1905 according to the Julian calendar then used in Russia (22 January in the Gregorian calendar), columns of St. Petersburg workers converged on the Winter Palace Square from the factory districts of the city's outskirts. They had been organized by a priest, Father Georgii Gapon, whose Assembly of Russian Factory and Mill Workers had grown out of an experiment in loyal, officially tolerated labor organization. They carried a petition addressed to Nicholas II that combined requests for shorter hours and better pay with demands for civil liberties and representative government, and that closed with an appeal to the tsar for justice. Troops were deployed across the city to prevent the marchers from reaching the palace. At several points, soldiers fired into the crowds. The event, remembered as Bloody Sunday, was not the beginning of the crisis this chapter examines, nor its end. It was the moment at which a crisis of war and administration became a crisis of the legitimacy of the monarchy itself.

This chapter studies the Russo-Japanese War of 1904–1905 and the revolutionary upheaval that accompanied and followed it. It asks why a war fought on the far eastern periphery of the empire, in Manchuria, Korea, and the seas around Japan, helped to produce the first great revolutionary crisis of the Russian autocracy at its center. The answer developed in the chapter does not rest on a single cause. Russia's eastward expansion, described in Chapters 39 and 44, committed the empire to strategic and commercial positions that its government managed poorly. Military defeat damaged the prestige of a regime that justified its authority partly through order, competence, and great-power status. At the same moment, industrial workers, peasants, soldiers and sailors, liberal professionals, revolutionary parties, and the peoples of the empire's borderlands were pursuing different grievances, and the war and its failures gave them a common political occasion.

The revolution of 1905 was not a single event but a sequence of overlapping upheavals in different places and social milieus. St. Petersburg workers petitioned and struck. Railway employees, printers, and professional associations paralyzed the administrative life of the empire in October. Peasants in the black-earth provinces and the Baltic countryside seized, burned, and cut timber on landed estates. Sailors on the battleship Potemkin mutinied in the Black Sea. In Poland, the Baltic provinces, the Caucasus, and Finland, national movements and social revolutionaries mobilized in ways that Chapter 45 prepared. Political violence also took darker forms: pogroms against Jewish communities and attacks on students and intellectuals by groups later called the Black Hundreds. A student of this period must therefore hold together several scales of analysis at once, from a single factory in Petersburg to a naval campaign across three oceans.

The chapter's central political turning point is the October Manifesto of 1905, issued after a general strike had made ordinary administration increasingly difficult. Nicholas II, advised by Sergei Witte, whom students met in Chapter 44 as the architect of state-led industrialization, promised civil liberties, an expanded electorate, and a legislature without whose approval no law would take effect. The manifesto did not create a parliamentary democracy, and contemporaries disagreed sharply about its sincerity and sufficiency. But it permanently altered the framework within which Russian politics would be conducted. Autocracy had been forced to concede, and yet it survived, because its opponents were divided, because most of the armed forces remained available to the government, and because concessions split the opposition. Understanding both halves of that outcome is the chapter's task.

Students should also be alert to what this chapter does not claim. It does not present 1905 as a rehearsal in which 1917 was already scripted. The revolution of 1905 created institutions, political experiences, memories, and unresolved conflicts that mattered greatly later, but the monarchy survived for another twelve years under substantially different domestic and international conditions, and its eventual collapse (Chapters 48 and 49) depended on the strains of a different, much larger war. Nor does the chapter treat industrialization alone as a cause of revolution. Chapter 44 showed that industrialization created concentrated workforces, urban networks, and new social relations; this chapter examines how particular political events activated those capacities.

The evidence for this period is unusually rich and unusually partisan. It includes government reports and police surveillance files, the petition itself, newspaper and journal reporting that became far freer after October, the minutes and proclamations of strike committees and soviets, memoirs by Witte, by participants such as Leon Trotsky and by loyalist officials, naval and military records, and the correspondence of the tsar. Each of these sources was created for a purpose, and each must be read critically. Casualty figures for Bloody Sunday, the number of strikers, the scale of peasant disturbances, and the numbers killed in pogroms and repression all vary according to the source, and this chapter reports ranges and identifies their origins rather than choosing one number for narrative convenience.

Finally, the chapter places Russia within a wider world. Japan's victory was a landmark in the history of modern empires, and its interpretation, as the first defeat of a European great power by an Asian one in the industrial era, was noticed across Asia and beyond. The Treaty of Portsmouth was negotiated under the mediation of the President of the United States. Russian revolutionaries communicated with and drew on socialist movements in Europe, while the autocracy borrowed money and relied on diplomatic support from France. The Revolution of 1905 also belongs to a wider age of constitutional and revolutionary upheaval that included Iran, the Ottoman Empire, China, and Mexico. By the chapter's end, students should be able to explain why war turned structural tensions into a general crisis of authority, and why the political order that emerged, examined in Chapter 47, was neither the old autocracy restored nor a completed democratic revolution.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why the Russian Empire's strategic, commercial, and railway interests in Manchuria and Korea brought it into conflict with Japan, without reducing the policy to irrational adventurism.
  2. Analyze the failure of diplomacy before February 1904, assessing the roles of strategic competition, court politics, and Russian miscalculation of Japan.
  3. Trace the main phases of the war on land and at sea, including Port Arthur, Mukden, and Tsushima.
  4. Evaluate competing explanations of Russian defeat, distinguishing logistical, strategic, command, and political causes from simple claims of backwardness.
  5. Interpret Bloody Sunday as a crisis of monarchical legitimacy, using the workers' petition as evidence while correctly describing the roles of Gapon, the authorities, and Nicholas II.
  6. Describe the escalation from local strikes to a general strike and the emergence of soviets, and distinguish the soviets of 1905 from later Bolshevik government.
  7. Compare peasant, military, and national-movement unrest as distinct but overlapping dimensions of the revolutionary crisis.
  8. Assess why Nicholas II issued the October Manifesto and what the manifesto did and did not establish.
  9. Explain why the regime recovered after October, with attention to opposition divisions, repression, loyalist violence, and the army.
  10. Evaluate whether 1905 should be described as a failed, incomplete, or constitutionally consequential revolution.
  11. Distinguish Old Style and New Style dates and apply them consistently when reading sources on 1905.
  12. Connect the events of 1905 to the later developments of Chapters 47 through 51 without treating 1917 as inevitable.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Did Russia's railway and military capabilities in the Far East increase its power faster than its government learned how to manage the commitments they created?
  2. Why did diplomacy between Russia and Japan fail in 1903–1904, and how much weight should historians give to individual decision-makers compared with structural rivalry?
  3. Why did Russia lose a war against a state with a much smaller population and economy, and why is "backwardness" an inadequate answer?
  4. Why did the shooting of petitioners on 9 January 1905 have such large political consequences?
  5. Were the soviets of 1905 primarily strike committees, embryonic governments, democratic workers' institutions, or several of these at different moments?
  6. How did peasants, sailors, soldiers, and non-Russian national movements experience and shape the revolutionary crisis differently from urban workers?
  7. Why did Nicholas II concede the October Manifesto, and why did the concession divide rather than unify his opponents?
  8. Why did the Revolution of 1905 force the autocracy to change but fail to overthrow it?

Historical Context

By the end of the nineteenth century the Russian Empire was a vast, multiethnic, multireligious continental state governed as an autocracy. In law and ideology the emperor held undivided authority, exercised through a bureaucracy, a police apparatus, and an army, as Chapters 38, 42, and 43 explained. After the assassination of Alexander II, the conservative reaction under Alexander III strengthened the powers of provincial and local officials, restricted the press and universities, promoted a state-supported Orthodoxy and Russification, and discriminated against religious and national minorities, particularly Jews (Chapter 43). Nicholas II, who succeeded in 1894, publicly rejected hopes for representative government as "senseless dreams," and held a strongly personal conception of his own duty to preserve autocratic authority intact.

Meanwhile the empire's economy was transforming. Chapter 44 traced the state-led industrialization of the 1890s associated with Witte: railway construction, protective tariffs, a gold-backed ruble, heavy foreign investment, and the growth of coal, iron, oil, and textile industries in the Donbas, Baku, Moscow, Petersburg, Poland, and the Urals. A new industrial workforce, much of it only one generation removed from the village, worked long hours in large factories and lived in crowded, poorly serviced districts. Legislation of 1897 limited the working day but left wages, safety, and the right to organize largely unregulated. The countryside, meanwhile, experienced rising population, land hunger, redemption payments left from the emancipation settlement (Chapter 42), and periodic harvest failure, including the severe famine of 1891–1892. Peasant communal institutions and collective memory of old grievances remained strong.

Political opposition was growing, but it was neither unified nor legally organized. Liberal members of the zemstvos, professional associations, and the educated public increasingly demanded a constitution and civil rights, and in 1903 many formed the Union of Liberation. The Socialist Revolutionary Party, founded around the turn of the century, drew on the populist tradition (Chapter 42) and combined agrarian socialism with a campaign of terrorist assassination against officials. Marxist Social Democrats, who held their founding congress in 1898 and split into Bolshevik and Menshevik factions in 1903, concentrated on the industrial working class. National parties and movements in Poland, Finland, the Baltic provinces, the Caucasus, and the Jewish population organized in their own spheres (Chapter 45). The government responded with repression, with limited reforms, and, in the early years of the century, with experiments in police-sponsored labor organization.

Internationally, Russia was a great power linked by alliance to France since the 1890s, a rival of Britain across Asia, a participant in the competitive scramble for influence in China, and a power whose Far Eastern frontier had advanced in the nineteenth century through treaties with the Qing empire (Chapter 39). The completion of the Trans-Siberian Railway and the building of its southern branch across Manchuria multiplied Russia's capacity and its commitments. The rise of Japan, which had defeated China in 1894–1895 and modernized its army, navy, and administration under the Meiji state, transformed the strategic environment. The chapter's story begins with this meeting of an overextended Russian eastern policy and a rapidly modernizing Japanese empire.

A note on dates. Russia used the Julian calendar (Old Style, O.S.) in civil life until 1918. In the twentieth century it ran thirteen days behind the Gregorian calendar (New Style, N.S.) used in Japan and most of Europe. This chapter gives dates of events in the Far East and in international diplomacy in New Style, and gives key domestic Russian dates in both systems, with the Old Style date first where Russian sources and later tradition use it, for example 9 January (O.S.) / 22 January (N.S.). The October Manifesto of 17 October (O.S.) corresponds to 30 October (N.S.). Students should always check which calendar a source or textbook is using.

Section 46.1

Russia in East Asia

Russian presence on the Pacific was the product of a long eastward movement across Siberia, traced in Chapter 28, and of nineteenth-century treaty settlements with the Qing empire, noted in Chapter 39. By the Treaties of Aigun (1858) and Beijing (1860), Russia acquired the left bank of the Amur and the Maritime region south of it, and in 1860 founded Vladivostok on the Sea of Japan as a naval and commercial post. These territories were thinly populated, remote from European Russia, and dependent on sea communication and on the Amur river system, which freezes in winter. A permanent overland link was therefore a strategic as well as an economic priority. In 1891 the government began building the Trans-Siberian Railway, supported by state finance and, indirectly, by the foreign capital and loans on which Witte's program depended (Chapter 44).

The railway gave Russia a new Far Eastern policy because it changed the calculation of cost and distance. A direct route to Vladivostok around the Amur bend was long and technically difficult. A shorter line across northern Manchuria, a Chinese territory, promised savings and would open a region of agricultural and mineral potential to Russian commerce. In 1896, in connection with a Russo-Chinese defensive agreement concluded after Japan's victory over China, the Qing government permitted a Russian-controlled company, the Chinese Eastern Railway, to build and operate such a line, under Russian guard. The railway's headquarters at Harbin grew rapidly into a Russian-managed city, with engineers, clerks, merchants, and soldiers from European Russia living alongside a much larger Chinese population. A southern branch toward the Liaodong Peninsula followed, and the Russo-Chinese Bank and Russian industrial interests followed the track.

The Liaodong Peninsula and Port Arthur were central to Russian naval planning. Vladivostok is ice-bound for part of the year, and the straits that lead out of the Sea of Japan can be controlled by an adversary. An ice-free port on the Yellow Sea seemed to many Russian officials a necessity for a Pacific fleet. In 1895 Russia had joined France and Germany in the Triple Intervention, pressing Japan to renounce its claim to Liaodong after the Treaty of Shimonoseki. In 1898, when European powers were extracting concessions from a weakened China, Russia obtained a twenty-five-year lease of Port Arthur and the commercial port of Dalny, later Dalian. Many Japanese leaders noted that the peninsula they had been compelled to give back to China was now held by Russia.

Historical Vocabulary
Leasehold (and "sphere of influence")

Definition: A leasehold, in the diplomacy of imperialism in China, was a territory formally leased by China to a foreign power for a fixed term (typically twenty-five or ninety-nine years) while Chinese sovereignty was nominally preserved. A sphere of influence was a region in which one power claimed preferential rights to railway, mining, or commercial concessions, usually through agreements with the local government and understandings with other powers.

Historical context: After the Sino-Japanese War of 1894–1895 Russia, Germany, Britain, France, and Japan all obtained leaseholds and spheres in China. Russia's Port Arthur lease (1898) and its railway rights in Manchuria belong to this pattern.

Why it matters: The terms show that Russian policy in East Asia was part of an international competition conducted through legal instruments and economic concessions, not only by conquest. They also help students see why Chinese sovereignty, though weak in practice, remained a legal and diplomatic issue in the settlement of 1905.

It would be a mistake to portray these policies simply as irrational adventurism. The Russian government had strategic reasons to want a warm-water naval base, secure railway access across Manchuria, and a voice in the future of Korea, which bordered the Russian Far East. Commercial groups and railway officials stood to benefit from the opening of Manchuria. Witte, who as finance minister was a principal architect of Manchurian railway policy, regarded the region mainly as a field for peaceful economic penetration, not for military occupation. After the Boxer uprising of 1900, in which Russian forces occupied Manchuria in response to attacks on the railway, military and civil officials, and a group around the court, pressed a more assertive policy. Russian elites were not unanimous, and the tension between Witte's economic strategy and the preferences of military and court figures became one of the sources of the crisis examined in Section 46.2.

But the Far Eastern expansion also illustrates overextension. The Chinese Eastern Railway and the Trans-Siberian were extraordinary engineering and financial achievements, but the line across Siberia was largely single-track, with limited carrying capacity, and for years a gap around Lake Baikal required ferries or, in winter, temporary track laid on the ice. Russian garrisons in Manchuria, the Kwantung leasehold, and the Pacific Squadron were far from reinforcement. Russia had created the means to project power but not the infrastructure, command arrangements, or political consensus needed to sustain a major war there. A useful question for students is whether the empire's new railway and military capabilities increased its power faster than its government learned to manage the commitments they created.

Japan, for its part, was not an inexplicably small and backward adversary. The Meiji government that had taken power in 1868 had centralized administration, introduced conscription, built modern industry and railways, created a constitution and elected Diet in 1889–1890, and constructed a navy largely from ships built in British and other European yards. Japan defeated Qing China in 1894–1895, obtained Taiwan and a large indemnity, and used the indemnity to expand its armaments. Its population, around forty-five million, was much smaller than Russia's population of about 130 million, but it was far closer to the theater of war and was organized for it. Korea, which Japanese strategists saw as a dagger pointed at their islands, was the center of Japan's security concerns, and the Russian presence at Port Arthur and in Manchuria made Korea a matter of vital interest to Tokyo.

Section 46.2

Origins of the Russo-Japanese War

The war's origins lie in the interaction of Russian and Japanese strategic aims, shaped by the weakness of China and the interests of other powers. After the Triple Intervention, Japan accepted a compromise, but it also embarked on a rapid naval and military expansion. In 1898 Russia and Japan reached an agreement that recognized Japanese commercial predominance in Korea, but it settled little. The Boxer rising of 1900 changed the situation. Russia sent troops into Manchuria in force, and although it announced that its occupation was temporary, it did not complete evacuation on the schedule agreed with China in 1902. In October 1903 Russia failed to carry out the second stage of the withdrawal. Meanwhile, Russian interests, including the timber concession on the Yalu River promoted by Alexander Bezobrazov, a retired guards officer with access to the court, appeared to Japan as a step toward Russian influence in northern Korea.

Japan responded diplomatically as well as militarily. In January 1902 it concluded the Anglo-Japanese Alliance with Britain, whose terms obliged each partner to remain neutral if the other fought a single adversary in East Asia, and to assist if a third power joined that adversary. The alliance reduced the risk that France or Germany would support Russia in a war against Japan, and it encouraged Japanese leaders to bargain firmly. From the summer of 1903 the two governments negotiated over a possible division of spheres, with Japan proposing in essence that Russia's special position in Manchuria be recognized in return for Russian recognition of Japan's interests in Korea. Russian counterproposals sought to limit Japanese influence in Korea, particularly the northern part, and to avoid binding commitments in Manchuria. Each side regarded the other's positions as evasive or excessive. In February 1904 Japan broke off the talks and severed diplomatic relations.

Decision-making in St. Petersburg was fragmented. Nicholas II took a close interest in Far Eastern affairs and distrusted advisers whom he regarded as too cautious. Witte, who favored negotiation and economic expansion, was removed as finance minister in August 1903. The new Viceroy of the Far East, Admiral Alekseev, held broad authority in the region but was not always coordinated with the capital. War Minister Kuropatkin, who had visited Japan and respected its army, urged caution, while the Bezobrazov group and officials who believed Japan would not fight argued for firmness. Some historians stress the influence of this "clique" on the tsar, while others argue that it has been overstated and that the policy reflected wider assumptions among Russian officials. The evidence does not allow the war to be explained by any one adviser.

One often-repeated explanation holds that the government deliberately sought a "short victorious war" to forestall revolution at home. The phrase is associated with Interior Minister Plehve, but its provenance rests mainly on the memoirs of Witte and others written later, and its exact meaning and significance remain uncertain. There is little reliable evidence that the government started the war for this purpose. It is more accurate to say that Russian leaders miscalculated. They underestimated Japan's willingness to fight and its capacity to mobilize and deploy forces quickly, they overestimated Russia's ability to bring its superior total resources to bear, and they believed that a firm posture would persuade Japan to accept a compromise. Some officials and commentators held Eurocentric or racialized assumptions about an Asian opponent, and such attitudes appear in sources of the period, but historians do not treat them as the sole explanation. Russian officials also had good reasons to think they were stronger than Japan on paper.

In the early hours of 8 February 1904 (N.S.; 26 January O.S.), Japanese destroyers attacked the Russian ships anchored at Port Arthur, and on the same day Japanese forces engaged Russian cruisers at Chemulpo (Incheon) in Korea. Formal declarations followed on 10 February. The war was thus the culmination of a failed bargaining process in which both sides believed that they could secure their aims, and in which Russia's leaders did not foresee that Japan would risk a war to win them. In Russia the opening of hostilities was met initially with patriotic demonstrations in some cities, a reminder that the war did not start as a revolutionary event; its political consequences developed as it progressed.

Section 46.3

Port Arthur and the War at Sea

The war opened with Japan taking the initiative at sea. The Japanese attack on Port Arthur on the night of 8–9 February 1904 (N.S.) came after the severing of diplomatic relations on 6 February but before the formal declaration of war on 10 February. The familiar formulation that Japan attacked "without declaration of war" is therefore accurate only if it is understood in this narrow sense: diplomatic relations had ended and war was widely expected, but the attack preceded the formal declaration. The torpedo attack damaged several Russian ships, including battleships, but did not destroy the squadron. Japanese naval forces under Admiral Togo then established a blockade of Port Arthur. The Russian Pacific Squadron, divided between Port Arthur and Vladivostok, never regained the initiative. In April 1904 the squadron's energetic commander, Admiral Stepan Makarov, was killed when his flagship struck a mine, a loss that deprived the Russian navy of its most capable leader in the theater. In August the Russian fleet tried to break out of Port Arthur toward Vladivostok but was turned back in the Battle of the Yellow Sea, in which its commander, Admiral Vitgeft, was killed.

On land, Japanese armies landed in Korea and on the Liaodong Peninsula, drove back Russian forces, and isolated Port Arthur. Russian commander Kuropatkin, who had been appointed commander of land forces, preferred to avoid decisive battle until the railway had brought reinforcements, and he fought a series of costly engagements, at Liaoyang (August–September 1904) and the Shaho (October 1904), in which the Russians withdrew in good order but lost ground and the initiative. Port Arthur became the focus of the Japanese campaign. General Nogi's Third Army mounted repeated assaults on the fortress's defenses, with extremely high casualties, running into tens of thousands on the Japanese side over the course of the siege. The turning point came in December, when the Japanese captured 203 Metre Hill, which allowed them to observe and bombard the harbor and destroy much of what remained of the Pacific Squadron at anchor. General Stoessel surrendered the fortress on 2 January 1905 (N.S.), with its garrison of tens of thousands. The decision was controversial in Russia; Stoessel was later court-martialed, and many officers believed the fortress could have held longer.

The fall of Port Arthur left the Pacific Squadron destroyed and Russian naval strategy in disarray. The government had already decided to send the Baltic Fleet, designated the Second Pacific Squadron, under Admiral Rozhestvensky, to the Far East. It departed in October 1904. In the North Sea, nervous Russian crews fired on British fishing trawlers off the Dogger Bank, killing fishermen and provoking a crisis with Britain that was resolved by arbitration. At Tangier the squadron divided. Rozhestvensky's main force, including the newer deep-draft battleships, went around the Cape of Good Hope, while a detachment under Rear Admiral Fölkersam passed through the Suez Canal. The two forces later reunited near Madagascar. Depending on supplies from German coaling ships and on the tolerance of French colonial ports, the main body covered, in some seven months, roughly eighteen thousand nautical miles. The journey was a remarkable feat of seamanship and endurance, but it left crews exhausted and ships worn, and the destruction of Port Arthur's squadron removed its original purpose. The Russian leadership nevertheless decided that the fleet must proceed to Vladivostok.

On 27–28 May 1905 (N.S.; 14–15 May O.S.), Rozhestvensky's squadron entered the Tsushima Strait between Korea and Japan, where Togo's fleet awaited it. In an engagement lasting essentially a day and a night, the Japanese, with superior speed, gunnery, coordination, and training, sank or captured most of the Russian ships. The Russian fleet lost the majority of its battleships and cruisers; thousands of sailors were killed and thousands more taken prisoner, while Japanese losses were comparatively very small, and only a handful of Russian vessels reached Vladivostok. Tsushima was more than another defeat. It destroyed the major fleet sent from Europe, ending any chance of reversing the naval balance, and it was dramatic and public, a result that reached the Russian press and society as a shock. For the Russian navy, the loss was a catastrophe; for many Russian observers it symbolized the failure of the regime and its military administration. For Japan and the wider world, it confirmed that Japan was a first-rank naval power.

Section 46.4

Military Defeat

The central question of this section is why Russia lost. The simplest answers, that Russia was backward and Japan modern, or that an Asian state could not defeat a European one, do not survive scrutiny. Russia in 1904 had a large army, a substantial arms industry, a modern navy that included new battleships, and a rail network that was one of the longest in the world. The empire's total resources far exceeded Japan's. The problem was that Russia could not bring those resources to bear in the theater of war. Several linked factors explain the outcome.

Logistics and distance. The war was fought thousands of kilometers from European Russia's population and industrial centers. The Trans-Siberian Railway was single-track for much of its length, with passing sidings that limited the number of trains per day. Its capacity, initially a few trains a day, was increased to a dozen or more during the war, but supplies and reinforcements still moved slowly. Lake Baikal, where the line was incomplete when the war began, caused further delay until the Circum-Baikal line was opened. The Russian army in Manchuria grew steadily and eventually became numerically larger than Japan's, but it did so too slowly to exploit that advantage when it mattered.

Command and coordination. The Russian command was divided. The Viceroy, Alekseev, the army commander, Kuropatkin, and the naval commanders were not always in harmony, and decisions were often delayed or hedged. Kuropatkin's cautious strategy, reasonable in logistical terms, left Russian forces consistently reacting, and it exposed him to criticism from superiors and subordinates. Many officers were brave and competent, but the leadership as a whole suffered from rivalries, from promotion systems that favored connection, and from a lack of coordinated planning.

Japanese advantages. Japan was close to the theater, so it could move troops and supplies by sea, and it had planned for the war carefully. Its army and navy possessed experienced commanders, high levels of training and cohesion, and a clear strategic goal, which was to destroy Russian naval power and gain control of Korea and southern Manchuria before Russian reinforcements arrived. Its alliance with Britain secured finance and neutrality, and its government mobilized a patriotic population for sacrifice. Japanese tactics, however, were costly: the assaults on Port Arthur and the battle of Mukden produced enormous casualties, and by 1905 Japan's manpower and finances were also strained.

Mukden and the end of the land war. The great land battle of Mukden (February–March 1905) was one of the largest land battles fought up to that time, involving roughly half a million or more troops. After days of heavy fighting, Kuropatkin ordered a retreat. Russian forces escaped destruction, but the result was a decisive Japanese victory, and Kuropatkin was replaced by General Linevich. Russia still had a large army in Manchuria, and some officers argued that a prolonged war might wear Japan down. The government, however, faced not just the defeat at Mukden and the destruction of the fleet at Tsushima, but also the unrest at home that is the subject of the following sections.

Treaty of Portsmouth. Both belligerents had reasons to end the war. Japan's finances and manpower were stretched, and Russia faced revolution. President Theodore Roosevelt offered mediation, and negotiators met in Portsmouth, New Hampshire, in August 1905, with Witte heading the Russian delegation and Komura Jutaro the Japanese. Witte, who recognized that Russia could not afford to continue and that the regime needed peace, nevertheless resisted the largest Japanese demands. The Treaty of Portsmouth, signed on 5 September 1905 (N.S.; 23 August O.S.), recognized Japan's paramount interests in Korea, transferred Russia's lease of Port Arthur and the Liaodong territory and the southern branch of the Chinese Eastern Railway to Japan, ceded the southern half of Sakhalin, and provided for the evacuation of Manchuria by both powers, though not for a large indemnity. The absence of an indemnity disappointed many Japanese and provoked riots in Tokyo. For Russia, Witte's negotiating success mitigated the humiliation, but it could not reverse the diplomatic and political consequences of defeat.

Defeat did not mechanically cause revolution. Several empires have lost wars and survived intact, and the Russian Empire survived this one. But defeat undermined the prestige of a monarchy that justified autocratic authority partly through its capacity to provide order, security, and great-power standing. The war also imposed fiscal burdens, took reservists from villages and workshops, and encouraged open criticism of the bureaucracy and the military command. Wartime conditions did not create the grievances of workers, peasants, or national minorities, but they changed the political context in which those grievances were expressed.

Section 46.5

Bloody Sunday

The story of Bloody Sunday begins not with revolutionaries but with an attempt to organize workers within the framework of the existing order. In the early years of the century, police officials such as Sergei Zubatov experimented with legal workers' organizations intended to address economic grievances and divert workers from revolutionary parties. Zubatov's experiments, which began in Moscow, were curtailed by 1903, but a related initiative developed in St. Petersburg around Father Georgii Gapon, a priest with a background in charitable and educational work. His Assembly of Russian Factory and Mill Workers, founded in 1904 with the knowledge and tolerance of the police authorities, offered workers tea rooms, lectures, mutual aid, and a place to discuss their concerns, and it grew to thousands of members in the capital's districts. Gapon's position was paradoxical. He was a loyalist who invoked the tsar and the church, yet his organization became a vehicle for expressing worker grievances that authorities could not easily control. Historians have debated whether Gapon is best seen as a police protégé, a sincere religious reformer, or an opportunist, and the surviving evidence supports a more complex picture than any single label.

In the winter of 1904–1905 conditions in St. Petersburg were tense. Wartime inflation and shortages, long hours, crowded housing, and the dismissal of workers combined with the sense of a government that was failing in the war. After Plehve's assassination in July 1904, the new Interior Minister, Prince Sviatopolk-Mirsky, signaled a conciliatory approach, and public meetings, the "banquet campaign" of liberal professionals, and a zemstvo congress in November 1904 had pushed political demands into the open. In late December, the dismissal of several members of Gapon's assembly at the Putilov Works, one of the city's largest factories, led to a strike that began in early January and spread rapidly to other factories until, by 8 January (O.S.), most large industrial enterprises in the capital had stopped work. The assembly, now committed to the strike, framed a petition to the tsar.

The petition deserves to be taken seriously as a political document. It was drafted by Gapon with workers' participation, revised in meetings, and it combined several kinds of demands. Economic requests included a shorter working day, better wages, and improved conditions. It asked for civil liberties such as freedom of speech, press, assembly, and conscience, for equality before the law, for amnesty for political prisoners, and for popular representation, which it presented as a constituent assembly elected by universal suffrage; as usually printed, it also touched on the war and its burdens. It addressed Nicholas II directly, using the language of a petitioner approaching a ruler whom the workers believed could correct the abuses of his officials. The marchers were thus not setting out to overthrow the monarchy. Their act expressed a form of popular monarchism: the belief that the tsar, once informed, would protect his people against exploitation. That is why Bloody Sunday is best understood as a crisis of monarchical legitimacy.

The authorities, informed of the plan, decided to prevent the procession from reaching the palace. Interior Minister Mirsky and the commander of the St. Petersburg military district, Grand Duke Vladimir, deployed troops at bridges, squares, and approaches. The government did not negotiate with Gapon; Gapon himself, in turn, seems to have believed until the end that the soldiers would not fire or that the tsar would receive the petition. On the morning of 9 January (O.S.), columns of workers, some accompanied by women and children and carrying icons and flags, advanced from several districts. At several points, including the Narva Gate, the Troitsky Bridge, the Palace Square, and Vasilievsky Island, soldiers fired on or charged the crowds. Gapon, who was in the Narva column, survived and was carried away; he soon issued a proclamation denouncing the tsar and calling on supporters to resist and to arm. He later fled abroad, returned, and was killed in 1906 by revolutionaries who suspected him of collaboration with the police. These facts, again, support neither a simple heroic nor a simple treacherous reading of his role.

Nicholas II was not in the Winter Palace; he was at Tsarskoe Selo, outside the capital, and he did not personally order troops to fire. His diary entry for the day expressed distress, and later he received a worker delegation chosen by the authorities. But the government acted in his name, in a political system in which ultimate responsibility rested with the emperor, and the regime's decision to meet the petition with force was a policy choice by his officials, which the tsar accepted. It is therefore inaccurate to say that the tsar personally ordered the shooting, and equally inaccurate to suggest that the monarchy bore no responsibility.

Casualty figures vary. Official government statements gave a number of dead in the range of about a hundred, while opposition publications, foreign correspondents, and some later estimates gave several hundred or more, with an even larger number of wounded. The disagreement reflects the confusion of the day, the interests of the sources, and the absence of any complete count, and students should treat any single number as an estimate. What is not in dispute is that unarmed petitioners were shot in significant numbers and that the effect on public opinion was profound.

The immediate effect was a wave of strikes across the empire. Many workers who had approached the tsar as petitioners no longer believed that the sovereign would correct abuses. In the weeks that followed, the government took contradictory steps. The Grand Duke Sergei, Moscow's governor-general and the tsar's uncle, was assassinated in February by a Socialist Revolutionary. A commission, the Shidlovsky Commission, was set up to investigate worker grievances but was dissolved after workers' representatives demanded political guarantees. In February the tsar announced his intention to consider the proposals of "trusted persons" for a consultative assembly, and a rescript to Interior Minister Bulygin outlined such a body. These measures showed the regime's willingness to concede something but also its determination to retain full authority.

Section 46.6

Strikes and Workers' Soviets

After Bloody Sunday, strikes spread through the empire with remarkable speed. In January 1905 alone, hundreds of thousands of workers struck in the Russian Empire, a number larger than in any previous year, and strikes continued in waves through the year. Many were at first economic, concerned with wages, hours, and working conditions, but economic and political demands were often mixed, and workers who struck over pay also called for civil rights, an end to the war, or a constituent assembly. Contemporary and later observers distinguish "economic" from "political" strikes, but in practice the line was porous, in part because in a system that criminalized strikes and independent unions, any collective action had political implications. Strikes were particularly intense in the Polish industrial centers, the Baltic cities, the Caucasus oil city of Baku, the Urals, and the Moscow region. In Ivanovo-Voznesensk, a textile center, a long strike in May–July 1905 produced a council of delegates that coordinated the workers' demands and has sometimes been described as an early soviet.

Workers also organized unions, mutual aid societies, and cultural and educational clubs. Professionals formed associations, among them lawyers, engineers, teachers, physicians, and writers, and in May 1905 many joined in the Union of Unions, a federation that demanded a constituent assembly. Students and faculty in the universities, aided by a government decree of August 1905 that granted university autonomy, turned lecture halls into forums for political meetings. Women's organizations, such as the Union for Women's Equality, took up suffrage. In this broad movement, liberals, socialists, and ordinary workers did not always share goals. Liberals wanted a constitution and the rule of law, while socialists envisioned deeper social transformation, and workers pursued both immediate economic gains and political rights.

The strike movement reached its climax in October. It began in September with printers in Moscow, who struck over pay, and spread to the railways in early October (O.S.) when employees on the Moscow–Kazan line walked out. Railway workers, who had an empire-wide network, communicated by telegraph, and were vital to every part of economic and administrative life, were extremely effective. Within days, rail traffic across much of the empire stopped; telegraph and postal workers, factory workers, shop employees, lawyers, teachers, and others joined in. Cities went dark, trams halted, and newspapers and administration were disrupted. The strike's strength came from its breadth and from the fact that it was supported by a wide coalition, not only by revolutionaries. The government had troops but could not simply order the railways back to work, because it needed the same railways to move them.

Historical Vocabulary
Soviet (sovet)

Definition: The Russian word sovet means "council." In 1905 it described a council of delegates elected by workers in factories and workshops to coordinate strikes and represent workers' interests.

Historical context: The first soviets arose in the strike movement of 1905, most notably in Ivanovo-Voznesensk in May and in St. Petersburg in October. Soviets were also formed in Moscow and other cities. They were not created by a single party; Mensheviks, Bolsheviks, Socialist Revolutionaries, and non-party workers took part in them with differing views.

Why it matters: A soviet in 1905 was not a Bolshevik government and did not have the later institutional meaning of the Soviet state. Students should not read the Soviet Union of 1922 or the soviets of 1917 (Chapters 49–51) backward into the autumn of 1905. The 1905 soviets are important because they showed that workers could create representative institutions that coordinated action across factories, and the memory of this experience shaped later movements.

The St. Petersburg Soviet of Workers' Deputies was founded on 13 October (O.S.) at the initiative of Mensheviks and other socialists, building on strike committees at the capital's factories. It elected delegates in proportion to factory size, had representatives from socialist parties, and met first in the Technological Institute. Its first chairman was Georgii Khrustalev-Nosar, a lawyer who was not a party leader; after his arrest in November, Leon Trotsky, who had returned from abroad and who had worked in the Soviet's Menshevik-leaning leadership and its newspaper Izvestiia, became one of its most prominent figures, though not its sole director. The Soviet organized strikes, published newspapers, demanded the eight-hour day, and supported peasants and soldiers, and it exercised something like a rival authority in the capital. Moscow's Soviet, formed in late November, was somewhat more influenced by the Bolsheviks, who in 1905 were wary and were more often suspicious of non-party bodies than Lenin's later writings suggest. The relations among soviets, trade unions, and party organizations were fluid.

What was a soviet? It may be fairly described as a strike committee, as an embryonic organ of revolutionary government, as a democratic institution of workers' self-representation, or as several of these at different moments. The Petersburg Soviet in October coordinated a strike and administered some functions in the strike's aftermath, such as protecting the city's press freedom. In November it called a general strike in response to martial law in Poland and to the mutiny at Kronstadt, which was less successful than the October strike, and it suffered from competing aims. Historians who stress its democratic character point to its elected delegates and open debates; those who stress its role as a rival power emphasize its claims to speak for the working class and its action in defiance of authority. Neither emphasis captures it fully. The question of what the Soviet was cannot be answered from the point of view of 1917.

Everyday Life and Social History

A St. Petersburg Worker Household in 1905

An industrial worker's household in the Narva or Vyborg districts typically occupied a single room or even part of a room in a crowded tenement, shared by a family and often boarders. Many adults were migrants from villages who maintained ties to the countryside, sent money home, and sometimes returned for the harvest. Workdays of ten to eleven hours were normal; wages differed widely by skill, with metalworkers earning more than textile or unskilled workers, and women and children, who were widely employed in textile and other mills, earning less. Food, particularly bread, cabbage, and tea, took most of a household's income, and wartime price rises made budgets tighter in 1904–1905.

When strikes began, they disrupted routines of work and income. Strikers lost wages but gained time for meetings in factory yards, in taverns, and in the premises of Gapon's assembly. After October, when censorship was relaxed in practice, newspapers, pamphlets, and speeches circulated more freely. Literacy varied sharply by occupation, age, and sex, but it was comparatively high among many St. Petersburg workers, especially skilled male workers. Newspapers and pamphlets were nevertheless also read aloud in meetings and shared among workers with different levels of education. Women took part in demonstrations and strikes, but female workers were underrepresented in unions and soviets. Many workers were frightened by arrests, unemployment, and violence; others were hopeful. Evidence about these experiences comes from police reports, labor surveys, memoirs, and press accounts, which describe men more than women and the organized more than the unorganized, so historians must be cautious about generalizing.

Section 46.7

Peasant and Military Unrest

Because the Russian Empire was overwhelmingly agrarian, any account of 1905 confined to cities is incomplete. Roughly four-fifths of the population were peasants, and the countryside experienced rising pressure from population growth, small landholdings, the burden of redemption payments, rents for land, and fluctuations in harvests, as Chapters 42 and 44 explained. Peasant disturbances increased from the spring of 1905 and spread widely in the autumn, with a further peak in 1906. They were concentrated in the central black-earth provinces such as Saratov, Tambov, Kursk, and Chernigov, where land pressure was acute, in the Volga region, in parts of Ukraine, in the Baltic provinces, and in Georgia, though conditions varied by region. Peasants cut timber in landlords' forests, refused to pay rents or redemption dues, seized grain and pasture, and, in a minority of cases, burned manor houses and attacked estate owners and managers. The government and landowners recorded these events as "disorders"; for participants they were often attempts to claim land and resources they regarded as theirs.

Peasant objectives were often local, specific, and framed in terms of customary justice, not in terms of party programs. Many peasants looked to the tsar as a protector against landlords and officials, and some were disappointed by his failure to act. The notion that "the land belongs to those who work it" resonated widely, but it was not the same as socialism, and peasants held diverse views about private property, village commune, and the state. The Socialist Revolutionaries sought to organize rural support, and in 1905 peasants formed the All-Russian Peasant Union, which met in Moscow in August and November and demanded land reform and political rights. Its leadership included intellectuals and rural professionals as well as peasants. The government responded with a combination of repression and concession: in November 1905 it announced the reduction and eventual abolition of redemption payments, an acknowledgment of rural grievances.

The armed forces were the regime's decisive instrument. Most soldiers did not mutiny, which is central to explaining the government's survival. Nevertheless, there were notable signs of unrest. Reservists mobilized for the war were often resentful, and troops were used in repeated domestic policing duties that strained discipline. Mutinies and disturbances occurred in garrisons and ships in several places, including Kronstadt, Sevastopol, Vladivostok, and Kiev, many of them linked to grievances about food, conditions, and officers as well as to political demands. In the autumn and in late 1905 a few units joined the revolutionaries, but the majority of the army did not.

The most famous mutiny was on the battleship Prince Potemkin of Tauride of the Black Sea Fleet in June 1905. On 14 June (O.S.; 27 June N.S.), while the ship was at sea on exercises, sailors protested the quality of meat that they believed to be maggot-ridden, and the dispute escalated into a confrontation in which officers were killed or thrown overboard and a sailor leader, Grigorii Vakulenchuk, was shot. The crew, partly influenced by Social Democratic sailors, took control, raised a red flag, and sailed to Odesa, which was then in the grip of a general strike and violent clashes between demonstrators and the authorities. The mutineers failed to win support from the rest of the fleet or to ignite a wider naval revolt. After several days the ship sailed to Constanța in Romania, where the crew surrendered; many later escaped, were caught, or lived in exile. The episode demonstrates how particular grievances, such as food and discipline, could fuse with political ideas. It also shows its limits: it was isolated, short, and ended without success.

The Potemkin acquired a symbolic importance far beyond its actual military significance, partly through Sergei Eisenstein's 1925 film, a work of Soviet revolutionary art that dramatized the mutiny and invented a massacre on the Odesa steps. Students should treat the film as evidence for how the revolution was remembered in the 1920s, and not as a documentary record. Real violence did occur in Odesa during the unrest, including fires in the port and clashes with troops, but the film's famous scene is a cinematic construction.

The empire's national movements also formed part of the crisis, extending the themes of Chapter 45. In the Kingdom of Poland, mass strikes began in January 1905 and continued into the year, with industrial Lodz the scene of an armed uprising in June, and with competition among the Polish Socialist Party, the National Democrats, and others. In the Baltic provinces, Latvian and Estonian peasants and workers, many with grievances against German landlords as well as Russian officials, attacked manors and demanded autonomy and civil rights. In Finland, a general strike in November 1905 forced the government to restore the autonomy and constitutional order it had curtailed earlier. In the Caucasus, social revolution, particularly in Georgian Guria, was accompanied by violence between Armenians and Azerbaijanis (then called "Tatars" in Russian administrative usage) in Baku and elsewhere. Jewish political life included the Bund and Zionist and liberal organizations, and many Jews were targets of pogroms. Muslim activists convened the first All-Russian Muslim Congress at Nizhny Novgorod in August, and demanded equal rights and religious and cultural freedoms. Ukrainian activists took advantage of looser press restrictions after 1905. These movements pursued different goals, from autonomy to full independence to civil equality, and not every upheaval of 1905 in the borderlands can be reduced to nationalism.

Regional Context

Borderlands in the Revolution of 1905

Seen from Warsaw, Riga, Helsinki, Tbilisi, or Baku, the revolution of 1905 did not always follow the chronology or priorities of Petersburg. In Poland the strike movement was intertwined with a struggle over language, schooling, and autonomy; martial law was proclaimed in parts of the Kingdom in the fall. In the Baltic provinces, the countryside was in revolt against German landowners, and the state's response included punitive expeditions in the winter of 1905–1906 that were notably violent. In Finland, which had a separate constitutional tradition, the general strike produced a restoration of constitutional rule and, later, a unicameral parliament with a broad suffrage. In the Caucasus, Georgian Social Democrats, many of them Mensheviks, led peasant and worker mobilization, while violence between Armenian and Azerbaijani communities in Baku and elsewhere took a form not easily reduced to either social revolution or state policy. In Siberia and the Far East, soldiers returning from Manchuria on the Trans-Siberian Railway joined railway workers in strikes and mutinies in Chita and elsewhere before punitive columns under General Meller-Zakomelsky and General Rennenkampf restored order along the line.

The variety of these experiences shows why historians describe 1905 as empire-wide and socially heterogeneous. The same state confronted workers, peasants, soldiers, national movements, and liberal reformers at once, but their grievances did not converge on a single program, and the regime was able in many places to respond to each in isolation.

Section 46.8

The October Manifesto

By the first half of October 1905 (O.S.), the general strike had paralyzed communication across much of the empire. Nicholas II faced a choice. Some conservative advisers urged a military dictatorship, with an authoritative general, such as the Grand Duke Nikolai Nikolaevich, given power to restore order. Others, including Witte, who had returned from Portsmouth as a statesman with prestige, argued that repression alone would be insufficient and potentially disastrous, given the extent of the strike, the unreliability of some troops, and the exhaustion of the army after the war. In a memorandum of 9 October, Witte advised that the government must either appoint a dictator or concede a constitutional program, and that he preferred the latter. Several sources, including Witte's memoirs, suggest that Nicholas considered dictatorship and was dissuaded by the lack of a willing commander and by the risk that bloodshed would worsen the situation, though the details of this account rest on partisan retrospective testimony.

On 17 October (O.S.; 30 October N.S.), Nicholas signed the Manifesto "On the Improvement of Public Order," drafted by Witte and others. It declared that the government would grant the population "unshakable foundations of civil liberty," on the basis of real inviolability of the person and freedom of conscience, speech, assembly, and association (a paraphrase of the main promises, not the verbatim text). It promised to extend the franchise for the planned Duma to classes of the population previously excluded, and it established as an unalterable principle that no law could come into force without the approval of the State Duma and that elected representatives would be able to supervise the legality of officials' actions. Witte became chairman of a reformed Council of Ministers, a step toward a unified cabinet government.

What the manifesto did not do is as important as what it did. It did not create a parliament with sovereign powers, did not establish ministerial responsibility to the legislature, did not abolish the emperor's authority, and did not specify the details of the franchise or the powers of the Duma. Nicholas himself understood it as a concession wrung from him under duress, and he continued to regard autocratic authority as a sacred inheritance. Yet contemporaries regarded the manifesto as an event of great significance, because the principle of legislative approval and the guarantee of civil liberties were in tension with unlimited autocracy, and because such promises, once made, created expectations and a framework for political organization.

The reaction in October and November was dramatic. In cities, crowds celebrated in the streets, and liberals saw the manifesto as a turning point. At the same time, revolutionaries, including the Petersburg Soviet, denounced the manifesto as insufficient or deceptive and continued the strike. Meanwhile, on the right, supporters of the autocracy saw the manifesto as a betrayal, and in many cities nationalist and monarchist crowds attacked students, leftists, and above all Jews in violent demonstrations. The opposition thus split. Moderates, who formed or joined the Union of October 17 (Octobrists) on the center-right and the Constitutional Democratic Party (Kadets) on the liberal left, prepared for the Duma elections; the Kadets, formed in October, wanted a more fully parliamentary system. Socialists and radical liberals disagreed with one another about participation, boycotts, and armed struggle. This division of the opposition is central to explaining why the manifesto helped the regime recover.

Historical Debate

What Caused the Revolution of 1905?

The question: Was the Revolution of 1905 primarily caused by the war, by structural social and economic tensions, by political exclusion, or by the interaction of all three?

Interpretation A — War as catalyst: Some historians emphasize the contingency of the revolution. They argue that the unpopular and humiliating war exposed official incompetence, increased economic hardship, and destroyed the government's prestige, and that without defeat the existing tensions might not have produced a crisis on this scale. Evidence includes the timing of the revolution, the political effect of Port Arthur's fall and Tsushima, and the public discussion of the war's failures in the press and in liberal gatherings.
Interpretation B — Structural tensions: Other historians, including many in the Soviet tradition, which described 1905 as a "bourgeois-democratic" revolution and later as a "dress rehearsal" for 1917, emphasized deeper social and economic contradictions: rapid industrialization, a working class concentrated in large factories, peasant land hunger, and an unreformed political system. They point to the strike wave and rural disorders that predated the war and to the persistence of grievances after it. Historians critical of this view warn that a structural account may make the revolution seem inevitable and may under-explain why it came in 1905 rather than earlier.
Interpretation C — Political exclusion and interaction: A third approach, used by many later scholars of society and politics, stresses that the regime's refusal to allow legal channels for political participation or labor organization turned grievances into confrontation, and that the interaction of war, social tension, and political exclusion explains the scale and character of the crisis. This approach also pays attention to the diversity of participants and to the regional and national dimensions.

Evidence: Historians use strike statistics, police and factory-inspector reports, records of peasant disturbances, newspapers, memoirs, party documents, and the records of the government's deliberations.

Points of agreement: Most scholars agree that no single cause suffices, that the war was an important catalyst, that Bloody Sunday sharply increased political mobilization, and that the revolution was socially and regionally varied.

Points of disagreement: Scholars differ about the relative weight of the war and of long-term structures, the degree to which the revolution was a unified movement, the importance of liberal and socialist leadership, and how far the outcome was the result of the regime's skill or of opposition divisions.

Why it matters: The answer shapes how students understand the relationship between the 1905 and 1917 revolutions and whether crises of authority are best explained by structures or by events.

Multiple Perspectives

Six Experiences of 1905

An industrial worker in Petersburg: For many workers, the revolution began as a protest against dismissals and long hours and became a movement for civil rights. Evidence from petitions, strike records, and police reports shows workers using the language of petition and later of class and political rights. Their risks included dismissal, arrest, and violence.
A peasant in a black-earth village: Peasants in land-short regions saw the weakened state as an opportunity to take timber, pasture, and rents that they regarded as rightfully theirs. Their actions often followed local customs and village assemblies, and many wanted the tsar to side with them, not to abolish the monarchy. Punitive expeditions and arrests followed in many districts.
A liberal zemstvo activist: Liberal professionals and gentry reformers wanted a constitution, civil rights, and legal order, and they feared both autocratic obstinacy and revolutionary violence, especially against property. The manifesto gave them hope that reform could now proceed legally.
A socialist organizer: Socialists, whether Menshevik, Bolshevik, or Socialist Revolutionary, saw the crisis as an opportunity to widen the revolution, but they disagreed over strategy, over the role of the liberals, and over whether to participate in the Duma. Their influence among workers was real but incomplete.
A sailor or soldier: Conscripts and sailors experienced harsh discipline, poor food, and the strains of war. Some mutinied; most did not. For many, the decisive questions were loyalty to officers and unit, fear of punishment, and uncertainty about the aims of civilian movements.
A non-Russian political activist: In Poland, Finland, the Baltic provinces, the Caucasus, and among Jews and Muslims, activists sought autonomy, language rights, equality, or a reduction in imperial control, and they also faced local conflicts, such as peasants against landlords or communities against one another. Jews were also the victims of pogroms.

These perspectives show why the revolution of 1905 was neither a single movement nor a single narrative. Power and coercion were unequally distributed, and the regime's resources far exceeded those of any one group of its opponents.

Section 46.9

Revolution, Repression, and Constitutional Change

The October Manifesto did not end the revolution. In the weeks that followed, strikes, peasant disorders, mutinies, and violence continued, and both revolutionaries and loyalists acted outside the law. In November there were mutinies in Kronstadt and Sevastopol; the government declared martial law in Poland and in parts of the Baltic provinces, while in Finland the November strike forced concessions. Peasant disturbances peaked in the autumn. The government, however, now began to recover its initiative. Witte, as chairman of the Council of Ministers, was a reformer but also an executor of repression, and the return of army units from Manchuria, which was gradually completed after the peace, restored the regime's reserves. The new Interior Minister, Pyotr Durnovo, was a hard-liner who organized the suppression of unrest.

Historical Vocabulary
Black Hundreds

Definition: A term for a range of monarchist, Orthodox nationalist, and often violently antisemitic groups and crowds that emerged in 1905, the best known of which was the Union of the Russian People, founded in November 1905. The name was used by their opponents and sometimes by sympathizers; it was not a single organization.

Historical context: These groups included landowners, clergy, shopkeepers, workers, officials, and others who opposed the revolution and the October Manifesto. Some enjoyed local official toleration or sympathy.

Why it matters: They show that popular politics in 1905 included conservative and reactionary mobilization as well as revolutionary and liberal movements, and that antisemitic violence was a major part of the revolution's history.

On 3 December (O.S.) the government arrested the Petersburg Soviet's executive committee, including Trotsky, who would later be tried. The Soviet had lacked effective means to resist and its call for a financial boycott failed to have any major effect. In Moscow, the Soviet and the local Bolshevik and other socialist organizations called a general strike in early December (O.S.) that became an armed uprising, with barricades erected in working-class districts such as Presnia. The fighting lasted about a week. The authorities, whose local garrison was unreliable, brought in loyal guards regiments from St. Petersburg, notably the Semenovsky Guards, and used artillery against the insurgents. The uprising was defeated with heavy casualties and destruction. Its suppression had two meanings. It showed that armed uprising by a minority of workers was insufficient against a government that retained effective regiments, and it demonstrated that revolutionaries who relied on the cities alone would be vulnerable.

Punitive expeditions followed. Along the Trans-Siberian Railway, columns commanded by Meller-Zakomelsky and Rennenkampf restored order, with executions and floggings. In the Baltic provinces, troops and Cossacks, sometimes accompanied by German landowners' militias, burned villages and executed participants. In the Caucasus, forces were used against revolutionary Georgian districts. These expeditions combined military operations with summary justice, and they did much to reestablish control in the winter of 1905–1906. Later, field courts-martial, introduced in 1906, would extend the use of rapid military trials, a policy associated with Chapter 47's Stolypin period. Thousands were killed or executed and many more imprisoned or exiled, though figures differ among sources and estimating totals is difficult.

Violence was also directed against Jews. Pogroms, as Chapter 43 explained, had occurred in the empire since 1881, and the Kishinev pogrom of 1903 had drawn international attention. In the wave that followed the October Manifesto, hundreds of communities across the empire, including Odesa, Kyiv, Kishinev, and many smaller towns, experienced anti-Jewish violence, some of it organized by local monarchist groups and sometimes with the passivity or complicity of officials and police, though the degree of central involvement is debated. Hundreds of Jews were killed, many more injured, and property was destroyed; the Odesa pogrom of October 1905 was among the deadliest. The pogroms were part of a broader conservative reaction that framed Jews as revolutionaries, and they also prompted Jewish self-defense groups. Some historians emphasize the roles of local actors and economic and social tensions, others the role of the state's discriminatory policies, and most agree that both mattered.

The government also moved to give institutional form to the promised representation. In August 1905 it had issued the Bulygin law establishing a consultative Duma with a narrow franchise, which was overtaken by the events of October. In December 1905 a new electoral law extended the franchise by creating curiae of landowners, townspeople, peasants, and workers, with indirect voting and unequal weights, and the government prepared for elections. Radical parties debated whether to boycott. The establishment of a Duma, with the principle that it would approve laws, was a real constitutional change, but its powers, and the Fundamental Laws that defined them, were issued in April 1906, shortly before the Duma met. The Fundamental Laws, the Duma elections, and the politics of the First and Second Dumas are the subject of Chapter 47. This section ends at the threshold of that new framework.

A crucial interpretive question is whether 1905 was a failed revolution, an incomplete revolution, or a successful constitutional rupture followed by autocratic recovery. There are plausible arguments for each, depending on what one means by revolutionary success. If the test is the overthrow of the monarchy and the transformation of social relations, the revolution failed. If it is the end of unrestricted autocracy, the creation of a legislature, civil liberties, legal political parties, and public political life, then it forced changes that were not reversed. If it is the democratic revolution that many participants hoped for, it was incomplete, because the regime retained great powers and the opposition was divided. The monarchy survived, but not unchanged: it did not return completely to the political order of 1904. That ambiguity is the bridge to Chapter 47.

Cause and Effect

The Revolutionary Crisis of 1905 and the Regime's Recovery

Long-Term Conditions

Autocratic political exclusion; rapid industrialization and concentrated urban labor (Chapter 44); rural land pressure and redemption burdens (Chapters 42, 44); national and religious grievances under imperial policy (Chapters 43, 45); organized liberal and socialist opposition.

Intermediate Developments

Far Eastern expansion and the commitments it created; military defeats; the assassination of Plehve and a brief loosening of controls; the banquet campaign and zemstvo petitions; the Gapon assembly's growth.

Short-Term Triggers

The Putilov dismissals and the January strike; Bloody Sunday; the fall of Port Arthur and Tsushima; the October railway and general strikes.

Event or Transformation

An empire-wide crisis of authority, with strikes, soviets, peasant and military unrest, and national mobilization, forcing the October Manifesto.

Immediate Consequences

Civil liberties and the promise of a legislature; division of the opposition; a wave of loyalist violence and pogroms; arrests of soviet leaders, the Moscow uprising and punitive expeditions.

Long-Term Consequences

Legal parties and public politics; a constitutional order whose character is the subject of Chapter 47; political experience and memory that shaped 1917, though not determining it; unresolved agrarian and national questions.

Historical complexity: No single chain explains the outcome. War weakened prestige and finances; industrial grievances produced strikes; Bloody Sunday created a legitimacy crisis; land pressure fed rural unrest; national grievances mobilized the borderlands; the general strike produced political paralysis. The regime recovered because the opposition was divided, because the army remained largely usable, and because concessions split moderates from radicals.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Russia in January 1904 and in Spring 1906

What Changed

  • The principle that laws required the approval of an elected legislature, and a promise of civil liberties, replaced the formal doctrine of undivided autocratic legislation.
  • Legal political parties, trade unions, and a much freer press, though subject to continuing restrictions and prosecutions, appeared.
  • Russia had lost territory and position in East Asia, and the empire's international standing, finances, and military confidence were altered.
  • Peasant redemption payments were being reduced and abolished, and the government began to reconsider rural policy.

What Remained

  • The monarchy, the emperor's authority over the ministers and the army, the bureaucracy, and the police remained largely intact.
  • Land hunger, poor working conditions, national and religious discrimination, and the weight of rural poverty persisted.
  • The regime retained the capacity for large-scale repression.

Pace and experience: Change came rapidly in autumn 1905 but its consequences were uncertain; those who experienced it most directly were urban workers, liberals, and activists, while many villagers experienced continuity in their material conditions even as they were drawn into politics. The pattern matters because it shows that a change of political form need not mean a transformation of society.

Russia and the World

An Age of Constitutional Upheaval

The Russo-Japanese War and the Revolution of 1905 were connected to larger international processes. Japan's victory, the first by an Asian power over a European great power in the industrial era, was widely noticed in Asia and the Middle East, where reformers and nationalists took it as evidence that modern institutions, including constitutions, could strengthen a state. Japan's own constitutional monarchy, established in 1889, became a model discussed by reformers elsewhere, though the lessons drawn were varied. The Russian Revolution, in turn, influenced and was compared with the Iranian Constitutional Revolution that began in 1905–1906, the Young Turk revolution of 1908 in the Ottoman Empire, and the revolution in China in 1911. These movements were not copies of one another, and their causes and outcomes differed, but they belong to a wider era in which long-established monarchies faced constitutional demands.

The war also reshaped the international system. The Treaty of Portsmouth, mediated by the United States, marked America's emergence as a diplomatic actor in East Asia. Russia's weakness in 1905 influenced the diplomacy of Europe, including Germany's testing of the Franco-Russian alliance in the Moroccan crisis, and encouraged Russia to settle disputes with Britain in the following years, as later chapters will show. France remained Russia's banker, and Russian socialists and liberals were connected with European socialist parties and press. Russian policy was not simply imitative; it was a participant in an interconnected world.

Section 46.10

Chapter Review and Historical Thinking Checkpoint

This chapter has argued that the Revolution of 1905 resulted from the interaction of long-term tensions, a failing war, and specific political events, and that it forced the autocracy to change without overthrowing it. The checkpoint below asks students to synthesize the dimensions of that argument before proceeding to the chapter review apparatus.

Causation. War, labor grievances, agrarian pressure, political opposition, and national movements all contributed, but none was sufficient alone. Defeat in the Far East was a catalyst because it weakened the government's prestige and capacity at a moment when many groups had grievances and few legal channels for expressing them. Section 46.1 and 46.2 showed that the war itself resulted from overextension and miscalculation, not from a plan to avert revolution.

Contingency. The crisis became revolutionary in 1905 and not earlier because of specific events: the outcome of the war, the loosening of controls after Plehve's death, the Putilov dispute, and the decision to meet a petition with force. Industrialization had created the capacity for collective action, but events activated it. Students should resist the idea that the revolution was merely waiting to happen.

Social diversity. Workers, peasants, sailors, soldiers, liberals, socialists, and the peoples of the borderlands experienced the crisis differently, and their goals diverged. Their diversity gave the revolution breadth but denied it unity.

State capacity. The regime nearly lost control in October but recovered, because most of the armed forces remained loyal or available, because peace freed the army, because the October Manifesto divided moderates from radicals, and because repression and loyalist violence were applied selectively and sometimes brutally. The regime's survival was not guaranteed, but neither was it accidental.

Evidence and interpretation. The petition shows what participants wanted and how they framed it, but not how many shared those aims; police and military records show official perceptions and decisions but serve the interests of those who wrote them; strike statistics, press reports, and casualty estimates vary by source and purpose; and Witte's memoirs, valuable for October 1905, were written afterward and with self-justification in mind. Historians must therefore corroborate rather than rely on any single record.

Competing interpretations. Scholars weigh the war as catalyst, structural social tensions, and political exclusion differently, and they disagree whether 1905 is best described as a failed revolution, an incomplete revolution, or a constitutional rupture followed by autocratic recovery. These are arguments about criteria and evidence, not merely labels, and each captures part of the outcome.

Broader significance. The crisis created mass politics, legal parties, a freer press, and representative institutions, and it gave workers, peasants, and national movements organizational experience. It did so without making 1917 inevitable, since the monarchy survived twelve more years in changed circumstances.

Change and continuity. Civil liberties, representative institutions, legal parties, and a public political life were new. The monarchy, bureaucracy, army, police, and the autocrat's claims to authority remained. The central checkpoint question is therefore not simply "why did 1905 fail?" but:

Why did the Revolution of 1905 force the Russian autocracy to change but fail to overthrow it?

A strong answer will explain both halves of the outcome: the pressures that compelled concessions (defeat, strikes, loss of legitimacy, paralysis of administration) and the reasons the regime endured (opposition divisions, continuing military loyalty, concessions, repression, and the geographic and social unevenness of the revolt). It will also avoid treating 1905 as a rehearsal for 1917. The experiences, organizations, memories, and unresolved conflicts of 1905 mattered later, but the monarchy survived another twelve years under very different conditions. Chapter 47 takes up the question of what kind of monarchy emerged.

Chapter Summary

This chapter examined how the Russo-Japanese War of 1904–1905 helped convert long-standing social and political tensions into the first great revolutionary crisis of the Russian autocracy. It began with Russia's expansion into northeast Asia, including the Amur and Maritime regions, the Trans-Siberian and Chinese Eastern railways, the lease of Port Arthur, and rivalry with a modernizing Japan. These policies had strategic and commercial logic, but they committed the empire to a position it was not organized to sustain.

The war's origins lay in the failure of negotiations over Manchuria and Korea, shaped by Russian reluctance to withdraw, the Anglo-Japanese Alliance, divided decision-making at court, and Russian miscalculation of Japan's willingness and ability to fight. The notion that the government deliberately sought a short victorious war to prevent revolution is not well supported by the evidence.

The war was marked by the siege and fall of Port Arthur, the destruction of the Pacific Squadron, the costly land battles of Liaoyang and Mukden, and the destruction of the Baltic Fleet at Tsushima. Russian defeat resulted not from simple backwardness but from logistical limits, distance, divided command, and Japanese preparation and proximity. The Treaty of Portsmouth, mediated by Theodore Roosevelt, recognized Japan's position in Korea and transferred Russia's Liaodong leasehold and southern Sakhalin, without an indemnity.

Defeat did not mechanically cause revolution, but it damaged the prestige of a monarchy that claimed authority partly through competence and great-power status. Bloody Sunday transformed a labor petition addressed to the tsar into a crisis of legitimacy. The authorities, acting in the emperor's name, met the procession with force; Nicholas II was not present and did not personally order the shooting, but the monarchy bore political responsibility. Casualty figures vary, and the event destroyed many workers' belief in the tsar as a protector.

Strikes spread through the empire, culminating in the October general strike, in which railway workers and many other groups paralyzed administration. Soviets emerged as councils of delegates coordinating strikes, but they were not yet Bolshevik institutions. Peasants seized and destroyed landlord property in pursuit of local goals, sailors and soldiers mutinied in a minority of cases, and national movements in Poland, the Baltic provinces, Finland, the Caucasus, and among Jews and Muslims mobilized in their own ways. The revolution was empire-wide and socially heterogeneous.

Under the pressure of the general strike, and advised by Witte, Nicholas II issued the October Manifesto, promising civil liberties, a broader electorate, and a legislature whose approval would be required for laws. The manifesto did not create a parliamentary democracy, but it altered the framework of Russian politics. It also divided the opposition, between those who welcomed it and those who rejected it, and it stimulated loyalist counter-mobilization, including the Black Hundreds and a wave of pogroms.

The government recovered after October through the arrest of the Petersburg Soviet, the suppression of the Moscow uprising, punitive expeditions, and the return of troops from the Far East. Electoral preparations and the principle of a Duma marked the transition to a new constitutional framework. Whether 1905 should be called failed, incomplete, or a constitutional rupture followed by autocratic recovery depends on the criteria of success; the monarchy survived but not unchanged.

The chapter also showed that the revolution should be understood in its regional and international contexts. The borderlands experienced it differently from the capitals, Japan's victory resonated across Asia, and the Russian crisis belonged to a wider age of constitutional upheaval. Historians debate the relative weight of war, structure, and political exclusion, and the comparison with 1917 requires caution.

These developments prepare directly for Chapter 47, which examines the Fundamental Laws of 1906, the First and Second Dumas, party politics, Stolypin's program of reform and repression, and the unresolved crisis of autocracy, and for Chapter 48, in which a far larger war brought the monarchy's final crisis. They also provide the background for the revolutions of 1917 (Chapters 49–51), although the chapter has emphasized that later outcomes were contingent.

Mapping and Interpreting the Revolution of 1905 in One Region

This project asks you to investigate how the revolution of 1905 unfolded in one region of the empire and compare it with the capital.

  1. Choose a region. Select a region such as the Baltic provinces, the Kingdom of Poland, the Caucasus, the Volga or black-earth provinces, Odesa and the Black Sea coast, or the Trans-Siberian corridor.
  2. Gather primary sources. Locate at least three primary sources, such as police or administrative reports, newspaper accounts, memoirs, petitions, or proclamations, and identify the author, date, and purpose of each.
  3. Gather secondary sources. Consult at least two scholarly works on the region or on 1905 and note their interpretive approaches.
  4. Map the events. Using a historical atlas and administrative units of the period, prepare a map showing strikes, rural disorders, mutinies, national mobilization, or violence in the region, and note what is uncertain.
  5. Compare. Explain how the region's experience differed from or resembled that of St. Petersburg, and what the differences reveal about the revolution's heterogeneity.
  6. Write. Produce a 5–7 page essay that distinguishes primary from secondary evidence and addresses the question: why did the revolution take the form it did here?

Library resources to use: The MGU History Library's collections on late imperial Russia, historical atlases, published document collections on 1905, regional histories, and periodical archives.

Chapter Reflection

The Revolution of 1905 exposed how a war could convert long-standing social and political tensions into a general crisis of authority. A distant campaign damaged the prestige of a government that relied on competence and great-power standing, while workers, peasants, soldiers and sailors, professionals, and national movements brought their own grievances into the public arena. The monarchy survived because its opponents pursued different goals, much of the armed force remained usable, and concessions divided the opposition. Yet survival required constitutional concessions that permanently changed the political framework of the empire.

The chapter also changed the political landscape that followed. It created legal parties, a freer press, trade unions, and a legislature, along with new forms of mass politics, including strikes, councils, and loyalist mobilization. It left unresolved the agrarian question, the status of national movements, the relationship between the emperor and elected representatives, and the memory of violence. These legacies mattered in 1917, but they did not determine it. The monarchy that survived 1905 operated in a different environment, and its collapse in 1917 depended on the pressures of a far larger war (Chapter 48), on the events of February (Chapter 49), and on the contingent choices examined in Chapters 50 and 51.

Larger themes of the textbook appear in this chapter: the relationship between empire and revolution, the interplay of center and periphery, the capacity of the state to adapt and to repress, and the importance of evidence and interpretation in judging whether an event "failed." Questions remain open, including the relative weight of war and structure, the degree to which the soviets were precursors of later institutions, the extent of official involvement in violence, and whether alternative constitutional paths were possible. Chapter 47 begins from the central question the revolution left behind: whether the Duma monarchy represented genuine constitutional government or a modified form of autocracy.
