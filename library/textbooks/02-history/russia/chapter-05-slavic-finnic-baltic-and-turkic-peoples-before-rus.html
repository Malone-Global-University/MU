Part I — The Lands and Peoples Before Rus'
5

Slavic, Finnic, Baltic, and Turkic Peoples Before Rus'

A History of Russia: From the Eurasian World to the Russian Federation

Chapter Opening

The twelfth-century compiler of the Primary Chronicle, looking back across some three centuries, tried to describe how many different peoples lived across the forests and river valleys of Eastern Europe before any prince ruled from Kyiv or Novgorod. His account named tribe after tribe — Polianians, Drevlianians, Krivichians, Viatichians, Radimichians — each attached to a river, a forest, or a way of life, and each, in his telling, gradually drawn into a single political story centered on Rus'. Read carefully, and cross-checked against archaeology, that same list reveals something the chronicler did not set out to show: a landscape of genuine diversity, only later simplified into a single narrative of unification.

In the centuries between the retreat of Sarmatian dominance from the open Pontic steppe, examined at the close of Chapter 4, and the appearance of a recognizable Rus' polity in the ninth and tenth centuries, examined beginning in Chapter 7, the lands that would eventually fall within the political orbit of Kyiv, Novgorod, and later Moscow were home to no single people, no single language family, and no single political tradition. Slavic-speaking communities, expanding gradually out of a more compact original homeland whose exact location remains debated among historians and linguists, spread across the forest and forest-steppe zones of Eastern Europe during the sixth through ninth centuries CE. At the same time, Finnic and other Uralic-speaking peoples had already inhabited the northern forests, lake country, and upper Volga basin for a very long period, developing their own distinctive economies adapted to a cold, densely wooded environment. Baltic-speaking peoples occupied a broad zone from the Baltic Sea coast deep into what is now Belarus and western Russia, a territory considerably larger than the modern Baltic states that preserve their name. Steppe peoples, meanwhile, continued and transformed the mobile pastoral traditions examined in Chapter 4, forming shifting confederations — the Avars, an Inner Asian confederation of debated linguistic composition, and the Turkic-speaking Bulgars, Khazars, and later Pechenegs — that dominated the open steppe corridor and interacted, sometimes violently and sometimes commercially, with the forest-zone populations to their north.

This chapter's central task is to resist two temptations that have shaped much writing about this period. The first temptation is to treat the ancestors of later Rus' as though they were already, in some essential sense, "Russian," "Ukrainian," or "Belarusian" — categories that did not exist in anything like their modern form during the period this chapter covers and that later national movements have projected backward with varying degrees of historical justification. The second temptation is to imagine the forest and steppe zones of early medieval Eastern Europe as ethnically uniform blocks separated by hard boundaries, when the archaeological and textual evidence instead points to overlapping settlement, extensive intermarriage, shared material culture, and fluid political allegiance among Slavic, Finnic, Baltic, and Turkic communities living, trading, and sometimes fighting within the same river valleys and forest clearings.

The evidence available for this period remains uneven and requires the same critical caution this textbook applied to Bronze Age archaeological cultures in Chapter 3 and to Herodotus's account of the Scythians in Chapter 4. Written sources are scarce, late, and often composed by outsiders — Byzantine officials, Arab and Persian geographers, and, for the Slavic tribes specifically, a Rus' chronicler writing centuries after the events he described, working from oral tradition, earlier written fragments, and his own understanding of how the story of Rus' ought to be told. Archaeology supplies a more direct, though still incomplete and sometimes ambiguous, record of settlement patterns, burial customs, pottery styles, and material exchange, and it is archaeology, more than any surviving text, that allows historians to reconstruct the approximate territorial extent of Slavic, Finnic, Baltic, and Turkic settlement during this period.

This chapter also continues a theme introduced in Chapter 4: the long, historically variable relationship between mobile steppe societies and settled forest-zone populations. Steppe confederations such as the Avars, the Turkic-speaking Bulgars and Khazars, and later the Pechenegs occupied major political and military roles across Eastern and Central Europe from the sixth through tenth centuries, even as new technologies, new religious influences, and new political structures reshaped the specific character of steppe power. Understanding this chapter's peoples on their own terms — rather than merely as background figures awaiting the arrival of Rurik and the formation of Rus', examined in Chapter 7 — is essential to avoiding a teleological account in which medieval Eastern Europe existed only to produce a single later state.

Finally, this chapter addresses directly a matter of considerable modern political sensitivity: the use, and frequent misuse, of early medieval ethnicity and language to support later national claims. Twentieth- and twenty-first-century nationalist movements in Russia, Ukraine, Belarus, the Baltic states, and elsewhere have periodically claimed direct, uninterrupted descent from specific early medieval peoples in order to establish historical priority or territorial entitlement. This chapter explains why professional historians treat such claims with considerable caution, without denying that language, culture, and settlement in this period left real and traceable legacies in later societies.

By the chapter's end, students should be able to describe the approximate territorial distribution of Slavic, Finnic, Baltic, and Turkic peoples in Eastern Europe before the formation of Rus', evaluate the strengths and limitations of the evidence historians use to reconstruct early medieval ethnicity, explain patterns of migration, settlement, and cultural exchange among these peoples, and critically assess modern nationalist uses of early medieval ethnic and linguistic evidence.

Historical Context

Chapter 4 traced the successive dominance of Cimmerian, Scythian, and Sarmatian confederations on the open Pontic-Caspian steppe from roughly the ninth century BCE through the fourth century CE, and closed by noting the gradual eclipse of Sarmatian power amid new migrations, including Gothic and Hunnic incursions. Those incursions, only briefly noted at the close of Chapter 4, form part of a much longer sequence of steppe political change often labeled, somewhat imprecisely, the "Migration Period," during which successive waves of steppe confederations — Huns in the fourth and fifth centuries, the Avars (a confederation of debated linguistic composition) from the sixth through early ninth centuries, various Bulgar groups dispersing from the later seventh century, and Khazars and Pechenegs in later centuries, examined further in Chapter 6 and in Section 5.7 of this chapter — moved across, or established dominance over, the Pontic-Caspian steppe corridor.

While these steppe confederations rose and fell in the south, a largely separate, though not unconnected, historical process was underway to the north and west: the gradual territorial expansion of Slavic-speaking agricultural communities out of a more geographically compact zone of origin, generally placed by most current scholarship somewhere in the region encompassing parts of present-day Poland, Belarus, and northwestern Ukraine, though the precise boundaries of this original Slavic homeland remain a matter of ongoing scholarly debate examined further in Section 5.2 and in this chapter's Historical Debate feature. By the sixth and seventh centuries CE, Slavic-speaking communities had begun expanding in several directions: southward into the Balkans, following the weakening of Byzantine frontier control; westward into Central Europe; and eastward and northeastward into the forest and forest-steppe zones of what is now Russia, Ukraine, and Belarus, a movement examined in detail in Section 5.3.

This eastward Slavic expansion did not move into empty territory. The forest zones of the upper Volga, the Oka basin, and the region around Lakes Ladoga and Ilmen had long been inhabited by Finnic and other Uralic-speaking peoples, whose ancestors, on current linguistic and archaeological evidence, had occupied these northern forest and lake environments for many centuries before Slavic settlement began, developing hunting, fishing, and forest-based economies well adapted to a cold climate and dense woodland examined in Chapter 2. To the west and northwest, Baltic-speaking peoples occupied a considerably larger territory in the early medieval period than the modern Baltic states of Lithuania, Latvia, and Estonia might suggest — a zone extending well into present-day Belarus and parts of western Russia before centuries of Slavic settlement gradually reduced its extent, a process examined in Section 5.5.

Students should also recall the methodological caution this textbook established in Chapter 3 regarding archaeological cultures and ethnic identity, extended in Chapter 4 to Greek ethnographic writing about the Scythians. That same caution applies with particular force to this chapter's subject matter, because the ethnic and linguistic map of early medieval Eastern Europe is reconstructed from an especially thin and uneven evidentiary base: archaeological material culture that does not always correspond neatly to language or self-identification; place names and hydronyms (the names of rivers and other bodies of water) that can preserve traces of earlier, vanished populations long after those populations were absorbed or displaced; and written testimony that is generally late, external, and shaped by the political and religious purposes of its authors. The most important written source for the Slavic tribes specifically, the Primary Chronicle (also known by its opening words as the Tale of Bygone Years), was compiled in Kyiv in the early twelfth century, drawing on earlier written fragments, Byzantine sources, and oral tradition to construct a connected narrative of Rus' origins reaching back several centuries before its own composition. Its account of the Slavic tribes, examined in this chapter's Primary Source Window, is invaluable but requires the same critical handling this textbook has applied to Herodotus and to later chronicle evidence throughout.

Section 5.1

The Problem of Early Ethnicity

Before examining any specific people, this chapter must address a methodological problem that shapes everything that follows: what does it mean to identify a "people" in the early medieval period, and how confidently can historians do so? Modern intuitions about ethnicity often assume that a people is defined by a combination of shared language, shared ancestry, shared territory, and shared self-identification, persisting across generations as a recognizable, bounded group. Historians and archaeologists studying the early medieval period have learned, often through hard experience with earlier, more confident scholarship, that none of these criteria can be assumed to align neatly with one another in evidence this old and this thin.

Language is the criterion for which the most direct evidence survives, chiefly through comparative linguistics: the reconstruction of relationships among languages based on shared vocabulary, sound changes, and grammatical structure. Linguists are reasonably confident, for example, that a "Common Slavic" language existed as a relatively unified linguistic system before differentiating into the various Slavic languages of later centuries, and that this Common Slavic language belonged to a wider Balto-Slavic branch of the Indo-European language family, closely related to the Baltic languages examined in Section 5.5. But language does not automatically indicate ethnic self-identification, political unity, or biological descent: people can and do adopt new languages through conquest, trade, intermarriage, or cultural prestige, and a shared language family does not by itself prove that speakers considered themselves a single people.

Archaeological material culture — pottery styles, house forms, burial customs, jewelry types — provides a second, independent line of evidence, but one that presents its own interpretive difficulties, familiar from this textbook's treatment of Bronze Age archaeological cultures in Chapter 3. Archaeologists often identify a distinct "culture" on the basis of shared material traits across a defined region, and some such cultures, including several examined in this chapter, are conventionally associated with early Slavic, Finnic, or Baltic populations. However, a shared archaeological culture does not prove a shared language, a shared political identity, or a shared sense of peoplehood among those who produced it; conversely, different archaeological cultures do not necessarily indicate different languages or peoples, since a single linguistic community can produce regionally varied material culture, and a single material culture can be shared by linguistically distinct populations in close contact.

Written sources, finally, are almost always external to the peoples they describe in this period, composed by Byzantine officials, Arab and Persian geographers and merchants, or, in the case of the Rus' tribes specifically, by a Kyivan chronicler writing centuries after the events he narrated. Such sources can preserve genuine information, including ethnonyms — names by which outside observers, and sometimes the peoples themselves, identified specific groups — but they also reflect the limited, sometimes secondhand, and occasionally politically motivated knowledge of their authors, a caution this textbook applied at length to Herodotus's account of the Scythians in Chapter 4 and that applies with equal force to the Primary Chronicle's account of the Slavic tribes.

Historians today generally understand ethnicity in this period not as a fixed, inherited essence but as a historically contingent process, sometimes called ethnogenesis: the formation, transformation, and sometimes dissolution of group identities through migration, conquest, intermarriage, religious conversion, political incorporation, and changing self-understanding. A "tribe" named in the Primary Chronicle, such as the Polianians examined in Section 5.3, may represent a genuine, self-conscious political community, a looser cultural or linguistic grouping recognized primarily by outsiders, or some combination of the two, and modern historians cannot always determine with confidence which case applies to any specific name in the sources. This chapter proceeds with these cautions constantly in view, using the best available combination of linguistic, archaeological, and textual evidence while resisting the temptation—common in nineteenth- and twentieth-century nationalist historiography across the region—to read modern national categories back into a period that did not recognize them.

Section 5.2

Early Slavic Communities

The question of where and when a distinct Slavic-speaking population first emerged remains one of the more actively debated problems in early medieval European history, examined further in this chapter's Historical Debate feature. Byzantine writers of the mid-sixth century CE onward, including the historian Procopius and the strategic writer conventionally known as Pseudo-Maurice, describe peoples they call Sclaveni and Antes living north of the Danube frontier, engaging in raiding, settlement, and eventually treaty relationships with the Byzantine Empire. These sixth-century references represent the first reasonably clear outside textual attestation of Slavic-speaking populations, though the writers who produced them, like Herodotus writing on the Scythians nearly a thousand years earlier, worked from limited, often military and diplomatic, points of contact rather than sustained ethnographic study.

Most current scholarship places the more compact zone from which Slavic-speaking populations began their wider expansion somewhere in the region spanning parts of present-day Poland, northwestern Ukraine, and Belarus, based on a combination of linguistic reconstruction (including the distribution of early Slavic hydronyms), archaeological evidence (including the Prague-Korchak and related archaeological cultures conventionally associated with early Slavic populations), and the geographic starting point implied by the direction and timing of later, better-documented Slavic expansion into the Balkans, Central Europe, and the East European forest zone. Large-scale ancient-DNA research published in 2025, analyzing several hundred ancient genomes from sites across Eastern and Central Europe, has substantially strengthened the case for genuine, large-scale population movement during the sixth through eighth centuries CE, rather than a primarily local process of cultural adoption. In the regional transects studied, this research found that ancestry associated with the migrating population accounted for a large majority of the post-transition gene pool in several sampled regions, with a somewhat lower but still substantial share in the Volga-Oka sample, and identified the strongest spatial signal for a likely source zone in the area of present-day southern Belarus and northern Ukraine — a result broadly consistent with, though more geographically specific than, the compact-homeland zone long proposed on linguistic and archaeological grounds. This evidence gives considerably stronger support to a genuine demic (population) expansion model than earlier scholarship based mainly on the spread of material culture and language without directly testable genetic evidence. At the same time, the same research found substantial regional admixture between newcomers and existing populations, and its authors explicitly cautioned against treating genetic ancestry, archaeological culture, language, and self-identified ethnicity as interchangeable categories — a caution fully consistent with this chapter's Section 5.1. The current scholarly picture, therefore, is best understood not as a simple choice between "migration" and "local cultural transformation," but as a question of how large-scale population movement, whose scale and timing varied by region, interacted with the assimilation, intermarriage, and cultural blending examined throughout this chapter.

By the sixth and seventh centuries CE, Slavic-speaking communities were expanding in multiple directions simultaneously: southward across the Danube into the Balkans, a movement closely connected to the weakening of Byzantine frontier defense and to pressure from the Avar Khaganate examined in Section 5.7; westward into parts of Central Europe; and eastward and northeastward into the forest and forest-steppe zones of what is now Russia, Ukraine, and Belarus, examined in detail in Section 5.3. This expansion should not be imagined as a single organized migration under unified leadership, comparable to later, better-documented medieval or early modern population movements; rather, the evidence suggests a more gradual, uneven process of agricultural settlement, population growth, and the establishment of new communities, sometimes displacing, sometimes absorbing, and sometimes coexisting alongside earlier populations, including the Finnic and Baltic peoples examined in Sections 5.4 and 5.5.

Economically, early Slavic communities practiced a mixed agricultural economy adapted to the forest and forest-steppe environments described in Chapter 2, including slash-and-burn cultivation of temporary forest clearings, animal husbandry, hunting, fishing, and the gathering of forest products such as honey, wax, and furs, the last of which would become central to the trade networks examined in Chapter 6 and Section 5.8 of this chapter. Settlement patterns generally consisted of small, dispersed agricultural communities rather than large fortified centers, though archaeological evidence indicates growing social complexity, including the appearance of larger fortified settlements (gorodishcha) at strategic river locations, by the eighth and ninth centuries CE; some of these became part of the settlement landscape from which later urban and political centers emerged, examined in Chapter 7's account of the formation of Rus', though not every hillfort developed directly into a later town.

Section 5.3

Eastern Slavic Expansion and Settlement

The Primary Chronicle, compiled in Kyiv in the early twelfth century, provides the most detailed surviving textual account of the specific tribal groupings into which Eastern Slavic-speaking communities were organized by the time of the chronicler's narrative, though, as this chapter's Primary Source Window examines in detail, this account must be read as a retrospective twelfth-century construction rather than a contemporary sixth- through ninth-century record. According to the chronicle, distinct groupings occupied specific river valleys and regions: the Polianians along the middle Dnieper around Kyiv; the Drevlianians in the forests to the west of the Dnieper; the Ilmen Slavs (sometimes called Slovenes) around Lake Ilmen in the north, in the vicinity of the later city of Novgorod; the Krivichians in the upper Dnieper, Western Dvina, and Volga headwaters region; the Viatichians along the Oka River; the Radimichians along the Sozh River; the Severians along the Desna and Seim rivers; and several other named groupings occupying other river systems across the wider forest and forest-steppe zone.

Archaeological evidence broadly supports the existence of regionally distinct communities across this territory during the eighth through tenth centuries, associated with specific pottery styles, burial customs (including a range of practices from cremation to, later, various forms of inhumation), and settlement patterns, though, consistent with the methodological caution established in Section 5.1, historians cannot simply equate every archaeologically distinct regional grouping with a named tribe from the chronicle, nor can they assume that the chronicle's list is complete, precisely bounded, or free from later retrospective simplification. The chronicler's own presentation, moreover, serves a specific narrative purpose: to describe a diverse set of peoples who would, in his account, be gradually drawn together into a single political community under the Riurikid dynasty examined beginning in Chapter 7, a narrative arc that historians must distinguish from a neutral ethnographic record of the underlying diversity itself.

This eastward and northeastward expansion of Slavic-speaking agricultural settlement did not occur into unoccupied land. As Sections 5.4 and 5.5 examine in detail, the forest zones into which Slavic communities expanded were already inhabited by Finnic-speaking and Baltic-speaking populations, and the archaeological and toponymic (place-name) evidence indicates a gradual, uneven, and regionally variable process of contact, coexistence, intermarriage, and, over an extended period, linguistic assimilation, rather than a single decisive wave of conquest or displacement. In some regions, particularly around Lake Ilmen and along the upper Volga, Finnic-speaking populations appear to have persisted for centuries alongside, and eventually merged with, expanding Slavic-speaking communities, a process examined further in Section 5.8's treatment of intermarriage and cultural exchange.

By the ninth century, several of these Eastern Slavic tribal groupings had come under the tributary authority of neighboring powers: the Primary Chronicle reports that the Polianians, along with several other groupings, paid tribute to the Khazar Khaganate examined in Chapter 6, before this tributary relationship was reportedly ended through the intervention of the emerging Rus' political authority examined in Chapter 7. Other groupings, particularly the Ilmen Slavs and neighboring Finnic populations around Novgorod, are described in the chronicle as engaging Scandinavian ("Varangian") figures, examined in Chapter 7, in a more directly political capacity. These ninth-century developments mark the beginning of the transition from the tribal world examined in this chapter to the more centralized political structures examined beginning in Chapter 7, though, as that chapter will explain in greater depth, this transition was itself gradual, contested, and shaped by the interaction of Slavic, Finnic, Baltic, Turkic, and Scandinavian populations rather than the achievement of any single ethnic community acting alone.

Section 5.4

Finnic and Uralic Peoples

Long before Slavic-speaking communities began their eastward and northeastward expansion, the forest and lake zones of what is now northern and northeastern European Russia were inhabited by speakers of Uralic languages, a large language family distinct from the Indo-European family to which Slavic, Baltic, and most other European languages belong. The Uralic family includes the Finnic branch (modern Finnish, Estonian, and several smaller languages), the Saami languages of the far north, the Mordvinic branch (Erzya and Moksha), the Mari branch, the Permic branch (Komi and Udmurt), the Hungarian/Hungaric branch, the Khanty and Mansi (Ob-Ugric) languages, and the Samoyedic languages of northern Siberia; the precise higher-order internal classification of these branches remains debated among specialists, and "Finno-Ugric" is a traditional umbrella term for most of the family, generally excluding Samoyedic, rather than itself a single settled branch. The Finnic, Mordvinic, Mari, and Permic peoples of the upper Volga, Kama, and lake country represented the linguistic and cultural fabric of an enormous northern forest territory extending from the Baltic coast across the upper Volga basin and into the Ural Mountains and beyond.

Archaeological and linguistic evidence indicates that Finnic-speaking populations had occupied this northern forest and lake environment for a very long period before Slavic settlement began, developing hunting, fishing, and forest-gathering economies, together with, in some regions and periods, small-scale agriculture and animal husbandry adapted to the short growing seasons and dense woodland described in Chapter 2. Early Rus' sources name several specific Finnic-speaking groups in close proximity to expanding Slavic settlement, including the Merya, associated with the region around Rostov and Lake Nero; the Muroma, associated with the middle Oka around the later town of Murom; the Ves, associated with the region around Lake Beloozero; and the Chud, a term used somewhat broadly in early Rus' sources for various Finnic-speaking populations, particularly around Lake Peipus and the Gulf of Finland.

The relationship between expanding Slavic settlement and these established Finnic populations was neither uniformly hostile nor uniformly peaceful, and it varied considerably by region and period. In some areas, archaeological and toponymic evidence indicates centuries of coexistence, in which Slavic and Finnic communities occupied neighboring or even intermingled settlements, engaged in trade and intermarriage, and gradually blended material culture, before a slow process of linguistic assimilation, examined further in Section 5.8, led most descendants of these Finnic populations in the more heavily Slavic-settled zones to adopt Slavic, and eventually Russian, as their primary language over the course of several centuries extending well beyond the period this chapter directly covers. Other Finnic populations, particularly those farther from the main routes of Slavic settlement and river trade, retained their distinct languages and identities for much longer, including many of the Mordvinic, Mari, and Permic peoples of the Volga-Kama region, whose descendants continue to maintain distinct linguistic and cultural traditions within the modern Russian Federation.

This chapter's treatment of Finnic peoples should be understood as a beginning rather than a complete account: Chapters 15 and 28, examining respectively the growth of northeastern Rus' and the conquest of Siberia, will return to the historical experience of Finnic and other Uralic-speaking peoples as Slavic and later Russian settlement, taxation, and administration extended progressively deeper into their territories across subsequent centuries. Students should resist any assumption that the Finnic peoples examined in this section simply vanished or were replaced; rather, many were gradually incorporated, through a lengthy and uneven historical process involving both assimilation and persistence, into the broader population later described as Russian, while others maintained continuous distinct identities into the modern period.

Section 5.5

Baltic Peoples

Speakers of Baltic languages, a branch of the Indo-European family closely related to Slavic within the broader Balto-Slavic grouping noted in Section 5.1, occupied a territory in the early medieval period considerably larger than the modern Baltic states of Lithuania, Latvia, and Estonia might suggest to students unfamiliar with this history — and, in fact, Estonia's indigenous language is Finnic rather than Baltic, a distinction this section addresses directly to avoid a common confusion. Archaeological and toponymic evidence, particularly the distribution of Baltic-origin hydronyms across a wide zone, indicates that Baltic-speaking populations in the early medieval period extended well beyond the coastal Baltic region into large parts of what is now Belarus and portions of western and central Russia, including areas around the upper Dnieper and the headwaters of the Volga, before the gradual eastward and northeastward expansion of Slavic settlement examined in Section 5.3 progressively reduced this territory over the course of several centuries.

The Baltic-speaking peoples of this period were organized into a range of tribal groupings rather than a single unified state, including the ancestors of the later Lithuanians and Latvians, together with several Baltic-speaking peoples who did not survive into the modern period as distinct linguistic communities, most notably the Old Prussians (whose language, related to but distinct from Lithuanian and Latvian, became extinct following centuries of German conquest, colonization, and assimilation examined in later chapters) and the Yotvingians, a Baltic people of the forests between the Baltic peoples proper and the Slavic-settled regions to their east and south, who were gradually absorbed into neighboring populations by the early modern period.

Economically, Baltic-speaking communities in this period generally combined agriculture, animal husbandry, hunting, and fishing in patterns broadly similar to their Slavic and Finnic neighbors, reflecting the shared forest-zone environment examined in Chapter 2, and archaeological evidence indicates extensive contact, exchange, and, in border regions, intermarriage with neighboring Slavic populations from an early period. Some historians and linguists argue that the close relationship between Baltic and Slavic languages reflects not only common Indo-European ancestry but also a lengthy period of especially close contact, or even a shared Balto-Slavic linguistic stage, before the two branches diverged, though the precise nature and duration of this relationship remains debated among specialists in historical linguistics.

The historical trajectory of Baltic-speaking peoples diverged significantly from that of most Finnic peoples examined in Section 5.4 during the centuries following this chapter's main period. Rather than being gradually absorbed into an expanding Slavic-speaking population, as occurred with many Finnic communities of the upper Volga, the Baltic-speaking peoples of the coastal and near-coastal zone consolidated, over subsequent centuries, into politically significant societies of their own — most notably medieval Lithuania, whose Grand Duchy would become, as Chapter 18 examines in detail, one of the principal successor claimants to the political and territorial legacy of Rus' following the Mongol conquest examined in Chapter 16. This later political development should not be read backward into the period covered by this chapter, during which Baltic-speaking peoples remained organized into smaller tribal groupings without the centralized political structures that would emerge in later centuries.

Section 5.6

Turkic Peoples of the Steppe

While Slavic, Finnic, and Baltic populations expanded, coexisted, and gradually blended across the forest and forest-steppe zones examined in the preceding sections, the open Pontic-Caspian steppe corridor, introduced in Chapter 2 and examined at length in Chapter 4, continued to host a succession of mobile, mounted, and militarily formidable confederations, many of them Turkic-speaking or Turkic-affiliated, following the pattern of steppe political organization this textbook first examined through the Scythians and Sarmatians. Turkic-speaking peoples originated, according to current linguistic and archaeological understanding, in the wider Inner Asian steppe zone and spread westward across the Eurasian steppe corridor over the course of the first millennium CE, a movement connected to, but historically and linguistically distinct from, the earlier Iranian-speaking Scythian and Sarmatian populations examined in Chapter 4.

The Bulgars represent one of the most historically significant Turkic-speaking steppe peoples for the purposes of this textbook. Emerging as a recognizable political confederation on the Pontic-Caspian steppe (a polity often called Old Great Bulgaria) by the earlier seventh century CE, the Bulgars came under pressure from the rising power of the Khazar Khaganate, examined in detail in Chapter 6, and fragmented into several groups during the later seventh century rather than splitting neatly into only two. One group, led by a figure named Asparukh, migrated southwestward and, around 679–680 CE, established a state south of the Danube that would become medieval Bulgaria; other Bulgar groups dispersed in different directions, and migration into the Middle Volga region, occurring over an extended period and particularly during the eighth century, contributed to the later formation of Volga Bulgaria, a state centered on the middle Volga and lower Kama rivers that would develop into a significant commercial power and, in the early tenth century, adopt Islam as its state religion, a development examined further in Chapter 6. In the Danubian case, the Turkic-speaking Bulgar ruling elite governed a predominantly Slavic-speaking population and, over the following centuries, gradually merged with it; Slavic became the dominant language of the resulting medieval Bulgarian polity, which stands as an important political predecessor of the modern Bulgarian state, even though modern Bulgarian itself is a South Slavic language rather than a descendant of Bulgar Turkic — an instructive example, discussed further in Section 5.9, of a ruling group's language being absorbed into that of a numerically larger subject population while its name persisted.

Other Turkic-speaking or Turkic-affiliated steppe confederations of this period include groups whose precise ethnic and linguistic composition remains debated among specialists, reflecting the same methodological caution regarding steppe "ethnicity" discussed throughout this chapter: steppe confederations were frequently multiethnic political and military coalitions organized around a dominant leading group, rather than single, linguistically uniform peoples, a pattern already familiar from Chapter 4's discussion of the loosely used Greek label "Scythian." The Khazar Khaganate itself, examined in full in Chapter 6, exemplifies this pattern, incorporating Turkic-speaking, Iranian-speaking, and other populations under a Turkic-speaking ruling elite.

Turkic steppe military organization in this period built directly upon, and in some respects further developed, the mounted archery traditions examined in Chapter 4's discussion of Scythian and Sarmatian warfare, combined with new political and administrative structures, including the khaganate, a form of steppe political organization headed by a khagan (or khan) exercising authority, of varying strength and permanence, over a confederation of subordinate tribes and populations. This form of political organization would recur repeatedly across the remainder of this textbook's treatment of the steppe frontier, from the Khazar Khaganate examined in Chapter 6 through the Mongol khanates examined in Chapters 16 and 17.

Section 5.7

Avars, Pechenegs, and Other Steppe Confederations

The Avar Khaganate represents one of the most militarily significant steppe powers to interact directly with Slavic-speaking populations during their period of westward and southward expansion. Arriving in the Carpathian Basin (in what is now Hungary and surrounding territory) around 567–568 CE, as a confederation displaced from Inner Asia by political upheaval there, the Avars established a powerful khaganate that dominated a large portion of Central and Southeastern Europe for more than two centuries, exercising military and tributary authority over neighboring Slavic populations and launching frequent campaigns against the Byzantine Empire. The linguistic composition of the early Avar confederation remains genuinely debated: ancient-DNA research indicates that at least the early Avar ruling elite carried a strong genetic signature linked to Inner Asian populations, including notable affinities with genomes associated with the earlier Rouran confederation of Mongolia, but the language spoken by this early elite is not securely attested; some later Avar-period linguistic evidence is compatible with Bulgar Turkic, though this should not be read backward as proof of the language spoken by the confederation at its sixth-century arrival. Byzantine sources of this period often describe Slavic groups operating as subordinate allies, tributaries, or auxiliary forces of Avar campaigns, illustrating a pattern of steppe-forest interaction — political and military subordination combined with cultural and, at times, demographic exchange — comparable in structure, though not in every specific detail, to the Scythian-agricultural relationship examined in Chapter 4. Avar power declined sharply following military defeats at the hands of the Frankish ruler Charlemagne around 800 CE, and the Avar Khaganate had effectively dissolved by the early ninth century. Rather than illustrating any single, uniform pattern of steppe political impermanence, the more-than-two-century duration of Avar dominance suggests that steppe confederations could prove remarkably durable while remaining vulnerable, in the end, to changes in leadership, coalition structure, military pressure, and tributary relationships.

Further east, the Khazar Khaganate, examined in full detail in Chapter 6, rose to dominance across the Pontic-Caspian steppe and Volga-Caspian region from the seventh century CE onward, exercising tributary authority over several Slavic tribal groupings of the forest-steppe, including, according to the Primary Chronicle, the Polianians, before this relationship was reportedly ended through the actions of the emerging Rus' political authority examined in Chapter 7. The Khazar case, examined at length in the next chapter, illustrates especially clearly the complex, multidimensional character of steppe-forest interaction: military tribute and political subordination coexisted with extensive, mutually beneficial trade relationships along the Volga trade route, also examined in Chapter 6.

By the later ninth and tenth centuries, the Pechenegs, a Turkic-speaking confederation, had displaced earlier steppe powers to become the dominant political and military presence on the open Pontic steppe directly bordering the emerging Rus' polity examined in Chapter 7. The Primary Chronicle and Byzantine sources describe a relationship with the Pechenegs that fluctuated between warfare, tribute, and occasional diplomatic or commercial cooperation, a pattern this textbook will examine in greater detail in Chapter 8's account of Rus'-steppe relations under Sviatoslav and in Chapter 12's discussion of Rus' fragmentation. The Pecheneg case demonstrates that the steppe-sedentary relationship examined throughout this chapter, and first introduced through the Scythians and Sarmatians in Chapter 4, remained a defining feature of Eastern European history well into the period of Rus' state formation, rather than a phenomenon confined to remote antiquity.

Section 5.8

Trade, Intermarriage, and Cultural Exchange

The peoples examined in this chapter did not exist in sealed, mutually isolated territories; rather, the forest, forest-steppe, and steppe zones of Eastern Europe in this period were connected by extensive networks of trade, seasonal movement, and, over time, intermarriage and cultural blending, whose cumulative effect was to make ethnic and linguistic boundaries considerably more porous and historically fluid than later national narratives sometimes suggest. Furs, honey, wax, and, in some contexts, enslaved captives moved from Slavic, Finnic, and Baltic forest communities toward steppe intermediaries and, ultimately, toward the Islamic world, Byzantium, and beyond, along river and overland routes examined more fully in Chapter 6's treatment of the Volga and Dnieper trade systems; in exchange, forest-zone communities received silver, glass beads, textiles, and other manufactured goods, archaeological traces of which appear across a wide area of Slavic, Finnic, and Baltic settlement.

Intermarriage, though considerably harder to document directly than trade goods, left traces in archaeological and, eventually, linguistic evidence. Regions of especially dense contact between Slavic and Finnic populations, such as the area around Lake Ilmen and the upper Volga, and between Slavic and Baltic populations, such as the upper Dnieper and Western Dvina basins, show archaeological evidence of blended material culture — combinations of pottery styles, burial customs, and ornament types associated with more than one linguistic or cultural tradition — consistent with sustained intermarriage and cultural mixing over an extended period rather than sharply bounded, mutually exclusive populations. Toponymic evidence provides a further, distinctive form of confirmation: rivers, lakes, and other geographic features across large portions of the East European forest zone bear names of Finnic or Baltic linguistic origin even in areas that became overwhelmingly Slavic-speaking in later centuries, indicating that the earlier population's language left a durable linguistic substrate even after that population's descendants had substantially adopted Slavic speech.

This process of gradual linguistic assimilation, sometimes referred to by historians and linguists as language shift, should not be equated with the disappearance or destruction of the assimilated population. Rather, it typically involved the descendants of Finnic- or Baltic-speaking communities gradually adopting Slavic language and, over further generations, a Slavic-associated cultural and eventually political identity, while contributing their own genetic ancestry, some cultural practices, and, as just discussed, place names to the resulting blended population. Large-scale ancient-DNA research published in 2025, discussed in Section 5.2, indicates that migration-associated ancestry formed a substantial, and in some sampled regions a large majority, component of the post-migration gene pool across much of the East European forest zone, meaning that large-scale population movement, not only local cultural adoption, drove much of the demographic change of this period. At the same time, that same research documented significant regional admixture between newcomers and existing populations, so the resulting picture combines substantial migration with subsequent, geographically variable intermarriage and cultural mixing, rather than either a purely local "Slavicization" of existing populations or a total replacement of one population by another.

Steppe-forest exchange, examined more fully in Section 5.7 and in Chapter 6, added a further dimension to this pattern of contact: tribute payments, seasonal trade fairs, mercenary service, and, at times, forced population movement (including the capture and trade of enslaved people, a grim but historically significant dimension of early medieval Eurasian commerce) connected forest-zone Slavic, Finnic, and Baltic communities to the political and commercial world of Turkic-speaking steppe confederations, and, through them, to the wider trading networks of Byzantium and the Islamic world. Students should understand this chapter's peoples, therefore, not as isolated ethnic blocs awaiting the arrival of Rus', but as participants in an already extensive, multidirectional Eurasian network of exchange, coercion, and cultural interaction.

Section 5.9

Language, Identity, and Later National Claims

The peoples examined in this chapter have, since the nineteenth century, become the subject of extensive and sometimes politically charged claims by modern national movements seeking to establish historical priority, territorial legitimacy, or cultural prestige. Russian, Ukrainian, and Belarusian national historiography, in particular, have each, at various points, emphasized different aspects of the Eastern Slavic tribal world examined in Section 5.3 in support of competing narratives about which modern nation represents the truest or most direct heir to this early medieval population — a debate connected to, but distinct from, the later and equally contested question of the legacy of Kyivan Rus' itself, examined in Chapter 12.

Historians today generally caution against treating any single modern nation as the exclusive or predetermined heir of the Eastern Slavic tribal groupings examined in this chapter, for several interlocking reasons already introduced in Section 5.1. First, the tribal groupings named in the Primary Chronicle did not correspond to later national boundaries, and their descendants dispersed across, and contributed to, the populations of several later medieval and modern states rather than a single one. Second, centuries of subsequent migration, intermarriage, political incorporation, religious change, and linguistic development separate the ninth-century tribal world from any modern national community, making any claim of simple, unbroken continuity a considerable oversimplification. Third, as this chapter has emphasized throughout, the tribal groupings themselves were not ethnically or linguistically uniform blocks but included, in varying degrees across different regions, populations of Finnic, Baltic, and even, through trade and political contact, Turkic and Scandinavian origin, whose descendants likewise cannot be assigned neatly to a single later national community.

Similar caution applies to modern claims involving the Finnic, Baltic, and Turkic peoples examined in this chapter. Some modern national movements among the Uralic-speaking peoples of the Volga-Kama region, the Baltic peoples, and various Turkic-speaking peoples of Russia and neighboring states have, at times, emphasized deep historical continuity with early medieval populations in ways that require the same critical scrutiny applied to Slavic-focused nationalist historiography. This chapter's approach, consistent with the methodological standard established in Chapter 3 regarding Bronze Age archaeological cultures and in Chapter 4 regarding modern claims to Scythian heritage, is to treat language, archaeology, and textual evidence as historically informative but never as automatic proof of unbroken modern national descent, and to explain later national uses of this history as a separate, and separately analyzable, historical and political phenomenon rather than as part of the early medieval record itself.

At the same time, students should not conclude that language and identity in this period are simply unknowable or unimportant. The linguistic and cultural developments examined in this chapter left real, traceable legacies: the Slavic languages of modern Russia, Ukraine, Belarus, Poland, and the Balkans; the Uralic languages still spoken by millions of people across Finland, Estonia, and the Volga-Ural region of Russia; and the Baltic languages of modern Lithuania and Latvia all descend, through many intervening centuries of change, from the linguistic communities examined in this chapter. The historian's task is to trace these genuine legacies with appropriate precision and caution, distinguishing established linguistic and archaeological evidence from later, politically motivated overstatement in either direction.

Primary Source Window

The Primary Chronicle on the Slavic Tribes (early 12th century CE)

Creator: A composite work with a debated and multi-stage redactional history, traditionally associated in part with the monk Nestor of the Kyiv Caves Monastery · Date of composition: assembled through a sequence of redactions in the early twelfth century (an initial redaction traditionally linked to Nestor around 1113, a further redaction attributed to the monk Sylvester around 1116, and another redaction around 1118), describing events beginning several centuries earlier and surviving today only through later manuscript copies · Type: Chronicle narrative

Context: The Primary Chronicle (also called the Tale of Bygone Years) is not the work of a single identifiable author writing at a single moment; it is a composite text that passed through at least three identifiable stages of redaction in the early twelfth century, and the earliest surviving manuscripts date from centuries after even this final redaction. It opens with an account of the origins and settlement of the Slavic peoples, listing numerous tribal groupings by name and associating each with a specific river or region, before moving into its account of the arrival of the Varangians and the formation of Rus', examined in Chapter 7.

Brief paraphrase of the account (not a verbatim translated quotation):

The chronicler describes the Slavic peoples as originally settled along the Danube before dispersing and taking names from the rivers and places where each group settled: the Polianians near Kyiv on the Dnieper, the Drevlianians in the forests to the west, the Ilmen Slavs around Lake Ilmen, and several other named groups along other rivers. He notes differences in customs among these groups, praising some for peaceful and orderly ways and criticizing others, and describes the Polianians as living in a manner he considered closest to civilized Christian custom even before their eventual conversion.

Analysis: The chronicle is invaluable as the fullest surviving connected account of the Eastern Slavic tribal world, but it was composed and redacted in stages roughly two to four centuries after the events it describes, by compilers and redactors working within a Christian, Kyiv-centered political and religious framework interested in explaining how a diverse set of peoples came to form the single political community of Rus'. The text's moral judgments about different tribes—praising some as orderly and criticizing others as uncivilized—reflect the values and political perspective of its compilers rather than neutral ethnographic observation, in a manner comparable to the Greek literary conventions this textbook examined regarding Herodotus's account of the Scythians in Chapter 4. Archaeology can confirm the approximate existence and location of some named groupings but cannot verify the chronicle's specific moral characterizations or confirm that its list is complete.

Questions for students:

  1. Why might a twelfth-century Kyivan chronicler organize his account of earlier Slavic peoples around rivers rather than another organizing principle?
  2. What purpose might the chronicler's positive portrayal of the Polianians serve within his larger narrative about the origins of Rus'?
  3. What kind of independent evidence would help historians evaluate the chronicle's account of tribal distribution and customs?
  4. How does the chronicle's retrospective composition, several centuries after the events described, compare to the evidentiary problems this textbook identified in Herodotus's account of the Scythians?
Historical Debate

How Did the Slavic-Speaking World Expand?

The question: Ancient-DNA research published in 2025 provides strong direct evidence that large-scale population movement was a major component of Slavic-period demographic change across much of Eastern Europe during the sixth through eighth centuries CE. This finding has shifted the scholarly debate away from a simple choice between "migration" and "local cultural transformation" and toward a more specific set of questions: precisely where the main source zone lay, how many migration pulses occurred and over what timeframe, how much the scale of migration varied by region, and how ancestry relates—or does not automatically relate—to language, material culture, and self-identified ethnicity.

The demic-expansion emphasis: The 2025 study found that migration-associated ancestry accounted for a large majority of the post-transition gene pool in several sampled regions, with a somewhat lower but still substantial share in the Volga-Oka sample, and identified the strongest spatial signal for a source zone in the area of present-day southern Belarus and northern Ukraine. Scholars emphasizing this evidence argue that earlier models relying mainly on the spread of pottery styles and language understated the sheer scale of population movement involved.
The regional-admixture and caution emphasis: Other scholars, including the authors of the 2025 study itself, stress that substantial regional admixture between newcomers and existing populations occurred alongside this large-scale movement, that the proportion of migrant ancestry varied considerably by region, and that genetic ancestry, archaeological culture, language, and self-identified ethnicity must not be treated as interchangeable categories. On this view, the historical process combined real, substantial migration with the assimilation and cultural blending long emphasized by earlier scholarship, rather than replacing that picture wholesale.

Evidence: Historians, linguists, and geneticists weigh ancient-DNA sampling and its regional coverage, comparative linguistic reconstruction, the distribution of Slavic and non-Slavic hydronyms, archaeological cultures associated with early Slavic populations, and the timing and direction of Byzantine and other outside textual references.

Points of agreement: The 2025 study strongly supports substantial population movement during the sixth through eighth centuries and identifies southern Belarus and northern Ukraine as the best current spatial proxy for the source population, while emphasizing that ancestry alone cannot establish language, ethnicity, or self-identification.

Points of disagreement: Scholars continue to debate the precise number and timing of migration pulses, the degree of regional variation in migrant-ancestry proportions, and how directly the genetic evidence should be used to date or localize specific archaeological cultures or textual references to particular tribal groupings.

Why it matters: This debate affects how historians understand the relationship between ancestry, migration, language, and identity more broadly, and it has been subject to considerable political interest from modern nationalist movements seeking to claim a specific homeland location or migration narrative for their own national story.

Student task: Using the 2025 ancient-DNA findings, explain why strong evidence for large-scale migration does not, by itself, tell historians how quickly migrants and existing populations intermarried, adopted a shared language, or came to see themselves as a single people.

Multiple Perspectives

A Shared Forest-Steppe World

A Slavic agricultural settler on the forest-steppe frontier: For a family clearing new farmland along an expanding settlement frontier, life meant negotiating relationships with established Finnic or Baltic neighbors, adapting to a challenging forest environment, and, in many regions, paying tribute to a distant steppe power such as the Khazar Khaganate in exchange for relative security.
A Finnic hunter-fisher household in the upper Volga forests: For a Finnic-speaking family whose ancestors had inhabited the northern forests for many generations, the gradual arrival of Slavic-speaking agricultural settlers brought new neighbors, new trade opportunities in furs and forest products, and, over an extended period spanning many generations, increasing pressure toward linguistic and cultural assimilation.
A Khazar tribute official on the forest-steppe border: For an official of the Khazar Khaganate responsible for collecting tribute from forest-zone Slavic populations such as the Polianians, the frontier represented a source of valuable goods—furs, honey, wax, and captives—that sustained the wider Khazar trading economy examined in Chapter 6, requiring periodic military demonstration of authority alongside ongoing commercial relationships.

These perspectives illustrate that the same forest-steppe frontier world could mean settlement and opportunity, gradual cultural change, or tributary extraction, depending on a person's position within it—an important corrective to any single, simple narrative of "Slavic expansion" as an unopposed or uncomplicated process.

Cause and Effect

Eastern Slavic Expansion into the Forest Zone

Long-Term Conditions

The environmental structure of the East European forest and forest-steppe zones, examined in Chapter 2, offering fertile though labor-intensive agricultural opportunity once cleared; the existence of established Finnic- and Baltic-associated populations of varying regional density across this territory.

Intermediate Developments

Gradual population growth and agricultural technique adaptation (including slash-and-burn cultivation) among Slavic-speaking communities in their more compact zone of origin; disruption and pressure from steppe confederations, including the Avars, affecting Slavic populations nearer the Danube and Carpathian region.

Short-Term Triggers

Specific waves of substantial population movement out of a best-fit source region in/around present-day southern Belarus and northern Ukraine, identified by 2025 ancient-DNA research, during the sixth through eighth centuries, documented both archaeologically, through the spread of characteristic Slavic material culture, and genetically.

Effects

The tribal distribution described in the Primary Chronicle; substantial demographic change combined with regionally variable admixture and gradual linguistic assimilation of many Finnic and Baltic communities; the establishment of the settlement and trade foundations upon which the Rus' polity examined in Chapter 7 would be built.

Historical complexity: Slavic expansion was not a single organized migration but a centuries-long, regionally uneven process; ancient-DNA evidence indicates that large-scale population movement was a major component of this process, while its scale varied by region and newcomers subsequently mixed, through intermarriage and cultural exchange, with existing Finnic and Baltic populations.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From Confederation to Settlement

What Stayed the Same

  • The steppe corridor continued to host successive mobile confederations—Avars, Bulgars, Khazars, Pechenegs—following the pattern established by the Scythians and Sarmatians examined in Chapter 4.
  • Forest-zone agricultural and hunting-fishing economies persisted among Slavic, Finnic, and Baltic populations alike, adapted to the same environmental conditions examined in Chapter 2.
  • Trade in furs, honey, wax, and other forest products remained a central economic link between forest-zone communities and steppe or Byzantine markets.

What Changed

  • The territorial extent of Slavic-speaking settlement expanded dramatically over the sixth through ninth centuries, while Finnic and Baltic territory correspondingly contracted in many regions.
  • Political organization among forest-zone peoples grew gradually more complex, with the appearance of fortified settlements, some of which became part of the settlement landscape from which later centers emerged.
  • Steppe political dominance passed successively from the Avars to the Khazars to the Pechenegs; these confederations could prove durable—the Avar Khaganate lasted more than two centuries—but remained vulnerable to changing leadership, alliance structures, military pressure, and tributary relationships.

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both continuity in economic and environmental adaptation and change in territorial and political structure helps students see this period as a genuine historical process rather than a static backdrop to the "real" history that begins with Rus' in Chapter 7.

Everyday Life and Social History

An Early Slavic Forest Village

Archaeological excavation of early Slavic settlement sites across the forest and forest-steppe zone commonly reveals small communities of sunken-featured or semi-subterranean timber-built dwellings, often heated by a stone or clay oven, clustered in groups of perhaps several households along a river or stream, though construction methods and settlement layout varied considerably by region and period. In many forest settings, families practiced a form of shifting or slash-and-burn agriculture, clearing and burning forest plots to plant grain crops such as millet and rye for several seasons before allowing the land to lie fallow and clearing a new plot elsewhere, a labor-intensive method suited to the heavily forested environment examined in Chapter 2, though other regions relied more heavily on more settled field cultivation depending on soil and forest cover. Animal husbandry, particularly of cattle, pigs, and sheep, supplemented crop agriculture, while hunting, fishing, and the gathering of wild honey and other forest products provided further subsistence and trade goods.

Women's labor in such households likely included food processing, textile production (spinning and weaving), childcare, and garden cultivation, alongside participation in agricultural labor more broadly, though direct evidence remains limited compared to what survives for elite society in later, better-documented periods. Archaeological evidence of graves containing spindle whorls, loom weights, and personal ornaments provides some of the more direct material evidence for women's roles in these communities. Social organization above the household level likely involved kin-based groupings and, increasingly by the eighth and ninth centuries, larger tribal or proto-political structures capable of organizing defense, tribute payment to steppe powers, and, eventually, the political consolidation examined in Chapter 7.

Historical Vocabulary
Ethnonym

Definition: An ethnonym is a name applied to a people or ethnic group, whether by outside observers, by the group itself, or both. Ethnonyms recorded in early medieval sources—such as "Sclaveni," "Polianians," or "Pechenegs"—may reflect genuine self-identification, a name applied by outsiders that the group itself may not have used, or a broad label covering a looser and more diverse population than the single name suggests.

Historical context: This chapter's evidence depends heavily on ethnonyms recorded in Byzantine, Arab, and Rus' sources, none of which can be assumed to correspond precisely to a bounded, self-conscious, linguistically uniform community.

Why it matters: Confusing an ethnonym with a precise, stable ethnic category has led to significant historical misinterpretation, including nationalist projects that treat a single ancient name as proof of a single, continuous modern nation. Careful historians treat ethnonyms as a starting point for investigation rather than a settled conclusion.

Historical Vocabulary
Finno-Ugric / Uralic

Definition: Uralic is a language family distinct from Indo-European (the family that includes Slavic, Baltic, and most other European languages), including the Finnic branch (Finnish, Estonian, Karelian, and related languages), the Saami languages of the far north, the Mordvinic branch (Erzya and Moksha), the Mari branch, the Permic branch (Komi and Udmurt), the Hungarian/Hungaric branch, the Khanty and Mansi (Ob-Ugric) languages, and the Samoyedic languages of northern Siberia. "Finno-Ugric" is a traditional umbrella term covering most of this family, generally excluding the Samoyedic branch; it is widely used but should not be mistaken for a single, settled branch, since the higher-order internal classification of Uralic remains debated among specialists.

Historical context: Uralic-speaking peoples occupied the northern forest and lake zones of what is now Russia and Finland for a very long period before Slavic settlement expansion began, and several Uralic-speaking peoples remain distinct linguistic and cultural communities within the modern Russian Federation.

Why it matters: Understanding this language family helps students avoid the common error of assuming that all peoples of the East European forest zone spoke related, Indo-European languages, and helps explain the enduring linguistic and cultural diversity of northern Russia into the present day.

Regional Context

The Middle Dnieper and Its Neighbors

The middle Dnieper region around the future city of Kyiv, home to the Polianians named in the Primary Chronicle, occupied a historically significant position at the junction of several distinct zones examined in this chapter: forest-steppe agricultural land to the immediate north and west, open steppe and its Turkic-speaking confederations to the south, the Dnieper trade route running toward the Black Sea and Byzantium beyond, and, through the wider river network, connections to Baltic and Finnic populations to the north examined in Sections 5.4 and 5.5.

This position, combining agricultural fertility, defensible terrain, and access to major trade routes, helps explain why Kyiv would emerge, in the narrative Chapter 7 examines in detail, as a principal political center of the Rus' polity—though, consistent with this chapter's caution against teleological explanation, this later importance should not be read as though the Polianians' regional advantages made Kyiv's later prominence inevitable, since Novgorod in the north, examined in Chapters 7 and 14, and other regional centers also possessed significant advantages of their own.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the diverse peoples—Slavic, Finnic, Baltic, and Turkic—who inhabited the forests, forest-steppe, and open steppe of Eastern Europe before the formation of a recognizable Rus' polity in the ninth and tenth centuries. It began by establishing a methodological framework for understanding early medieval ethnicity: language, archaeological material culture, and outside written testimony each provide valuable but imperfect and non-equivalent evidence, and historians must resist the temptation to treat any single ethnonym as proof of a fixed, bounded, self-conscious ethnic community.

The chapter traced the debated origins and rapid sixth- through ninth-century expansion of Slavic-speaking populations. Large-scale ancient-DNA research published in 2025 strongly supports substantial, large-scale population movement out of a best-fit source region in/around present-day southern Belarus and northern Ukraine during the sixth through eighth centuries, while also documenting significant regional admixture between newcomers and existing populations—a finding that has reframed the older debate away from a simple choice between migration and local cultural transformation and toward questions of scale, timing, regional variation, and the relationship between ancestry, language, and identity. The chapter described the specific tribal groupings—Polianians, Drevlianians, Ilmen Slavs, Krivichians, Viatichians, and others—named in the Primary Chronicle and associated, with appropriate caution, with archaeological evidence from the eighth through tenth centuries.

It examined the Finnic-speaking peoples of the northern forest and Volga basin, including the Merya, Muroma, Ves, and Chud, who had inhabited this territory long before Slavic expansion began, and traced the gradual, uneven, multi-generational process by which many Finnic communities were linguistically assimilated into an expanding Slavic-speaking population, while others, particularly in the Volga-Kama region, retained distinct languages and identities into the modern period.

The chapter examined Baltic-speaking peoples, whose early medieval territory extended well beyond the modern Baltic states into present-day Belarus and western Russia, and traced the divergent historical trajectories of Baltic peoples who consolidated into later medieval states, particularly Lithuania, and those, including the Old Prussians and Yotvingians, who did not survive as distinct linguistic communities into the modern period.

Turning to the steppe, the chapter examined the succession of steppe confederations—the Avars (a confederation of debated linguistic composition with an Inner Asian-linked elite), the Turkic-speaking Bulgars (whose polity, Old Great Bulgaria, fragmented in the later seventh century into groups that established the Danubian Bulgar state and, through later Middle Volga migration, contributed to Volga Bulgaria), and the Turkic-speaking Pechenegs—that continued the pattern of mobile steppe political organization first examined through the Scythians and Sarmatians in Chapter 4, while introducing the khaganate as a characteristic steppe political structure.

The chapter's central analytical theme, examined directly in Section 5.8, held that trade, intermarriage, and cultural exchange connected these peoples in a dense, historically variable network rather than separating them into sealed ethnic blocks, a conclusion supported by toponymic, archaeological, and, increasingly, genetic evidence.

Finally, the chapter addressed the modern political use and misuse of this early medieval evidence, cautioning against nationalist claims of exclusive or unbroken descent from any single people examined in this chapter, while affirming that genuine, traceable linguistic and cultural legacies connect this period to the modern Slavic, Uralic, and Baltic-speaking peoples of Eastern Europe.

These lessons—methodological caution regarding ethnicity, attention to the full range of steppe-forest interaction, and skepticism toward nationalist appropriation—prepare students directly for Chapter 6's examination of the Khazars, Volga Bulgars, Vikings, and the Eurasian trade networks that would soon give rise to the political formation of Rus', examined beginning in Chapter 7.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Ethnonym
A name applied to a people, whether by outsiders or by the group itself, requiring critical evaluation rather than acceptance as a precise ethnic category.
Ethnogenesis
The historical process by which a group identity forms, transforms, or dissolves, rather than existing as a fixed, inherited essence.
Hydronym
The name of a river, lake, or other body of water, often preserving linguistic traces of earlier populations.
Uralic languages
A language family distinct from Indo-European, including Finnic, Saami, Mordvinic, Mari, Permic, Hungarian, Khanty-Mansi (Ob-Ugric), and Samoyedic branches; internal classification remains debated.
Finno-Ugric
A traditional umbrella term for most of the Uralic family, generally excluding the Samoyedic branch, rather than a single settled branch in itself.
Balto-Slavic
The proposed close linguistic relationship, or shared ancestral stage, between the Baltic and Slavic branches of Indo-European.
Common Slavic
The relatively unified early Slavic language believed to have existed before differentiating into later Slavic languages.
Tribal union
A loose political and social grouping of related communities, the likely organizational form of many Eastern Slavic tribes.
Khaganate
A steppe political structure headed by a khagan (khan), exercising authority of varying strength over subordinate tribes.
Tribute
Payment, often in goods such as furs or silver, rendered by a subordinate population to a dominant power in exchange for security or to avoid raiding.
Forest zone / Forest-steppe
Environmental zones of Eastern Europe, introduced in Chapter 2, shaping the economic adaptations of the peoples examined in this chapter.
Primary Chronicle
An early twelfth-century Kyivan chronicle narrating the origins of the Slavic tribes and the formation of Rus'.
Varangians
Scandinavian traders and warriors active along the Volga and Dnieper routes, examined further in Chapter 7.
Assimilation (linguistic)
The gradual, multi-generational process by which a population adopts a new language, without necessarily disappearing demographically.
Slash-and-burn agriculture
A method of temporary forest clearance and cultivation used by early Slavic and other forest-zone communities.
Confederation
A loose political-military alliance of related but distinct groups, characteristic of steppe political organization.
Linguistic substrate
Traces of an earlier population's language, such as place names, persisting after that population's descendants adopt a new language.
Sclaveni / Antes
Byzantine sixth-century terms for early Slavic-speaking groups near the Danube frontier.
Volga Bulgaria
A Turkic-speaking state on the middle Volga formed through Bulgar migration into the region, especially during the eighth century, following the fragmentation of Old Great Bulgaria, examined further in Chapter 6.
Pechenegs
A Turkic-speaking steppe confederation dominant on the Pontic steppe from the late ninth century, bordering early Rus'.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has carried students across a densely populated, linguistically diverse Eastern Europe in the centuries before any Rus' polity existed—a world of Slavic farmers clearing new forest plots, Finnic hunters and fishers adapting to a demanding northern environment, Baltic communities occupying a territory far larger than their modern successor states, and Turkic-speaking steppe confederations rising and falling in succession along the Pontic corridor first examined in Chapter 4. None of these peoples existed in isolation, and none can be identified in any simple, unbroken line with a single modern nation.

The chapter's central methodological lesson—that ethnicity, language, and archaeological culture do not automatically align, and that retrospective sources such as the Primary Chronicle require the same critical handling this textbook has applied to Herodotus and to Bronze Age archaeological evidence—will recur throughout this textbook, particularly in later chapters examining Soviet nationality policy, the legacy of Kyivan Rus', and the historical claims surrounding contemporary Russia, Ukraine, and Belarus.

As the textbook proceeds to Chapter 6's examination of the Khazars, Volga Bulgars, Vikings, and the Eurasian trade networks that connected this world to Byzantium and the Islamic world, students should carry forward this chapter's critical approach to ethnicity and evidence, its attention to the full range of contact and exchange among diverse peoples, and its skepticism toward nationalist appropriation of the early medieval past for modern political purposes.
