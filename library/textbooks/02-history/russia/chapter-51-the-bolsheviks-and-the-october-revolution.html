Part IX — Revolution and the Birth of the Soviet Union
51

The Bolsheviks and the October Revolution

A History of Russia: From the Eurasian World to the Russian Federation

Chapter Opening

Dates in this chapter are Old Style (O.S.) unless marked. In the twentieth century the Julian calendar ran thirteen days behind the Gregorian: 24–25 October O.S. = 6–7 November N.S.

On the night of 3 April 1917 (O.S.), a crowd of soldiers, workers, and socialist delegates waited at Petrograd’s Finland Station for a man who had been absent from Russia for most of a decade. Vladimir Lenin’s arrival, by way of Germany in a sealed railway carriage, was unremarkable by the standards of a revolutionary spring in which exiles were returning daily. Yet within seven months the small party he led would take central political power in the capital.

This chapter studies that transformation. Its question is narrower than that of Chapter 50, which explains why the Provisional Government and the moderate socialist leadership lost authority: how did the Bolsheviks go from a minority revolutionary faction to a party capable of seizing central power in October? Answering it requires attention to ideas, organization, personalities, mass opinion, and institutions, as well as to chance.

In spring the Bolsheviks were not poised for victory. At the First All-Russian Congress of Soviets in June, they held roughly one delegate in eight. Their leaders disagreed about strategy, and after the July Days the party was hunted and discredited. Outcomes were contingent: they depended on the failure of alternatives, the Kornilov crisis, and the choices of individuals.

The evidence is problematic. Party minutes, newspapers, and Lenin’s letters survive, but many were edited, and Soviet-era memoirs and films shaped a heroic narrative of a mass rising led by a united party. Western Cold War scholarship often emphasized conspiracy; later social historians stressed popular radicalization. This chapter considers both.

The chapter follows Chapter 50 and ends with the seizure of power and the Second Congress of Soviets. The dissolution of the Constituent Assembly, the Brest-Litovsk settlement, and the civil war belong to Chapter 52, and the establishment of the Soviet state to Chapter 53. It connects Russia to a wider war and to a European socialist movement that was itself divided by the conflict.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why Lenin’s return and the April Theses changed Bolshevik strategy.
  2. Analyze the internal resistance Lenin faced in the party.
  3. Describe how the party’s organization and press operated.
  4. Assess Trotsky’s role and his relationship with Lenin.
  5. Evaluate the effect of the July Days and the Kornilov Affair on Bolshevik fortunes.
  6. Distinguish support for soviet power from support for Bolshevik one-party rule.
  7. Trace the debate over insurrection and the role of the Military Revolutionary Committee.
  8. Reconstruct the events of 24–26 October O.S. with correct dating.
  9. Compare interpretations of October as revolution, insurrection, or coup.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  2. How did organization turn slogans into political power?
  3. What did “All power to the soviets” mean at different moments?
  4. Why did Bolshevik leaders disagree about an armed rising?
  5. How did the Military Revolutionary Committee make a seizure possible?

  7. Why do historians disagree about the term “coup”?

Historical Context

The February Revolution (Chapter 49) produced dual power (Chapter 50): a Provisional Government of liberals and moderate socialists, and the Petrograd Soviet, which commanded the loyalty of workers and soldiers. The war continued, food and transport worsened, peasants pressed for land, and workers formed factory committees. Russia’s war with Germany and Austria-Hungary (Chapter 48) created pressure that no leadership resolved.

Bolshevism had grown out of the 1903 split in the Russian Social Democratic Labor Party. It was a Marxist current emphasizing a disciplined party of professional revolutionaries, though in practice its membership and views were more varied. The Revolution of 1905 (Chapter 46) had introduced soviets as organs of workers' self-organization. In 1917 the party competed with Mensheviks, Socialist Revolutionaries, and anarchists, in a political field of many parties and national movements.

Section 51.1

Lenin Returns to Russia

Vladimir Lenin spent most of the decade before 1917 in emigration. When the February Revolution (Chapter 49) overthrew the monarchy he was in Zurich, and the usual routes home through the Entente countries were closed to a known opponent of the war. Russian socialists in Switzerland, with the Swiss socialist Fritz Platten acting as intermediary, negotiated passage through Germany. Lenin’s group, about thirty people including Nadezhda Krupskaya and Grigory Zinoviev, traveled under a specially negotiated extraterritorial arrangement, without ordinary passport or border formalities. The journey later became famous as the “sealed train.” They crossed Sweden and Finland and reached Petrograd’s Finland Station on the night of 3 April (O.S.; 16 April N.S.).

Germany facilitated the journey because a more radical, antiwar Russia served its military interests. That fact fed accusations of treason, but it does not show that Lenin was a German agent: facilitating a journey is not directing a policy, and Lenin pursued revolutionary aims that Germany did not share. Whether Bolshevik activity received German money, directly or through intermediaries, was investigated in 1917 and has been argued since; forged documents circulated, and the evidence for a controlling relationship has not been established.

Lenin entered a capital in which the Provisional Government and the Petrograd Soviet shared authority (Chapter 50) and in which most socialist leaders favored conditional support for the government while the war continued. He was greeted by Soviet delegates and a crowd of workers and soldiers, and he spoke at the station and then at the Bolshevik headquarters in the Kshesinskaya mansion. On 4 April he presented his program to Bolshevik delegates and then to a wider gathering of Soviet delegates at the Tauride Palace. His arrival mattered because he rejected the prevailing socialist consensus at once, in public, and in terms his own colleagues found startling.

Section 51.2

The April Theses and the Reorientation of Bolshevik Strategy

Lenin set out his program in the April Theses, first presented on 4 April (O.S.) and published in Pravda on 7 April. They declared that the war remained imperialist despite the fall of the monarchy and rejected “revolutionary defencism,” the view held by most socialist leaders that Russia must defend the revolution against Germany. They refused support to the Provisional Government, called for a republic of soviets rather than a parliamentary republic, for the confiscation of landed estates and nationalization of land, for a single national bank under soviet control, and for soviet supervision of production and distribution. They also proposed renaming the party and founding a new International.

The theses challenged orthodox Marxist expectations. Most Russian Marxists, Bolsheviks among them, held that Russia, a largely peasant country, was passing through a bourgeois-democratic revolution, and that a socialist revolution lay in the future. The Bolshevik formula before Lenin’s return had been a “democratic dictatorship of the proletariat and peasantry.” Lenin argued that the revolution had entered a second stage, in which power should pass to workers and the poorest peasants through the soviets. He distinguished this from an immediate introduction of socialism: the theses called for control of banks and production, not for expropriating everything at once. Critics beyond the party, including the veteran Marxist Georgy Plekhanov, were scornful, and many socialists thought the scheme invited civil war.

Reaction within the party was cautious. During March, Kamenev and Stalin, who had returned from Siberian exile, had steered Pravda toward pressure on the government and conditional support for defense. After Lenin’s arrival, Pravda printed Kamenev’s statement that the theses were Lenin’s personal view and that the bourgeois-democratic stage was incomplete. Local committees and the Petrograd leadership at first hesitated, and a Petrograd conference in mid-April did not adopt the theses wholesale.

Several things moved the party. Lenin argued relentlessly, in speeches and articles, and he insisted on a patient strategy: Bolsheviks should win a majority in the soviets by explaining their program rather than seize power prematurely. The April crisis of 20–21 April, when a note on war aims by the foreign minister Pavel Miliukov provoked demonstrations, showed workers and soldiers growing impatient with the government. At the All-Russian April Conference (24–29 April) Kamenev again argued against Lenin, but the conference adopted resolutions condemning the Provisional Government and endorsing soviet power as the party’s goal. Lenin did not win every point. The result was a negotiated reorientation within a party that remained broad, plural, and argumentative.

Section 51.3

Bolshevik Organization, Press, and Propaganda

Slogans spread because an organization could carry them, but that organization was not the perfectly centralized machine of later legend. At the top stood the Central Committee, a group of about nine members elected at the April Conference, which included Lenin, Zinoviev, Kamenev, Joseph Stalin, and Yakov Sverdlov. Below it were the Petrograd Committee, district committees, and cells in factories and regiments. The Vyborg district, with its large metalworking plants, was often more radical than the leadership. A separate Military Organization, headed by figures such as Nikolai Podvoisky, worked among soldiers and published Soldatskaya Pravda.

Local committees had substantial initiative. They often set their own tactics, argued openly with party newspapers, and sometimes ignored central cautions or exhortations. The party’s growth in 1917 intensified this: membership rose from perhaps 24,000 in February to roughly 350,000 by autumn, with many members new, young, and politically inexperienced. Estimates are approximate because local records were fragmentary. This is one reason Alexander Rabinowitch’s work, discussed in the Historical Debate, challenged the image of a monolithic conspiracy.

Propaganda worked through the press, leaflets, street speeches, and meetings in barracks and factories. Pravda was suppressed and revived under new titles, which became a pattern after July. Messages were short and tied to concrete grievances: peace, land, bread, and soviet authority. Money came from dues, collections, sales, and donations; the scale of any outside funding remains unresolved.

Numbers need context. Bolshevik membership was large for a socialist party in Russia, but the Socialist Revolutionaries claimed far more members nationally, largely because of peasant support. The Bolsheviks’ advantage was concentration. They were strong in the capitals, in industrial districts, in sections of the garrison and the Baltic Fleet, and, from September, in the soviets. Organizational concentration and political geography mattered more than nationwide numerical dominance.

Section 51.4

Trotsky, the Mezhraiontsy, and the Petrograd Soviet

Leon Trotsky’s relationship with Lenin was long and adversarial before 1917. At the 1903 party congress he sided with the Mensheviks, though he soon broke with them, and in the years before the war he tried to reconcile the factions while criticizing Lenin’s methods of party organization. He had been a leading figure in the Petersburg Soviet in 1905 (Chapter 46), and he opposed the war from exile, in Paris, Spain, and New York. After the February Revolution he was detained in Halifax by British authorities and arrived in Petrograd on 4 May (O.S.).

In Petrograd he did not immediately join the Bolsheviks. He led the Inter-District Organization (Mezhraiontsy), a group of a few thousand Social Democrats formed in 1913 in the capital that stood apart from both main factions. Its members included Anatoly Lunacharsky, Adolf Ioffe, Moisei Uritsky, and Vladimir Volodarsky. It supported soviet power and opposed the war, and so was close to the Bolsheviks, but its members distrusted Bolshevik factionalism, and Trotsky and Lenin held talks in which the question of fusion was explored. The organization formally merged with the Bolsheviks at the Sixth Party Congress at the end of July and beginning of August, while Trotsky was in prison.

Trotsky’s importance in 1917 lay in his gifts as a public speaker, which drew large crowds at the Cirque Moderne and in factories and barracks, and in his work within the Petrograd Soviet, where he sat on the Central Executive Committee elected by the First Congress of Soviets. He was arrested after the July Days and released on bail in early September. When the Bolsheviks won a majority in the Petrograd Soviet, Nikolai Chkheidze, the Menshevik chairman, resigned, and on 25 September Trotsky was elected chairman.

This did not make him Lenin’s subordinate. His political convergence with Lenin in 1917 was driven by shared judgments about the war, the weakness of the coalition, and soviet power, not by personal reconciliation, and differences remained. He held independent authority in the Soviet and, as the next sections show, favored a different approach to the timing and form of the seizure.

Section 51.5

From the July Defeat to Political Recovery

Chapter 50 narrates the July Days. This section asks why the Bolsheviks survived the repression that followed. The government issued warrants for Lenin and other leaders, raided the offices of Pravda and Bolshevik organizations, disarmed some units, and arrested activists, including Kamenev and, later, Trotsky. Newspapers carried accusations that Lenin was a German agent, based on allegations that were widely repeated and never proved. Lenin and Zinoviev went into hiding, first near Petrograd and later in Finland. Alexander Kerensky became prime minister on 8 July.

The government’s coercive power was limited. It depended on a small number of reliable units, such as some Cossack and cadet detachments, while the great majority of the garrison was uncertain, and it could not count on the Soviet leaders, who feared splitting the socialist camp. Arrests numbered in the hundreds, not in the tens of thousands, and prosecutions moved slowly. The accusation of treason also lost force: leading Soviet figures declined to endorse it, the evidence was thin, and the government’s own legal officers were uneasy. Lenin decided against surrendering for trial, fearing mistreatment, a decision that divided his colleagues.

Meanwhile the party’s organization persisted. Pravda was closed, but replacement papers, such as Rabochii i Soldat and later Rabochii Put, appeared under changed titles. Local committees, factory cells, and the Military Organization continued to meet. The Sixth Party Congress, held semi-legally in Petrograd from 26 July to 3 August, elected a new Central Committee, reported a much larger membership than in April, and formally set aside the slogan of peaceful transfer of power to the soviets, which Lenin had declared obsolete.

Attitudes in factories and barracks also mattered. Many workers, especially metalworkers, resented the arrests and held the government responsible for worsening conditions, even where they distrusted Bolshevik methods. Recovery was therefore not guaranteed. In July and August the Bolsheviks were weakened, and their path back depended on the failure of the government’s own attempt to restore order, the subject of the next section.

Section 51.6

The Kornilov Affair and the Acceleration of Bolshevik Support

Chapter 50 narrates the Kornilov Affair of late August (O.S.). The question here is what it did to Bolshevik fortunes. When rumors spread that General Lavr Kornilov’s troops were marching on the capital, a Committee for the Struggle Against Counterrevolution, including Bolsheviks, Mensheviks, and Socialist Revolutionaries, coordinated defense. Railway workers diverted and delayed trains, telegraph operators passed information, and agitators, including soldiers’ delegates, met Kornilov’s units and persuaded many to halt.

For the Bolsheviks the consequences were large. Activists were released from detention and rejoined public politics. The Soviet distributed weapons, and Red Guard detachments, which had been disbanded or disarmed after July, were reconstituted in factories on a larger scale. Kronstadt sailors were sent to protect the capital. Bolsheviks worked openly alongside moderate socialists, which reduced the stigma of the July accusations.

The affair discredited the policy of coalition with liberals. Liberal politicians and officers appeared to many workers and soldiers to have flirted with Kornilov, and Kerensky himself was suspected of having first encouraged and then opposed him. Kadet ministers had resigned in the preceding crisis, and the collapse of the government’s authority left moderate socialist leaders, who had defended coalition, exposed. Soldiers’ committees that had once loyally supported the government’s officers now distrusted the officer corps.

The Bolsheviks’ warnings about counterrevolution gained credibility, and many who had not been Bolshevik sympathizers drew conclusions from events. They did not cause the crisis, and many supporters moved toward them while still hoping for a unified socialist government. Lenin himself, in early September, wrote of a possible compromise in which the Mensheviks and SRs would take power through the soviets and the Bolsheviks would remain in opposition. The proposal was ignored. On 31 August the Petrograd Soviet adopted a Bolshevik-sponsored resolution, and the affair became the hinge between July weakness and September strength.

Section 51.7

Growing Bolshevik Support: Workers, Soldiers, Sailors, and the Soviets

Bolshevik support in autumn 1917 was concentrated, uneven, and varied by social group. Among Petrograd workers, metalworkers at large plants such as the Putilov works and in the Vyborg district were the core. The factory committees, which grew out of workers’ efforts to oversee management and prevent closures, moved toward the Bolsheviks over the summer, and their conference in Petrograd adopted a Bolshevik line. Workers’ militias, the Red Guards, expanded after Kornilov to the tens of thousands in the capital, though exact numbers are uncertain.

The garrison of Petrograd, well over a hundred thousand men, was drawn to the Bolsheviks less by doctrine than by war weariness, fear of transfer to the front, and anger at officers. The Baltic Fleet, with Kronstadt and Helsingfors as centers, was among the most radical forces, though Kronstadt’s politics also included anarchists and Left Socialist Revolutionaries. In Moscow, the Soviet passed a Bolshevik resolution on 5 September, and in the September city elections the Bolsheviks won about half the vote, though in a much reduced turnout. Soviets in industrial areas such as Ivanovo-Voznesensk and parts of the Urals also shifted.

Elsewhere support was weaker. Most peasants continued to look to the Socialist Revolutionaries, and the land seizures spreading across the countryside were largely locally organized. Many soldiers on the southwestern and southern fronts remained less radical than those on the northern and western fronts. In the borderlands, such as Ukraine, Finland, the Baltic provinces, and the Caucasus, national movements and local parties set the agenda, and Bolsheviks were often a minority except in some industrial centers.

What did the September soviet majorities show? They showed that in the capitals and in some industrial cities, the largest organized bodies of workers and soldiers had turned against coalition government and were prepared to endorse soviet power. They did not show that a majority across the country backed a Bolshevik one-party government. Soviets were not elected on equal franchise, they over-represented urban and military constituencies, and many delegates voted for resolutions on peace, land, and soviet authority while expecting a coalition of socialist parties. The distinction between support for soviet power and support for a Bolshevik government is critical for understanding why the legitimacy dispute began immediately after the seizure. The elections to the Constituent Assembly in November (Chapter 52) later gave a different picture of national opinion.

Section 51.8

“All Power to the Soviets” and the Question of Political Authority

The slogan “All power to the soviets” was raised by the Bolsheviks in April, but its political meaning changed with the soviets themselves. In spring, the soviets were led by Mensheviks and Socialist Revolutionaries, and the slogan meant that moderate socialists should take power from the Provisional Government and end the coalition with liberals. The Bolsheviks were a small minority in the soviets, and the call was an invitation to others to govern, not a plan to govern themselves.

After the July Days Lenin judged that this had changed. In writings of July he argued that the soviets, led by moderates who had backed the repression, had become appendages of a counterrevolutionary government, and that the peaceful development of the revolution through them was no longer possible. He withdrew the slogan and considered other organs, such as factory committees, as the basis for a new rising. The Sixth Congress reflected this shift.

The Kornilov Affair reversed it. When the soviets themselves resisted Kornilov and Bolshevik resolutions began to win majorities, Lenin restored the slogan, now with a different political implication. In spring, soviet power meant moderate socialist rule; in September and October it meant, in practice, a government led by the party that controlled the largest soviets. Lenin’s early September writings on compromise, offering the moderates one last opportunity, were ignored. In these months he was also drafting, in hiding, the work later published as The State and Revolution, on the form of a state based on soviets.

Different groups understood the slogan differently. For many workers and soldiers it meant government answerable to their own councils, a break with the coalition, and a path to peace and land; many expected all socialist parties to take part. For Bolshevik leaders such as Lenin it was both a program and a means of legitimizing a seizure of power. For moderate socialists it posed dangers: they feared that a soviet government would lose the support of the peasantry and of educated society, provoke civil war, and endanger the war effort. Treating the slogan as a timeless formula hides these changes.

Section 51.9

Debate Within the Bolshevik Leadership

In mid-September, writing from hiding in Finland in letters such as “The Bolsheviks Must Assume Power” and “Marxism and Insurrection,” Lenin argued that having won majorities in the Petrograd and Moscow soviets the party must take power immediately. He rejected waiting for the Constituent Assembly and treated the Congress of Soviets as a secondary question. His reasons included fear of a further rightist attempt, the German advance, the threat of surrendering Petrograd, and the conviction that the moment would pass. The Central Committee did not publish all these letters, and some members considered them dangerously impatient.

Several positions existed. Lenin wanted an armed rising, led by the party, as soon as possible. Kamenev and Zinoviev argued that the party’s strength outside the capitals was uncertain, that a rising risked defeat and isolation, and that a strong showing in the Constituent Assembly elections offered a safer route. Trotsky favored preparing force while tying the transfer of power to the Second Congress of Soviets, whose opening the Soviet executive had postponed to 25 October. This would give action the appearance, and arguably the substance, of soviet authority. Others, including some provincial leaders, doubted that workers would fight.

Lenin returned secretly to Petrograd in early October. On 10 October, at a meeting in a private apartment, the Central Committee voted ten to two for armed rising, with Kamenev and Zinoviev opposed. The resolution set no date. At an enlarged meeting on 16 October, with party, military, and trade-union representatives, Lenin’s resolution passed again by a large majority, with continued dissent and abstentions. The meeting also created a Military Revolutionary Center, which had little independent effect and was overshadowed by the Soviet’s own committee (§51.11).

The dispute went public. On 18 October Kamenev criticized the plan in a non-party newspaper, Novaya Zhizn. Lenin called this a betrayal of secrecy and urged expulsion of Kamenev and Zinoviev. The party did not expel them. Distinguishing the bodies matters: Lenin’s insurrection plan, the Central Committee, the Military Organization, the Petrograd Soviet, and the Military Revolutionary Committee had different leaders and aims. The party appears divided, disputatious, and responsive to events, not mechanically obedient.

Section 51.10

The Democratic Conference, Pre-Parliament, and the Decision for Insurrection

In the aftermath of Kornilov, moderate socialists tried to rebuild a stable government. The Soviet executive convened a Democratic Conference of delegates from soviets, trade unions, cooperatives, city councils, and other organizations, which met in Petrograd from 14 to 22 September (O.S.). Its task was to settle the question of coalition and to provide a broader base for authority until the Constituent Assembly could meet. Roughly fifteen hundred delegates attended, and the Bolsheviks were a minority.

The conference was divided. Its delegates debated at length whether to continue a coalition with liberals, and in votes first approved coalition in principle and then rejected cooperation with the Kadets. Kerensky nevertheless formed a third coalition government on 25 September that included liberal ministers, in effect ignoring the conference’s majority. A consultative body, the Provisional Council of the Russian Republic, or Pre-Parliament, was created to provide a forum, but it had no power to bind the government and little prestige.

The Bolsheviks argued over whether to take part. Lenin urged a boycott and argued that political maneuvering distracted from the preparation for a rising. The party leadership narrowly voted to take part in the Pre-Parliament, and the Bolshevik group entered it when it opened on 7 October. On its first day Trotsky read a declaration condemning the government and the body as unrepresentative, and the Bolsheviks walked out.

These episodes show that other arrangements were still being attempted. The Democratic Conference and Pre-Parliament were efforts to reconstruct a broad socialist and democratic coalition before an elected Constituent Assembly; elections to it were scheduled for November. They failed to win popular confidence, and the government’s weakness, the German advance in the Baltic, and fears of another rightist attempt strengthened Lenin’s conviction that delay was dangerous. It would be wrong, however, to treat these institutions simply as preliminaries to October. Whether a more representative government could have emerged is a question historians continue to debate, and the Bolshevik decision for insurrection was made while these alternatives were still operating.

Section 51.11

The Military Revolutionary Committee

The Military Revolutionary Committee (MRC) originated as a Petrograd Soviet institution. In early October the German advance in the Baltic and talk of moving the government and transferring garrison units out of the capital made defense a live issue. The Soviet’s executive approved the idea of a committee for the defense of the capital and the control of the garrison, and the MRC was established in the second week of October. Its formal chairman was the Left Socialist Revolutionary Pavel Lazimir. Bolsheviks such as Vladimir Antonov-Ovseenko and Podvoisky held key positions, and Trotsky, as Soviet chairman, was its political guide.

The MRC’s function was to assert Soviet authority over military forces. It sent commissars to regiments, arsenals, and strategic points. Garrison representatives voted to recognize the Soviet’s authority. On 21 October an MRC delegation informed military-district headquarters that orders to the garrison would require the committee’s sanction. General Georgy Polkovnikov rejected the claim, and on 22 October the MRC circulated a declaration that directives to the garrison were invalid unless countersigned by the committee. This turned a dispute over control into an open contest between the government’s military command and the Soviet. Units, including the garrison of the Peter and Paul Fortress, shifted their loyalty in the following days.

Its character is the crucial point. The MRC was not a secret Bolshevik headquarters at its birth. It was a Soviet body, with Left SR members, whose legitimacy gave its orders weight. Bolsheviks came to dominate it and transformed it into the operational instrument of the seizure. That transformation allowed the party to present preparations as defense of the Soviet while building the capacity to act. The institution mattered not because it commanded large forces, but because garrison units, Red Guards, and sailors accepted its orders when the government’s orders no longer commanded obedience.

Section 51.12

The October Insurrection and the Seizure of Petrograd

On the night of 23–24 October (O.S.; 5–6 November N.S.) Kerensky’s government moved to close the Bolshevik papers Rabochii Put and Soldat and to guard the bridges and key buildings with cadets and loyal units. Soldiers sealed the printing offices in the early hours, but the MRC countered, sending guards to restore the presses and to reopen the Neva bridges. Kerensky addressed the Pre-Parliament on 24 October seeking support and received a divided response. The government’s actions gave the MRC a plausible defensive justification and a pretext for acting.

During 24 October and the following night, MRC forces, Red Guards, soldiers, and sailors took control of bridges, railway stations, the telephone and telegraph exchanges, power stations, and the State Bank, usually with little resistance. Lenin, who had remained in hiding, went to the Smolny Institute late on 24 October. On the morning of 25 October (7 November N.S.) Kerensky left the capital to seek loyal troops, and the MRC announced that the government had been overthrown. Lenin drafted an appeal “To the Citizens of Russia,” and the Pre-Parliament was dispersed by MRC troops.

The Winter Palace, where the ministers sat, was surrounded during 25 October. Its defense eroded over the day. Cossack units initially present left before the final entry, some cadet units also departed, and the remaining force included cadets, engineering-school personnel, and a small detachment of the Women’s Battalion. A ministerial account lists these groups. The cruiser Aurora and the Peter and Paul Fortress took part in the pressure on the Palace, and it was entered late at night, around two in the morning of 26 October. Ministers were arrested and taken to the Peter and Paul Fortress. Kerensky was not among them.

Casualties during the seizure were limited, and estimates vary. Most of the city functioned normally: trams ran, theaters opened, and many residents learned of the change from newspapers. The later image of a massive, heroic storming owes much to the 1920 mass re-enactment staged in Petrograd and to Sergei Eisenstein’s 1928 film October, which became in popular memory the event itself. Historians therefore treat such images as evidence of Soviet commemoration, not as a record of what occurred.

The speed of the seizure reflects the government’s lack of reliable defenders, not a lack of preparation or planning. The Bolsheviks controlled the key points, but Petrograd was not the country. Kerensky’s attempt to bring troops back, soon followed by a Cossack advance, showed that control of the capital did not settle the question of power.

Section 51.13

The Second Congress of Soviets and “Revolution, Coup, or Both?”

The Second All-Russian Congress of Soviets opened at about 10:45 p.m. on 25 October in the Smolny. Contemporary counts differ, but roughly 650–670 delegates attended. A preliminary count gave about 670, roughly 300 of them Bolsheviks, with about 193 Socialist Revolutionaries and 82 Mensheviks, while another party count gave 649 with 390 Bolsheviks. The composition can be determined only approximately. The Bolsheviks were the largest single bloc, and together with the Left Socialist Revolutionaries they commanded a majority.

The Congress opened amid gunfire from the Winter Palace area. Many Mensheviks and Right Socialist Revolutionaries protested the seizure as illegitimate and left. Julius Martov proposed a negotiated broad socialist government, which found sympathy but was overtaken by the walkout. Trotsky is reported to have dismissed those leaving as consigned to history’s dustbin, a phrase that became famous. The Left SRs stayed but initially declined to join the government. On 26 October the Congress adopted an appeal announcing the transfer of power to the soviets and issued decrees on peace and on land. It approved a Council of People’s Commissars (Sovnarkom) with Lenin as chairman and only Bolsheviks as members, and elected a new Central Executive Committee.

The legitimacy problem followed immediately. The Railwaymen’s Union (Vikzhel) threatened a strike unless a socialist coalition were formed, negotiations began, and fighting in Moscow lasted until early November (O.S.). Many peasants and soldiers outside the capitals had yet to speak. The seizure had been made in the name of the Soviets by a party that did not hold a majority in the country.

Was October a social revolution, an armed insurrection, a coup, a soviet transfer of power, or some combination? “Coup” stresses an organized, partly secret seizure of governmental institutions by one party. “Revolution” stresses the mass radicalization, collapse of the old authority, and social upheaval that produced the context. “Soviet transfer” stresses the Congress’s role and its limits. The terms answer different questions, which is why the historiographical debate does not turn on facts alone.

Historical Vocabulary
Soviet and Military Revolutionary Committee

Soviet means “council.” In 1917 soviets were elected councils of workers, soldiers, and later peasants; they were not Bolshevik institutions, and until September most were led by Mensheviks and SRs. The Military Revolutionary Committee was a Petrograd Soviet body that came under Bolshevik direction.

Distinguishing the two prevents treating “soviet” as a synonym for “Bolshevik.”

Historical Debate

Revolution or Coup?

The question: How should historians characterize the October seizure of power, and what weight should they give to popular support, party organization, and the manner of the seizure?

Interpretation A — the Soviet revolutionary account: Official Soviet historiography presented October as a socialist revolution led by a unified party and carried out by workers, soldiers, and sailors, with Lenin as architect. It treated the Bolshevik path as the expression of historical necessity and played down internal dissent, the role of other parties, and the limits of support.
Interpretation B — the conspiratorial coup account: Richard Pipes described October as a coup d’état, the capture of governmental power by a small, disciplined minority exploiting a collapsing state. He stressed Lenin’s leadership, the small scale of the fighting, and the party’s lack of a national majority. This view draws on the manner of the seizure, the use of the MRC, and the immediate legitimacy dispute.
Interpretation C — the social-political synthesis: Alexander Rabinowitch and others argued that the Bolsheviks were a flexible, decentralized party with real worker and soldier support and internal disagreement, and that their growth reflected popular radicalization. This work challenges the picture of a perfectly centralized conspiracy, though it does not deny that the seizure was organized.

Evidence: Party minutes and letters, soviet votes and resolutions, newspapers, Red Guard and garrison records, memoirs from several sides, and election results.

Points of agreement: The seizure was organized, quick, and limited in violence in the capital; the Bolsheviks gained influence among workers, soldiers, and sailors in the autumn; and they did not hold a national majority for one-party rule.

Points of disagreement: How far popular support legitimized the seizure, how centralized the party was, and what the terms imply.

Why it matters: The labels carry political weight and shape later narratives. A careful conclusion does not split the difference mechanically: “coup” accurately captures aspects of the organized seizure of governmental institutions, while “revolution” captures the larger social and political transformation that produced the constituency and institutional environment in which the seizure succeeded.

Student task: Select three pieces of evidence and explain which interpretation each supports best and what it cannot show.

Multiple Perspectives

October as Experienced

Petrograd workers and soldiers: Many sought peace, bread, and soviet authority and backed Bolshevik resolutions.
Moderate socialists: Many regarded the seizure as destructive to a democratic transition and left the Congress.
Government defenders: Cadets and the women’s battalion defended a government with little remaining support.
Continuity and Change Over Time

What changed? Bolshevik status, strategy, and institutional position. What remained? War, food shortages, peasant land hunger, and soviet institutions. When? Rapidly from late August. Who experienced most change? Capital workers, soldiers, and officials. Who experienced continuity? Most peasants. Why it matters: A change of government did not immediately remake society.

Cause and Effect

Long-term

War strain, social inequality, weak institutions.

Intermediate

Dual power, failed coalitions, party organization, Kornilov.

Trigger

Government move against Bolshevik papers and MRC response.

Consequences

Bolshevik-led government, contested legitimacy, and civil war (Chapter 52).

No single cause suffices: organization without crisis, or crisis without a ready party, would have led elsewhere.

Everyday Life and Social History

Petrograd’s residents faced bread lines, rising prices, and winter fuel shortages. Many workers attended meetings after shifts; soldiers feared transfer to the front. On 25 October trams and shops largely operated, and many residents learned of the change from newspapers and rumor. Experiences differed by class, neighborhood, and gender, and elite memoirs do not represent them all.

Regional Context

Outside the capital, change came unevenly: Moscow saw days of fighting before Bolshevik control, many provincial soviets acted within days or weeks, and in Ukraine, the Caucasus, Finland, and the Baltic, national movements pursued their own aims (Chapter 50). The October seizure was not a single national event.

Russia and the World

The seizure occurred within a world war and appeared to many Europeans as a possible start of broader revolution. German policy toward Russia, the divisions of the Second International, and Allied fears of a separate peace shaped contemporaries’ responses.

Section 51.14

Chapter Review and Historical Thinking Checkpoint

Chapters 49 and 50 explain the loss of the old center; this chapter explains the party that filled it. The Bolsheviks combined mass support, organization, and decisive leadership, and benefited from the failures of their rivals. Their path was not linear: they faced internal disagreement, repression after July, and recovery after Kornilov. October was an organized seizure of power within a genuine revolutionary crisis. Support for soviet power was broader than support for a one-party government, which is why the legitimacy dispute began immediately. Problems: (1) Explain how organization and slogans interacted. (2) Show why Kornilov mattered more than the July Days to Bolshevik growth. (3) Assess the claim that October was a coup. (4) Explain how myth shaped memory of the Winter Palace.

Chapter Summary

This chapter traced the Bolsheviks' rise from a minority faction to the seizure of central power in October 1917 (O.S.). Lenin’s return and the April Theses reoriented strategy after contested debate within the party.

A network of committees, presses, and agitators gave the party capacity to circulate demands for peace, land, bread, and soviet authority. Trotsky and the Mezhraiontsy strengthened its leadership and its place in the Petrograd Soviet.

The July Days brought repression, yet the organization survived. The Kornilov Affair then restored the party’s legitimacy and weakened moderates, leading to majorities in the capital soviets in September.

Support was real but uneven, and it often meant support for soviet power, not necessarily for Bolshevik exclusivity. Lenin pressed for insurrection against objections from Kamenev and Zinoviev, while moderate socialists tried to build the Pre-Parliament.

The Military Revolutionary Committee became the operational tool. The seizure of Petrograd on 24–26 October (6–8 November N.S.) was swift and largely bloodless, and the Second Congress endorsed it amid a walkout that exposed the legitimacy problem.

Historians disagree over the terms revolution, insurrection, and coup, each emphasizing different features. Chapter 52 follows the consequences: the Constituent Assembly, Brest-Litovsk, and civil war, none of which were settled by October alone.

Chapter Reflection

The October seizure changed who held central power in Russia, but not at once what that power could do. The Bolsheviks inherited an exhausted army, a fractured economy, and a society in which peasants, national movements, and rival socialists pursued their own goals.

The chapter shows a path of contingency: a party that began as a minority gained influence through organization and crisis, and its leaders were divided until the last moment. Later Soviet memory simplified this, and later hostile accounts sometimes did the same in reverse.

The next chapter turns to the Constituent Assembly, the treaty with Germany, and the Civil War (Chapter 52), where the legitimacy question raised by October would be fought out in politics and war.
