Part XI — The Post-Stalin Soviet Union
66

National Revolutions, the August Coup, and the Collapse of the Soviet Union

A History of Russia: From the Eurasian World to the Russian Federation

Chapter Opening

On the evening of December 25, 1991, Mikhail Gorbachev told a television audience that he was ending his activity as President of the USSR. In his own judgment, a state he had set out to reform was being broken apart by decisions he could not accept. (A paraphrase of the sense of his farewell address, not a verbatim quotation.)

Shortly after 7:30 p.m. Moscow time on December 25, 1991, the red flag of the Soviet Union was lowered over the Kremlin and the white-blue-red tricolor of Russia was raised in its place. Minutes earlier, in a televised address of about twelve minutes, Mikhail Gorbachev had announced that he was resigning as President of the USSR, a post that had existed for less than two years. The ceremony was brief and the images were stark, and they have often been treated as the moment the Soviet Union "ended." Historians nonetheless treat the flag-lowering as a symbolic punctuation mark, not as a cause. The Union ended through a sequence of decisions made over about three and a half years in many different places and by many different institutions.

Those decisions were made in Tallinn, Riga, and Vilnius, where hundreds of thousands of people sang, marched, and joined hands to assert that their states had been annexed in 1940 and should be restored. They were made in Tbilisi, where troops killed demonstrators in April 1989, and in Baku and Stepanakert, where the dispute over Nagorno-Karabakh produced pogroms, deportations, and military intervention. They were made in Kyiv, Minsk, and Chisinau, in the Kuzbass and Donbas coal fields, in the Russian "White House" in Moscow in August 1991, in the Belarusian hunting lodge at Viskuli, and in the Kazakh capital Alma-Ata. This chapter follows those decisions and asks how they connected.

The central question is why efforts to reform and renegotiate the Soviet Union ended in the disappearance of the Union itself. Answering it requires distinguishing processes that are often run together: nationalist mobilization in the republics, the contest between the Russian republic's government and the Union government, the collapse of Communist Party authority, the attempt to negotiate a new Union Treaty, the failed coup of August 1991, and the legal and political acts of December 1991. These processes were related, but they were not the same, and they did not unfold on a single schedule. The chapter also distinguishes four ideas that were easily confused at the time and are still confused today: sovereignty, independence, institutional collapse, and constitutional dissolution.

The people who lived through these events experienced them very differently. Estonian, Latvian, and Lithuanian citizens often described what they were doing as the restoration of states that had been unlawfully occupied. Armenian and Azerbaijani families fled violence that targeted them because of their nationality. Georgian demonstrators, Ukrainian students, striking miners, Communist officials, army conscripts, Russian-speaking residents of the Baltic republics, and Muscovites who gathered around the Russian parliament building each saw a different crisis. The Soviet Union contained about 286 million people in 1989, and no single vantage point, least of all that of the Kremlin, can capture the whole.

Historians reconstruct these months from an unusually rich and unusually problematic body of evidence: constitutional laws and declarations, referendum results, presidential and parliamentary decrees, the diary of Gorbachev's adviser Anatoly Chernyaev, transcripts of meetings, newspapers and broadcasts, investigative records from the coup trial, and a large memoir literature. Memoirs written after the Union's collapse are shaped by their authors' need to explain their own roles. Official statements, whether from Moscow or the republics, were written to claim legitimacy. Archival access has been uneven and has narrowed in some respects since the 1990s. Casualty figures, vote counts, and the attribution of responsibility are therefore reported in this chapter with their sources, and uncertainty is marked where it exists.

The chapter also resists several tempting simplifications. The Soviet Union was not Russia, and its end was not simply a duel between Gorbachev and Boris Yeltsin. The collapse was not inevitable: until the late summer of 1991 serious politicians in Moscow and in most republics were negotiating a reconstituted Union, and the August coup was a violent attempt to stop that negotiation, not a plot to destroy the Union. Nor did the coup's failure make dissolution automatic, because the decisions that followed it, above all Ukraine's referendum of December 1 and the agreements of December 8 and 21, were consequential choices made by people who might have chosen otherwise.

This chapter closes Part XI. It builds on Chapter 64's discussion of nationality problems and the Afghan war and on Chapter 65's account of perestroika, glasnost, Chernobyl, and the revolutions of 1989, and it takes the 1922 formation of the USSR (Chapter 53), the Molotov-Ribbentrop Pact (Chapter 58), and the annexation of the Baltic states (Chapter 59) as essential background. It also places the event in a wider world: the end of the Cold War, the reunification of Germany, anxiety over thousands of nuclear warheads, and the international debate over which new states to recognize. The chapter ends on December 26, 1991. The economic and political reconstruction of the Russian Federation belongs to Chapter 67, which opens Part XII. All dates in this chapter are New Style, since the Julian calendar was abolished in 1918.

  3. Distinguish among sovereignty declarations, independence declarations, institutional collapse, constitutional dissolution, and international recognition.

  5. Trace Boris Yeltsin's path to the Russian chairmanship and presidency and evaluate the "war of laws" between the Russian and Union governments.
  6. Evaluate the March 17, 1991 referendum and the draft Union Treaty as evidence that a reconstituted Union was a genuine political alternative.
  7. Reconstruct the planning, execution, resistance, and collapse of the August 1991 coup, using documentary evidence.
  8. Assess competing explanations for the coup's failure and for the Communist Party's loss of governing authority afterward.
  9. Interpret the Ukrainian referendum of December 1, the Belavezha Accords, the Alma-Ata Declaration, and Gorbachev's resignation as separate decisions with different legal and political meaning.

  11. Connect the dissolution of the USSR to the beginning of the Russian Federation's history without treating later outcomes as predetermined.

Historical Context

The Soviet Union was formed in 1922 (Chapter 53) as a union of formally sovereign republics, and by 1989 it comprised fifteen union republics, twenty autonomous republics, eight autonomous oblasts, and ten autonomous okrugs. The 1977 Constitution gave each union republic its own constitution and legislature, a seat in the Soviet of Nationalities, and, in Article 72, the right to secede. These provisions coexisted with a centralized party-state. The Communist Party of the Soviet Union (CPSU) held a constitutional monopoly on political leadership under Article 6, and central ministries, the KGB, and the armed forces were organized on an all-Union basis. For decades the right of secession had no procedure and no practical meaning.

Soviet nationality policy had also built the foundations of national identity. Republics had their own capitals, academies, universities, writers' unions, and titular languages, and the internal passport recorded each citizen's nationality (Chapters 53 and 58). Local Communist elites typically came from the titular nationality. Soviet policy thus produced national territories, national elites, and national cultures while forbidding independent national politics. When glasnost and democratization after 1986 (Chapter 65) permitted public debate, these institutions and categories could be turned to political use.

The Baltic states were a special case. Estonia, Latvia, and Lithuania had been independent between the wars. They were incorporated into the USSR in 1940 under the terms of the Molotov-Ribbentrop Pact and its secret protocols (Chapters 58-59), and a large share of their populations had experienced deportation, wartime destruction, and Sovietization. Their continued attachment to the memory of statehood gave their movements a legal and moral argument unavailable elsewhere. In other republics, memories of famine (Chapter 56), repression, language policy, or the dismantling of historic autonomies supplied different grievances.

The political reforms of 1988-1990 reshaped the arena. The Congress of People's Deputies elected in spring 1989 was the first partially competitive national legislature in Soviet history. In March 1990 the Congress amended Article 6, ending the party's constitutional monopoly, and created an executive presidency for Gorbachev. Competitive elections to republican legislatures in the first half of 1990 gave those bodies a democratic mandate independent of the party hierarchy. Meanwhile the command economy was deteriorating, and the Chernobyl disaster of 1986 had damaged public trust in Moscow, especially in Ukraine and Belarus (Chapters 64-65).

Finally, the Russian republic was an unusual component of the Union. The RSFSR contained about half of the Soviet population and most of its resources, yet for most of Soviet history it lacked institutions of its own: until 1990 there was no Russian Communist Party organization, no Russian academy, and no Russian KGB. Union institutions in Moscow therefore stood in an ambiguous relationship to the Russian republic. When Russian leaders began to claim authority for the republic in 1990, they were not seceding from a foreign center so much as contesting who spoke for the Union's capital and its institutions.

Division I — The Baltic National Revolutions and the Restoration of Independence

Section 66.1

The Rise of Baltic National Movements, 1987–1989

In 1989 the Baltic republics differed sharply from the rest of the USSR in the composition of their populations and in their political memory. Lithuanians made up about four-fifths of Lithuania's population, but Latvians were only about half of Latvia's and Estonians about three-fifths of Estonia's, a result of postwar migration from other Soviet republics that Estonians and Latvians in particular perceived as a threat to their language and cultural survival. All three republics had living memories of independence between the wars, of the deportations of 1941 and 1949, and of the postwar suppression of armed resistance. Their living standards and educational levels were relatively high by Soviet standards, and their intelligentsias were closely connected to Scandinavia and the wider Baltic region.

Glasnost provided the opening (Chapter 65). In 1986 the "Helsinki-86" group in Liepāja, Latvia, protested Soviet human-rights violations, and in 1987 Estonian protests against planned phosphorite mining, framed as an environmental issue, gave a safe vehicle for broader criticism. On June 14, 1987, Latvians laid flowers at Riga's Freedom Monument to commemorate the 1941 deportations, and on August 23, 1987, the anniversary of the Molotov-Ribbentrop Pact, demonstrations took place in all three republics. In September 1987 Estonian economists proposed a plan for a self-managing Estonian economy that would operate on an independent financial basis within the USSR.

The organizational breakthrough came in 1988. The Estonian Popular Front (April), Sąjūdis in Lithuania (June, founded by an initiative group of intellectuals that included Vytautas Landsbergis), and the Latvian Popular Front (October) began as movements to support perestroika and rapidly acquired mass followings. Estonia's "Singing Revolution" gathered an estimated 300,000 people, a quarter of the Estonian population, at the Tallinn Song Festival Grounds in September 1988. The movements demanded language rights, environmental protection, historical truth, and republican control over the economy. Reform-minded Communists participated, and the republican Communist parties divided between those willing to cooperate with the movements and those who remained loyal to the CPSU leadership, with pro-Soviet organizations such as Interfront in Latvia and Intermovement in Estonia mobilizing parts of the Russian-speaking population.

The key shift was a change in what was being demanded. In November 1988 Estonia's Supreme Soviet declared the republic sovereign and claimed the right to veto Union laws, and Moscow's Presidium annulled the declaration within days. During 1989 the republics passed language laws, and the movements' programs moved from autonomy toward independence. By the end of 1989, in a development examined in §66.3, the Lithuanian Communist Party itself had declared independence from the CPSU. The pace of change was set partly by Moscow's refusal to define a lawful path for greater autonomy and partly by the movements' discovery, after the Soviet leadership repeatedly declined to use force against the Baltic movements in 1988-1989 (in contrast to its use of troops in Tbilisi in April 1989, discussed in §66.8), that mass peaceful mobilization worked.

Section 66.2

Historical Memory, the Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact, and the Baltic Way

The central historical argument of the Baltic movements concerned 1939-1940. On August 23, 1939, the Soviet Union and Nazi Germany signed a non-aggression pact accompanied by a secret protocol dividing Eastern Europe into spheres of influence; Estonia, Latvia, and Finland were assigned to the Soviet sphere and, after a revision in September 1939, Lithuania as well (Chapter 58). In autumn 1939 the Soviet Union compelled the Baltic states to accept mutual-assistance treaties and military bases. In June 1940 it issued ultimatums and occupied all three countries, organized elections in July with single approved lists of candidates, and in August 1940 admitted them to the USSR as union republics. The United States and some other governments declared that they did not recognize the annexation.

Until 1988-1989 the official Soviet position was that the Baltic peoples had voluntarily joined the USSR and that no secret protocols existed. Baltic activists, along with historians who obtained evidence in the West, challenged both claims. Pressed by the movements, the Soviet Congress of People's Deputies established a commission headed by Alexander Yakovlev, and on December 24, 1989, the Congress condemned the secret protocols and declared them legally invalid from the moment of signing. The resolution did not, however, accept that the 1940 annexation was illegal, and the question of what legal consequences followed from the protocols' invalidity became a dispute between Moscow and the Baltic governments.

The movements marked the anniversary of the pact on August 23, 1989, with the Baltic Way. An estimated two million people joined hands in a human chain of about 675 kilometers from Tallinn through Riga to Vilnius. The event, organized by the Popular Fronts and Sąjūdis, was disciplined, nonviolent, and widely televised abroad; it linked the memory of 1939-1940 explicitly to the demand for political change. The CPSU Central Committee denounced the movements' actions in a statement issued days later, warning that they were leading the Baltic peoples toward catastrophe.

Memory in the Baltic region was not simple. The wartime years included Nazi occupation, the murder of the region's Jewish communities, and varying degrees of collaboration and resistance, and some Russian-speaking residents associated the end of the war with liberation from fascism. The Baltic movements' emphasis on the Soviet occupation, and their silence about other aspects of that history, would continue to be debated in the 1990s and afterward. As evidence about 1939-1940, the movements' claims rest on documents and on the broad scholarly consensus that the incorporation was carried out under military pressure and without free choice. How best to describe it in legal and political terminology has remained disputed; Russian officials have at times rejected the term "occupation," while Baltic governments and most Western governments use it.

Section 66.3

Lithuania's Declaration of Restored Independence, March 1990

Lithuania's path was shaped by Sąjūdis's mass base, the early split in the republic's Communist Party, and the republic's relative ethnic homogeneity. In December 1989 the Lithuanian Communist Party, led by Algirdas Brazauskas, declared its independence from the CPSU, and Gorbachev traveled to Vilnius in January 1990 in an unsuccessful attempt to dissuade the party and the public from separation. In multiparty elections held in February and March 1990 candidates supported by Sąjūdis won a clear majority in the republic's Supreme Council.

On March 11, 1990, the new Supreme Council elected Vytautas Landsbergis as its chairman and adopted the Act on the Re-Establishment of the State of Lithuania by a vote of 124 in favor, none against, and 6 abstentions. The act declared that the Act of the People's Seimas of July 1940, which had requested admission to the USSR, was void, and that Lithuania was again an independent state. It rested on the argument that the interwar Lithuanian state had never legally ceased to exist and that Soviet rule since 1940 was an illegal occupation. Because of this argument, Lithuanian leaders maintained that they were restoring statehood, not seceding from the USSR under the Union's constitutional procedures.

Moscow rejected the declaration as unconstitutional. On March 15 the Congress of People's Deputies declared the act invalid, and in the following weeks the Soviet leadership combined pressure with negotiation. Soviet paratroopers took control of party and other buildings in Vilnius, military aircraft and columns made demonstrative appearances, and the Soviet Union cut off deliveries of oil and natural gas to Lithuania beginning in April, an economic blockade that lasted until the end of June. On April 3 the Union had adopted a secession law establishing a multistage process requiring a referendum, a two-thirds majority, and a transition period of up to five years, which Lithuanian leaders regarded as irrelevant because they considered the 1940 annexation legally void.

The standoff ended in June 1990 when Lithuania's Supreme Council agreed to suspend implementation of the declaration during negotiations, and the blockade was lifted. Lithuania's declaration was not recognized by other states at the time. Western governments, focused on German unification and the future of Soviet-American cooperation, offered sympathy but avoided formal recognition, and the Soviet leadership maintained that the act had no legal force. Nevertheless, the declaration had established a precedent: a republic had publicly renounced the Union's authority, and the Union had been unable to reverse that step without large-scale force.

It treats the 1940 acts by which Lithuania was admitted to the USSR as lacking legal force.

Analysis: The text's central claim is continuity: the act does not describe a new state but the end of an interruption. That framing explains why Lithuanian leaders refused to treat the USSR's secession law as controlling. The document represents the view of the majority of the Supreme Council and of Sąjūdis; it does not represent the Lithuanian Communist Party members who opposed it, Soviet military and party officials in the republic, or the substantial Polish-speaking and Russian-speaking communities that held diverse positions. It cannot by itself show whether Lithuania's population shared the legislature's view, although the nearly unanimous vote and the later referendum of February 1991 are evidence of broad support.

Historical Debate

Restoration of Statehood or Secession from the USSR?

The question: Did the Baltic republics leave the Union by exercising a constitutional right to secede, or did they restore states whose annexation had been unlawful?

Interpretation A: Restoration: The Baltic governments argued that the 1940 incorporation was imposed by military force and was therefore invalid, so the Union's constitutional procedures for secession did not apply. Western non-recognition policy supported the premise that annexation had not been lawfully accepted.
Interpretation B: Constitutional secession: Soviet officials argued that Lithuania, Estonia, and Latvia were union republics with constitutional rights and obligations and that any exit had to follow the Union's laws, including the 1990 secession procedure. In this view the declarations were unilateral acts.
Interpretation C: Political process: Some scholars emphasize that the legal arguments mattered less than the balance of political power. The Baltic republics' claims succeeded when the Soviet leadership proved unwilling or unable to use sustained coercion and when the failed coup of August 1991 destroyed the Union's authority.

Evidence: Historians use the text of the 1939-1940 agreements and protocols, the resolutions of the Soviet Congress (December 1989), the republican declarations, Western governments' nonrecognition statements, and records of negotiations between Moscow and the republics.

Points of agreement: Scholars broadly agree that the secret protocols existed and that the 1940 incorporation was conducted under military pressure and without a free choice by the Baltic peoples.

Points of disagreement: Interpretations differ over the proper legal terminology and the legal consequences of annexation, and over how much weight to give legal arguments in explaining the outcome.

Why it matters: The framing determined who held legal title to the republics' territory, shaped later disputes about citizenship and the status of Russian-speaking residents, and influenced foreign governments' recognition decisions.

Section 66.4

Estonia and Latvia: Sovereignty, Transitional Governments, and Independence

Estonia and Latvia followed Lithuania but adopted a more gradual legal strategy. Both republics held elections to their Supreme Councils in March 1990 in which candidates supported by the Popular Fronts won large majorities, although the Latvian vote included strong support for pro-Soviet candidates in heavily Russian-speaking districts. Estonia also held a parallel election in early 1990 for a Congress of Estonia, composed of citizens of the interwar republic and their descendants, reflecting a movement that insisted on legal continuity with the pre-1940 state.

On March 30, 1990, Estonia's Supreme Council declared Soviet power in Estonia illegal since 1940 and announced the beginning of a transitional period toward restoration of the Republic of Estonia. On May 4, 1990, Latvia's Supreme Council adopted a declaration on the restoration of independence that also provided for a transitional period, passing with only a narrow margin over the required two-thirds, which showed how divided the republic remained. Both declarations restored the legal continuity of the interwar states but postponed their full implementation to a period of negotiation with Moscow, during which Soviet laws could continue to apply unless they contradicted the republics' constitutions.

The distinction between a transitional government and completed independence mattered. During 1990 and the first half of 1991 the three Baltic governments (those of Prime Minister Edgar Savisaar in Estonia and Ivars Godmanis in Latvia, along with Landsbergis's Lithuania) controlled the republican legislatures and administration, but Soviet army and security units remained in the republics, the central government controlled borders, currency, and key enterprises, and Moscow refused to negotiate on the basis that the republics were already independent.

Citizenship was an especially difficult question in Estonia and Latvia. Both movements advocated, and in 1989-1991 publicly debated, the principle that citizenship should be restored to those who had been citizens in 1940 and their descendants, which would leave a large share of the Russian-speaking residents who had arrived after 1945 to apply for naturalization. The formal decisions came largely after independence was restored: Latvia's Supreme Council adopted its core citizenship resolution on October 15, 1991, and Estonia's citizenship legislation followed in 1992. Defenders of the policy said it applied the continuity of the interwar state and protected the titular nations; critics, including some Russian-speaking residents and international observers, regarded the policy as potentially exclusionary. Russian speakers were not uniformly opposed to independence, and many supported the Popular Fronts. These issues, considered in the post-Soviet period, began in the debates of 1989-1991.

The mechanics of restoration differed by republic, and those differences shaped what "transitional" meant. In Estonia the Supreme Council elected in March 1990 coexisted with the Congress of Estonia, elected by citizens of the interwar republic and their descendants, which argued that only a restored citizenry could legitimately re-found the state; a parallel citizens' committee movement and a Congress of Latvia emerged in Latvia in 1990. The Popular Fronts favored negotiation and cooperation with the existing legislatures, whereas the citizens' committees stressed legal continuity and rejected Soviet institutions as illegitimate. The two approaches sometimes clashed over tactics but pursued the same end. Latvia's May 1990 declaration restored the 1922 Constitution in principle while suspending much of its text during the transition. Moscow rejected both republics' declarations as unconstitutional.

Residents who were not of the titular nationality formed a large part of the story: in 1989 roughly two-fifths of Estonia's population and almost half of Latvia's were not Estonian or Latvian. Their political positions varied. Some supported the Popular Fronts and independence, some joined Intermovement or Interfront and defended the Union, and many were uncertain about their future status. Because the March 1991 referendums, open to permanent residents, produced majorities of roughly three-quarters in favor of independence while Estonians and Latvians were about 62 and 52 percent of the population, a significant number of residents of other nationalities must have voted yes, although the data do not permit a precise breakdown. The citizenship rules examined above were therefore not settled during the movements' campaigns but were decided in the months after independence was restored, and they became one of the most contested legacies of the period.

Section 66.5

The January 1991 Baltic Crisis: Vilnius and Riga

In the winter of 1990-1991 Soviet policy shifted toward coercion. Eduard Shevardnadze resigned as foreign minister on December 20, 1990, warning of a coming dictatorship, and conservative officials gained influence in Moscow (see §66.18). In Lithuania, the government's price increases on January 8, 1991 provoked demonstrations, and a pro-Moscow group of Communists formed a "National Salvation Committee" that claimed to be taking power. Soviet units seized buildings in Vilnius between January 11 and 13.

On the night of January 12-13, 1991, Soviet paratroopers and KGB special forces stormed the Vilnius television tower and the Radio and Television Committee building while crowds surrounded the structures. Lithuanian authorities document 14 civilian deaths, caused by gunfire and by armored vehicles, and several hundred injuries; a Soviet special-forces officer also died. Landsbergis called on residents to defend the Supreme Council building, and thousands formed barricades around it. Soviet forces did not attack the parliament.

On January 20, Soviet Interior Ministry OMON units attacked the Latvian Interior Ministry in Riga, killing five people, including two filmmakers; after the Vilnius violence, Latvians had erected barricades around key buildings. Boris Yeltsin, who traveled to Tallinn on January 12-13, signed statements with the Baltic leaders recognizing each other's sovereignty and appealed to Russian soldiers not to take part in actions against civilians. Tens of thousands demonstrated in Moscow against the use of force.

Responsibility remains contested. Gorbachev said he had not been informed in advance and learned of events after the fact, while Defense Minister Dmitry Yazov, Interior Minister Boris Pugo, and KGB chairman Vladimir Kryuchkov defended the actions as a response to requests from the republic's pro-Moscow committee. Historians disagree over whether Gorbachev approved, tolerated, or lost control of the operation, and Lithuanian courts have in later decades tried former Soviet officers in absentia, proceedings Russia rejects. The consequences were clear. The violence failed to restore central control, increased public support for independence (Lithuania's referendum of February 9, 1991 produced an approximately 90 percent majority among participants for independence, and Estonia and Latvia held referendums on March 3 with majorities of about three-quarters), and damaged Gorbachev's standing with democratic reformers. Violence continued: on July 31, 1991, OMON forces attacked a Lithuanian border post at Medininkai and killed seven officials.

Division II — Nationality Conflicts and the Crisis of Soviet Federalism

Section 66.6

The Soviet Federal System and the Contradictions of National Self-Determination

The Soviet Union's constitutional design contained a contradiction that democratization exposed. Formally, it was a voluntary union of sovereign republics, and the 1977 Constitution guaranteed each union republic the right to secede. In practice, power was exercised by a single party organized on a hierarchical, centralized basis, and by all-Union ministries, security services, and the army. Republican institutions administered, but did not decide; republican Communist first secretaries were appointed with the approval of Moscow. In this arrangement the right of secession was a formal guarantee with no procedure for exercising it.

The federal structure was also layered. Within the fifteen union republics, twenty autonomous republics, eight autonomous oblasts, and ten autonomous okrugs were designated for smaller nationalities; sixteen of the autonomous republics lay within the RSFSR. This hierarchy meant that a nationality could be a titular nation of a union republic and, at the same time, a minority within another republic's autonomous unit. Abkhaz and South Ossetians in Georgia, Gagauz and Transnistrians in Moldova, Armenians in Nagorno-Karabakh, and Tatars and Chechens in the Russian republic each lived in such nested arrangements. When a union republic claimed sovereignty, the autonomous units within it could claim a similar status against it, with Moscow as a potential ally.

Democratization changed the relationship between these institutions. The competitive elections of 1989 and 1990 gave republican legislatures legitimacy derived from voters instead of party appointment, while the March 1990 amendment of Article 6 ended the party's monopoly. The "parade of sovereignties" that followed, discussed in §66.12, was a use of the Soviet system's own formal language. Moscow's April 3, 1990 Law on Secession, which required a referendum, a two-thirds majority, and a transition period of up to five years, and which let autonomous units within a seceding republic vote separately, was a defensive response and a source of leverage over the republics most determined to leave.

These constitutional contradictions did not determine the outcome. The same structure that gave republics legal standing also gave the Union government real tools: the army, the security services, control of finances and supply, and the ability to appeal to autonomous units. Whether the Union would be reconstituted, repressed, or dissolved depended on political choices over which the system's design offered no guidance. The following sections show that the goals of the various movements differed widely, from restoration of independence to territorial change, language rights, or a renewed place within the Union.

Section 66.7

Armenia, Azerbaijan, and Nagorno-Karabakh, 1988–1991

Nagorno-Karabakh was an autonomous oblast within the Azerbaijan SSR whose population, about three-quarters Armenian in 1979, had been assigned to Azerbaijan by Soviet decisions of the 1920s. Armenians had long raised complaints about cultural and economic neglect and, in the glasnost atmosphere of 1987-1988, circulated petitions seeking transfer to Armenia. On February 20, 1988, the oblast's soviet voted to request the transfer. Moscow refused to change the boundaries, which Azerbaijanis regarded as an assault on the republic's territorial integrity, and which Soviet leaders feared would set a precedent for all internal borders.

On February 27-29, 1988, in the industrial city of Sumgait near Baku, mobs attacked Armenian residents. Soviet prosecutors reported 32 dead (26 Armenians and 6 Azerbaijanis); Armenian sources believe the number of victims was higher. Troops arrived late, and the attacks accelerated the flight of Armenians from Azerbaijan and, later in 1988, of Azerbaijanis from Armenia. The December 1988 Spitak earthquake devastated northern Armenia, and the arrest of the "Karabakh Committee" activists, including Levon Ter-Petrosyan, in December 1988 further radicalized Armenian politics. Hundreds of thousands of Armenians and Azerbaijanis were eventually displaced.

The conflict escalated in 1989-1990. An Azerbaijani Popular Front blockade of Armenian rail traffic began in the summer of 1989, and in December Armenia's Supreme Soviet and the Karabakh soviet issued a declaration of unification. In January 1990 violence against Armenians in Baku killed dozens of people (Soviet official figures; other sources give higher numbers). On January 19-20, Soviet troops entered Baku in force, killing more than a hundred civilians by most accounts, in an operation Moscow justified as protecting the republic from nationalist takeover and many Azerbaijanis remember as "Black January." In spring 1991 Soviet army and interior forces, together with Azerbaijani police, conducted "Operation Ring," deporting Armenian villagers from several communities in and around Karabakh.

By late 1991 the dispute had become an armed conflict between two republics that were themselves leaving the Union. Armenia's government under Ter-Petrosyan, elected chairman of the Supreme Council in 1990, held an independence referendum on September 21, 1991; Azerbaijan, under Ayaz Mutalibov, declared the restoration of independence on August 30. On September 2, Karabakh Armenian leaders proclaimed a republic, Azerbaijan abolished the oblast's autonomy in November, and a referendum on December 10 was boycotted by the Azerbaijani population. Full-scale war followed in 1992. The conflict demonstrated that the Soviet state could neither resolve a territorial dispute between republics nor prevent its escalation, and that its coercive interventions deepened mistrust without settling the underlying questions.

Section 66.8

Georgia: Independence Movements, the Tbilisi Tragedy, and Territorial Tensions

Georgia's national movement developed in a republic where Georgians made up about 70 percent of the population and where two autonomous territories had their own claims: Abkhazia, an autonomous republic on the Black Sea, and South Ossetia, an autonomous oblast. In March 1989 an Abkhaz assembly at Lykhny asked Moscow to restore the republic's status as a union republic, which triggered large Georgian rallies in Tbilisi in April and violent clashes in Sukhumi in July. South Ossetian activists in turn formed Adamon Nykhas, a popular front, and in November asked for upgraded status.

On April 9, 1989, Soviet troops attacked the demonstrators gathered at Government House in Tbilisi, using sharpened entrenching tools and, according to medical investigators, a toxic chemical agent. Roughly twenty people died, most of them young women, and hundreds were injured. Gorbachev was abroad at the time, and a Soviet commission found that local officials had requested troops but also criticized the military leadership; responsibility for the order remained disputed. The event discredited the Georgian Communist leadership and, in the memory of many Georgians, turned the movement toward independence as a response to coercion.

In October 1990 the "Round Table-Free Georgia" coalition led by Zviad Gamsakhurdia won the republic's Supreme Council elections and Gamsakhurdia became its chairman. His government refused to take part in the March 17, 1991 Union referendum. On March 31 a Georgian referendum recorded turnout of approximately 90.6 percent and support of about 99 percent among participants for restoring independence, and on April 9, 1991, the second anniversary of the Tbilisi killings, the Supreme Council proclaimed the restoration of the independence that had been lost in 1921. Gamsakhurdia was elected president in May. His rhetoric toward minorities and his style of rule alienated many in Georgia, including democratic opponents, as well as Abkhaz and Ossetian leaders.

Conflict with South Ossetia escalated sharply. The South Ossetian soviet proclaimed a republic in September 1990, and the Georgian Supreme Council abolished the oblast's autonomy in December. Fighting began in Tskhinvali in January 1991, with casualties and displacement on both sides. Authorities in Abkhazia and South Ossetia encouraged participation in the Union referendum that Georgia boycotted, and they supported Union institutions as a counterweight to Tbilisi. These local conflicts, whose causes involved history, demography, and political incentives, would become major disputes after 1991.

The Georgian political landscape in 1990-1991 included more than Gamsakhurdia's coalition. The October 1990 elections gave Round Table-Free Georgia a clear majority and placed the Communist Party in opposition, but the new government's confrontational stance toward Moscow and toward the autonomous regions, and Gamsakhurdia's increasingly personal style of rule, alienated many former allies, including intellectuals and members of the National Guard. Armed opposition to the president grew through the autumn, and in late December 1991 fighting broke out in Tbilisi that forced him to leave the capital in January 1992. Georgia thus entered independence with a political crisis that also left the Abkhaz and South Ossetian questions unresolved, and its government did not take part in the Commonwealth negotiations of December 1991.

Section 66.9

Moldova, Transnistria, and Competing National Projects

Moldova's population of about 4.3 million in 1989 was roughly 64 percent Moldovan, with substantial Ukrainian, Russian, Gagauz, and Bulgarian minorities. The republic had been formed in 1940 from Bessarabia, annexed from Romania under the Molotov-Ribbentrop Pact, and the left-bank Transnistrian region, which had been part of an autonomous Soviet republic in the interwar period and had never belonged to Romania. The question of whether the majority language was Moldovan or Romanian, and whether Moldova's identity was distinct or part of a Romanian nation, was itself contested.

In 1989 the Popular Front of Moldova organized mass rallies demanding language rights, and on August 31, 1989 the Supreme Soviet adopted laws making Moldovan the state language, written in the Latin alphabet, which in practice moved the language closer to Romanian. Russian-speaking workers, particularly in Transnistria's industrial centers, responded with strikes and organized opposition. In 1990 the republic adopted a tricolor flag, declared sovereignty on June 23, and ended the Soviet-style name; some Popular Front activists called for unification with Romania.

Minority regions announced their own autonomy. Gagauz leaders, a Turkic-speaking Orthodox population in the south, proclaimed a Gagauz Republic in August 1990. Transnistrian leaders, including Igor Smirnov, proclaimed a Dniester Moldavian Republic on September 2, and violence at Dubăsari in November caused the first deaths. Moldova's government boycotted the March 1991 referendum while the Transnistrian and Gagauz authorities organized voting, and Moldova declared independence on August 27, 1991, following the failed coup. Full-scale fighting between Moldovan forces and Transnistrian forces, backed by units of the former Soviet 14th Army, took place in 1992 and is beyond the scope of this chapter.

The Moldovan government's position shifted during 1991. President Mircea Snegur, elected by the legislature in December 1990, distanced himself from the Popular Front's demands for union with Romania and emphasized an independent Moldovan state, and the August 27 declaration of independence defined the republic within its Soviet-era borders, which the Transnistrian and Gagauz leaders rejected. The result was a state whose majority identified in several ways, as Moldovan, Romanian, or both, and whose territory was claimed by two separatist movements that regarded the Union, and later Russia, as a potential protector.

Regional Context

Moldova, Transnistria, and Gagauzia

The Moldovan case shows how the same disputes could be seen from three vantage points. For Moldovan nationalists, the revival of the Latin alphabet and the Romanian-language heritage was a reversal of Sovietization; for Russian- and Ukrainian-speaking communities in the industrial left bank, the language laws and the prospect of union with Romania threatened their status and their ties to the Union's economy; for Gagauz, a small group with its own language and Orthodox faith, the question was whether they would be protected in a Moldovan state. Section 66.10

Central Asia: Political Continuity, Ethnic Violence, and Divergent Responses to Soviet Decline

Central Asian republics followed a different path. Their leaders were Communist officials who had come through the party hierarchy, their economies relied on Union subsidies and on cotton and raw-material exports, and the movements opposing them were weaker or more local. Nursultan Nazarbayev in Kazakhstan, Islam Karimov in Uzbekistan, and Saparmurat Niyazov in Turkmenistan retained and expanded their powers by winning presidencies in 1990. In Kyrgyzstan, physicist Askar Akayev was elected president in October 1990. Tajikistan's Communist leadership was under greater pressure from opposition groups.

The region was not calm. In June 1989 violence in the Fergana Valley of Uzbekistan between Uzbeks and Meskhetian Turks, who had been deported from Georgia in 1944 (Chapter 58), left on the order of a hundred people dead and tens of thousands displaced. In June 1990 a dispute over land and housing in and around Osh in Kyrgyzstan escalated into fighting between Kyrgyz and Uzbek communities, with official counts of several hundred dead and unofficial counts higher. In February 1990 rioting in Dushanbe, Tajikistan, set off by rumors about housing for refugees, left dozens dead. Demographic pressures, land shortages, and unemployment, along with Soviet administrative boundaries drawn in the 1920s and 1930s, contributed to these conflicts.

Language and culture were also political issues: Uzbekistan and Kazakhstan adopted language laws in 1989, and popular movements such as Birlik in Uzbekistan and the anti-nuclear movement in Kazakhstan, which led to the closure of the Semipalatinsk test site in August 1991, challenged the authorities. The Russian population of Kazakhstan, about 38 percent in 1989, was nearly equal to the Kazakh population, and Nazarbayev was careful not to alienate it.

Central Asian governments were among the strongest supporters of a renewed Union. All five republics took part in the March 1991 referendum, with majorities above 90 percent, and their leaders joined the Nine Plus One negotiations. Their preference was linked to economic dependence and to the interests of existing elites, who feared the instability of immediate independence. When the Union collapsed in the second half of 1991, they became independent states largely as a consequence of decisions made by others, and Kazakhstan was the last republic to declare independence, on December 16.

The Central Asian republics followed distinct political paths. In Kazakhstan, Nursultan Nazarbayev became first secretary in June 1989 after the unrest of December 1986 had followed the imposition of an outside party leader. He combined Communist institutions with a cautious accommodation of Kazakh national demands and Russian-speaking residents, and he supported the anti-nuclear movement that led to the closure of the Semipalatinsk test site. In Uzbekistan, Islam Karimov, who became first secretary in 1989 after investigations of corruption in the cotton sector had disrupted the republic's leadership, restricted the Birlik movement and was elected president by the Supreme Soviet in March 1990. In Kyrgyzstan, a deadlock between rival Communist candidates in October 1990 led to the election of the physicist Askar Akayev, who was open to democratic movements. In Tajikistan, where opposition groups, including nationalist and Islamic movements, were stronger, the Communist leader supported the August coup and resigned afterward. In Turkmenistan, Saparmurat Niyazov faced little organized opposition and later secured approval for independence in a referendum on October 27.

These differences were consistent with a general pattern: Central Asian leaders valued the Union's subsidies and markets, feared instability, and had less reason than Baltic or Caucasian elites to seek a break. They were nonetheless active participants in the negotiations over the Union Treaty, and Nazarbayev in particular played a mediating role between Gorbachev and Yeltsin. When the Union collapsed, these republics' leaders sought to ensure that they would be treated as equal founders of whatever succeeded it, as the Ashgabat meeting of December 13 and the Alma-Ata summit demonstrated.

Division III — Russia, Ukraine, Belarus, and the Crisis of Communist Authority

Section 66.11

Ukraine's National Movement and Declaration of Sovereignty, 1989–1990

Ukraine, with about 52 million people, the second most populous republic and a major agricultural and industrial center, had a political landscape divided by region and history. The western oblasts, which had been incorporated into the USSR only in 1939-1945 (Chapter 59), had strong Ukrainian-language traditions and a Greek Catholic heritage; the east and south were more urbanized, industrialized, and Russian-speaking; Crimea was Russian-majority. Ukrainian memory of the 1932-1933 famine (Chapter 56), of Stalinist repression, and of the Chernobyl disaster of 1986 (Chapter 65) became the subject of public debate.

The People's Movement of Ukraine (Rukh), organized in 1989 by writers and intellectuals, held its founding congress in September 1989. It brought together cultural figures like Ivan Drach, former political prisoners such as Vyacheslav Chornovil, and reformers. In October 1989 the Supreme Soviet made Ukrainian the state language, and in January 1990 hundreds of thousands formed a human chain from Lviv to Kyiv. The Ukrainian Greek Catholic Church emerged from the underground and the Autocephalous Orthodox Church was revived. In the March 1990 elections the Democratic Bloc won roughly a quarter of the seats, mostly from the west and from Kyiv, while a large Communist majority remained in the Supreme Soviet.

On July 16, 1990, the Supreme Soviet, by an overwhelming majority that included many Communist deputies, adopted the Declaration of State Sovereignty of Ukraine. It proclaimed the supremacy of Ukrainian law on Ukrainian territory, the republic's right to its own armed forces, and Ukraine's intention to be a permanently neutral and non-nuclear state. It did not declare independence, and it was accompanied by statements supporting a renegotiated Union. Leonid Kravchuk, who had been the party's ideology secretary and was elected chairman of the Supreme Soviet on July 23, moved between the Communist establishment and the national democrats, a position that would prove decisive in 1991. In October 1990 a student hunger strike in Kyiv forced the resignation of the prime minister.

Regional differences shaped the politics of the period. Donbas miners struck in 1989 and 1991 mainly for economic and social demands, not national ones, and Crimeans voted in January 1991 to restore the peninsula's status as an autonomous republic within Ukraine. Ukrainian political forces thus did not move along a single track toward independence; the leadership's decision in August 1991 was a response to the coup and its aftermath.

Historical Vocabulary
Sovereignty, Independence, and Self-Determination

Definition: Sovereignty is the claim to supreme legal authority within a territory. Independence is the status of being a separate state that is not legally subordinate to another. Self-determination is the principle that a people may decide its own political status, which can mean autonomy, federation, or separation.

Historical context: In 1990 most Soviet republics declared sovereignty while remaining in the USSR; those declarations asserted the supremacy of republican law but expected a renegotiated Union. The Baltic republics instead claimed the restoration of independence. After August 1991 many sovereignty declarations were converted into independence acts.

Why it matters: Treating the sovereignty declarations of 1990 as independence declarations makes the later course of events look inevitable and obscures that many leaders expected a reformed federation.

Section 66.12

Belarus and the Wider Parade of Sovereignties

Belarus offers a contrast to Ukraine. The Belarusian Popular Front "Adradzhenne," led by Zianon Pazniak, gained attention through revelations about the mass graves at Kurapaty near Minsk, where victims of Stalin-era executions lay, and through the issue of Chernobyl, which contaminated a large portion of Belarusian territory. The Front, whose founding congress took place in Vilnius in June 1989 after Minsk's authorities had refused it permission, held a minority of seats after the March 1990 elections, and established Communist officials led the Supreme Soviet. A language law in January 1990 made Belarusian the state language. Belarus declared sovereignty on July 27, 1990, but its leadership favored a renewed Union.

Belarus's declaration was part of a wave. Between November 1988 and December 1990 almost every republic adopted a sovereignty declaration: Estonia (November 1988), Lithuania (May 1989), Latvia (July 1989), Azerbaijan (September 1989), Russia (June 12, 1990), Uzbekistan (June 20), Moldova (June 23), Ukraine (July 16), Belarus (July 27), Turkmenistan (August 22), Armenia (August 23), Tajikistan (August 24), Kazakhstan (October 25), Georgia (November 1990), and Kyrgyzstan (December 15). Autonomous republics within the RSFSR also joined: Tatarstan declared sovereignty on August 30, 1990, and other units followed. During a visit to Tatarstan in August 1990, Yeltsin is widely reported to have told regional leaders to take as much sovereignty as they could handle, an invitation that strengthened the Russian republic's position against the Union but also raised questions about Russia's own territorial integrity.

The declarations varied in meaning. Some, like Lithuania's, were claims to restore independence; others claimed sovereignty within a Union; and some, particularly those of autonomous republics, were bargaining positions in negotiations with their parent republics. Taken together, they hollowed out the Union's authority because each republic's legislature began to assert that its laws overrode Union laws. The effect was to give every republic a vote in the Union's future, which made a negotiated settlement more difficult and more necessary.

Section 66.13

Boris Yeltsin's Political Comeback and the Rise of Russian Republican Power

Boris Yeltsin, born in 1931 in the Sverdlovsk region, rose through the party hierarchy and in 1985 was brought to Moscow as head of the city's party organization. In October 1987 he criticized the slow pace of reform and the party leadership at a Central Committee plenum and was removed from his post in November. His political future seemed over, but in March 1989 he won election to the Congress of People's Deputies from a Moscow district with about 89 percent of the vote, and he became a co-chairman of the reformist Interregional Deputies Group alongside Andrei Sakharov and others.

The Russian republic's elections of March 1990 produced a legislature in which a bloc called Democratic Russia held a substantial minority of seats while Communists held a large share. On May 29, 1990, after several rounds of voting, the Russian Congress of People's Deputies elected Yeltsin chairman of the Russian Supreme Soviet with 535 votes, only a few above the required majority, defeating Gorbachev's candidate, Prime Minister Alexander Vlasov. The narrowness of the margin shows how contingent the result was. Yeltsin's platform combined Russian sovereignty, market reform, and an attack on the privileges of the party apparatus. His allies included Ruslan Khasbulatov, Alexander Rutskoi, and Gennady Burbulis.

The significance of this election lay in the Russian republic's position. About 51 percent of Soviet citizens and a majority of industrial output and resources were located in the RSFSR, yet the republic had never had its own governing institutions, and a Russian Communist Party was formed only in June 1990. Russian political debate included multiple views, from democrats who saw Russian sovereignty as a means of freeing the republic from the Union's party-state, to nationalists who believed Russia had been exploited by the other republics, to conservatives who wanted to preserve the Union under a strong center. In July 1990, at the party's 28th Congress, Yeltsin dramatically resigned from the CPSU. He was becoming the leader of a competing source of authority in Moscow.

Section 66.14

Russia's Declaration of State Sovereignty, June 12, 1990

On June 12, 1990, the Russian Congress of People's Deputies adopted the Declaration of State Sovereignty of the RSFSR by a vote of 907 to 13, with 9 abstentions. The declaration proclaimed the sovereignty of the RSFSR, asserted the supremacy of Russian constitution and laws on the territory of the republic, and declared Russian ownership of the republic's land and natural resources. It adopted the principle of separation of powers and stated that relations with the USSR and the other republics would be built on a treaty basis.

The declaration was not a declaration of independence, and contemporaries understood the distinction. Many deputies, including those who voted for it, expected the RSFSR to remain in a renewed Union, and Yeltsin presented the text as a way of strengthening Russia within that Union. Yet the principle that Russian laws overrode Union laws, whatever its intent, challenged the system in which the Union's authority overlay all republics. In December 1990 the Russian Congress amended the republic's constitution to reflect the principle of priority for Russian law.

The declaration also faced the reality of the Russian federation's own diversity. Autonomous republics such as Tatarstan, Bashkortostan, and Chechen-Ingushetia soon made their own claims to sovereignty, which Moscow and the Russian leadership had to address in their negotiations. The date of the declaration, June 12, was later established as a Russian public holiday, often popularly called Independence Day though officially the day of adoption of the Declaration of State Sovereignty, and, since 2002, Russia Day. This shows how the memory of the declaration changed after 1991: it is remembered as the origin of the Russian Federation's statehood, although it did not itself create an independent state.

Section 66.15

The War of Laws: Competing Russian and Union Governments

After June 1990 the Russian and Union governments contested their respective powers through legislation, a conflict that Russian commentators called the "war of laws." The Russian Supreme Soviet passed laws on property, banking, taxation, and natural resources, while the Union Supreme Soviet and cabinet issued their own. Russia claimed the right to collect taxes on its territory and to remit only part to the Union budget; the Union government insisted that its fiscal and monetary authority was essential to prevent economic collapse. The two sides also created parallel institutions: in 1991 Russia established its own KGB, radio and television, and bank, in a pattern the Union leadership regarded as a challenge.

The clash was visible in the question of economic reform. In the summer of 1990 an economic team associated with Stanislav Shatalin and Grigory Yavlinsky prepared a "500 Days" program of rapid transition to a market economy based on a transfer of economic powers to the republics. The Russian Supreme Soviet endorsed it in September, but Gorbachev, under pressure from conservatives and from the cabinet of Prime Minister Nikolai Ryzhkov, chose a more gradual compromise. The decision strengthened the perception in Russia that the center blocked reform.

The conflict also involved coercive questions. In July 1991 Yeltsin issued a decree banning party organizations in state bodies, enterprises, and institutions on Russian territory, an action that threatened the CPSU's basic structure and angered conservatives. In the winter and spring of 1991, as striking miners in the Kuzbass and elsewhere demanded Gorbachev's resignation and Russian-republic status for their enterprises, Yeltsin supported the strikers.

The legal uncertainty had a corrosive effect. Enterprise directors, regional officials, and ordinary citizens did not know which authority's laws applied. Because both sides claimed jurisdiction over the same territory and institutions, each could be defied by the other's supporters. The conflict was not necessarily a conflict of nations, since many Russian officials wanted a continuing Union; it was a conflict over who would govern the Union's central institutions. A compromise, the treaty process described in §66.21, offered a way out.

The conflict can be understood through its mechanisms. The Union budget depended largely on revenues collected in the republics, including turnover taxes and enterprise profits, so when republican governments withheld payments or redirected them to their own budgets, the Union relied on credits from the State Bank and on printing money to cover its obligations. Because all republics shared a single currency issued by a central bank in Moscow, deficits created anywhere in the system added to inflationary pressure everywhere, which gave each government an incentive to defect from collective discipline. Administrative uncertainty also split the nomenklatura: officials in ministries and enterprises had to decide whether to follow Union instructions, Russian ones, or the preferences of their own republics, and many sought arrangements that preserved their positions under either authority. The war of laws was therefore not only a clash of principles but a series of practical decisions about who paid, who controlled enterprises, and whose orders to follow.

Section 66.16

The Crisis of the Communist Party and the Fragmentation of Political Authority

The abolition of the Communist Party's constitutional monopoly in March 1990 deprived the CPSU of its legal basis and exposed it to competition. At the party's 28th Congress in July 1990, reformers including Yeltsin and Moscow mayor Gavriil Popov left or distanced themselves, the Politburo was reorganized to include the republican first secretaries, and the Russian Communist Party emerged with a conservative leadership under Ivan Polozkov. Party membership declined by millions between 1989 and 1991 as people resigned or stopped paying dues. Republican Communist parties pursued their own paths: the Lithuanian party had split from the CPSU in 1989, and other parties adapted to national movements or became their opponents.

Gorbachev remained general secretary while also serving as president, which left him in the difficult position of leading both a party increasingly divided between reformers and conservatives and a state whose institutions he was trying to reconstitute. Conservative groups, such as the "Soyuz" faction in the Supreme Soviet, demanded a strong Union and the use of emergency powers. New political organizations, including Democratic Russia and republican popular fronts, offered alternative loyalties. In July 1991 the party's Central Committee adopted a draft program with a social democratic orientation, and Alexander Yakovlev left the party and warned of a conservative conspiracy.

The party's decline did not mean the end of its officials' power. Local party secretaries and the apparatus continued to control resources and to staff the state, but without a legal monopoly and without the discipline of a central hierarchy, they acted more in their own interests. When the coup came, no single party line governed the response of the apparatus: some officials supported the emergency committee, others waited, and many took the side of republican governments.

Continuity and Change Over Time

CPSU Authority Before and After Constitutional Reform

What changed? The CPSU's monopoly on political leadership, written into Article 6 of the Constitution, was removed in March 1990. Competitive elections gave republican and local legislatures independent authority, and party organizations lost their control of nominations.

What remained? The party retained a vast organization, property, and presence in the armed forces, the security services, enterprises, and local government. Its officials continued to hold senior government posts, and Gorbachev remained both general secretary and president.

When did change occur? Formal change was fast (the amendment passed in a single session), but erosion of real authority was gradual, accelerating after the 28th Congress and ending only after the August coup.

Why the pattern matters: Authority had been legal and organizational at once; removing the legal foundation did not at once remove the organization, and this mismatch helps explain both the party's continued power in 1990-1991 and its swift collapse in August 1991.

Division IV — Economic Crisis, the Union Referendum, and Negotiations for a New Union

Section 66.17

The Economic Emergency of 1990–1991 and the Disintegration of Central Management

By 1990 the Soviet economy was suffering from the combined effects of the structural problems described in Chapters 63 and 65 and of partial reforms that had loosened central control without creating functioning markets. Official Soviet statistics and Western estimates agree that output fell in 1990 and fell sharply in 1991, with estimates of the 1991 decline varying but running into double digits. Budget deficits grew, much of the money supply was unmatched by goods, and retail prices were fixed far below what consumers would pay, so shelves emptied. Many cities introduced coupon rationing for basic goods.

The Union government's attempts at stabilization added to the strain. In January 1991 Prime Minister Valentin Pavlov withdrew 50- and 100-ruble notes from circulation on short notice, a measure that angered the public, and on April 2 retail prices were raised sharply (by roughly 60 percent on average according to commonly cited figures) in a partly compensated increase. Miners in the Kuzbass, Donbas, and Vorkuta struck in March-April 1991, demanding higher wages and political change, including Gorbachev's resignation.

The disintegration of central management was visible in the supply system. Enterprises and republics withheld deliveries, bartered goods, and imposed export restrictions on other republics. Republican governments withheld tax payments, claiming the right to control their revenues, and the Union budget therefore lacked income to cover its commitments. The economic crisis thus fueled sovereignty movements, since republics believed they could manage resources better themselves, and sovereignty claims worsened the economic crisis by breaking supply links. Beyond the Baltic and Caucasus republics, many leaders concluded that preserving the Union required a new economic arrangement.

Cause and Effect

Economic Breakdown and Republican Competition

Long-Term Conditions

A planned economy with rigid prices, heavy military spending, declining productivity growth, and dependence on energy exports (Chapter 63).

Intermediate Developments

Partial reforms from 1987 that expanded enterprise autonomy and cooperatives without creating markets (Chapter 65); the fall in oil prices after the mid-1980s; loss of party discipline over supply and distribution.

Short-Term Triggers

Republics' withholding of taxes and deliveries, the 1991 currency and price measures, strikes, and the war of laws.

Consequences

Shortages and rationing, falling output, regional protectionism, and stronger incentives for republican governments to claim control of resources.

Historical complexity: Economic decline did not by itself dissolve the Union, but it reduced the center's capacity to buy loyalty or enforce decisions, and it gave republican leaders reasons to assert independence from Moscow's management.

Everyday Life and Social History

Shortages, Queues, and Coupons

For ordinary families the crisis was experienced as a daily problem of obtaining food, soap, and cigarettes. Women, who bore most responsibility for household provisioning, spent hours in queues, kept "just in case" bags, and used personal networks, workplace distribution, and garden plots to obtain goods. Many cities introduced coupons for sugar, meat, and other staples, and local authorities sometimes restricted sales to local residents, prompting disputes between neighboring regions. Evidence of these practices comes from memoirs, press reports, and oral histories and varies by place: large cities and closed industrial towns were better supplied than many provincial areas, and rural households with gardens often fared better than urban ones. The experience contributed to a loss of confidence in the Union government, but it did not translate uniformly into support for any one alternative.

Section 66.18

Gorbachev Between Reform, Coercion, and Compromise

In the fall and winter of 1990-1991 Gorbachev's policies shifted in ways historians still debate. In October 1990 he obtained extraordinary presidential powers; in November and December he reorganized the government, replacing the Council of Ministers with a cabinet under a prime minister, abolishing the Presidential Council, and creating a Federation Council of republican leaders. He appointed more conservative figures: Gennady Yanayev as vice president at the Congress of People's Deputies in December, Valentin Pavlov as prime minister, Boris Pugo as interior minister, and later Alexander Bessmertnykh as foreign minister after Shevardnadze's resignation. Kryuchkov remained KGB chairman and Yazov defense minister.

The shift brought repeated efforts to use force, as in the Baltic republics in January 1991, and joint army-police patrols in cities after a presidential decree. Conservative officials publicly warned about Western conspiracies and demanded emergency measures. On June 17, 1991, in a closed session of the Supreme Soviet, Pavlov, Kryuchkov, Yazov, and Pugo criticized Gorbachev's policies and Pavlov sought expanded powers for the cabinet; Gorbachev blocked the request. At the same time Gorbachev pursued compromise, culminating in the Nine Plus One agreement of April 1991 (§66.21), which reversed his drift toward the right and shifted toward the republics.

Historians offer different interpretations. Some see tactical balancing, with Gorbachev using the conservatives to maintain order while negotiating with the republics. Others see a leader whose commitment to preserving a reformed Union and whose reluctance to break with the party or to rely wholly on the democrats left him dependent on people who would eventually try to remove him. In either case, his authority weakened, and international assistance did not offset the decline: at the Group of Seven meeting in London in July 1991 Gorbachev received sympathy and promises of technical cooperation but no large financial package, although the United States and Soviet Union signed the START I treaty on July 31.

Section 66.19

The March 17, 1991 Referendum on Preserving a Renewed Union

In January 1991 the Supreme Soviet scheduled an all-Union referendum for March 17. The question asked, in substance, whether voters considered it necessary to preserve the USSR as a renewed federation of equal sovereign republics in which the rights and freedoms of people of any nationality would be fully guaranteed. Nine republics held the vote: Russia, Ukraine, Belarus, Kazakhstan, Uzbekistan, Kyrgyzstan, Tajikistan, Turkmenistan, and Azerbaijan. The three Baltic republics, Armenia, Georgia, and Moldova declined to participate, holding their own plebiscites or none (the Baltic referendums on independence, Georgia's on March 31, Armenia's in September), although in parts of these republics, including areas of Moldova, Georgia, and military garrisons, voting took place.

Official results gave an overall turnout of about 80 percent and about 76 percent in favor among participants. Support was highest in Central Asia and Azerbaijan, above 90 percent, and lower in Russia (about 71 percent), Ukraine (about 70 percent), and Belarus (about 83 percent). Ballots were not identical. Russia added a question on a directly elected presidency of the RSFSR, which won about 70 percent; Ukraine added a question on whether Ukraine should be part of a Union of Soviet sovereign states on the basis of its Declaration of Sovereignty, which won about 80 percent; Kazakhstan altered the wording; and Moscow and Leningrad voted on city mayoralties.

What the result means depends on what one asks of it. It shows that majorities of those who voted in nine republics favored a Union of some kind. It does not show support for the old Soviet system, because the question referred to a "renewed" federation of "sovereign" republics, and it does not describe the preferences of the six republics where the vote was not held on the Union's terms. The result gave Gorbachev a claim to legitimacy for a new Union Treaty and strengthened the republics that wanted to be in negotiations, but it did not settle what powers the center would hold.

Section 66.20

Boris Yeltsin's Election as Russian President, June 12, 1991

The March referendum approved the creation of a directly elected Russian presidency, and the Russian Congress then amended the constitution and set an election for June 12, 1991. Yeltsin ran with Alexander Rutskoi as his vice-presidential candidate. His opponents included former Prime Minister Nikolai Ryzhkov, supported by the party establishment (about 17 percent), Vladimir Zhirinovsky (about 8 percent), Aman Tuleyev, General Albert Makashov, and Vadim Bakatin. Yeltsin won about 57 percent of the vote in a turnout of about 75 percent, winning in the first round.

The election changed the political balance. Yeltsin could now claim a popular mandate, while Gorbachev had been elected president by the Congress of People's Deputies in March 1990 and had avoided a direct election, in part because his advisers feared the result. The Russian president had authority over Russian territory as the chief executive of the largest republic. The vote did not signal that Russians wanted the Union to end: Yeltsin campaigned on Russian sovereignty, reform, and the end of party privileges, and he cooperated with Gorbachev on the Union Treaty. It did, however, give him standing to negotiate as an equal and, in August, to lead resistance to the coup.

Section 66.21

Novo-Ogaryovo, the Nine Plus One Agreement, and the Proposed Union Treaty

The Union Treaty process was the main attempt at constitutional renewal. Gorbachev's first draft in November 1990 proposed a "Union of Soviet Sovereign Republics"; republican representatives, especially Russia and the Central Asian leaders, argued for more power to the republics. After the March referendum, on April 23, 1991, Gorbachev and the leaders of nine republics (Russia, Ukraine, Belarus, Kazakhstan, Uzbekistan, Azerbaijan, Tajikistan, Turkmenistan, and Kyrgyzstan) met at the government estate of Novo-Ogaryovo outside Moscow and issued a joint statement, known as "Nine Plus One." They agreed on the urgency of a new Union Treaty, a new constitution afterward, and elections for Union bodies. The agreement also helped end the miners' strike and reconciled Gorbachev and Yeltsin.

Negotiations continued through the summer. The draft renamed the state a "Union of Sovereign States" and transferred many powers to the republics, leaving the Union with defense, foreign policy, and certain financial functions. A compromise on taxation was reached in July between Yeltsin, Nazarbayev, and Gorbachev. Several republics, including the Baltic states, Armenia, Georgia, and Moldova, did not take part, and Ukraine's leaders sought changes and delay. The text was published on August 15, and the first signing was scheduled for August 20, with Russia, Kazakhstan, and Uzbekistan as initial signatories and others to follow.

The treaty deserves analysis as a political alternative, not just as a prelude to the coup. It would have created a looser federation or confederation in which Russia and Kazakhstan, among others, would keep power over most domestic matters, and it would have ended many of the central institutions that conservatives in the party, the military, and the KGB regarded as essential. Whether it would have been stable is uncertain, because the division of tax and economic powers, the position of Ukraine, and the status of the Baltic republics remained unresolved. But it was a serious, negotiated alternative, and its imminent signing helped provoke the plotters.

The substance of the drafts mattered. As published in August 1991, the treaty envisioned a Union president, a bicameral parliament, a constitutional court, and Union competences in defense, foreign policy, border security, and a portion of the budget, while the republics would collect taxes and transfer an agreed share. Accounts differ on whether the arrangement is best described as a federation or a confederation, and the text left open important questions: the control of the armed forces, the ownership of natural resources, the status of autonomous units within republics, and the treatment of republics that did not sign. For many republican leaders the draft recognized their sovereignty; for conservative officials it removed the powers on which the center's institutions depended.

Division V — The August 1991 Coup Attempt

Section 66.22

The Conservative Coalition and the Formation of the State Committee on the State of Emergency

The group that attempted the coup consisted of senior officials who shared a conviction that Gorbachev's policies were destroying the Union. Eight men formed the State Committee on the State of Emergency (GKChP): Vice President Gennady Yanayev; Prime Minister Valentin Pavlov; KGB chairman Vladimir Kryuchkov; Interior Minister Boris Pugo; Defense Minister Dmitry Yazov; Oleg Baklanov, first deputy chairman of the Defense Council; Vasily Starodubtsev, chairman of the Peasants' Union; and Alexander Tizyakov, president of the Association of State Enterprises. Others played essential roles without joining the committee, among them Gorbachev's chief of staff Valery Boldin, CPSU secretary Oleg Shenin, General Valentin Varennikov, and Supreme Soviet chairman Anatoly Lukyanov, whose involvement has been debated.

Their motives overlapped but were not identical. Many believed the Union Treaty would end the Soviet state they had served and would strip central institutions of their power; some feared the economic collapse and the nationality violence; some feared for their positions and for possible prosecution; and some, like Kryuchkov, held a view of Western conspiracy. They had attempted constitutional means earlier, as in the June 17 session of the Supreme Soviet. By late July they had concluded that Gorbachev had to be removed or neutralized before the treaty was signed. A recording of the July 29-30 Gorbachev-Yeltsin-Nazarbayev conversation, in which they discussed replacing officials including Pavlov, Yazov, and Kryuchkov, reportedly sharpened their fears.

The conspirators met on August 17 at a KGB guesthouse in Moscow and decided to act the following day. Their plan had notable gaps: it did not arrest Yeltsin, who returned to Moscow from Kazakhstan on August 18; it relied on Gorbachev's possible cooperation; it did not secure all communications; and it lacked a coherent political program beyond "restoring order." Historians draw on the investigation files and trial records, the testimony and memoirs of participants, and Chernyaev's diary. Claims by some defenders of the plotters that Gorbachev secretly encouraged the plan have not been supported by credible evidence, though his appointments of the officials involved remain part of the historical debate.

Section 66.23

August 18: Gorbachev's Isolation at Foros

Gorbachev began his vacation at the presidential dacha at Foros on the Crimean coast in early August. On August 18, in the late afternoon, a delegation arrived without an appointment: Oleg Baklanov, Oleg Shenin, Valery Boldin, General Valentin Varennikov, and the KGB's Yuri Plekhanov. Shortly before they arrived, the dacha's telephone lines, including the government and strategic communications, were cut. Gorbachev learned from his security chief that visitors were at the gate and understood that he was being isolated.

The delegation demanded that he sign a decree declaring a state of emergency, or transfer his powers to Vice President Yanayev, or resign. Gorbachev refused. According to his later accounts, which are supported in outline by other participants, he told them that they were acting unconstitutionally and that the Union Treaty and the matter of emergency measures should be considered by the Supreme Soviet and the republics. The delegation returned to Moscow, and the dacha was surrounded by guards who prevented the president and his family from leaving. His wife, Raisa Gorbacheva, fell seriously ill during the confinement. Gorbachev listened to foreign radio broadcasts, which was his main source of news, and recorded a statement on video that became public only later.

The political significance of Foros was twofold. The conspirators needed to give the appearance of legality, and Gorbachev's refusal meant they had to claim that he was unable to carry out his duties for reasons of health, a claim without medical foundation that offended many officials. The isolation also meant that on the crucial days Gorbachev played no active role, leaving the republics, the army, and the public to decide how to respond to the coup, and later made it possible for Yeltsin to claim leadership of the resistance.

The conspirators appear to have assumed that Gorbachev would sign, or at least acquiesce, and that the Supreme Soviet and the public would accept a fait accompli. Supreme Soviet chairman Anatoly Lukyanov had written a statement criticizing the draft Union Treaty, which was released on August 19, and the GKChP's materials suggested that the committee would seek the legislature's approval. The plan's reliance on a legal pretext, the claim of presidential incapacity, meant that Gorbachev's refusal removed the basis on which many officials might have followed orders. Historians reconstruct these expectations from later testimony, which was given by participants with strong reasons to minimize their intentions, and the evidence should therefore be read with caution.

Section 66.24

August 19: The Coup Proclamation and Military Deployment

At about 6 a.m. on August 19, Soviet radio and television announced that, because Gorbachev was unable to perform his duties for reasons of health, Vice President Yanayev had assumed the presidency and that a State Committee on the State of Emergency had been formed. A statement of the Soviet leadership and an appeal to the Soviet people were broadcast, and decrees declared a six-month state of emergency in parts of the country, banned strikes and demonstrations, suspended the activity of opposition parties and organizations, and restricted the press. Soviet television showed classical music and ballet and little news.

Troops and armored vehicles entered Moscow, among them units of the Taman motor rifle division and the Kantemirovka tank division and airborne forces, to occupy key sites. Reports differ on numbers, but thousands of soldiers and hundreds of armored vehicles were involved. Soldiers also moved in other cities and in the Baltic republics. In Leningrad, Mayor Anatoly Sobchak denounced the emergency, and the military commander did not impose it. The GKChP held a press conference on the afternoon of August 19, where Yanayev appeared nervous and a journalist asked whether the committee understood that it had carried out a coup d'état, a scene broadcast around the world.

Reactions inside the Soviet Union were mixed. Some officials and citizens welcomed the promise of order, many waited, and many were frightened or indifferent; Western governments, including that of the United States, condemned the action as unconstitutional and refused recognition. The emergency committee's proclamations spoke of restoring order, protecting the Union, and continuing reforms, and they also said that the draft Union Treaty would be reconsidered. Yet the absence of Gorbachev, the weakness of Yanayev, and the failure to control information began to undermine the plan from its first day.

Section 66.25

Yeltsin, the Russian White House, and Civilian Resistance

Boris Yeltsin was at his dacha outside Moscow when the news came and was not arrested. He drove to the House of Soviets of the RSFSR, known as the White House, on the Krasnopresnenskaya Embankment, where Russian Vice President Rutskoi, Prime Minister Ivan Silayev, and Supreme Soviet acting chairman Ruslan Khasbulatov joined him. The Russian leadership issued an appeal "To the Citizens of Russia" declaring the removal of the legally elected president an unconstitutional coup and calling for a general strike and for resistance. At around midday Yeltsin climbed onto a tank outside the building and read the appeal to a crowd, an image that became iconic; crews of tanks that had come to the building had by then declared that they would not attack it.

The resistance was broader than Yeltsin's leadership. Tens of thousands of people gathered at the White House at the peak of the crisis, and many more joined rallies in Moscow and in Leningrad, where about 200,000 people assembled on Palace Square on August 20. Defenders built barricades from construction materials and vehicles, organized supplies and shifts, and included students, veterans, workers, Cossack groups, and members of Moscow's new business community. The cellist Mstislav Rostropovich flew in from Paris to join them. Journalists played an essential role: independent newspapers published a joint issue despite the ban, the radio station Ekho Moskvy and Russian radio continued to broadcast, and fax machines, copiers, and foreign broadcasts spread information and Yeltsin's decrees.

The two sides made competing claims of legitimacy. The GKChP invoked the Union Constitution's provisions on emergency rule and the president's incapacity. Yeltsin invoked the Union Constitution's guarantee of Gorbachev's tenure, the Russian republic's electoral mandate, and the principle that the committee was unlawful. Yeltsin's decrees placed the armed forces and security agencies on Russian territory under his authority, which the military mostly did not accept, but his open defiance mattered because it gave officers and officials a reason to question their orders. His leadership was important but not sufficient; the resistance depended on the choices of many other actors, as the following sections show.

Section 66.26

The Army, Security Services, and the Republics During the Coup

The coup's success depended on obedience. Defense Minister Yazov and Varennikov backed it, but the armed forces were divided. Air Force commander Yevgeny Shaposhnikov refused to support the emergency, and several senior airborne officers, including Pavel Grachev and Alexander Lebed, hesitated or sided with the defenders. KGB special units, which would have been used to storm the White House, were reluctant: their commanders, including the head of the Alpha group, declined to carry out an assault. Interior Ministry forces and OMON were also uncertain. Many officers and soldiers did not know what they were being asked to do, and some feared civilian casualties. The command structure was fragmented by a lack of clear orders, doubt about the legal basis of the emergency, and unwillingness to fire on crowds.

The republics' responses varied widely. Estonia and Latvia moved to complete their independence, while Soviet troops in the Baltic seized television installations; Moldova's and Armenia's governments condemned the coup; Kazakhstan's Nazarbayev criticized the emergency and distanced himself from it; Kyrgyzstan's Akayev opposed it; Ukraine's Kravchuk took an ambiguous position at first, then declared the committee's decrees unlawful; Belarus's leadership publicly supported the emergency, and its chairman later resigned; Azerbaijan's Mutalibov issued a favorable statement; and in Uzbekistan, Karimov's response was cautious. Local Communist officials and soviets in Russia also differed, with many waiting for the outcome.

These differences show that the Union had no single center that could impose a decision. The plotters failed to secure the loyalty of the armed forces, the security services, the republics, or the public, and they acted without a plan for dealing with resistance. Historians point to several factors: poor planning and divided leadership; hesitation to use mass force; Yeltsin's ability to organize an alternative authority; the speed of information flow; the absence of Gorbachev; and the new political culture produced by glasnost and elections, in which many officials no longer accepted orders from a self-appointed committee.

Command decisions can be distinguished from later claims about them. It is documented that Defense Minister Yazov issued orders for troops to enter Moscow and that General Varennikov traveled to Ukraine on August 20 to press the republic's leadership to support the emergency, which Kravchuk did not endorse. It is also documented that the Alpha group's commanders did not carry out an assault on the White House. Later accounts differ over who gave which orders, whether an assault was seriously planned, and whether officers' refusals were decisive or only confirmed a loss of momentum already under way. Memoirs by Shaposhnikov, Lebed, and others were written in a context of political competition and tend to emphasize their authors' roles, while accounts by Kryuchkov and Yazov tend to minimize their intent to use force.

Multiple Perspectives

Section 66.27

August 20–21: Confrontation, Deaths, and the Collapse of the Coup's Command

On August 20 the GKChP declared a curfew in Moscow for that night. During the day Pavlov, who had been ill, was absent from the committee's meetings, and the committee's members disagreed about how to proceed. The White House defenders expected an assault, and the plans reportedly involved Alpha and Vympel special forces and airborne troops. But commanders were uncertain, and some of those who were asked to prepare the attack declined. Large rallies in Moscow and Leningrad on the evening of August 20 showed the committee's inability to prevent public gatherings.

In the early hours of August 21, armored vehicles of the Taman division approached the Garden Ring near Novy Arbat, where crowds had blocked an underpass. In the confrontation, some demonstrators threw objects or flammable liquids at the vehicles, and soldiers fired; three civilians, Dmitry Komar, Ilya Krichevsky, and Vladimir Usov, were killed. Accounts differ on details, including who gave orders and whether any commander intended to disperse the crowds forcibly. The deaths made clear what the use of force would cost.

By morning, the committee's authority was collapsing. Yazov ordered troops to leave Moscow, the Defense Ministry's board met and reconsidered, and the Russian Supreme Soviet in an extraordinary session condemned the coup. Several GKChP members sought to open talks with Gorbachev, and by afternoon some had flown to Crimea. Whether the committee ever would have ordered a full assault remains uncertain; the available testimonies disagree, and the evidence does not settle what orders were given. What is clear is that no unit carried out a decisive attack and that the plotters' command broke down within about two days.

The available evidence supports a distinction among three levels of claims. Documented facts include the troop deployments, the curfew order, the deaths of Komar, Krichevsky, and Usov, and the order to withdraw on August 21. Claims that rest on testimony include the preparation of an assault on the White House and the identification of specific officers who refused to take part. Questions that remain unresolved include whether the committee as a whole would have approved a large-scale attack and what role fear of the consequences played in the commanders' hesitation.

Section 66.28

August 21–22: Failure of the Coup and Gorbachev's Return

On August 21 two separate delegations flew to Crimea: one made up of Kryuchkov, Yazov, Baklanov, Lukyanov, and others who hoped to speak with Gorbachev, and another led by Rutskoi and Silayev, representing the Russian government, who came to bring him back. Gorbachev's communications were restored, and he refused to meet the GKChP delegation and spoke by telephone with Yeltsin and with foreign leaders. The Russian delegation's group, together with Gorbachev, returned to Moscow, landing in the early hours of August 22, and the officials who had flown with the committee's delegation were detained on arrival. Arrests of GKChP members followed. Interior Minister Boris Pugo fatally shot himself on August 22; his wife also shot herself, survived at first, and died afterward in hospital. Marshal Sergei Akhromeyev, a former chief of the General Staff who had been advising Gorbachev, was found dead in his Kremlin office on August 24.

Gorbachev emerged weakened. At a press conference on August 22 he spoke of his continued commitment to the party and to socialist renewal, which struck many observers as out of touch with the changed situation. On August 23, in the Russian Supreme Soviet, Yeltsin confronted him with evidence that members of his government had supported the coup and signed a decree suspending the Russian Communist Party. Crowds pulled down the statue of Felix Dzerzhinsky, the founder of the Soviet secret police, in front of the KGB building on the night of August 22. The Russian tricolor was raised over the White House.

The coup had failed, but the Union government had not been restored. Its leading officials were in prison or had been discredited, the KGB and the army were in turmoil, and the party was under attack. The republican leaders, above all Yeltsin, had shown that they could act independently. Gorbachev was formally still president but now depended on the support of those whose authority had been enhanced by the resistance.

The legal aftermath is part of the evidence for how the coup was later understood. Russian and Union investigators charged twelve people, and in February 1994 the State Duma passed an amnesty covering the participants in the August events. Varennikov refused the amnesty, was tried, and was acquitted by a military court in the summer of 1994, while other defendants were released. Critics argued that the absence of a full trial left questions about responsibility unresolved, whereas supporters of the amnesty argued that it promoted political reconciliation.

Historical Debate

Why Did the Coup Fail?

The question: What best explains the rapid collapse of the August 1991 coup?

Interpretation A: Popular and political resistance: The decisive factor was the willingness of Yeltsin, the Russian government, and thousands of citizens to resist, which created a political alternative and made it costly for troops to act.
Interpretation B: The plotters' weakness: The conspirators were poorly organized, lacked Gorbachev's cooperation, did not arrest key opponents, were unwilling to use mass force, and could not command the armed forces and KGB.
Interpretation C: Structural change: The Soviet system had been transformed since 1985; elections, glasnost, and republican institutions meant that a traditional emergency decree could no longer command obedience, so the coup attempted to use old methods in a new environment.

Points of agreement: Most historians agree that multiple factors interacted and that the coup was poorly planned.

Points of disagreement: Historians differ about the weight of the factors, whether commanders would have used force in other circumstances, and whether the coup was inevitably doomed or could have succeeded with a more determined use of violence.

Why it matters: The explanation affects the interpretation of Yeltsin's role, the strength of the Soviet state, and the claim that the coup accelerated the Union's end.

Division VI — After the Coup: The Collapse of Communist Authority and the Independence of the Republics

Section 66.29

The Disintegration of the CPSU and the End of Its Governing Authority

In the days after the coup the party's institutional position collapsed. On August 23 Yeltsin signed a decree suspending the activities of the Russian Communist Party, and Russian authorities sealed party offices in Moscow. On August 24 Gorbachev resigned as general secretary, called on the Central Committee to dissolve itself, banned party organizations in the armed forces and the security services, and ordered that party property be placed under the control of Soviets. On August 29 the Supreme Soviet of the USSR suspended CPSU activity throughout the country. In November 1991 Yeltsin issued a decree banning the CPSU and Russian Communist Party organizations on Russian territory and nationalizing their property, a measure whose legality was later reviewed by Russia's Constitutional Court, which in 1992 upheld parts of it and rejected others.

Investigations into the involvement of party officials in the coup, the seizure of archives, and the opening of party and KGB records revealed parts of the system's operations. Statues of Lenin and other Communist figures were removed in many cities. Many party officials left or reorganized, and a number of former Communist leaders in the republics re-emerged as presidents of newly independent states. The party's disappearance did not mean that its networks vanished, because many of its people continued in government, business, and politics under new labels.

The significance for the Union was fundamental. The CPSU had been the institution that tied the republics to the center and tied the state's ministries, army, and security services to a common line. Without it, the Union government had no instrument to enforce decisions in the republics. Its disintegration was therefore not a side effect but a central part of the Union's collapse.

Section 66.30

Ukraine, Belarus, and the Acceleration of Republican Independence

On August 24, 1991, Ukraine's Supreme Council adopted the Act of Declaration of Independence of Ukraine by an overwhelming majority, including many Communist deputies who had been part of the majority that had supported sovereignty in 1990. The act stated that, in view of the mortal danger created by the coup in the USSR on August 19, Ukraine was proclaiming itself an independent democratic state, that only Ukrainian laws were valid on its territory, and, in the accompanying resolution that adopted it, that the decision would be submitted to a referendum on December 1. Three days earlier Ukraine's leadership had been cautious; now the logic had reversed, since remaining in a Union whose center had shown it could be seized by force seemed riskier than leaving. On August 30 the Supreme Council suspended the activity of the Communist Party.

On August 25 Belarus's Supreme Soviet gave constitutional status to its 1990 sovereignty declaration, effectively establishing independence; its chairman, who had backed the emergency committee, resigned, and the physicist Stanislav Shushkevich became chairman in September. The country was renamed the Republic of Belarus. Moldova followed on August 27, Azerbaijan on August 30, and Kyrgyzstan and Uzbekistan on August 31. Other republics acted in September and October, and Kazakhstan declared independence on December 16 (see the timeline in §66.31).

These decisions were driven by different calculations. In Ukraine, national democrats and former Communists agreed that the coup proved the risk of the center; in Belarus and Central Asia, leaders who preferred a renewed Union faced republics' legislatures that now demanded independence or feared being left behind. The Russian government's reaction also mattered: in late August Yeltsin's press secretary declared that Russia reserved the right to raise border questions with republics that left the Union, which alarmed Ukrainian and Kazakh leaders until Yeltsin's emissaries gave reassurances. The sequence of declarations in August thus produced competing incentives to move faster.

Section 66.31

The Baltic States Regain Effective Independence and International Recognition

The coup accelerated the outcome in the Baltic republics. On the night of August 20, Estonia's Supreme Council voted to complete the restoration of independence and end the transitional period, and on August 21 Latvia's Supreme Council passed a constitutional law ending its transition. Soviet forces had seized key communications sites in Tallinn and Riga on August 19-20, but they withdrew as the coup collapsed. Lithuania's leaders, who had declared independence in 1990, reaffirmed their position.

Foreign recognition followed quickly. Iceland and Denmark were among the first to recognize the Baltic states, followed by Russia, which recognized them on August 24, the European Community on August 27, and the United States on September 2. On September 6 the USSR's State Council, a transitional body composed of Gorbachev and the republican leaders, recognized the independence of Estonia, Latvia, and Lithuania, and on September 17 the three states were admitted to the United Nations. Soviet troops, however, remained in the Baltic republics after 1991; Russian forces withdrew from Lithuania in 1993 and from Estonia and Latvia in 1994, and questions about citizenship and the status of Russian-speaking residents, set out in §66.4, continued to affect relations with Moscow.

The Baltic outcome demonstrates that effective independence required more than declarations: it depended on the failure of Moscow's coercion, on the weakness of Union institutions after August, and on international recognition. It also showed the importance of the legal framing discussed in §66.3, because the Baltic states were recognized as restored states with continuous legal personality, while other republics were recognized as new successor states.

Section 66.32

The Erosion of Union Institutions and the Transfer of Effective Power

In early September 1991 the Congress of People's Deputies, meeting for the last time in an extraordinary session, effectively dissolved itself. It approved a transitional arrangement: a State Council consisting of Gorbachev and the leaders of the republics that wished to participate, a reorganized Supreme Soviet in two chambers, and an Interrepublican Economic Committee. The State Council, on September 6, recognized the Baltic states. Gorbachev appointed new heads of the defense and foreign ministries, and he reorganized the KGB into separate bodies, with Vadim Bakatin as chair of the interrepublican security service.

Power increasingly passed to the republics, above all Russia. Yeltsin's government took over Union ministries, financial institutions, and Kremlin property by decree, and Russian officials began to control budgets and the central bank. On October 18, 1991, eight republics signed a treaty on an economic community, though Ukraine did not. On November 14, Gorbachev and the leaders of seven republics agreed in principle to form a "Union of Sovereign States" as a confederation, but when the draft was discussed on November 25 the republics' leaders did not initial it, and Ukraine was not taking part. Gorbachev's authority over the army and nuclear weapons remained formally intact, but control over most functions of government had passed to republican authorities.

The result was a power vacuum in which the Union existed but could not act. Historians describe this period as one in which the central institutions did not so much collapse as lose a constituency: the republics were willing to keep them only if they obtained real power, and the Union leadership could no longer provide it. Chapter 67 examines how Russia absorbed many of these institutions and the economic consequences.

The transfer of power occurred through a series of specific steps. The Congress of People's Deputies' September session replaced the Union's governing arrangements with the State Council and an Interrepublican Economic Committee headed by Ivan Silayev. In late October, Yeltsin told the Russian Congress that Russia would begin radical economic reform, including price liberalization, which would be carried out by the Russian government, and in November he formed a new Russian government that included Gennady Burbulis and Yegor Gaidar. Russian decrees in the autumn placed Soviet economic and financial institutions on Russian territory under Russian control, and in December Russian authorities took over the State Bank of the USSR. Other republics acted similarly: Ukraine placed military units on its territory under its jurisdiction after August 24 and created its own defense ministry in the following weeks.

Some Union structures continued to operate, especially in defense. Marshal Shaposhnikov became defense minister and tried to maintain a unified command, and the strategic nuclear forces remained under central control. The security services were divided into separate bodies under Gorbachev's decrees and then absorbed by republican and Russian agencies. The practical result was a state whose formal institutions remained but whose budgets, supplies, and personnel increasingly answered to the republics, and Gorbachev's attempts to conclude a treaty at Novo-Ogaryovo in November failed because the republican leaders were no longer willing to commit to a binding union.

Section 66.33

The International Response: Recognition, Nuclear Weapons, and the Future of the Soviet State

Foreign governments did not uniformly seek the end of the USSR. The administration of President George H. W. Bush, concerned with stability, arms control, and the safety of nuclear weapons, had supported Gorbachev and a negotiated reform of the Union; in a speech to Ukraine's parliament on August 1, 1991, Bush warned against what he called suicidal nationalism, a statement criticized at the time as pro-Moscow. Western governments condemned the coup, and after its failure most recognized the Baltic states quickly. Recognition of other republics was slower and was tied to commitments on democracy, human rights, minority protections, the inviolability of borders, and the control of nuclear weapons. In December 1991 the European Community adopted guidelines on recognition containing such conditions.

The nuclear question was central. The Soviet Union held a vast arsenal, commonly estimated at around 27,000 warheads, located in Russia, Ukraine, Belarus, and Kazakhstan. Western governments and the Soviet leadership worried about command and control, the security of tactical weapons, and the prospect of new nuclear states. In late September and early October 1991 Bush and Gorbachev announced reciprocal unilateral reductions in tactical nuclear weapons, and in November the U.S. Congress approved the Soviet Nuclear Threat Reduction Act, which funded cooperative efforts to secure and dismantle weapons. In the December agreements, the republics with nuclear weapons agreed on unified control of strategic forces for the interim, and the details were left to later negotiations.

Russia and the World

Foreign Governments and Nuclear Security Concerns

The end of the USSR was a global event as much as a regional one. The Cold War structure of the international system depended on two superpowers, and its disappearance created a question about who would inherit the Soviet position, including its permanent seat on the UN Security Council and its treaty obligations such as START I. Western governments, Japan, China, and states in Eastern Europe and Central Asia each reacted according to their interests. Rather than copying Western models, republican governments sought international recognition on their own terms, using arguments from international law and the principles of the UN Charter. The nuclear question gave the Western powers a strong interest in the stability of the transition and in the identity of the successor authorities.

Division VII — December 1991: The Dissolution of the Soviet Union

Section 66.34

Ukraine's December 1 Independence Referendum and the Collapse of the Union Alternative

On December 1, 1991, Ukrainian voters were asked whether they confirmed the Act of Declaration of Independence of August 24. Turnout was approximately 84 percent, and approximately 90.3 percent of participants voted yes. The vote was held at the same time as the presidential election, which Leonid Kravchuk won with about 62 percent, ahead of the nationalist leader Vyacheslav Chornovil, who received about 23 percent. Majorities in favor were recorded in every region, from about 54 percent in Crimea, where turnout was lower, to nearly 99 percent in some western oblasts, while eastern and southern regions, including Donbas, gave substantial majorities, in part because many Russian-speaking voters, as well as Ukrainians, associated independence with economic hopes and rejection of the discredited center.

The referendum answered a different question from the March vote. In March a majority in Ukraine had voted for a renewed Union of sovereign states, a question that assumed the Union would be reformed and that Ukraine's sovereignty would be protected. By December the coup had occurred, the party had collapsed, the Union's institutions were weak, and the Ukrainian Supreme Council had declared independence; voters were asked to confirm it. The two results are consistent with a population whose preferences shifted as circumstances changed.

The consequences were immediate. Poland and Canada were among the first to recognize Ukraine, and it became clear to Yeltsin and Gorbachev that a Union without Ukraine was not viable: Ukraine had about 52 million people, a large share of Soviet industry and agriculture, and a large share of the strategic forces. Gorbachev's hopes of a renewed Union, which had depended on Ukraine's participation, ended. The vote also changed the options facing Yeltsin: either Russia would join a Union without Ukraine, in which it would have to carry the burden alone, or the Union would be replaced by a looser association.

Section 66.35

The Belavezha Accords of December 8, 1991

On December 7-8, Boris Yeltsin, Leonid Kravchuk, and Stanislav Shushkevich met at Viskuli, a government hunting lodge in the Belovezhskaya Pushcha forest in Belarus, along with advisers including Gennady Burbulis and the prime ministers of Ukraine and Belarus. The meeting was held in part to discuss the Union Treaty. The three leaders concluded that a renewed Union with the Soviet center was no longer possible, and they drafted an agreement. Their motives differed: Yeltsin wished to end the Union government and the dual power it embodied and to establish Russian control over resources, Kravchuk wanted to secure Ukraine's independence and to avoid being drawn into a new federal structure, and Shushkevich, the host, sought a place for Belarus in a new arrangement.

The Agreement on the Establishment of the Commonwealth of Independent States was signed on December 8. Its preamble stated that the USSR, as a subject of international law and a geopolitical reality, was ceasing to exist, and it declared the creation of a Commonwealth open to other former Soviet republics, with coordinated policies on foreign affairs, economic matters, and security, and unified command over strategic nuclear forces. The signatories recognized each other's territorial integrity and the inviolability of existing borders. The Commonwealth was not a state and had no president.

After signing, Yeltsin telephoned President Bush; Shushkevich telephoned Gorbachev, who learned of the agreement from him. Gorbachev denounced the agreement as unconstitutional and as an act made without the participation of the other republics and the Union's organs. Nazarbayev and the Central Asian leaders objected to being presented with a decision by the three Slavic republics, and the Soviet defense minister, Shaposhnikov, expressed support for the arrangements on military control in the following days. The meeting was therefore the decisive act of the three, not of the Union as a whole, and the question of its legitimacy became central.

Section 66.36

Constitutional Legality, Parliamentary Ratification, and Competing Claims to Authority

The legal basis of the agreement was disputed. The Soviet Constitution gave republics the right to secede through a process specified in the April 1990 law, but it contained no procedure by which three republics could declare that the Union had ceased to exist. Gorbachev and his supporters argued that the signatories had violated the Constitution and the March 1991 referendum. The signatories argued that Russia, Ukraine, and Belarus were founders of the 1922 Union and could denounce the 1922 treaty, that the Union had in practice ceased to function after August, and that Ukraine's independence had been confirmed by a vote of its population.

Ratification gave the agreement political legitimacy. The Supreme Soviets of Ukraine and Belarus ratified it on December 10, and the Russian Supreme Soviet ratified it on December 12 by a vote of a reported 188 to 6, with 7 abstentions (a figure to be checked against the parliamentary record), also voting to denounce the 1922 Union treaty. The Russian parliament thus acted for the largest republic. In March 1996 the Russian State Duma adopted a resolution questioning the legal validity of the 1991 denunciation, an act that the Russian government regarded as without practical effect, and it illustrates how the question has continued as a matter of historical memory and political argument.

Historians distinguish between legal and political aspects. Many hold that the dissolution lacked a clear constitutional basis in Soviet law but became effective because the republics, the army, the security services, and international actors accepted the new arrangement; others emphasize that by December the Union had already lost the capacity to exercise its constitutional powers and that no legal process could have restored it. The disagreement is not about the facts of the agreement but about which standard, legal procedure or political effectiveness, should be applied.

Several legal frameworks apply. Article 72 of the 1977 Constitution gave each union republic the right to secede, and the April 3, 1990 law established the procedure; neither provided for dissolution of the USSR by the agreement of some of its republics. The 1922 treaty, which the three Belavezha signatories treated as the foundation of the Union, had been superseded by the constitutions of 1924, 1936, and 1977, and critics argued that it had no operative force. The signatories replied that the successive constitutions rested on the original union and that, as founders, they could withdraw. The Russian Supreme Soviet's ratification on December 12 was accompanied, according to commonly cited accounts, by a decision to recall Russian deputies from the Union's Supreme Soviet, which would have deprived that body of a quorum. The exact roll-call tallies of the ratification votes, including the reported 188-6-7 in Russia (one retrospective account gives 187 in favor), should be checked against the parliamentary record.

International law added another distinction. Russia took the position that it continued the USSR's international legal personality, with the seat at the United Nations and the treaty obligations, whereas the other former republics were successor states, and the Baltic states, whose annexation they regarded as unlawful, claimed to be restored states. Foreign governments largely accepted these arrangements, which helps explain why the legal objections raised in Moscow did not prevent the dissolution from taking effect.

Historical Debate

Constitutional Legality and Political Effectiveness

The question: Was the dissolution of the USSR a lawful act, a political fact, or both?

Interpretation A: Unconstitutional act: The Belavezha signatories had no authority under the Soviet Constitution or the 1990 secession law to dissolve the Union, and the March referendum had supported its preservation.
Interpretation B: Lawful denunciation: As founders of the 1922 Union, the three republics could withdraw from the treaty, and republican sovereignty and ratification by their legislatures gave the act legal force.
Interpretation C: Political effectiveness: The legal arguments were secondary because the Union's institutions had lost power and because republics, armies, and foreign governments accepted the new order.

Evidence: The 1977 Constitution, the 1990 secession law, the 1922 treaty, ratification votes, Gorbachev's statements, the Alma-Ata documents, and recognition decisions of other governments.

Points of agreement: There was no procedure in Soviet law for dissolution by the means used, and the arrangement was accepted in practice.

Points of disagreement: Whether the founders' denunciation of the 1922 treaty was valid, and how much weight to give procedural legality.

Why it matters: The question affects the legitimacy of the successor states and the way the end of the USSR is remembered and used in political arguments.

Student task: Explain how each interpretation would treat the ratification votes of December 10-12.

Section 66.37

The Alma-Ata Declaration of December 21 and the Formation of the CIS

The Central Asian leaders objected to being treated as acceding parties. On December 13, in Ashgabat, the leaders of Turkmenistan, Uzbekistan, Tajikistan, Kyrgyzstan, and Kazakhstan, with Nazarbayev, agreed to join the Commonwealth only as founders on an equal basis. Kazakhstan declared independence on December 16. On December 21, in Alma-Ata, the leaders of eleven republics (Armenia, Azerbaijan, Belarus, Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, Moldova, Russia, Tajikistan, Turkmenistan, Ukraine, and Uzbekistan) signed a protocol to the Belavezha agreement and the Alma-Ata Declaration. The Baltic states, which had been recognized as independent, were not invited, and Georgia, then in the midst of an armed crisis in Tbilisi, did not attend.

The declaration stated that with the formation of the Commonwealth the USSR ceased to exist and that the signatories were equal founders. The documents also addressed the nuclear question by providing for unified control of strategic forces and for the consultation of the leaders of the four republics with nuclear weapons on their use, and they supported Russia's succession to the USSR's seat in the UN Security Council. The leaders differed about the Commonwealth's functions: Ukraine rejected any supranational authority or joint armed forces, Russia favored coordination of military and economic matters, and the Central Asian leaders wanted a framework that preserved economic ties.

The Alma-Ata meeting widened the legal basis of the dissolution beyond the three Slavic republics and gave the arrangement a wider international legitimacy. It did not resolve the details of common institutions, military command, or economic relations, which remained unsettled in 1992 and afterward.

The negotiation of the Commonwealth's form took place over about two weeks. After Belavezha, leaders consulted by telephone and in meetings; Nazarbayev and the Central Asian leaders objected to a process that had excluded them, and at Ashgabat on December 13 they agreed to join as founding members only on an equal basis. The Alma-Ata summit adopted, in addition to the declaration, a protocol to the Belavezha agreement, an agreement on coordinating institutions including councils of heads of state and government, and agreements on nuclear weapons and armed forces. Ukraine opposed any supranational authority and joint armed forces, and Moldova, Azerbaijan, and Ukraine each qualified or delayed their participation in the Commonwealth's institutions in the following months. Georgia, absorbed in an armed crisis, did not attend and joined later. The summit also reflected an effort to assure nuclear security, since the four states with Soviet nuclear weapons on their territory needed an arrangement for their custody.

Section 66.38

Gorbachev's Resignation on December 25, 1991

After the Belavezha agreement Gorbachev struggled to find a role. On December 17 he met Yeltsin and agreed that Union institutions would cease to exist by the end of the year. In the following days negotiations covered his office, residence, and the transfer of powers. On December 24 Yeltsin notified the United Nations that Russia would continue the USSR's membership, including its Security Council seat.

On the evening of December 25, Gorbachev delivered a televised address from the Kremlin of about twelve minutes. He announced that he was ending his activity as President of the USSR, saying that he did so on grounds of principle. He defended his reforms, citing political freedom, the end of totalitarianism, free elections, the press, and the ending of the Cold War and the arms race, and he said that he opposed the dismemberment of the country and the dissolution of the Union, which he regarded as a mistake. He thanked citizens and expressed hope for the future. He signed a decree transferring the powers of commander-in-chief, including control over nuclear weapons, to Yeltsin. Shortly after 7:30 p.m. the Soviet flag over the Kremlin was lowered and the Russian tricolor raised. The United States and other governments announced recognition of Russia and several other republics, with others following.

The resignation did not abolish the Soviet state by itself. Soviet institutions still existed, and the final legal act came the next day. But Gorbachev's decision ended the presidency and removed the last official who could contest the Commonwealth's claim to succeed the Union. His speech expressed a judgment that has been disputed since: he saw the Union as a unity to be preserved by consent, while the republican leaders and many citizens regarded it as an arrangement that had lost its legitimacy.

Section 66.39

December 26, 1991: The Formal End of the USSR and Its Historical Meaning

On December 26, the Council of Republics, the upper chamber of the Soviet Supreme Soviet, held a session attended by delegations from only some republics and adopted a declaration recognizing the independence of the former republics and stating that the USSR had ceased to exist as a state and subject of international law. The other chamber, the Council of the Union, lacked a quorum. Questions have been raised about the quorum and the representativeness of the session, but it was the final act of the Union's legislature. Yeltsin took over Gorbachev's Kremlin office. By the end of the year fifteen independent states existed in place of the Union: Armenia, Azerbaijan, Belarus, Estonia, Georgia, Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, Latvia, Lithuania, Moldova, Russia, Tajikistan, Turkmenistan, Ukraine, and Uzbekistan. Soviet ministries and many central institutions ceased operation or were absorbed by the successor states in 1992.

The dissolution of the Union must be distinguished from the transformation of the Russian Federation. The RSFSR became the Russian Federation, as the Russian Supreme Soviet decided around the end of the year, and it inherited the Soviet seat at the UN, much of the central apparatus, and the Soviet nuclear forces, as well as the Soviet Union's external debts and assets in later agreements. The Russian Federation's political, economic, and constitutional development is the subject of Chapter 67.

Historians explain the dissolution in several ways. Structural interpretations emphasize the economic problems of a planned economy and the contradictions of an ethnofederal system. Nationalist-mobilization interpretations, such as Mark Beissinger's Nationalist Mobilization and the Collapse of the Soviet State (2002), emphasize the waves of mobilization that spread across republics and made the Union's coercion untenable. Stephen Kotkin's Armageddon Averted: The Soviet Collapse, 1970-2000 (2001) stresses the weakness of the state and its elites, while Vladislav Zubok's Collapse: The Fall of the Soviet Union (2021) gives considerable weight to Gorbachev's reform choices and the state's capacity. Serhii Plokhy's The Last Empire (2014) stresses contingency and the decisions of leaders, especially Ukraine's. Most recent work combines these elements rather than selecting one. In later Russian political memory, the dissolution has been described in very different terms: Vladimir Putin, for instance, called it in 2005 a major geopolitical catastrophe of the twentieth century, while many citizens of other former republics celebrate December 1991 as a liberation or as the start of independent statehood. These are evidence about later societies and not an answer to the historical questions.

The final days also involved questions of succession and legal continuity. Russia notified the United Nations on December 24 that it continued the USSR's membership, a step accepted by other members, while most other former republics became members in their own right in 1992 (Ukraine and Belarus had been members since 1945, and the Baltic states were admitted in September 1991). For ordinary people the dissolution meant immediate uncertainty about citizenship, passports, savings, pensions, and army service, which each successor state addressed in its own way in 1992. The ruble remained the common currency for a time, which tied the new states together economically and created a problem that Chapter 67 examines. The practical meaning of December 26 was therefore less a single break than the start of a series of negotiations over the inheritance of Soviet institutions.

Continuity and Change Over Time

What Ended, and What Survived Soviet Dissolution?

What changed? The Soviet state, the CPSU's rule, the Union presidency, Soviet citizenship, the central ministries, and the integrated command economy ended, and fifteen states acquired international recognition.

What remained? The administrative borders of the union republics became international borders. Much of the Soviet elite, enterprise networks, infrastructure, the nuclear arsenal, the ruble zone for a time, and the Russian language as a common medium continued, as did unresolved disputes in Karabakh, Transnistria, Abkhazia, and South Ossetia.

When did change occur? The formal changes took place in December 1991, but the underlying processes of institutional loss and republican assertion ran from 1988 to 1991.

Who experienced the greatest change? Millions of people found themselves in a different state from the one in which they had been born, among them ethnic Russians living outside Russia and many members of other nationalities.

Why the pattern matters: The mixture of rupture and continuity explains why the post-Soviet period began with deep discontinuities in institutions and equally deep continuities in people and networks.

Section 66.40

Chapter Review and Historical Thinking Checkpoint

This chapter has traced a sequence: national movements in the Baltic and elsewhere, the spread of sovereignty declarations, the confrontation between the Russian republic and the Union government, an attempted constitutional settlement in the Union Treaty, the August coup, the collapse of the Communist Party's authority, republican independence decisions, and the agreements of December 1991. The central question is whether the dissolution of the Soviet Union was primarily the result of long-term structural weaknesses, the national movements of its republics, Gorbachev's reforms, Yeltsin's political decisions, or the consequences of the August coup. The evidence points to an interaction: structural weaknesses limited the center's capacity; reforms opened political space; national movements and republican elites used that space; the Russian leadership's challenge deprived the Union of its largest base; the coup destroyed the remaining authority of the center; and the decisions of December, above all Ukraine's, ended any prospect of a renewed Union.

Four habits of historical reasoning apply. First, distinguish concepts: sovereignty, independence, collapse, dissolution, and recognition were different things that occurred at different times. Second, treat evidence critically: declarations, referendums, memoirs, and official statements each have purposes and limits, and numbers such as casualty counts and vote totals should be attributed to their sources. Third, consider contingency: in March 1991 and even in early August a reconstituted Union was a plausible outcome, and the coup, the Ukrainian referendum, and the Belavezha meeting were choices with alternatives. Fourth, resist single-factor explanations and reductions of a multinational event to a contest between two leaders.

Students should be able to explain why the Union was not simply Russia, why the republics' experiences differed, and why the end of the Soviet state did not by itself determine the character of the post-Soviet states. They should also be prepared to explain how the later memory of these events has varied across Russia, Ukraine, the Baltic states, the Caucasus, and Central Asia, and why such memory is evidence about later societies as well as about 1991. The review apparatus that follows will test these skills.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how efforts to reform and renegotiate the Soviet Union ended in its disappearance. Its central problem was to explain a process that unfolded across fifteen republics and many institutions without reducing it to a single cause, a single place, or a contest between two leaders. The governing sequence ran from national revolutions to sovereignty conflicts, from the Russian-Union confrontation to an attempted constitutional settlement, and from the August coup to the collapse of party authority and the decisions of December 1991.

The Baltic movements came first and set an example. Beginning as reform movements within glasnost, the Popular Fronts and Sąjūdis grounded their claims in the argument that the 1940 annexation under the Molotov-Ribbentrop Pact was unlawful, and in 1990 Lithuania, Estonia, and Latvia declared the restoration of their statehood. Moscow's economic pressure and, in January 1991, the use of force in Vilnius and Riga did not reverse the process, though they killed civilians and hardened positions.

Other nationality movements followed different paths. In the Caucasus, the Nagorno-Karabakh dispute produced pogroms, deportations, and military intervention, while Georgia's movement radicalized after the Tbilisi killings and combined independence with conflicts over Abkhazia and South Ossetia. In Moldova, language laws and competing national projects prompted Transnistrian and Gagauz autonomy movements. Central Asian leaders, facing ethnic violence but little mass independence sentiment, mostly favored a renewed Union.

The institutional structure of Soviet federalism shaped these movements. Republics possessed constitutions, legislatures, and a nominal right of secession, while the party and central institutions held real power. After the end of the party's constitutional monopoly and the competitive elections of 1990, republican legislatures acquired their own democratic legitimacy, and the "parade of sovereignties" asserted the supremacy of republican law, often within a still-existing Union.

The Russian republic's challenge was distinctive. With Yeltsin's election as chairman of the Russian Supreme Soviet and the June 1990 declaration of sovereignty, the largest republic claimed authority over its territory and resources, producing a war of laws and parallel institutions. This was not an independence movement; Russian leaders mostly expected to remain in a renewed Union, but they contested control of its institutions. Economic breakdown and the weakening of the Communist Party deepened the fragmentation of authority.

The March 17, 1991 referendum, the Nine Plus One agreement, Yeltsin's election as Russian president, and the draft Union Treaty show that a reconstituted Union remained a serious option in 1991. The referendum was approved by majorities in the nine republics that held it, but its meaning was ambiguous and six republics did not take part. The treaty would have created a looser union; its approaching signing on August 20 alarmed conservatives who saw it as the end of the central state.

The August coup was an attempt by senior officials to prevent that outcome. It failed within days because of poor planning, the commanders' reluctance to use mass force, the refusal of key units to attack, the resistance led by Yeltsin and thousands of citizens, and the absence of Gorbachev's consent. Three civilians died in Moscow. The failure did not restore the Union government: the party collapsed, the KGB and army were in turmoil, republican leaders gained authority, and the Baltic states and many other republics completed or declared independence.

Decisions after the coup were decisive. Ukraine's referendum of December 1, with about 90 percent voting for independence, ended the prospect of a Union including Ukraine. The Belavezha Accords of December 8 declared that the USSR had ceased to exist and created the Commonwealth of Independent States; the Alma-Ata Declaration of December 21 broadened participation to eleven republics; Gorbachev resigned on December 25; and the Soviet of Republics' declaration on December 26 formally ended the Union's institutions. The legality of these acts remains debated, and the distinction between legal procedure and political effectiveness is central.

Historians explain the outcome through combinations of structural weakness, national mobilization, elite choices, the coup, and contingency, and no single factor is sufficient. Later political memory in Russia, Ukraine, the Baltic states, and elsewhere interprets the events differently, which makes the history itself an object of study. The dissolution of the USSR created fifteen states; the transformation of the Russian Federation, its economy, and its constitution form the subject of Chapter 67.

Chapter Reflection

Chapter 66 matters because it explains how a state of 286 million people, with a vast army, a nuclear arsenal, and a seventy-year history, ceased to exist in a few years without a major war between its parts. The answer lies in the interaction of institutions, national movements, elite choices, and chance, and the chapter has tried to show that the outcome was neither predetermined nor merely accidental. It also shows that the Soviet Union was a multinational federation whose end must be understood from Tallinn, Tbilisi, Kyiv, Alma-Ata, and Moscow alike.

The chapter changed the map of Eurasia and the structure of international politics. Fifteen states emerged, the Cold War's bipolar order ended, and questions about nuclear weapons, borders, minorities, and economic ties became matters for new governments. What continued included people, enterprises, elites, infrastructure, and unresolved territorial disputes in Karabakh, Transnistria, Abkhazia, and South Ossetia, as well as millions of people living outside the republic of their nationality.

The chapter also reveals larger themes of the textbook: the tension between center and periphery, the role of institutions designed for one purpose and used for another, the importance of contingency, and the difference between events and their memory. Earlier chapters showed Rus', Muscovy, and the Russian Empire as political forms that were not inevitable; the Soviet Union was another, and its end was one among several possible outcomes.

Questions remain, among them how much weight to give structural factors, whether a different Union Treaty could have survived, and how best to describe legal questions that governments still contest. With this chapter the story of the Soviet period closes. Part XII begins with Chapter 67, which examines the Russian Federation's inheritance of the Soviet state, the economic transformation of the 1990s, and the constitutional crisis of 1993, without assuming that its outcomes were determined by what came before.
