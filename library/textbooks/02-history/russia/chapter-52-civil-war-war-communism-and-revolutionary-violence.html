Part IX — Revolution and the Birth of the Soviet Union
52

Civil War, War Communism, and Revolutionary Violence

A History of Russia: From the Eurasian World to the Russian Federation

Chapter Opening

"Soviets are a higher form of democracy than any parliament," Bolshevik leaders argued in December 1917; their socialist and liberal opponents replied that only a freely elected assembly could speak for all of Russia. — a paraphrase of the two competing claims to legitimacy that met in the Tauride Palace in January 1918.

On the afternoon of 5 January 1918 (Old Style; 18 January New Style), several hundred deputies gathered in the Tauride Palace in Petrograd, the building where the State Duma had met before the war and where the Petrograd Soviet had taken shape in February 1917. They had been elected in the first nationwide election in Russian history held on the basis of universal suffrage and competitive party lists: the elections to the All-Russian Constituent Assembly, held in most districts between November and December 1917, in which more than forty million ballots were cast. Socialists of the peasant-based Socialist Revolutionary (SR) party had won the largest share of seats. The Bolsheviks, who had taken power in Petrograd ten weeks earlier, had won roughly a quarter. The galleries were filled with armed soldiers and sailors sympathetic to the government; the hall was surrounded by guards. The deputies elected the SR leader Viktor Chernov as chairman over the Left SR candidate Maria Spiridonova, and when the Assembly declined to endorse the Bolshevik declaration of Soviet power, the Bolshevik and Left SR deputies withdrew. Early in the morning of 6 January the commander of the guard, according to a widely repeated account from participants, told the remaining deputies that it was time to go home because the guards were tired. The Assembly never met again. A government decree dissolved it the same day.

This chapter examines what happened next. The Bolsheviks controlled the capitals, the main railway junctions of the central region, and much of the disintegrating army's northern and western fronts, but in early 1918 their authority was contested or absent across most of the former empire. Within three years, the government headquartered in Moscow had defeated every major organized opponent that tried to overthrow it, had lost territory in the west that it would later partly reconquer, had created a mass army, a security police, and a system of compulsory grain requisitioning, and had presided over an economic and demographic catastrophe. The chapter asks how this happened and what it did to the regime that survived. It argues that Bolshevik victory in the civil war was neither inevitable nor the triumph of a single unified revolutionary movement, and that the war changed the revolutionary government at least as much as the government shaped the war.

The conflict that historians call "the Russian Civil War" was in fact several overlapping conflicts, a point that recent scholarship has emphasized by sometimes speaking of civil wars in the plural. There was a struggle between the Bolsheviks and the loosely allied anti-Bolshevik forces known collectively as the Whites, a coalition of monarchists, conservative officers, liberals, and moderate socialists who agreed on little except opposition to Soviet power. There were wars of national self-determination and state-building in Finland, the Baltic lands, Ukraine, the Caucasus, and Central Asia, in which nationalist, socialist, and Bolshevik movements competed to organize the empire's non-Russian populations. There were peasant uprisings against all armies that requisitioned grain and conscripted sons. There were interventions by Germany, Britain, France, Japan, the United States, and other powers, whose motives changed after the end of the First World War in November 1918. And there was a war inside the former empire's towns and villages, in which neighbors, classes, confessions, and ethnic communities settled scores that predated 1917.

The institutions that emerged from this war shaped Soviet history for decades. The Red Army, created in 1918 out of volunteer detachments and then turned into a conscript force commanded in large part by officers of the former imperial army, became a model of centralized mobilization. The Extraordinary Commission, the Cheka, founded in December 1917 as an investigative body against sabotage, became an instrument of summary punishment, hostage-taking, and mass terror, and the ancestor of a lineage of security organizations. The system of grain requisitioning and economic centralization that Bolshevik leaders later called War Communism became, for some Communists at the time, a vision of how socialism might be built, and for most citizens a regime of confiscation, rationing, and scarcity. Terror, practiced by Bolsheviks and by their opponents alike, produced tens of thousands of executions and, in the pogroms that accompanied the fighting in Ukraine and Belarus, the killing of Jewish civilians on an enormous scale by soldiers of several different armies.

Students will meet a wide range of evidence in this chapter, and almost all of it is partisan. Decrees and speeches of the Bolshevik leaders are available, but they describe intentions and justifications, not outcomes. The memoirs of White generals such as Anton Denikin and Pyotr Wrangel, and of Red commanders and Bolshevik leaders such as Leon Trotsky, were written to defend their authors' records. Diaries and journalism by observers who were hostile to Bolshevism, such as the writers Maxim Gorky in his 1917–1918 newspaper essays and Ivan Bunin in his 1918–1919 diary, record how the war looked from the city street, but they reflect the authors' own politics. Statistics on mobilization, desertion, grain procurement, and casualties are incomplete and were often produced by agencies with reasons to inflate or conceal. Soviet historians for decades presented the war chiefly as a defense of the revolution against foreign intervention and domestic counterrevolution; Western and émigré scholars often emphasized Bolshevik coercion and the role of ideology; scholarship since the opening of the archives after 1991 has added regional studies, social history, and comparative work that complicate both accounts.

The chapter is also about Russia's place in a larger war. The Treaty of Brest-Litovsk, signed in March 1918, tied the fate of the revolution to the military strategy of imperial Germany. The Allied powers, still fighting Germany in 1918, intervened in Russia to keep war supplies from falling into German hands, to reopen an eastern front, and, increasingly, to oppose Bolshevism; after the armistice of 11 November 1918 their motives and their commitment became less clear. Bolshevik leaders in turn saw the civil war as part of an international revolutionary crisis, founded the Communist International in Moscow in March 1919, and sent the Red Army toward Warsaw in 1920 partly in the hope of carrying revolution westward. The history of the Civil War is therefore part of the history of Eastern Europe, the Caucasus, Central Asia, Siberia, and the North Pacific as well as of Russia.

By the chapter's end, students should be able to explain why the Bolsheviks survived, why that outcome was contingent, how the war reshaped Bolshevik institutions and practices, and how historians weigh the competing arguments about emergency, ideology, and violence. They should also be able to distinguish Soviet Russia, as the Bolshevik-controlled territory, from the many other political entities, armies, and movements that existed within the borders of the former empire. The chapter follows Chapter 51's account of the seizure of power in October 1917 and the Second Congress of Soviets, and it ends with a devastated country and a regime whose character had changed, a situation examined in Chapter 53's account of the retreat from War Communism and the formation of the Soviet Union.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain the competing claims to legitimacy represented by the Constituent Assembly and the soviets, and why the dissolution of the Assembly mattered for the character of Bolshevik rule.
  2. Analyze the debates within the Bolshevik party over the Treaty of Brest-Litovsk and explain how survival was prioritized over territory and ideological preference.
  3. Distinguish among the political goals, social bases, and regional organizations of the White movements, and explain why they did not form a unified alternative government.
  4. Evaluate the role of the Czechoslovak Legion and of foreign intervention in widening the civil war, distinguishing the differing motives of Britain, France, the United States, Japan, and Germany.
  5. Trace the creation of the Red Army and assess the roles of conscription, former imperial officers, political commissars, and railways in its development.
  6. Reconstruct the major campaigns of 1918–1920 and explain why 1919 was the decisive military year without treating Red victory as predetermined.
  7. Interpret the civil war in the borderlands as a set of national, social, and political struggles rather than as a contest only between Reds and Whites.
  8. Describe peasant resistance to requisition and conscription and explain the significance of the Greens and of local armed movements.
  9. Analyze the origins and development of the Cheka and the practice of Red Terror, and compare it carefully with White Terror and with anti-Jewish pogroms without treating all forms of violence as identical.
  10. Assess the competing interpretations of War Communism as emergency policy, ideological project, or a mixture of both, using documentary and economic evidence.
  11. Synthesize the causes of Bolshevik victory, identifying structural conditions, institutions, policies, and contingent events.
  12. Evaluate the costs of victory, including demographic loss, economic collapse, and the militarization of political institutions, and explain how these set the agenda for Chapter 53.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did the Bolsheviks dissolve an Assembly that the revolutionary movement had demanded for decades, and what did the competing claims of election and soviet power reveal about the meaning of democracy in 1918?
  2. Why did Lenin insist on accepting German terms at Brest-Litovsk when many party leaders preferred war, and what does the decision reveal about Bolshevik priorities?
  3. Why were the Whites unable to unite politically, and how did their fragmentation compare with the centralization of the Bolshevik side?
  4. How did the actions of foreign powers and the Czechoslovak Legion widen the war, and how did their limits shape its outcome?
  5. How did a government with few reliable troops in early 1918 create a mass army, and what were the costs of the methods it used?
  6. How should historians describe a war in which Reds, Whites, nationalists, peasant armies, and foreign forces all fought, sometimes allied and sometimes opposed?
  7. How did emergency institutions such as the Cheka and the requisitioning system become part of normal Bolshevik governance?
  8. How do historians compare Red and White terror and the pogroms of the period, and what evidence limits such comparisons?
  9. Was War Communism chiefly a response to emergency, chiefly an ideological experiment, or something produced by their interaction?
  10. Why did the Bolsheviks win, and why should historians resist explaining that outcome as a foregone conclusion?

Historical Context

A note on dates. Until the Soviet government introduced the Gregorian calendar, Russia used the Julian calendar, thirteen days behind the Gregorian in the twentieth century. The Soviet government decreed that 1 February 1918 (Old Style, O.S.) would become 14 February 1918 (New Style, N.S.). In this chapter, dates before that change are given in Old Style with the New Style date in parentheses where it helps, and dates from 14 February 1918 onward are given in New Style. The date of an event in Western sources, which used New Style throughout, may therefore differ from the date in Russian documents of the same year.

Chapters 49 to 51 traced the collapse of the monarchy in February 1917, the unstable partnership of the Provisional Government and the Petrograd Soviet, and the Bolshevik seizure of power in Petrograd on 25 October (7 November). By the opening of this chapter, the Second Congress of Soviets had approved decrees on peace and land, had established the Council of People's Commissars (Sovnarkom) under Vladimir Lenin as a government answerable to the soviets, and had been followed in December by a coalition between the Bolsheviks and the Left Socialist Revolutionaries, who took several commissariats. Bolshevik authority spread rapidly in the weeks after October through the network of local soviets, particularly along railway lines and in garrisons and industrial towns, a process Lenin called the "triumphal march of Soviet power." But this process often meant the formal recognition of a local soviet's declaration rather than effective central control, and it met resistance or indifference in the Don and Kuban Cossack territories, in Ukraine, in Finland, in the Caucasus, in many rural districts, and in much of Siberia.

The war with Germany and Austria-Hungary, discussed in Chapter 48, was the most urgent problem the new government inherited. The old army was dissolving through desertion, committee politics, and the departure of millions of soldiers for their villages to join the land redistribution (Chapter 50.8). In December 1917 the government abolished ranks and endorsed the election of officers, and it opened armistice negotiations with the Central Powers at Brest-Litovsk. The economy was in collapse: wartime inflation, transport breakdown, and food shortages had been severe before 1917 (Chapter 48.10), and the revolution accelerated them. Factory committees and workers' control (Chapter 50.9) had produced local control without national coordination. In the countryside, peasants had seized and redistributed gentry, church, and crown land during 1917, and the Bolshevik Decree on Land of 26 October (8 November), which in effect ratified these seizures, gave the party a measure of peasant acquiescence that it would soon test through grain procurement.

Politically, the revolutionary left was divided. The Mensheviks and the Right SRs had left the Second Congress in protest at the seizure of power and refused to recognize the new government; the Left SRs supported Soviet power but differed over the war and the countryside; anarchists, who had supported the October insurrection, objected to the new state; and the liberal Constitutional Democrats (Kadets) were soon declared by decree to be "enemies of the people." The Bolsheviks themselves were not unified. Disputes over the composition of the government, the freedom of the press, and especially the war were conducted openly within the party's Central Committee. The Constituent Assembly, summoned by the Provisional Government and long demanded by every part of the revolutionary movement, therefore stood as an unresolved question: it was the one institution that all socialists had declared sovereign, and it had been elected under conditions in which the Bolsheviks were a substantial but minority party. The chapter begins with the collision between that institution and the new government.

Section 52.1

The Constituent Assembly and the Crisis of Revolutionary Legitimacy

The demand for a Constituent Assembly, a body elected by universal suffrage to decide Russia's constitution, had been a staple of Russian liberal and socialist politics since the early twentieth century. The Provisional Government repeatedly postponed it, citing the difficulty of organizing elections in wartime, and one of the Bolsheviks' most effective arguments in autumn 1917 was that the government was delaying the Assembly in order to protect propertied interests. The Bolsheviks pledged after October to hold the elections on schedule, and they did so, partly because repudiating the Assembly would have contradicted their own propaganda and partly, evidence suggests, because they expected to do better than they did.

Voting began on 12 (25) November 1917 and continued in different districts into December and, in a few places, January. Roughly forty to forty-five million votes were counted, though the results are incomplete, and the election was not held everywhere: areas under German occupation, parts of the front, and some remote regions did not vote. The results, most thoroughly analyzed by the historian Oliver Radkey, gave the SR party and its allied lists the largest vote, in the neighborhood of forty percent, and the largest delegation, drawing on peasant support across the Volga region, central Russia, Siberia, and Ukraine. The Bolsheviks received about a quarter of the vote, with strongest support in Petrograd and Moscow, in the northern and western garrisons and the Baltic Fleet, and in the industrial towns of the north and center. The Kadets received a small share of the national vote but a large share in the cities; the Mensheviks fared poorly; and national parties, notably Ukrainian parties, took large shares in non-Russian regions. The SR list had been drawn up before the party split, so the Left SRs, who by the time of the Assembly supported the Bolsheviks, were a small minority among SR deputies.

These numbers can be read in several ways, and each reading was used at the time. For the Bolsheviks, the vote showed that the Assembly's composition lagged behind the revolution: the SR lists had been drawn up before the October split, voters in distant villages had not yet understood the difference between Right and Left SRs, and the party's strength in the capitals, in the armed forces nearest the fronts, and in the industrial working class was decisive in a revolutionary situation. For their opponents, the vote showed that a large majority of the population, especially the peasantry, had voted for socialist parties that rejected Bolshevik rule, and that a minority party had taken power by insurrection and was now preventing the verdict of the people from being heard. Neither claim was frivolous. The first rested on a theory of revolutionary legitimacy that had precedents in the 1917 soviets themselves; the second rested on the same principle of popular sovereignty that the revolutionary movement had upheld for decades.

Lenin addressed the question directly in December 1917 in theses that argued that the soviets were a higher form of democratic institution than a parliament, that the Assembly's lists no longer reflected the people's will, and that if the Assembly refused to recognize Soviet power it would "meet a political death." The government meanwhile took pre-emptive steps. In December it declared the Kadet party an organization of "enemies of the people" and arrested several of its leaders. A demonstration in support of the Assembly in Petrograd on 5 (18) January was fired on by troops; reports of the number killed range from about ten to more than twenty. When the Assembly met, the Bolshevik delegation demanded that it adopt a Declaration of the Rights of the Working and Exploited People, which would have subordinated the Assembly to Soviet power. The Assembly rejected the demand by a vote of roughly 237 to 136, the Bolsheviks and Left SRs walked out, and the remaining deputies began to consider SR land legislation and a statement on peace before being dispersed. The All-Russian Central Executive Committee (VTsIK), the standing body of the soviets, issued a decree dissolving the Assembly. The Third Congress of Soviets, meeting later in January, declared Russia a republic of soviets and endorsed the dissolution.

The significance of the episode is often underestimated. The dissolution did not produce mass resistance in the capitals, and some historians have therefore treated it as a footnote to October. It was in fact a turning point in three respects. First, it closed the possibility, however slim, of resolving the revolutionary crisis through a negotiated constitutional settlement among socialist parties. Second, it gave the Bolsheviks' opponents a legal and moral principle around which to organize: the leaders of the Komuch government in Samara in June 1918 described themselves as members of the Constituent Assembly, and many Whites claimed they were fighting to restore the Assembly's authority or to allow a future assembly to decide the form of government. Third, it established a precedent for the Bolshevik practice of defining popular legitimacy through institutions the party controlled or dominated rather than through competitive elections. This did not mean that the Bolsheviks had no popular support. It meant that they would henceforth define support in terms of class and soviet representation, and treat electoral opposition as counterrevolution.

Section 52.2

The Treaty of Brest-Litovsk

The Bolsheviks had come to power promising immediate peace, and the Decree on Peace of 26 October (8 November) had proposed a general democratic peace without annexations or indemnities. Neither Britain, France, nor the United States replied. Soviet Russia signed an armistice with Germany, Austria-Hungary, Bulgaria, and the Ottoman Empire on 2 (15) December 1917, and peace negotiations began at Brest-Litovsk, the German army's eastern headquarters, a week later. The Central Powers had their own constraints: Germany and Austria-Hungary faced food shortages and political strain at home, and Germany wished to shift forces to the western front. But they were negotiating from a position of military superiority and aimed at securing control of large areas on Russia's western periphery, in the Baltic provinces, Poland, and Ukraine.

The negotiations created a deep division within the Bolshevik leadership. Lenin argued, from early January 1918, that the revolution could not fight: the old army had dissolved, the new Red forces did not yet exist, and the Germans could march on Petrograd. He insisted that the party sign whatever terms the Germans offered, however humiliating, because the survival of the Soviet government, which he saw as the only socialist government in the world, took priority over territory and over the abstract commitment to international revolution. Opposing him were the Left Communists, led by Nikolai Bukharin, who argued that a revolutionary war against German imperialism would inspire European workers to rise and that a separate peace betrayed internationalism. Leon Trotsky, who headed the Soviet delegation as Commissar for Foreign Affairs, proposed a third position: declare the war ended and demobilize, without signing a treaty. For several weeks in January Lenin's position commanded only a minority of the party's leaders.

The impasse was broken by events. On 9 February 1918 the Central Powers signed a separate treaty with the Ukrainian Central Rada, the government of the Ukrainian People's Republic, which promised grain deliveries in exchange for recognition and military support. On 10 February Trotsky announced to the German delegation that Soviet Russia would not sign a peace but would also not continue the war, the formula usually summarized as "neither war nor peace." The Germans resumed their offensive on 18 February, advancing rapidly across northwestern Russia and Ukraine against almost no resistance, taking Dvinsk, Pskov, and positions within a few days' march of Petrograd. After a close and bitter vote on 23 February, in which Lenin threatened to resign if his policy was not adopted, the Central Committee agreed to accept the German terms. The terms had by then become harsher.

The Treaty of Brest-Litovsk was signed on 3 March 1918. Soviet Russia renounced claims to Poland, Lithuania, and Courland, accepted the German and Austrian occupation and future disposition of Livonia and Estonia, recognized the independence of Finland and of the Ukrainian People's Republic, and ceded the districts of Kars, Ardahan, and Batum in the Caucasus to the Ottoman Empire. It agreed to demobilize its army and fleet. Commonly cited estimates, which should be treated as approximate, suggest that the treaty deprived the former empire of a third of its population, more than half its industrial enterprises, and most of its coal mines. A supplementary agreement in August 1918 added financial payments to Germany. The Seventh Congress of the Bolshevik party, meeting in March, approved ratification by a vote of 30 to 12 with 4 abstentions and renamed the party the Russian Communist Party (Bolsheviks); the Fourth Extraordinary Congress of Soviets ratified the treaty in mid-March, and the Left SRs, who opposed it, left Sovnarkom in protest. In March the government also moved its seat from Petrograd to Moscow, a city farther from the German front.

Brest-Litovsk shows several features of Bolshevik politics that mattered for the civil war. The party's decisions were made through open argument within a leadership whose members held strong and contradictory convictions, and Lenin's prestige, though great, was not sufficient to win without a crisis. Lenin's own position showed a pattern that would recur: willingness to sacrifice territory and ideological preference to hold the central institutions of power. The treaty also altered the map of the war. German forces occupied Ukraine, parts of Belarus and the Baltic region, and, with Ottoman support, parts of the Caucasus. It provoked the Allies, who feared German access to Russian resources and who had a stake in the military supplies stockpiled in northern ports. And it was temporary: after the German armistice of 11 November 1918, the Soviet government annulled it on 13 November, though the territories that Germany evacuated were by then claimed by independent governments, as Section 52.9 examines.

Section 52.3

The Emergence of the White Armies

The term "Whites" was applied, at first by their opponents, to the anti-Bolshevik forces that appeared in 1917 and 1918, in contrast to the Red color of the revolution. It is a convenient label but a misleading one if it implies a single movement. The Whites were a collection of regional armies, governments, and political tendencies whose leaders agreed on opposition to Bolshevik rule and, with varying degrees of conviction, on the preservation of Russia as a great power, but disagreed about nearly everything else: the form of government, the future of the land, the status of nationalities, relations with foreign powers, and the role of the revolution of February 1917.

The first major nucleus was in the south. In November and December 1917, General Mikhail Alekseev, formerly chief of staff of the imperial army, and General Lavr Kornilov, who had led the failed August confrontation with the Provisional Government known as the Kornilov Affair (Chapter 50.13), gathered officers, cadets, and volunteers at Novocherkassk in the Don region to form what became the Volunteer Army. The Don Cossack ataman Aleksei Kaledin tried to organize resistance but was unable to rally the Cossack population, many of whom were war-weary, and he shot himself in January 1918. The Volunteer Army, a few thousand men, retreated from Rostov in February on what its veterans called the Ice March into the Kuban region, where it besieged Ekaterinodar; Kornilov was killed there on 13 April 1918, and General Anton Denikin took command. In the spring of 1918 the Don Cossacks, provoked by Bolshevik requisitions and reprisals, rose in large numbers, and a new ataman, Pyotr Krasnov, organized a Don Army that cooperated, awkwardly, with Germany, whose forces occupied Ukraine. Denikin's Volunteer Army, formally committed to the Allies, refused such cooperation, and the relationship between the Volunteers and the Don command remained tense.

In the east, anti-Bolshevik governments emerged from a different political tradition. Socialist members of the dispersed Constituent Assembly formed Komuch at Samara in June 1918, drawing on the SR party, the Czechoslovak Legion's protection, and a People's Army recruited among volunteers and mobilized peasants. In September a State Conference at Ufa created a Provisional All-Russian Government, the Directory, with socialist and liberal members. On 18 November 1918, officers in Omsk arrested the socialist members of the Directory, and Admiral Alexander Kolchak, who had recently arrived from the Far East and been named minister of war, was proclaimed Supreme Ruler of Russia. In the north, a government under the veteran populist Nikolai Chaikovsky was established at Archangel under British protection in August 1918, and in the northwest, Nikolai Yudenich organized an army in Estonia in 1919.

The political spectrum within the White camp was broad. Monarchists, who hoped for the restoration of the Romanovs, were a minority among the leadership, as the Romanov family had been executed in Yekaterinburg in July 1918 and the Whites' principal slogan was not restoration but "Russia, one and indivisible," combined with the postponement of the decision on the form of government until a future national assembly. Conservatives and military officers, who held most of the commands, favored strong executive rule, order, and the protection of property. Liberals, including many Kadets, supplied administrators and advisers and hoped for a constitutional regime. Moderate socialists, notably in the Samara and Ufa governments, wanted a democratic republic. These groups often quarreled among themselves, and their political divisions repeatedly obstructed the construction of a coherent anti-Bolshevik alternative. Kolchak's coup against the socialist members of the Directory is the clearest example.

The Whites' weaknesses were as much political and social as military. They had no unified command, no shared territory, and no single political center. The principal armies operated on distant peripheries and could not coordinate: Denikin formally recognized Kolchak as Supreme Ruler in June 1919, but the two forces were separated by thousands of kilometers and cooperated only nominally. The movements' officers corps, drawn heavily from the imperial army, often viewed the peasantry and the revolutionary parties with suspicion and sometimes contempt, and many units conducted requisitions and reprisals that alienated the populations they needed. Land policy was ambiguous: most White leaders refused to confirm the peasant land seizures of 1917, promising a future settlement, and only Wrangel's government in Crimea in 1920 offered a land law that recognized peasant holdings, too late to matter. Nationality policy was a further handicap. Their commitment to a restored undivided Russia led most White leaders to refuse recognition of Polish, Finnish, Baltic, Ukrainian, or Caucasian independence, which cost them potential allies and, in the Ukrainian and Caucasian cases, produced active opposition. Antisemitism was also present in parts of the White armies and administrations, and some units took part in pogroms, as Section 52.12 describes. The Whites also had real strengths: experienced officers, a core of committed volunteers, Cossack cavalry, and in 1919 considerable material support from Britain and France. Whether their advance could have succeeded on a different political foundation is one of the counterfactual questions that Section 52.14 considers.

Section 52.4

The Czechoslovak Legion and the Expansion of Civil War

The Czechoslovak Legion originated in volunteer units formed from Czech and Slovak residents of Russia and from prisoners of war of the Austro-Hungarian army who wished to fight for the creation of an independent Czechoslovak state. After the Bolshevik seizure of power, the Legion, numbering some thirty to forty thousand men in Ukraine and central Russia, came under the political authority of the Czechoslovak National Council in Paris led by Tomas Masaryk, and was recognized by France as part of the Allied forces. After Brest-Litovsk, the legionaries wished to leave Russia to fight on the western front. With the Black Sea ports and the western route blocked, an agreement with Soviet authorities in March 1918 allowed them to travel eastward by the Trans-Siberian Railway to Vladivostok, where Allied ships would take them to France, on condition that they surrender most of their weapons.

The transport was a logistical nightmare. The Legion's trains were strung along some six thousand kilometers of single-track railway, mingled with trains carrying released prisoners of war, including many Hungarian and German soldiers, and subject to the often hostile control of local soviets. Tension between the legionaries and the authorities rose in May. On 14 May 1918 a fight at Chelyabinsk between legionaries and Hungarian prisoners escalated into a confrontation with the local soviet. Trotsky, who suspected the Legion of plotting with the Allies, ordered on 25 May that the legionaries be disarmed and that any found armed be shot. The Legion's commanders refused to comply, and in late May the Czechoslovak units took over the stations and towns along the railway. By the end of June they controlled the line from the Volga near Penza and Syzran through Samara, Chelyabinsk, and Omsk to Vladivostok. On 6 August they took Kazan, where they also captured a portion of the imperial gold reserve.

The importance of the revolt was not its size, which was modest, but its location and timing. The Legion was the first well-organized, well-armed force to seize territory from Soviet power across a large area, and it did so along the railway on which control of Siberia and the Volga depended. Its presence gave protection to the socialist Komuch government at Samara and to Siberian regional governments, and it gave a rallying point to officers and volunteers who had been scattered. Without the Legion's intervention, the anti-Bolshevik forces in the east might have remained fragmented and ineffective, as they were in early 1918. The Legion's revolt also drew the Allies more deeply into Russia, since they had a stake in protecting the force they regarded as an ally and in keeping open the route to the Pacific. The Soviet government responded by mobilizing the Volga region, as Section 52.7 describes.

Siberia and the Far East became the stage of a distinctive subset of the war. The region's political landscape included Cossack hosts of the Transbaikal and Amur regions, whose atamans, such as Grigory Semenov and Ivan Kalmykov, ran semi-independent forces supported by Japan and notorious for atrocities; urban and rural soviets with substantial Bolshevik and SR followings; settler farmers who had come to Siberia in the preceding decades and had little love for either side's requisitions; and indigenous Siberian peoples, whose lands were again crossed by armies. Local administration collapsed in many areas into rival authorities. The Legion's role diminished as the Kolchak government took shape in the winter of 1918–1919 and as the legionaries, increasingly unwilling to fight a war that no longer served the purpose of returning home, withdrew to guard the railway. When Kolchak's army disintegrated in late 1919, the Legion's command, intent on evacuation, agreed to let the Admiral be handed over to a revolutionary committee in Irkutsk in January 1920, and he was shot on 7 February. The Legion's evacuation from Vladivostok was completed in 1920. Czechoslovak historical memory treats the Legion as part of the founding of the Czechoslovak state, while Russian and Soviet narratives treated it, with greater or lesser emphasis, as an instrument of intervention; both views capture something of its actual role.

Section 52.5

Foreign Intervention and the International Civil War

Soviet and many popular accounts have described foreign intervention as a coordinated invasion by the capitalist powers to strangle the revolution at birth. Historians have complicated that picture considerably. Between 1918 and 1920 troops of more than a dozen countries were present on the territory of the former empire, but their purposes differed, their forces were generally small and cautious, their governments were divided, and their commitment changed sharply after the armistice in Europe. Intervention was important, but it was a set of interconnected and often incoherent policies rather than a single campaign.

The initial motives were tied to the First World War. Britain and France feared that the stockpiles of weapons and supplies that they had sent to Russian ports, including Murmansk, Archangel, and Vladivostok, would fall into German hands. They wanted to keep Russian resources from the Central Powers, and they hoped to reconstitute an eastern front against Germany with whatever Russian or Czechoslovak forces were available. A small British force landed at Murmansk in March 1918, at first with the cooperation of the local soviet and, according to several accounts, the tacit consent of Trotsky's commissariat; the British and French moved to Archangel in August. In the Far East, Japan landed troops at Vladivostok in April and, with an Allied agreement in August 1918 to send contingents of modest size, ultimately deployed far more, at times some seventy thousand men, motivated by strategic and economic interests in Siberia and the Russian Far East. The United States sent about five thousand men to North Russia and about eight thousand to Siberia, in the latter case partly to guard the railway and the Czechoslovak evacuation, partly to restrain Japan, and with explicit instructions not to intervene in Russia's internal politics, instructions that the American commander, General William Graves, interpreted strictly.

After the armistice of 11 November 1918, the original rationale disappeared, but the interventions continued, driven by different motives. Some leaders, notably the British Secretary of State for War Winston Churchill, favored sustained support for the Whites to overthrow Bolshevism. Others, including the British prime minister David Lloyd George and the American president Woodrow Wilson, favored negotiation or withdrawal, and their governments were constrained by war-weary publics and by labor movements: in 1920 British dockworkers organized "Hands Off Russia" actions, and French sailors in the Black Sea fleet mutinied in 1919. The Allies at the Paris Peace Conference proposed in January 1919 a conference of Russian factions on the island of Prinkipo, which the Whites rejected and the Bolsheviks answered conditionally; it produced nothing. France occupied Odessa in December 1918 and withdrew in April 1919 after local resistance, Bolshevik agitation among its troops, and political confusion. The British supplied Denikin's army heavily in 1919, with rifles, artillery, uniforms, and tanks, and a British military mission advised him, but London reduced its commitment during the year. The United States withdrew from North Russia in 1919 and from Siberia in April 1920. Japan remained longest, withdrawing from Vladivostok in October 1922 and from northern Sakhalin in 1925.

Foreign powers other than the Allies also played roles. Germany, before the armistice, occupied Ukraine, supported the Hetmanate there, helped the Finnish Whites defeat the Finnish Reds in 1918, and tolerated or aided the Don Cossacks. The Ottoman Empire and, later, Britain operated in the Caucasus. Poland and Romania pursued their own territorial aims. These forces complicate any account in which intervention is simply foreign hostility to revolution, since the powers were in conflict with one another.

Intervention's actual military effect was limited, but its political effect was large. Material support from Britain and France was important to Denikin and, to a lesser degree, to Kolchak, but it did not create effective armies, and the diversion of Allied attention after 1918 reduced it. The Bolsheviks, however, used the presence of foreign troops to portray the war as a struggle against foreign invaders and their Russian clients, an argument that helped with patriotic officers and many peasants, and to discredit the Whites by association. The Whites' reliance on foreign supply also exposed them to charges that their cause was one of foreign puppets. The dilemma was a real one for White leaders, who needed materiel and who also needed to be seen as Russian patriots. Intervention is thus best understood as a factor that widened the war and shaped its propaganda, not as a decisive military cause of the outcome. The feature "Russia and the World" below returns to its international setting.

Section 52.6

The Red Army and the Militarization of Bolshevik Power

The Bolsheviks' original military doctrine, shaped by Marxist suspicion of standing armies and by the experience of 1917, favored a people's militia organized by soldiers' committees and elected officers. The Red Guards that had carried out the October insurrection were factory-based volunteer detachments, effective in street fighting and ineffective against organized troops. A decree of 15 (28) January 1918 created the Workers' and Peasants' Red Army as a voluntary force; the anniversary of the first mass recruitment drive, 23 February 1918, which coincided with the German advance, later became the Soviet army's official holiday. The early volunteer units suffered from poor discipline, local loyalties, and independent-minded commanders. The partisan style of the Red Guard campaign in the south in early 1918 gave the new government little confidence in the approach.

The turn came in the spring and summer of 1918. Trotsky became Commissar for Military Affairs in March 1918 and, in September, chairman of the Revolutionary Military Council of the Republic, the supreme military authority. In April the government introduced universal military training, and in May and June it began conscription in selected districts, expanding the program through the year. The rank and file of the Red Army thus became increasingly composed of drafted peasants and workers, including large numbers of men who did not wish to serve. Desertion became a mass phenomenon, and the Red Army's leaders responded with a combination of amnesties, commissions to track down deserters, village-level penalties, and, increasingly, executions. Estimates of the number of men who deserted, were caught, or evaded service are very imprecise but run to the millions over the course of the war, and the same problem afflicted the Whites.

The most contentious decision was to employ officers of the old imperial army. Trotsky argued that a modern army required professional expertise that the party lacked, and that the Red Army must use "military specialists" under political supervision. By the end of the war, tens of thousands of former imperial officers, by some estimates fifty thousand or more, were serving, and a large proportion of the senior commands were held by men with imperial training, among them former colonels and generals such as Sergei Kamenev, who became commander in chief in July 1919, and Mikhail Tukhachevsky, a former junior guards officer who became a Red commander in his twenties. Many served from conviction or patriotism, others from fear for themselves or their families, since the authorities imposed reprisals on the families of officers who defected. Opponents inside the party, including a "military opposition" at the Eighth Party Congress in March 1919 and local leaders such as Joseph Stalin and Kliment Voroshilov, who clashed with Trotsky's central command over the defense of Tsaritsyn in 1918, objected to reliance on former officers and favored more partisan methods. Lenin backed Trotsky.

Historical Vocabulary
Military Specialists and Political Commissars

Definition: "Military specialists" (voenspetsy) was the official Soviet term for former imperial officers employed in the Red Army. A commissar was a political officer, usually a party member, appointed to units from April 1918 to supervise commanders, ensure political reliability, and conduct propaganda; commissars countersigned operational orders and were responsible for morale.

Historical context: The system was created in 1918 to solve a double problem: the army needed professional commanders, but the party did not trust them. Commissars were often former workers or party activists with little military training. Their relationships with commanders ranged from cooperation to open conflict, and commissars became a central element in Red Army culture. Political departments and party cells embedded in units formed a parallel hierarchy under the army's political administration.

Why it matters: The dual system is an example of how Bolshevik institutions combined technical dependence on specialists with political control. It was later reproduced in other Soviet institutions, including industrial management and scientific bodies, and it shaped the Soviet military's relationship with the party until the end of the USSR.

Other organizational features mattered as much. Control of the railway network allowed the Red command to shift troops along interior lines between fronts, a capacity the more dispersed White forces lacked. The army's political administration ran schools, newspapers, and agitation trains that carried propaganda to the front and the countryside. The party mobilized members for military service in large numbers; in some campaigns, as in the defense of Petrograd in October 1919, local party organizations, trade unions, and women's detachments were mobilized to dig trenches and defend the city. Latvian Riflemen, who had been among the best units of the imperial army and who remained loyal to the Bolsheviks, formed a reliable core in several critical episodes in 1918. By late 1920 the Red Army numbered about 5.5 million personnel on paper, although only a much smaller share served as active combat troops; the rest were in rear services, training, hospitals, or deserter-hunting units. Formations such as Semyon Budyonny's First Cavalry Army, formed in late 1919, restored a mobile arm that the Reds had lacked against Cossack horsemen.

The Red Army's creation had implications far beyond its military role. It was a school of discipline and administration for a generation of Bolsheviks, many of whom would later hold high office. Its methods, including conscription, summary justice, and mass mobilization, spread to civilian policy. The language and the practices of military campaigns, such as "campaigns" for grain procurement, "fronts" of economic work, and "shock" brigades, entered Soviet administrative culture. The reliance on coercion and the use of hostages and reprisals against deserters or their families, practices introduced under wartime pressure, set precedents that would be applied in other contexts. The transformation of the party itself, from a conspiratorial organization of revolutionaries into a body whose members commanded troops, ran requisition detachments, and staffed security organs, is one of the major long-term consequences of this militarization.

Section 52.7

The Civil War, 1918–1919: The Struggle on Multiple Fronts

Understanding the war requires a spatial picture. By mid-1918, Soviet Russia was a compact territory centered on Moscow and Petrograd, roughly the old Muscovite heartland and the central industrial region, with a population of tens of millions but with limited resources of food and fuel. It was ringed by hostile or independent territories: the Don and Kuban to the south, Ukraine under German occupation to the southwest, the Baltic region and Finland to the northwest, the Volga, Urals, and Siberia to the east controlled in various ways by the Komuch government, the Czechoslovak Legion, and Siberian regional governments, and the Arctic north under British protection. The Bolsheviks held the center and the main railway hub of Moscow, from which lines radiated to most of the fronts; their opponents held the periphery and the grain-surplus and mineral-rich regions. This geography did not determine the war's outcome, but it shaped the strategy of every participant.

The first major crisis came on the Volga in the summer of 1918. The Czechoslovak revolt and the Komuch's advance threatened the Soviet east. In July the commander of the Eastern Front, the Left SR Mikhail Muravyov, attempted to turn his forces against the Bolsheviks at Simbirsk and was killed. Kazan fell to a combined force of Czechs and Komuch troops on 6 August. Trotsky took personal charge, moving by armored train along the front, enforcing discipline, shooting some deserters and commanders, and rallying a new force that retook Kazan on 10 September and Simbirsk and Samara in the following weeks. These victories established the Red Army's credibility and removed the immediate threat from the east. At the same time, in the south, the Volunteer Army cleared the Kuban of Bolshevik forces in the autumn and Krasnov's Don Army besieged Tsaritsyn, which Stalin and Voroshilov helped to defend. In July 1918 anti-Bolshevik conspirators organized by Boris Savinkov seized Yaroslavl and held it for two weeks until Soviet forces retook it with heavy damage to the city.

The end of the First World War changed the situation. German forces withdrew from Ukraine and the Baltic region in late 1918, leaving a vacuum that Ukrainian, Polish, Baltic, and Soviet forces competed to fill. The Soviet government annulled Brest-Litovsk and sent forces west, taking Riga for a time and Kyiv in February 1919, but encountered nationalist armies and local resistance, as Section 52.9 describes. The Whites obtained Allied supplies and, in the south, landings at Odessa and Crimea. In the east, Kolchak's government in Omsk assembled an army and, in March 1919, launched an offensive from the Urals toward the Volga, advancing to within a short distance of the river. A Soviet counteroffensive in April and June, led by Mikhail Frunze on the southern wing of the Eastern Front, retook Ufa in June and pushed the Whites back across the Urals into Siberia.

Alongside these main campaigns were secondary fronts and local wars. In the north, British, French, and American forces at Archangel and Murmansk and a small anti-Bolshevik army fought indecisive actions against the Red Army along the Northern Dvina and the railway lines; the Allies began withdrawing in the summer of 1919, and the Reds took Archangel in February 1920. In the northwest, Estonian and Finnish forces, and Yudenich's small White army, operated near Petrograd. In Ukraine, control changed repeatedly among the Ukrainian People's Republic, the German-backed Hetmanate of Pavlo Skoropadsky, the Red Army, the Volunteer Army, Makhno's insurgents, and other forces. The Soviet Eastern Front's victory of 1919 and the repulse of the White advances on the southern and northwestern fronts, examined in Section 52.8, thus unfolded within a patchwork of conflicts in which no side controlled all of its own territory.

The war's character shaped both strategy and politics. Battle lines were long and thinly held; cavalry raids, such as General Konstantin Mamontov's Cossack raid behind Red lines in August and September 1919, could have strategic effects out of proportion to their size; armies moved by rail, where possible, and rail control was essential. Supply, medical care, and munitions were chronic problems for everyone. Typhus, which killed millions across the former empire in 1919–1920, devastated armies and cities. Armies lived off the land, and requisition, whether by Reds, Whites, nationalists, or insurgents, was a feature of every front. The war was thus experienced by many civilians less as a contest of ideologies than as a succession of armed men taking horses, grain, and sons.

Section 52.8

The Decisive Campaigns of 1919–1920

The year 1919 was the decisive military year because it was the one in which the Whites had the greatest opportunity. They received the largest volume of Allied supplies, had experienced armies on two major fronts, and faced a Soviet government that was still organizing its forces and was deeply short of food and fuel. That they failed, and that the Red Army won against forces advancing from east, south, and northwest in succession, is one of the central facts of the war, but it should not be read backward as proof that the result was fixed. Each campaign ended in a close contest in which decisions, errors, and chance mattered.

Kolchak and the Eastern Front

Kolchak's spring offensive was the first test. His army, built around Siberian and Urals units and mobilized peasants and supported by Czechoslovak control of the railway, advanced in March and April 1919 toward the Volga, capturing Ufa and approaching Kazan and Samara. The Red Eastern Front, under Sergei Kamenev as front commander and with Frunze leading the key counterattack, struck at the White flank in April and drove the Whites back. The White advance had stretched supply lines, the Siberian peasant population resented mobilization and requisitions, and partisan uprisings in the Siberian rear, some led by Bolsheviks and others by independent peasant leaders, drew off forces. By July the Red Army had taken Perm and Chelyabinsk, and in November Omsk, the capital of Kolchak's government, fell. The remnants of the army retreated eastward along the railway in a march of enormous suffering in the Siberian winter, and the collapse of the regime in Irkutsk led to Kolchak's handover and execution.

Denikin's March on Moscow

In the south, Denikin's Armed Forces of South Russia, which had conquered the Don and Kuban and expanded rapidly, launched a major advance in the summer of 1919 under what became known as his "Moscow Directive" of July. The force took Kharkiv and Tsaritsyn in June, Kyiv in late August, and Kursk and Orel in the autumn, coming within some four hundred kilometers of Moscow by October. The advance encountered a Red Army that was reorganizing its southern forces, aided by the Soviet strength in central industrial supplies; a major raid by Budyonny's cavalry and a counterattack from the northern flank in October and November broke the Whites' overextended line. Contributing factors included Makhno's insurgent army, which in September 1919 attacked the White rear in southern Ukraine, peasant opposition to White requisitions and to their refusal to confirm land seizures, and the disorder of White administration in the captured territories, in which corruption, looting, and pogroms undermined popular support. By December Kharkiv and Kyiv were back in Red hands, and by March 1920 the remnants of the Volunteer Army had been evacuated from Novorossiisk to Crimea, an operation marked by chaos and the abandonment of many troops and refugees.

Yudenich at the Gates of Petrograd

In October 1919, Yudenich's North-Western Army, supported by Estonian territory and a small British naval presence but not by active Estonian or Finnish participation, advanced from the west and reached the heights south of Petrograd, within some fifteen kilometers of the city. The Soviet defense, directed by Trotsky, who came to Petrograd and, by his own account, ordered the mobilization of the city's population and the defense of the city street by street, held; reinforcements arrived by rail, and the exhausted attackers retreated in November into Estonia, where the remnants of the army were interned. The campaign illustrates the contingent character of the war: the Whites came closer to taking the former capital, the symbol of the revolution, than is often remembered. It also shows the limits of White coordination, since Yudenich's attack coincided with Denikin's advance at Orel but was unconnected to it.

Wrangel, Poland, and the End of the Main White Fronts

In April 1920 General Pyotr Wrangel succeeded Denikin and reorganized the army in Crimea. He proposed a more conciliatory policy, issuing in May a land law confirming peasant holdings in the territory he controlled and seeking some cooperation with local self-government and with Ukrainian and Cossack leaders. The change came too late and the base was too small. Wrangel's army held out through the summer and attempted to expand into northern Tavria, but in the autumn, as the Soviet government concluded the war with Poland, forces under Frunze, including Makhno's insurgents, then in a temporary alliance with the Bolsheviks, attacked the isthmus of Perekop and the Chongar crossing in early November. The Red forces broke through, taking heavy losses, and Wrangel evacuated about 150,000 soldiers and civilians from Crimean ports to Constantinople in mid-November. They formed the core of what would become the Russian emigration, with large communities in Constantinople, Belgrade, Prague, Paris, Berlin, and Harbin. The Red occupation of Crimea was followed by mass executions of officers, officials, and others, estimates of which range widely, from several thousand to tens of thousands, a question on which the surviving documentation is incomplete and political positions have long diverged.

Section 52.9

National Revolutions, Borderlands, and the Fragmentation of Empire

The Russian Empire, as Chapter 45 showed, was a multinational polity, and the revolutions of 1917 had unleashed national movements across it (Chapter 50.10). By the end of 1917 and through 1918–1921, these movements, working with or against Bolsheviks, Whites, and foreign powers, produced independent states, autonomous regions, short-lived republics, and wars that cannot be understood as episodes in a Red–White struggle. The resulting outcomes varied widely. Some of the new states survived and others were conquered; others were absorbed into Soviet structures that preserved formal national identity, a process examined in Chapter 53.

Finland, the Baltic, Poland, and Belarus

Finland declared independence on 6 December 1917, and Sovnarkom recognized it at the end of the month. A civil war between Finnish Reds, supported by Soviet Russian arms, and Finnish Whites under General Carl Gustaf Mannerheim, supported by German troops from April, lasted from January to May 1918 and ended with a White victory and mass reprisals against the Reds. In Estonia, Latvia, and Lithuania, independence movements declared statehood in 1918 under German occupation and fought for survival after the German withdrawal against Soviet offensives, which at one point established short-lived Soviet governments in Latvia, and against local Baltic German and White forces. Estonia signed a peace treaty with Soviet Russia at Tartu on 2 February 1920, the first Soviet recognition of an independent Baltic state; Lithuania and Latvia followed in July and August 1920. Poland, restored as a state in November 1918, had its own territorial program, which led to the Soviet-Polish War of 1919–1921. In Belarus, a Belarusian People's Republic was proclaimed in March 1918 under German occupation, and a Soviet Belarusian republic was proclaimed in January 1919, but the territory was fought over by Polish, Soviet, and German forces.

Ukraine and the Polish War

Ukraine was among the war's most contested regions, and its experience is examined in the Regional Context feature. The Ukrainian Central Rada, formed in Kyiv in March 1917, declared the Ukrainian People's Republic in November and full independence in January 1918. Bolshevik forces seized Kyiv in February 1918; German troops, invited by the Rada under the 9 February treaty, retook it in March, and after an April coup the Germans backed a Hetmanate under Skoropadsky until the German collapse in December. A Directory led by Simon Petliura and Volodymyr Vynnychenko then restored the republic, which was driven from Kyiv by the Red Army in February 1919. In Galicia, a West Ukrainian People's Republic proclaimed in November 1918 fought a war with Poland that it lost by mid-1919. In April 1920, Petliura allied with Poland's Jozef Pilsudski, and Polish and Ukrainian troops took Kyiv in early May. The Red Army counterattacked, advanced to the outskirts of Warsaw in August, and was defeated in the Battle of Warsaw and in the retreat that followed. An armistice in October and the Treaty of Riga in March 1921 divided Ukrainian and Belarusian lands between Poland and the Soviet republics. For Bolshevik leaders, the Polish War was in part an attempt to carry revolution westward, a hope that collapsed with the failure of Polish workers to rally to the Red Army, an outcome that shaped Soviet strategy in later years.

The Caucasus

The Transcaucasian Federation, formed after October, dissolved in May 1918 into independent Georgia, Armenia, and Azerbaijan, each facing wars, refugees, and shifting foreign presences: Ottoman and German forces, then British troops from late 1918 until 1920. In Baku, a Bolshevik-led commune seized power in the oil city in the spring of 1918, accompanied by violence in the "March Days," in which thousands of Azerbaijani civilians were killed; the commune collapsed in July, and twenty-six of its leading members were executed in September in Transcaspia, an event that became a Soviet martyrology. Soviet Russia recognized Georgia's independence in a treaty of May 1920, but the Red Army entered Baku in April 1920 and Armenia by the end of the year, and invaded Georgia in February 1921, events considered further in Chapter 53. In the North Caucasus, a mountaineer republic proclaimed in 1917 collapsed under Denikin and Bolshevik pressure, and the region's peoples endured successive occupations.

Central Asia and the Volga-Ural Region

In Turkestan, the Tashkent Soviet, dominated by European settlers and soldiers, seized power in November 1917 and excluded Muslim political organizations; the Muslim-led Kokand Autonomy, declared in December 1917, was crushed in February 1918 with heavy civilian casualties. The Turkestan Autonomous Soviet Republic was proclaimed in April 1918. The resentment in the region, still raw from the suppression of the 1916 revolt against labor conscription, fed the Basmachi movement, a diffuse guerrilla resistance that continued for years. In the steppe, Kazakh nationalists organized the Alash Orda government, which cooperated with the Whites and then, in 1919–1920, negotiated with the Bolsheviks. Red forces under Frunze took Khiva in early 1920 and Bukhara in September 1920. In the Volga-Ural region, Bashkir leader Zeki Validi Togan moved from alliance with the Whites to an agreement with the Bolsheviks in early 1919 and became part of the founding of an autonomous Bashkir republic; Tatar national communists, among them Mirsaid Sultan-Galiev, argued for a distinct Muslim path within Soviet power.

This record shows that the civil war in the borderlands cannot be reduced to a competition between Moscow and its rivals for control of "Russian" land. National movements pursued their own goals, made alliances of convenience, and fought against each other as well as against Reds and Whites. The Bolsheviks, who proclaimed a right of nations to self-determination, sometimes recognized independence and sometimes reconquered it, and their ability to offer national autonomy within a federal framework, in contrast with the Whites' refusal to recognize most claims, was one of the sources of their eventual success, as Section 52.14 argues. The reconquest was also a use of military force against national governments, and it left a legacy of memory, still contested, that Chapters 53, 58, and 66 will take up.

Section 52.10

Peasant War, Greens, and Resistance to Both Sides

Roughly eighty percent of the population of the former empire lived in the countryside, and the war could not be won without peasant grain, horses, and soldiers. Peasants were not passive. The land seizures of 1917 had given them an interest that every government threatened. Their experience of the war was shaped less by the programs of the parties than by the behavior of the armed men who arrived in their villages, and their response included acceptance, evasion, flight, and revolt.

The Bolsheviks' relationship with the peasantry was contradictory from the start. The Decree on Land confirmed peasant redistribution and attracted support, but the government's need to feed cities and army led, from the spring of 1918, to the grain monopoly and forcible procurement described in Section 52.13. In June 1918 the government established Committees of Poor Peasants, which were supposed to identify hoarders and help requisition grain and which were also meant to bring class war to the village. Many committees became instruments of local scores, and the Bolsheviks dissolved them in December 1918 and shifted toward the "middle peasant," whom Lenin described as the crucial figure. Requisition detachments, composed of urban workers and soldiers, searched farms and confiscated grain, sometimes leaving too little for seed or subsistence. Peasants hid grain, reduced the area they sowed, sold on black markets, attacked requisition parties, and killed officials. The Soviet government in turn sent punitive expeditions and took hostages.

The Whites faced parallel problems. Their mobilizations were resented, their land policy was ambiguous, and their armies requisitioned and sometimes looted. In Siberia and the Urals, uprisings against Kolchak's government, some Bolshevik-led and others independent, forced the diversion of troops. In southern Russia, peasants and Cossacks resisted Denikin's mobilizations and requisitions. The Cossack population, which had initially divided, was alienated by Bolshevik policies in early 1919. A directive of January 1919 by Bolshevik party organs called for severe measures against Cossack elites, including mass confiscation and execution, a policy often described as "decossackization," and although it was partly reversed within weeks, it contributed to the Upper Don uprising of March–June 1919, in which tens of thousands of Cossacks rose against the Red Army, opening a gap in the front that aided Denikin's advance. Historians debate the extent to which the January directive was implemented and how far the uprising reflected longstanding Cossack grievances.

"Greens" is a general term for armed groups that opposed both Reds and Whites, sometimes called "third force" movements. They included local self-defense forces, deserters' bands, peasant insurgents, and larger movements with political leaders. In Ukraine, Makhno's Revolutionary Insurgent Army, based in Huliaipole and inspired by anarchist ideas of local self-rule, fought German and Hetmanate forces in 1918, Petliura's troops, Denikin's army in 1919, and, in alliance with the Bolsheviks, Wrangel in 1920, before being crushed by the Red Army in 1921. Other leaders, such as Nykyfor Hryhoriv, who revolted against the Bolsheviks in May 1919 and whose forces were involved in pogroms, were warlords with shifting alliances. Large rural rebellions, such as the Chapan War in the Middle Volga in March 1919 against requisitioning and the later Tambov uprising of 1920–1921, examined in Chapter 53, showed the depth of rural opposition to War Communism.

The Greens complicate any account of the war as a simple contest of ideologies. Peasants often chose among bad options, sometimes tolerating the Bolsheviks because they feared a restoration of the landlords and sometimes rising against them because requisitioning threatened survival. For the Bolsheviks, whose revolutionary legitimacy rested on the claim to represent workers and peasants, peasant rebellion was a political problem as well as a military one, and it shaped the decision in 1921 to replace requisition with a tax in kind. The rural war also left a legacy of violence that fed into the later confrontation between the state and the village in the collectivization of the 1930s (Chapter 56), though it would be an error to treat that later event as inevitable.

Section 52.11

The Cheka and the Construction of Revolutionary Coercion

The Bolsheviks inherited a revolutionary tradition that distrusted the old police and courts, and they dismantled the imperial judicial system and the police within weeks of seizing power. They also came to power facing strikes by government employees, sabotage, and conspiracy, and they responded with improvised institutions. In late 1917 they established revolutionary tribunals and, on 7 (20) December 1917, the All-Russian Extraordinary Commission for Combating Counter-Revolution and Sabotage, known by its Russian acronym, the Cheka, under Felix Dzerzhinsky, a Polish-born revolutionary who had spent years in tsarist prisons. It was meant to be a small investigative body.

Historical Vocabulary
Cheka (VChK)

Definition: The Cheka, from the Russian initials Ch-K for "Extraordinary Commission," was the Bolshevik government's political police from December 1917. Its personnel were called Chekists. It was formally subordinate to Sovnarkom, but local Cheka bodies often acted with substantial independence.

Historical context: The Cheka's powers expanded rapidly: from investigation to arrest, imprisonment, and, from 1918, summary execution without trial. It acquired its own armed units, ran detention camps, and created "special departments" to monitor the Red Army. It was reorganized in 1922 as the State Political Directorate (GPU), a subject treated in Chapter 53.

Why it matters: The Cheka was the first of a succession of Soviet security agencies and established practices, such as extrajudicial punishment and the category of "counterrevolutionary" offense defined by political rather than legal criteria, that later agencies, including the NKVD examined in Chapter 57, inherited.

Several circumstances drove its growth. The war's progress made opposition into armed rebellion. In January and February 1918 the government's "Socialist Fatherland in Danger" decree authorized the shooting of enemy agents and saboteurs, and Chekists began to carry out executions that spring. The death penalty, abolished in October 1917 by the Second Congress, was restored for revolutionary tribunals in June 1918. The government suppressed anarchist headquarters in Moscow in April 1918. In June, it expelled Mensheviks and Right SRs from the All-Russian Central Executive Committee. In July 1918 the crisis escalated. The Left SRs, who opposed Brest-Litovsk and the grain policy, assassinated the German ambassador, Count Wilhelm von Mirbach, on 6 July in an attempt to provoke renewed war with Germany, and rose briefly in Moscow, arresting Dzerzhinsky for some hours. Latvian riflemen and other loyal units suppressed the uprising within a day or two. The Fifth Congress of Soviets, meeting at the same time, adopted the first constitution of the Russian Soviet Federative Socialist Republic on 10 July, which disenfranchised former exploiters, clergy, and police and weighted urban workers' votes more heavily than the peasantry's. After the uprising, Bolshevik authorities arrested many Left SR delegates and excluded from the soviets those Left SR organizations and representatives associated with the revolt. The suppression of what remained of the Bolshevik–Left SR partnership, which had already lost its place in Sovnarkom when the Left SRs resigned in March 1918, accelerated the movement toward one-party Bolshevik rule, although other socialist groups continued a precarious legal or semi-legal existence for some time.

The Cheka's methods included mass arrest, the taking of hostages from "bourgeois" or officer families, imprisonment in camps, and execution, often by regional bodies with little oversight. Its victims included active opponents, saboteurs, conspirators, and large numbers of people identified by class origin, profession, or association: former officers, priests, merchants, and landowners. It also held and punished common criminals, speculators, and workers on strike. In 1918–1919 it expanded the use of concentration camps, a term that in this period meant camps for the isolation of political opponents and hostages. The September 1918 decree "On the Red Terror" called for class enemies to be isolated in such camps but did not itself prescribe forced labor; compulsory labor in camps was regulated by later measures, in 1919 and after. The Solovetsky camp of 1923 and the Gulag proper, which Chapter 57 examines, belong to a later phase, though historians trace their institutional ancestry to these camps.

A central analytical point concerns how emergency institutions became normal. The Cheka was justified as a temporary response to emergency, but it acquired permanent officials, budgets, and a role in many areas of life. Its personnel numbered in the tens of thousands, including its military units. The party debated its powers, and some leaders, including some Bolsheviks, criticized abuses; a number of the party's leaders, including Lenin, defended it. The Cheka's work was never fully brought under judicial control during the war, and the principle that the security organs could act outside the law in the interests of the revolution became part of the party's governance. This principle, rather than any single episode, is the Cheka's most significant legacy. It is also true that the Cheka did not operate in a vacuum: the imperial government and its opponents had a history of political violence, the First World War had normalized mass mobilization and coercion, and the civil war's other armies created their own security services, including the White counterintelligence agencies.

Section 52.12

Red Terror, White Terror, and Revolutionary Violence

Violence did not begin with the declaration of Red Terror in September 1918. Revolutionary violence had been present throughout 1917, in mutinies, lynchings, and the killing of officers, and in the early months of Soviet rule the civil war's violence was expanding on all sides. Murders of Bolshevik officials, the killing of the commissar Volodarsky in June 1918, uprisings, and the executions of prisoners by various armies preceded the formal declaration. The Romanov family, Nicholas II, his wife, their five children, and several attendants, was shot in Yekaterinburg on the night of 16–17 July 1918 by order of the Ural Soviet as White and Czechoslovak forces approached. Historians generally conclude that the decision had the approval of the central leadership in Moscow, though the exact chain of authorization and the documentary evidence remain debated.

The immediate trigger for the formal declaration was a pair of attacks on 30 August 1918. Moisei Uritsky, the head of the Petrograd Cheka, was assassinated by a young man, Leonid Kannegisser, and the same day Lenin was shot and seriously wounded in Moscow. Fanny Kaplan, a former anarchist whom Soviet authorities associated with Socialist Revolutionary circles, was arrested and executed within days after being identified as the assailant; later historians have questioned whether the surviving evidence conclusively establishes that she fired the shots. On 5 September 1918 the government issued the decree "On the Red Terror," discussed in the Primary Source Window, which ordered the isolation of class enemies in camps and the shooting of those connected with White Guard organizations and conspiracies. In Petrograd and other cities, hundreds of hostages and prisoners were shot in reprisal, and the Cheka and its local branches carried out mass arrests and executions in the autumn of 1918. Cheka publications and speeches by officials such as Martyn Latsis, one of its leaders, described the terror in the language of class: the question to ask about an accused person, one such statement said in substance, was his class origin, education, and profession, which would decide his fate. Lenin's own telegrams of the period demanded harsh measures, among them a telegram of August 1918 to officials in Penza ordering that a public example be made by hanging a large number of kulaks and other "bloodsuckers"; the telegram is preserved in the Soviet-published collections of his writings.

No reliable aggregate count of the victims exists. The Cheka's own statistics are partial and inconsistent. Documented Cheka executions and scholarly estimates run into the tens of thousands, and the wave of reprisals of September–October 1918 alone is estimated by some historians at roughly ten to fifteen thousand. Broader totals vary greatly depending on whether reprisals, the suppression of rebellions, deaths in prisons, and deaths in camps are included, and on the chronology adopted. Historians therefore cite ranges, attribute estimates to their sources, and avoid false precision.

White Terror took different forms. It was less centralized and often conducted by regional commanders, atamans, counterintelligence officers, and local militias, and its victims included captured Bolsheviks and Red Army soldiers, suspected sympathizers, members of soviets, Jews, and civilians in areas that had supported the Reds. Kolchak's regime in Siberia carried out mass reprisals and imprisonments, particularly in suppressing the uprisings behind its lines. In southern Russia, Denikin's administration and Cossack units executed Bolsheviks and suspected sympathizers, and Wrangel's government conducted its own executions. Atamans such as Semenov and Kalmykov in the Far East presided over brutal regimes. In Finland in 1918 the victorious Whites killed thousands of Reds in reprisals and held tens of thousands in camps where many died.

Among the most devastating forms of civilian-targeted violence was the wave of anti-Jewish pogroms that accompanied the fighting in Ukraine, Belarus, and southern Russia from 1918 to 1921. Hundreds of pogroms occurred, and conservative estimates place the number of Jews killed at roughly 50,000–60,000, while other scholarship places the toll at 100,000 or more, with many more wounded, raped, and displaced; incomplete records and differences in geography and methodology prevent a precise total. The perpetrators belonged to many forces: units of the Ukrainian People's Republic, including those associated with Petliura's command, as at Proskurov in February 1919; the forces of the warlord Hryhoriv; units of Denikin's Volunteer Army and Cossack formations in 1919; Polish troops in some towns in 1919–1920; peasant insurgent bands; and Red Army units, notably some cavalry formations in 1920. Violence against Jews drew on older antisemitism, discussed in Chapter 43.7, and on wartime accusations that Jews supported Bolshevism, a charge that the White press and propaganda amplified even though Jews were deeply divided politically and many Jewish communities suffered from Bolshevik policies as well. The Soviet government outlawed antisemitism by a decree of July 1918, punished some units, and in some cases disbanded those involved, but it did not prevent all violence by its own forces. Commanders of the Ukrainian People's Republic and of the White armies were less consistent in condemning or restraining pogroms.

Historians compare these forms of violence with care. The Red Terror was a declared policy of a government and, from September 1918, was formally proclaimed, directed by a central apparatus, and justified by a theory of class struggle, although local initiative and excess were substantial. White violence was generally less centrally directed and was seldom theorized as policy, yet it was extensive and often indiscriminate, and the Whites' counterintelligence and punitive units held significant power. Pogroms were frequently carried out by soldiers and bands in conditions of military disorder, though commanders' tolerance or encouragement differed. A comparison should therefore address institutions, intentions, targets, scale, and the degree of central control, without implying that the moral weight of killing depends on who killed. Equating all forms of violence flattens differences that matter for understanding the regimes; dismissing any of them as incidental to the war misrepresents the historical record.

Primary Source Window

Council of People's Commissars, Resolution "On the Red Terror" (5 September 1918)

Creators: Issued by Sovnarkom, signed by the commissars of internal affairs and justice and the manager of the Council's affairs · Date: 5 September 1918 (N.S.) · Place: Moscow · Type: Government decree, published in the official press

Context: The resolution followed the assassination of Uritsky and the attempt on Lenin on 30 August 1918, the Czechoslovak and Komuch advances, the Yaroslavl uprising of July, and the Left SR revolt. It was published in the official newspapers and circulated to local soviets.

Paraphrase of the content (not a verbatim translated quotation):

The resolution states that, in view of reports about attempts on the lives of Soviet leaders and the conspiracies of counterrevolutionaries, the Soviet Republic must be secured against its class enemies by isolating them in concentration camps. It orders that persons connected with White Guard organizations, plots, and uprisings be shot, and that the names of those executed and the reasons for their execution be published.

Analysis: The decree is the central official statement of the policy it names, and it is valuable precisely because it is a public instrument: the government chose to proclaim terror rather than to conceal it. It shows the legal vocabulary of "isolation" and "class enemies," the use of camps, and the linkage of terror to specific attacks. Its limitations are those of any official proclamation. It does not tell historians how many people were killed, how victims were chosen, how closely local organs followed it, or how far its publication requirement was observed in practice; the published lists were incomplete. It records the perspective of the government and says nothing of victims' experience, of the opposition's actions beyond the government's characterization, or of the reactions of ordinary citizens. It must be read together with Cheka records, local reports, memoirs, hostile journalism, and demographic evidence.

Section 52.13

War Communism: Requisition, Nationalization, and Economic Collapse

"War Communism" is a retrospective name. The party's leaders did not use it at the time to describe a coherent system; Lenin applied the label in 1921 when explaining the shift to the New Economic Policy. It refers to the cluster of economic measures taken between mid-1918 and early 1921: the requisition of grain, the nationalization of industry, the centralization of distribution, the suppression of private trade, and the mobilization of labor. Historians disagree about how coherent these measures were, and whether they were improvised responses to emergencies or the product of a doctrine.

Historical Vocabulary
Prodrazverstka (Food Requisition)

Definition: Prodrazverstka was the system, formalized by decree in January 1919, under which the state assigned grain and other food quotas to provinces, districts, and villages, and collected them by compulsion, with little or no payment in goods of equivalent value. Quotas were set on the basis of estimated production rather than on what peasants could spare.

Historical context: It built on the grain monopoly proclaimed in May 1918 and on the earlier practice of the imperial government, which in December 1916 had introduced compulsory grain quotas in wartime. It was replaced in March 1921 by a tax in kind under the New Economic Policy (Chapter 53).

Why it matters: The system fed the cities and army at the cost of peasant livelihoods and motivation, and was a principal source of rural revolts. It is also a key example of continuities between the First World War and the Soviet period.

The central problem was food. By 1918 grain deliveries to cities had collapsed because of transport disruption, the loss of Ukraine and the Volga and Siberian grain regions, the end of the old market incentives, and the peasants' preference to hold grain or sell it informally for goods. In May 1918 Sovnarkom decreed a "food dictatorship," confirming the state's monopoly on grain and authorizing armed detachments of workers to requisition it from "kulaks," the term applied to peasants accused of hoarding. In January 1919, the system of quotas, the prodrazverstka, replaced the earlier decrees. Procurement figures rose in successive agricultural years, from the order of 1.5 million tons in 1918–1919, to over three million in 1919–1920 and, by some estimates, around six million in 1920–1921, though the harvests from which they were taken were shrinking, so the burden on producers was growing faster than the totals suggest. Peasants responded by planting less, as noted in Section 52.10, and the area under cultivation declined.

Industrial policy moved in parallel. The Supreme Council of National Economy (Vesenkha), created in December 1917, directed the nationalization of enterprises. A decree of 28 June 1918 nationalized large-scale industry, partly in response to owners' sabotage and the threat of German acquisition of property, and further decrees in 1919 and November 1920 extended state ownership to smaller enterprises, so that the state formally owned tens of thousands of establishments, many tiny. Central boards (glavki) controlled the supply and distribution of raw materials and finished goods by branch. Private trade was banned in principle, though illegal markets flourished. Rationing was introduced by class category, giving the highest rations to workers in priority industries and the lowest to "bourgeois" households. Money lost its value through inflation, which the government encouraged by printing notes to cover its deficits, and by 1920 wages were paid largely in kind and many services were provided free. Some Communists, including the economists Evgeny Preobrazhensky and Nikolai Bukharin, saw the decline of money as a step toward a moneyless economy.

Labor was also militarized. The 1918 constitution made labor a universal duty for those not exploiting others. In 1920, the Ninth Party Congress endorsed "labor armies," and Trotsky argued for the militarization of labor as a temporary response to the crisis. Demobilized army units were put to work on transport and reconstruction, and trade unions were pressed into state service, provoking the dispute in the party over the role of unions in 1920–1921, treated in Chapter 53.

The results were catastrophic by any measure. Large-scale industrial production in 1920 was a small fraction of its 1913 level, commonly cited as roughly a fifth or less. Cities lost population as workers fled to the countryside in search of food: Petrograd's population fell from around two and a half million to well under a million, and Moscow's declined substantially. Urban residents survived by bartering belongings with peasants, relying on "bagmen" (meshochniki) who carried food into cities in defiance of the ban on private trade, and cultivating plots; official rations were insufficient, and illegal markets such as Moscow's Sukharevka provided a large part of the food supply. Epidemics of typhus, cholera, and influenza killed millions. The drought and famine of 1921–1922, part of the crisis examined in Chapter 53, were intensified by the collapse of agriculture.

Historical Debate

Was War Communism an Emergency Response or an Ideological Project?

The question: Did the Bolsheviks introduce requisitioning, nationalization, and the abolition of markets because the war and economic collapse left them no alternative, or because they believed these measures would carry Russia toward socialism?

Interpretation A — Emergency: Lenin himself in 1921 described War Communism as imposed by war and ruin, and some historians emphasize the improvised and reactive character of the policies: requisition followed the failure of the market and the loss of grain regions; nationalization followed owners' sabotage and the war's demands; rationing and labor controls followed scarcity. On this view, ideology supplied vocabulary and rationalizations, but necessity drove decisions.
Interpretation B — Ideology: Other historians argue that the Bolsheviks pursued a vision of a non-market economy from the start and that the measures went beyond what military need required. They point to the party's own enthusiasm in 1919–1920, as reflected in Bukharin and Preobrazhensky's popular exposition The ABC of Communism (1919–1920), the decrees nationalizing small enterprises long after the main fighting ended, the discussion of abolishing money, and the continued use of labor mobilization into 1920.
Interpretation C — Interaction and continuity: Silvana Malle's study of the economic organization of War Communism argues against both a purely ideological and a purely emergency explanation, stressing the interaction of external events, ideology, class struggle, and social pressures. Lars Lih has illuminated continuities in food-supply policy within the wider food crisis of 1914–1921. Peter Holquist has interpreted 1914–1921 as a "continuum of crisis," tracing food management, surveillance, and political violence across imperial, revolutionary, and Soviet wartime governance. On these views, the question presents a false choice: improvisation and conviction reinforced one another, and the experience of crisis shaped beliefs.

Evidence: Historians use decrees, party congress debates, Vesenkha and Commissariat of Food records, procurement statistics, contemporary writings by Bolshevik economists, peasant letters and reports, and comparisons with the economic controls of other belligerents in 1914–1918.

Points of agreement: Historians agree that the measures arose in conditions of acute crisis, that they were applied with growing coercion, that many party members believed they were steps toward socialism, and that they contributed to the economic collapse and peasant resistance that forced their abandonment in 1921.

Points of disagreement: They disagree about whether the policy's extension after the main military threats had receded in 1920 shows ideological commitment or inertia, about how far the system was centrally planned rather than improvised, and about what weight to give Bolshevik leaders' later accounts.

Why it matters: The answer affects how historians understand the relationship between Bolshevik ideology and Soviet institutions, and whether later Soviet economic policy should be read as a continuation of 1918–1920 impulses or as a response to different circumstances.

Student task: Select two specific measures, for example the grain monopoly and the nationalization decree of November 1920, and evaluate which interpretation each best supports, citing the type of evidence that would be needed to decide.

Section 52.14

Why the Bolsheviks Won—and the Costs of Victory

Explaining Bolshevik victory requires combining several kinds of cause without reducing the outcome to one. The war's result was not predetermined: in autumn 1919 the Red Army's position was extremely precarious, with Denikin's forces near Orel and Yudenich's within sight of Petrograd. But a number of structural features, institutional capacities, and political choices tilted the balance, and most historians accept that several of them were jointly necessary.

Geography, Logistics, and Resources

The Bolsheviks held the central region, with its densest population, its major cities, its arms factories, including the Tula and Petrograd works, its stockpiles inherited from the imperial army, and its railway hub, which enabled interior lines of communication. Their opponents operated on a long periphery, with poor internal communications and less population. Soviet Russia's food supply was precarious, but it controlled enough of the grain-producing center to feed army and cities at a minimum level, and it could shift forces from front to front.

Organization and Institutions

The Bolsheviks were a disciplined party with tens of thousands of committed members who could be mobilized for military, administrative, and propaganda work. They used the structure of the soviets and the party to administer the territory they held, created a conscript army with a centralized command, a political apparatus, and the extensive use of professional officers, and built coercive institutions, the Cheka, tribunals, and requisition detachments. Their propaganda, including posters and agitation trains, connected the war to patriotic and class appeals; one of the best-known posters of the war, by the artist Dmitry Moor in 1920, asked the viewer directly whether he had volunteered for the Red Army. The White armies relied on officer corps and volunteers, had a much weaker political apparatus, and ran a more fragmented administration.

Political Divisions Among the Opponents

The Whites were divided among themselves and unable to coordinate. They disagreed about political aims, had no program that appealed to the peasant majority, and often alienated non-Russian peoples and local populations. Socialist anti-Bolsheviks were hostile to the military's conservatism, and the military was hostile to socialist governments. The Whites' reliance on the peripheries, where population density was lower and local loyalties differed, hampered mobilization. Their agents also sometimes behaved in ways that reinforced the Bolshevik narrative of a reactionary restoration.

Nationality, Land, and the Peasantry

Bolshevik policy on land, which confirmed peasant holdings, and on nationalities, which formally recognized a right to self-determination and then built federal structures, gave them a political advantage over opponents who refused most national claims and equivocated on land. Peasants who resented requisitions often feared a landlord restoration more, and many, including some who rebelled against the Bolsheviks in one season, fought or refrained from fighting the Reds in another because the Whites were worse for their interests. This calculation of lesser evils is among the central elements of the outcome. It did not mean enthusiastic support for the Bolsheviks, and the peasant revolts of 1920–1921 showed the limits of tolerance.

Intervention, Leadership, and Contingency

Foreign intervention, as Section 52.5 argued, was too limited and divided to be decisive, and it contributed to Bolshevik narratives. The leadership of Lenin and Trotsky, whatever their flaws, enabled quick and ruthless decisions; the Whites' commanders made strategic errors, notably the overextension of Denikin's advance and the lack of coordination among armies. Contingent events mattered: the timing of the armistice in Europe, the unexpected Cossack uprising on the Don in 1919, the Mamontov raid, Makhno's attack on Denikin's rear, and Poland's war in 1920 each altered the military balance, and a different sequence might have produced a different result. Historians who stress structural factors and those who stress contingency do not usually disagree about the facts, but about how much weight to give each; the account here treats structure as having made Bolshevik survival more probable without making it certain.

The Costs of Victory

The victory left a devastated country. The upheavals of 1918–1922 caused millions of excess deaths from combat, political violence, epidemic disease, and famine. Demographic totals vary greatly according to territory, chronology, and method, but disease and hunger killed far more people than battlefield combat. Typhus, cholera, and influenza spread through armies and cities. Perhaps one to two million people left the former empire permanently, forming the Russian emigration and including a large share of the educated and professional classes. Millions of children were orphaned or abandoned, forming a population of homeless children who became a prominent social problem in the early 1920s. Industry produced a fraction of its pre-war output, agriculture had contracted, and cities had lost much of their population. The war had also transformed Soviet politics: the party had more members and many of them had military and coercive experience; the Red Army and the Cheka had become permanent institutions; and the political system had moved from a multiparty soviet system toward single-party rule. In many places the population was exhausted and, in the countryside and in some factories, openly hostile. The regime had survived, but its leaders knew that it had not yet secured stable support, a recognition that led to the retreat of 1921 examined in Chapter 53. The conclusion of the Civil War did not end the violence: rebellions, the Basmachi war, conflicts in the Caucasus, and the Kronstadt uprising of March 1921 continued into the next phase.

Multiple Perspectives

Requisition, Conscription, and Terror: The Civil War as Experienced by Different Groups

Peasant households in the grain provinces: For farm families on the Volga or in central Russia, the war meant visits from armed requisition detachments, conscription of sons, and demands for horses and carts. Many welcomed the 1917 land redistribution and feared a return of the landlords, but the quota system threatened seed grain and survival. Evidence from Chekist and party reports, peasant petitions, and the pattern of rebellions suggests responses ranging from hiding grain and cutting sowing to armed resistance.
Urban workers and the city population: Industrial workers were the Bolsheviks' declared base, and many enlisted, joined requisition detachments, or took administrative posts. They also endured falling rations, cold, closed factories, and disease, and many left for the villages. Strikes, protests by Menshevik-influenced workers, and the fall in industrial production show that working-class support was conditional and eroded as the war continued. Middle-class urbanites, priests, merchants, and former officials faced ration discrimination, housing seizures, hostage-taking, and arrest.
Former imperial officers and Red military specialists: Tens of thousands of officers served in the Red Army, some from patriotism or conviction, others from fear for their families; others joined the Whites; many were killed by one side or the other. Their documents, including memoirs written afterward, show divided loyalties and a precarious position under political surveillance.
Jewish townspeople in Ukraine and Belarus: Jewish communities, targeted by pogroms from forces of several armies and by bandit groups, organized self-defense, appealed to commanders, and sought refuge in larger towns. Many Jews supported or joined socialist parties of different kinds, and some joined the Red Army, while others saw Bolshevik policies as threatening their commerce and religious institutions; Jews were thus neither a political bloc nor merely victims, but a diverse population caught between armed forces.
Cossack communities and non-Russian peoples of the borderlands: Cossacks, Ukrainians, Georgians, Kazakhs, Bashkirs, and others experienced the war through their own political projects, whether autonomy, independence, or cultural recognition. Their relations with Reds and Whites shifted with the treatment they received.

These perspectives show why the civil war's meaning varied with social position and region. Power, coercion, and exclusion were distributed unequally, and sources produced by the government, the army, or the press represent some of these groups much better than others.

Cause and Effect

Bolshevik Survival in the Civil War

Long-Term Conditions

The disintegration of the imperial army and administration during the First World War; the peasant land revolution of 1917; the economic collapse and food crisis; the weakness of liberal institutions; and the geography of the central region and its railways.

Intermediate Developments

The Bolshevik seizure of the capitals; the dissolution of the Constituent Assembly; the peace of Brest-Litovsk; the emergence of fragmented White governments; the Czechoslovak revolt and Allied intervention.

Short-Term Triggers

The Volga crisis of summer 1918; Kolchak's, Denikin's, and Yudenich's offensives in 1919; the Polish war of 1920; the German collapse of November 1918, which changed the international situation.

Effects

Preservation of Soviet power; creation of the Red Army and the Cheka; requisition and economic collapse; reconquest of much of the former empire's borderlands; a militarized, single-party political order.

Historical complexity: Geography without organization, organization without political appeal, or coercion without peasant calculation of lesser evils would each have been insufficient. The Whites' fragmentation, the Bolsheviks' institutional capacity, and a series of contingent events combined, and no single cause can explain the outcome.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Wartime State Practices from 1914 to 1921

What Stayed the Same

  • State control of grain, from the wartime measures of the imperial and Provisional governments to the Bolshevik monopoly and quota system.
  • Mass conscription, military requisition, and the use of reprisals against civilians, which were features of all armies in the First World War and the civil war.
  • Reliance on professional officers of the imperial army, and the persistence of rail transport as the principal military infrastructure.
  • Peasant resistance to state demands, and the countryside's concern with land and local autonomy.

What Changed

  • The state itself: a revolutionary party-led government replaced the monarchy and the Provisional Government, and claimed to rule in the name of workers and peasants through soviets.
  • The scale and ideological justification of coercion: terror was declared policy, class origin became a criterion of punishment, and the Cheka acted outside ordinary courts.
  • Property and markets: private ownership of large and then small industry was abolished, and private trade was banned in principle.
  • The party and its relationship to the military: a conspiratorial party became one that commanded armies and administered territory.

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing continuities with the First World War prevents the civil war from being treated as a purely ideological creation, while recognizing changes prevents the opposite error of treating the Bolshevik state as merely a continuation of earlier practices.

Everyday Life and Social History

The City and the Village in the Civil War Years

For a resident of Petrograd or Moscow in 1919–1920, daily life centered on food, fuel, and shelter. Ration cards were issued by category, and the rations were insufficient; residents bartered clothing, furniture, and books for flour and potatoes, traveled to villages with sacks, and bought food at illegal markets, often at the risk of confiscation. Winter heating was scarce, and wooden buildings and furniture were burned for fuel. Housing policy compressed bourgeois families into fewer rooms and settled workers in confiscated apartments. Newspapers, theaters, and schools continued under severe constraints; the government launched literacy campaigns and expanded access to education for workers and children. Women's roles expanded: many worked in offices, hospitals, and factories, served as nurses and, in smaller numbers, as soldiers, and in 1919 the party established the Women's Department (Zhenotdel) to organize women. Epidemics were severe; Lenin, speaking in late 1919, warned that either socialism would defeat the louse or the louse would defeat socialism.

For a peasant family in the same years, the experience depended on location and on which army had last been through. Households might hide grain in pits, drive livestock into forests, or cut back on sowing; they might send a son to the Red Army or lose him to a White mobilization or a Green band. Religious practice continued, though the Bolshevik state seized church property and arrested clergy in some places; later Soviet antireligious campaigns are examined in Chapter 58.3. Children's experiences included the loss of parents and the hunger that sent many into the streets as homeless children by the early 1920s. Sources for ordinary life include diaries, letters, memoirs, local reports, photographs, and later oral histories; they are scarce and uneven, favor the literate and urban, and should not be generalized without attention to region and class.

Regional Context

Ukraine, 1917–1921

Seen from Kyiv, Kharkiv, or the villages of the Dnieper, the war looked very different from the war seen from Moscow. Ukraine contained rich grain land, major industrial regions in the Donbas and Kryvyi Rih, a large peasant population, substantial Jewish and Polish minorities, and a national movement whose leaders were mostly socialists. Between 1917 and 1921, the Central Rada, the Bolsheviks, the German army, the Hetmanate, the Directory, the Volunteer Army, the Red Army, Poland, Makhno's insurgents, and numerous local otamans exercised authority at various times. Kyiv changed hands more than a dozen times, according to commonly cited counts, in the period.

Ukrainian national governments struggled to build effective armies and administrations in this environment, faced peasants' resistance to requisitions and conscription by all sides, and were divided over alliances with Germany, Poland, and the Entente. The Bolsheviks, whose Ukrainian party organization was weak and whose support was concentrated in industrial cities and among Russian-speaking workers, found it difficult to win peasant support until they combined military power with concessions on land and, by 1920, a more accommodating approach to Ukrainian language and culture, a policy that would develop into the program discussed in Chapter 53.7. The experience included the pogroms of 1919, Makhno's attempt to build a stateless peasant society, and the Great Famine of 1921–1922 that struck southern Ukraine and the Volga. The memory of this period, and the arguments over whether the Ukrainian People's Republic was a legitimate government defeated by invasion or an unsuccessful experiment, remain central to modern Ukrainian and Russian historical discourse, and historians treat these later uses of the period separately from its documented events.

Russia and the World

The Civil War and the International Order of 1918–1921

The civil war occurred at the moment when the First World War was ending and the postwar order was being built. The Paris Peace Conference did not invite any Russian government, and the Allies' policy toward Russia oscillated between support for the Whites, negotiation, and withdrawal. Woodrow Wilson's Fourteen Points had included a statement on Russia's right to determine its own development, and the contradiction between this principle and the presence of Allied troops on Russian soil became a source of controversy within the Allied countries.

For Bolshevik leaders, the revolution was part of an international movement. They proclaimed the Communist International in Moscow in March 1919, as short-lived Soviet republics emerged in Hungary (March–August 1919) and Bavaria (April–May 1919), and the Second Comintern Congress in 1920 adopted conditions for admission to the organization. The Polish campaign of 1920 was conceived by Lenin and others as a possible bridge to revolution in Germany. Its failure weakened the expectation of imminent revolution in the West and encouraged a shift toward consolidation of Soviet power in a single country, though that formula belongs to a later chapter (54.7). At the same time, the Soviet government sought trade and recognition: it signed treaties with the Baltic states and Finland in 1920, with Persia and Turkey in 1921, and began negotiations that led to the Anglo-Soviet trade agreement of 1921. Russia was thus connected to the wider world through war, ideology, diplomacy, and commerce, and Soviet leaders adapted to the failure of the world revolution without abandoning the hope of it.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how Bolshevik rule, established in Petrograd in October 1917, survived a multi-sided civil war and how the experience of that war transformed the regime. The central historical problem was not simply why the Bolsheviks defeated the Whites but how a government with limited territory and unreliable armed force in early 1918 outlasted a series of military, political, and social challenges, and what it became in the process.

The chapter began with the Constituent Assembly, whose election in November 1917 produced a plurality for the Socialist Revolutionaries and whose single session in January 1918 ended in dissolution by decree. The episode set competing claims of electoral and soviet legitimacy against each other, closed the possibility of a parliamentary settlement, and gave the Bolsheviks' opponents a principle around which to organize.

The Treaty of Brest-Litovsk, signed in March 1918 after the Germans resumed their offensive, showed the priority the Bolshevik leadership, under Lenin's pressure, gave to survival over territory and over ideological preference. The treaty cost the former empire large territories and resources, split the party, ended the coalition with the Left SRs, and left German forces in control of much of the west until their collapse in November 1918.

Opposition to Soviet power took the form of the White movements, which were a collection of regional armies and governments rather than a unified movement. Their leaders disagreed about political aims and often alienated the populations on whose support they depended. The revolt of the Czechoslovak Legion in May 1918 and the foreign intervention that followed widened the war, but intervention's military effect was limited and its motives changed after the armistice. The Bolsheviks used it politically, and the Whites' dependence on foreign supply became a political liability.

The Red Army, built from volunteer units into a conscript force with former imperial officers and political commissars, was the principal instrument of military victory. Together with control of the central region and its railways, it allowed the Soviet government to defeat the White offensives of 1919: Kolchak's from the east, Denikin's from the south, and Yudenich's from the northwest. The following year Wrangel's Crimean stronghold fell. The campaigns showed that Soviet survival was repeatedly in question, and that outcomes depended on decisions and contingencies as well as structure.

The civil war was also a set of national and social wars. Finland, the Baltic states, Poland, Ukraine, the Caucasus republics, Central Asia, and the Volga-Ural region produced independent or autonomous governments, wars of state-building, and conflicts with Bolsheviks, Whites, and each other. Peasants, Cossacks, and local insurgents, including the Greens and Makhno's army, resisted all armies that requisitioned and conscripted. Treating the war as a contest between Reds and Whites alone obscures the experiences of most of the population.

The war reshaped Bolshevik institutions. The Cheka, created in December 1917 as an investigative commission, became an agency of arrest, hostage-taking, and summary execution. The Left SR rebellion of July 1918 and the 1918 constitution marked movement toward single-party rule. The Red Terror, formally declared in September 1918, was state policy, while White Terror was generally more decentralized but also extensive, and pogroms killed large numbers of Jewish civilians at the hands of soldiers of many armies. Historians compare these forms of violence by attention to institutions, scale, and intention while declining to equate them or to minimize any of them, and the estimates of victims remain uncertain.

War Communism, a retrospective label for the grain monopoly, requisition quotas, nationalization, rationing, and labor mobilization of 1918–1921, was both an emergency response and, for many party members, a step toward socialism, as historians arguing from emergency, ideology, and continuity with wartime practice have debated. It fed the army and cities at the cost of rural livelihood and urban industry, and it contributed to economic collapse and the rebellions that forced its abandonment.

The Bolshevik victory resulted from the combination of geographic and logistical advantages, organizational capacity, a flexible policy on land and nationalities relative to the Whites, the fragmentation of opponents, peasants' calculation of lesser evils, the limits of foreign intervention, and contingent events. Its costs were demographic loss, emigration, economic collapse, social hostility, and a militarized, increasingly single-party state. These costs set the agenda for the retreat of 1921 and the construction of the Soviet Union examined in Chapter 53.

Historiographically, the chapter has shown how the war has been interpreted as a defense against intervention, a tragedy of revolutionary violence, a set of overlapping civil wars, and a crucible of Soviet institutions, and it has asked students to weigh each against the evidence. It has also shown how later memory, Soviet, émigré, and post-Soviet, in Russia and in the successor states, has reshaped the war's meaning, an issue taken up in the thematic reviews of the textbook's back matter.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it explains how an uncertain seizure of power became a durable state and why that state had the character it did. The war converted a government that held two capitals into one that governed most of the former empire's center, and it did so through institutions, the Red Army, the Cheka, the requisitioning apparatus, and the single-party state, whose origins lay in emergency but whose life extended far beyond it. Understanding these origins helps students see both what the Bolsheviks inherited and what they made.

The chapter also changed the map and the memory of the former empire. Finland, the Baltic states, and Poland emerged as independent states; most other borderland peoples were reincorporated into Soviet structures that preserved forms of national identity; and the Russian emigration carried one version of the war abroad while Soviet institutions built another at home. The competing memories, of the Whites as patriots or reactionaries, of the Reds as liberators or oppressors, of the Ukrainian People's Republic and of the Baltic and Caucasian governments, are themselves evidence about later societies and are examined in the textbook's Historical Memory appendix. They should be distinguished from the documented course of events.

What continued afterward was both coercion and negotiation. The party leaders who emerged from the war retained habits of mobilization and command; they also learned, in 1921, that the peasantry and the workers could not be governed indefinitely by requisition and force. Chapter 53 follows the retreat to the New Economic Policy and the construction of the Soviet Union, and Chapters 54 to 57 follow the struggle for power and the later transformation of the state. It would be a mistake to treat any of these later developments as the inevitable product of the civil war. The war created conditions and precedents, but later leaders made choices, and alternatives existed at each stage.

Questions remain open. Historians continue to debate the weight of ideology and emergency in War Communism, the scale and chain of command of the Terror, the numbers killed in the pogroms and in the famine and epidemics of the period, and the extent to which the war should be seen as one conflict or several. Opening archives and new regional studies continue to refine the picture. The chapter's central lesson, that the outcome of a revolutionary war was shaped by structure, institutions, social conflict, and contingency, and that the experience of the war reshaped the regime that won it, is one that students should carry into the study of the Soviet state in the chapters that follow.
