Part XII — The Russian Federation
67

Yeltsin, Market Transformation, and the Constitutional Crisis

A History of Russia: From the Eurasian World to the Russian Federation

Chapter Opening

On 2 January 1992, the Russian government removed price controls from most consumer goods. The decision ended one of the defining features of the Soviet economy, administered prices, and began the most contested economic experiment in modern Russian history.

In the first weeks of 1992, accounts of Russian city life describe a new and unsettling scene. Shelves in state shops that had been empty began to fill, but at prices that most households could not easily afford. On pavements and in underground passages, pensioners, teachers, and engineers stood with a few possessions, a jar of preserves, a pair of shoes, a book, hoping to earn something from passers-by. Markets, kiosks, and small traders multiplied almost overnight. For some, especially younger and mobile people in large cities, the change opened opportunities that the Soviet system had forbidden. For many others, it meant that the savings and expectations of a lifetime were suddenly worth far less. The same weeks produced both fear and possibility, and historians of the period must hold both in view.

This chapter studies the first seven years of the independent Russian Federation, from the beginning of price liberalization in January 1992 to the aftermath of the financial crisis of August 1998 and the early months of 1999. Chapter 66 explained how the Soviet Union ended in December 1991. This chapter begins from what that ending left behind: a new state, with Boris Yeltsin as president, inheriting Soviet ministries, enterprises, armed forces, debts, and constitutional rules, but no longer inside a union of republics or governed by a ruling party. The governing question is why the effort to build a market economy and a new constitutional order produced both substantial institutional change and severe economic, social, and political instability.

The chapter treats four connected transformations. The first is economic: price liberalization, stabilization policy, and the transfer of state property into private hands through voucher privatization and the later loans-for-shares program. The second is social: falling output, unpaid wages, collapsing savings, widening inequality, and a demographic crisis that touched families in every region. The third is constitutional: the struggle between President and parliament that ended in the armed confrontation of October 1993 and the adoption of a new constitution that December. The fourth concerns the state's territorial integrity: the negotiation of federal relationships with regions such as Tatarstan, and the First Chechen War of 1994–1996, which began when Moscow used force against a region that claimed independence.

These developments involved many people beyond the Kremlin. Factory directors and shop-floor workers, collective-farm chairs and household gardeners, bankers, miners, teachers, soldiers, mothers of conscripts, Chechen civilians, journalists, and regional governors all experienced the transition from different positions. Evidence for their experiences comes from official statistics, which have real limitations in this period, decrees and laws, parliamentary transcripts, election results, international financial and observer reports, human-rights investigations, memoirs by participants, surveys, press coverage, and oral testimony. Students will repeatedly be asked to ask who produced a source, for what purpose, and what it cannot show.

The period is also deeply contested in historical memory. In Russia the 1990s are remembered in different and sometimes opposed ways: as years of freedom, open debate, and new opportunity; as years of poverty, humiliation, and disorder; or as both at once. Political leaders and movements since have drawn on these memories for their own purposes. Scholars disagree, sometimes sharply, about whether alternative policies were available, how much the outcomes owed to policy choices rather than to the inherited Soviet economy, and how far the methods of reform weakened the democratic institutions they were meant to create. This chapter presents those disagreements and the evidence on which they rest, and it avoids treating either triumphant or catastrophic accounts as settled.

The chapter also connects Russian developments to wider processes. The reformers drew on international economic advice and sought assistance from the International Monetary Fund, the World Bank, and Western governments, while other post-communist states in Central and Eastern Europe chose their own paths. Russia's financial crisis of 1998 was shaped by global market shocks. The Chechen war attracted international criticism and involved European human-rights institutions. Readers should remember, finally, that in 1992 nobody knew what the Russian Federation would become. This chapter explains decisions in the circumstances in which they were made and leaves the political developments after 1999 to Chapter 68.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain what the Russian Federation inherited from the Soviet Union in institutions, property, obligations, and constitutional rules, and distinguish legal succession from practical continuity.
  2. Describe the policies of price liberalization, stabilization, and privatization, and distinguish liberalizing prices from creating competitive markets.
  3. Distinguish voucher privatization (1992–1994) from the loans-for-shares program (1995) and from later secondary-market concentration of ownership.
  4. Analyze the social consequences of the transition, including wage arrears, pension insecurity, inequality, and the rise in mortality, while recognizing regional and group differences.
  5. Evaluate the competing constitutional claims of President Yeltsin and the Congress and Supreme Soviet in 1993 and trace the stages of the crisis.
  6. Assess the Constitution of December 1993 and the institutional balance it created.
  7. Compare Moscow's negotiated arrangements with Tatarstan to its confrontation with Chechnya, and trace the course of the First Chechen War to the Khasavyurt agreements.
  8. Interpret the 1996 presidential election, distinguishing documented unfairness from unproven claims of falsified results.
  9. Explain the causes and immediate consequences of the August 1998 default and devaluation using fiscal, external, and political factors.
  10. Evaluate competing interpretations of the Yeltsin era without assuming that later Russian political developments were predetermined.

Historical Context

The Soviet economy that Russia inherited had been organized around state ownership, central planning, and administered prices, as Chapters 55 and 63 explained. Enterprises received plans and supplies through ministries, prices often bore little relation to scarcity, and many factories were built as parts of tightly linked, union-wide production chains. Cities such as Magnitogorsk or Norilsk had grown around single industries; military production absorbed a large share of skilled labor and resources. By the late 1980s, as Chapter 65 showed, the system was suffering from shortages, hidden inflation, a large budget deficit, and falling confidence. Gorbachev's reforms had loosened central control without replacing it, and Chapter 66 traced how the political union collapsed in 1991. Russia therefore began independent life with an economy that was already in disarray, and with the added disruption of the end of the Soviet trading area.

The political inheritance was equally unsettled. Russia's institutions dated from the late Soviet period: a Congress of People's Deputies elected in 1990 under partly competitive rules, a smaller Supreme Soviet chosen by that Congress, a directly elected president (Yeltsin, in June 1991), and a Constitution dating from 1978 that had been amended many times. These bodies had been created for a different purpose, the contest within a Soviet federation, and the document had no settled answer to what should happen when president and legislature disagreed. In August 1991 they had acted together against the coup, but their cooperation did not last. At the same time Russia's own internal federal structure, a mixture of ethnically defined republics and ordinary regions inherited from Soviet administration, needed redefinition now that the central Soviet state no longer held it together.

Internationally, the early 1990s were a moment of great optimism and great uncertainty. The Cold War had ended; Western governments and international institutions were eager to support reform, though they disagreed about how much assistance to give and under what conditions. Central European states such as Poland and Czechoslovakia had begun their own market reforms in 1990–1991 and offered examples, but not templates: Russia was larger, more industrialized in ways tied to defense production, and more dependent on state-run supply networks, and it had no historical memory of functioning private markets for most of seven decades. Students should keep in mind that Russian reformers, their critics, and foreign advisers were all working with limited information and under severe time pressure. Evaluating their choices requires neither assuming that success was assured nor that failure was inevitable.

Division I — The Russian Federation Inherits the Soviet State

Section 67.1

The Russian Federation at Independence: Statehood, Institutions, and Soviet Inheritance

When the Soviet Union ceased to exist on 26 December 1991, the Russian Soviet Federative Socialist Republic, discussed in Chapter 66, became the independent Russian Federation. A law taking effect on 25 December 1991 renamed the state the Russian Federation (Russia), and constitutional amendments adopted by the Congress in 1992 confirmed the change. Russia comprised roughly seventeen million square kilometers and a population of about 148 million; according to the last Soviet census (1989), ethnic Russians made up about four-fifths of that population, while a large minority included Tatars, Ukrainians, Bashkirs, Chuvash, Chechens, and dozens of other peoples. Roughly twenty-five million ethnic Russians now lived in other newly independent states. These facts mattered politically from the start: the new state had to define its citizenry, borders, and obligations toward co-ethnics abroad without adopting an ethnic definition of citizenship.

Russia's international status rested on a claim of continuity. In December 1991 Yeltsin notified the United Nations that Russia continued the Soviet Union's membership, including its permanent seat on the Security Council, and no state objected. In agreements among the former republics, Russia accepted responsibility for the Soviet Union's external debts in return for control of its foreign assets, an arrangement sometimes called the "zero option." Nuclear weapons were gradually concentrated on Russian territory, with Ukraine, Belarus, and Kazakhstan agreeing to give up weapons stationed on their soil in the early 1990s. A Russian Ministry of Defense and armed forces were formally created in May 1992 from the Soviet military's units on Russian soil and elsewhere.

Legal succession, however, was not the same as the practical transfer of institutions. Soviet union-level ministries disappeared or were absorbed into Russian bodies, but their personnel, budgets, files, and habits often transferred with them. Many senior officials remained in office, a continuity encouraged by the lack of alternative trained administrators and by the speed of events. The new state had few functioning institutions of its own: a border service, a tax system, a customs regime, and a currency policy all had to be constructed or adapted. This gap between declared authority and administrative capacity is a recurring theme of the chapter.

Questions of citizenship and territory were among the first practical problems. The RSFSR law on citizenship of November 1991 granted citizenship to permanent residents of Russian territory who did not opt out, a civic rather than ethnic criterion that reflected the multinational composition of the population. The new state's frontiers with its neighbors were mostly former administrative boundaries between Soviet republics. They had never been designed as international borders, and many were undemarcated and unguarded. The Kaliningrad region became an exclave separated from the rest of Russia by Lithuania and Belarus. Parliamentary resolutions in 1992–1993 questioned the status of Crimea and of Sevastopol, home to the Black Sea Fleet, which the Russian government did not adopt as policy; the division of that fleet and the status of its base were negotiated with Ukraine over several years (see Chapter 70 for the later history).

The armed forces illustrated the distinction between legal and practical succession. Soviet units on Russian territory, and many stationed abroad, became Russian responsibilities. The former Group of Soviet Forces in Germany and troops in the Baltic states were withdrawn, the latter by 1994, a process that required housing and employment for returning officers in a period of shrinking budgets. A nominal joint command of the Commonwealth of Independent States gave way in 1992 to separate national forces, and Russian troops remained in conflict zones in Moldova, Georgia, and Tajikistan under various mandates. Control of nuclear weapons was centralized under the Russian president, though the safety and security of the arsenal and its personnel were a concern for Russian and foreign governments alike.

Continuity in personnel was also striking. The KGB was divided in late 1991 and 1992 into separate agencies, among them a foreign intelligence service and the domestic security bodies that later became the Federal Security Service, but there was no systematic vetting or purge of the kind adopted in some Central European states. Many ministry officials, enterprise directors, and regional party secretaries remained in positions of authority, now as state administrators or businessmen. This continuity meant that reform policies were carried out by an apparatus whose habits and interests had been formed under the old system, a fact that shaped the course of privatization and regulation.

Section 67.2

The Political and Constitutional Inheritance of the RSFSR

Russia's political institutions in 1992 rested on a constitution adopted in 1978 for a Soviet republic and amended repeatedly from 1989 onward. At its summit stood the Congress of People's Deputies, a body of roughly a thousand members elected in March 1990, which met several times a year and held formal supremacy. It chose from its own ranks a smaller, standing Supreme Soviet, the working legislature, led by a chairman. In 1991 the offices of president and vice president were added by popular election, giving Yeltsin a direct mandate. A Constitutional Court was created in the autumn of 1991 to adjudicate disputes among institutions.

These institutions had not been designed to function together. The Congress claimed the right to amend the constitution by a two-thirds majority and to review the acts of other bodies; the president claimed a popular mandate and executive authority over government; the Supreme Soviet's chairman, Ruslan Khasbulatov, controlled legislative business and had his own political ambitions. Because the Congress could amend the constitution at will, no stable allocation of powers existed. Each side could argue that the existing law supported its position, and each could change the law when it held a majority.

In late 1991, as the Soviet Union fell, the Congress granted Yeltsin special powers for a limited period, permitting him to rule by decree on economic reform and to appoint government ministers without parliamentary confirmation. That grant was a political bargain made in an emergency. As the effects of the reforms became clear, deputies began to withdraw support and to seek to restore the Congress's authority. The conflict that resulted is analyzed in Division IV; students should note here that its roots were structural as well as personal, and that it concerned unresolved questions of constitutional design that no one in 1991 had time to settle.

The deputies themselves were not a unified bloc. Many had been elected in 1990 as supporters of reform and remained so; others were communists, nationalists, or representatives of industrial and agricultural interests; many shifted allegiance as conditions changed. Treating "parliament" as a single opponent of "reform" would misrepresent a body whose members held a range of views and whose positions moved over time.

The numbers and powers of these bodies help explain the later conflict. The Congress of People's Deputies had about 1,000 members, elected in March 1990 in partly competitive elections in which its 1,068 seats were elected in constituencies (900 territorial and 168 national-territorial), without the organizational-seat reservations used in the 1989 all-Union Congress; it met in sessions of several days and could take up any question within the state's competence. From its members it chose a two-chamber Supreme Soviet of roughly two hundred and fifty deputies, which sat continuously and prepared legislation. Under the constitution as amended, the Congress could override the Supreme Soviet and revise the constitution itself, so the highest authority was a body that met only occasionally and whose procedures were open to manipulation by its leadership.

The constitutional text had been altered so many times by 1993 that it contained contradictory provisions. Amendments of 1991 created the presidency and gave the president authority over the executive; others retained the Congress's supremacy and its power to suspend presidential decrees that conflicted with the law. The Central Bank answered to the Supreme Soviet, not the government, and the procuracy and other bodies had ambiguous subordination. When the Congress granted Yeltsin extraordinary powers in November 1991 for a period of one year, it was understood as a temporary delegation, not a permanent redistribution of authority; as the delegation approached its end in late 1992, its renewal became a central political question.

Work on a new constitution had begun in 1990 in a Constitutional Commission of the Congress. Its draft documents varied: some favored a strong president, others a parliamentary system closer to the legislature's interests, and the Commission's chairman was Yeltsin himself. The inability to agree on a draft is part of the explanation of why a constitutional settlement was postponed until after the violent confrontation of 1993. Students should resist the view that the crisis simply pitted democrats against conservatives; the disagreement concerned constitutional design, the sequence of reforms, and who should control the instruments of power.

Section 67.3

Yeltsin's Government and the Reform Coalition

Boris Yeltsin, a former Communist Party official from Sverdlovsk who had broken with Gorbachev, entered office with great popular prestige after the August 1991 coup attempt. In October 1991 he announced that Russia would begin radical economic reform, and he assembled a group of younger economists and officials to carry it out. Yegor Gaidar, an economist in his mid-thirties, became deputy prime minister for economic policy and then, from June 1992, acting prime minister; Gennady Burbulis, a philosopher by training, was a senior political adviser until late 1992; Anatoly Chubais, who had worked in Leningrad's economic reform circles, headed the State Property Committee and directed privatization. Yeltsin himself acted as prime minister until June 1992.

The reformers believed that gradual change would fail, since without price reform the shortages and fiscal deficits would worsen, and that a rapid transition was needed to establish private property, stabilize the currency, and make a return to central planning impossible. They drew on economic advice from foreign and Russian specialists and on the experience of Poland's 1990 reforms. Their critics argued that the economy needed careful sequencing, a strong state to supervise it, and protection for vulnerable groups and strategic industries. Industrial managers, regional leaders, and many legislators held versions of this view, as did economists who advocated a more gradual or "Chinese" approach, though how far China's experience could apply to a much more urbanized and industrial Russia remained disputed.

The government's limits were severe. The president's formal powers over the bureaucracy were wide, but the effective capacity of the state to collect taxes, enforce contracts, or control regional governments was weak. The Central Bank answered to parliament, not the cabinet, and its leaders often followed different policies. Regional leaders, who had also gained autonomy in 1990–1991, could ignore Moscow's instructions. A government with decree powers could announce policy, but implementing it depended on institutions it did not command, and the reform coalition itself was narrow, containing few experienced administrators and little organized political support.

The reform team was small, young, and largely drawn from research institutes and academic economics rather than from the administrative apparatus. Gaidar's group included Pyotr Aven, who became minister of foreign economic relations, and Alexander Shokhin, a labor and social policy minister, while Andrei Kozyrev directed foreign policy. Yeltsin's own role was ambiguous: he gave the team authority and public backing at crucial moments, but he also removed or reshuffled members under political pressure, and he did not build a political party to defend the reforms. This lack of a parliamentary or party base left the government dependent on the president's willingness to defend it.

Critics came from several directions. Industrial managers and their representatives, organized in the Civic Union formed in 1992 and associated with figures such as Arkady Volsky and Alexander Rutskoi, argued for protecting enterprises through subsidies and credit. Economists such as Grigory Yavlinsky, author of the 1990 "500 Days" program, advocated a more carefully sequenced approach with stronger social protection. Others, including later critics abroad, argued that the reformers underestimated the importance of institutions. Supporters, such as the economist Anders Åslund, who advised the government, argued that radical measures were the only realistic response to a collapsing system; critics such as Joseph Stiglitz and Stephen Cohen later argued that the policies were mistaken or harmful. The debate involved real disagreement about evidence and counterfactuals, not simply ideology.

It is helpful to remember what the reformers feared. In the winter of 1991–1992 many Russian cities faced shortages of food and fuel, rationing by coupon had spread, and the government's foreign exchange reserves were nearly exhausted. Policymakers feared that without immediate action the country would face breakdown of supply and social unrest. Whether their remedy was the best available is the subject of a continuing debate, but the sense of emergency was real and shaped their decisions.

Section 67.4

The Transition Strategy: Markets, State Capacity, and International Assistance

The reform program rested on three linked objectives: macroeconomic stabilization (bringing down inflation by limiting government deficits and credit), liberalization (freeing prices and foreign trade from administrative control), and privatization (shifting ownership from the state to private owners). Reformers also sought an integration into the world economy through trade, currency convertibility, and links to international institutions. The questions of sequence and pace were the main subjects of debate: whether to stabilize before privatizing, whether to restructure enterprises before selling them, and whether to build legal and regulatory institutions first.

The International Monetary Fund, World Bank, and Group of Seven governments offered technical advice and loans, usually tied to conditions on budget deficits and monetary policy. Russia joined both institutions in 1992. The promised financial support was smaller and slower than many Russian reformers hoped, partly because Western governments faced their own budget constraints and partly because Russian authorities did not always meet the conditions set. Critics at the time and later have argued that outside advice overlooked the weakness of Russian institutions; defenders have responded that the Russian government made its own decisions and that political opposition, rather than foreign advice, prevented fuller implementation.

A central point is that "market economy" was not a single policy but a set of institutions: property rights, courts, bankruptcy procedures, tax systems, competition rules, and banking supervision. Soviet planners had never built these. A government could abolish price controls in a day, but creating the legal and administrative structure to make markets function took years and was still incomplete at the end of the decade. This gap between formal reform and institutional capacity is essential to understanding both what the reforms achieved and why many of their results disappointed.

Foreign assistance was announced with fanfare but delivered more slowly than Russian officials hoped. In April 1992 the Group of Seven announced a support package of about twenty-four billion dollars, much of which consisted of export credits, debt deferrals, and a currency stabilization fund that depended on agreement with the International Monetary Fund. Russia joined the IMF in 1992, but the first major lending arrangements came later, in 1993 and 1994, and disbursements were interrupted when Russia missed targets for budget deficits and inflation. Russian critics complained that Western aid was inadequate and conditional; some Western critics complained that Russian policy was inconsistent. Both complaints contain part of the truth.

Institutional building proceeded in parallel but more slowly than price reform. Value-added tax was introduced in January 1992 at a high rate, and a new tax system was gradually created, but collection was weak because enterprises, regions, and individuals found ways to avoid payment. A bankruptcy law was adopted in 1992, though bankruptcies were rare. The first part of a new Civil Code, which provided a foundation for contracts and property, came only in 1994. A securities market, commercial courts, and bank supervision were created piecemeal. As a result, firms and households made decisions in a legal environment in which rules existed but were uncertain, enforcement was uneven, and personal connections and informal arrangements often mattered as much as law.

The debate about sequencing is central. Advocates of speed argued that delay would let old interests organize against reform and that institutions could be built after liberalization. Advocates of gradualism argued that markets cannot function without legal and regulatory institutions, and that opening the economy before they existed created opportunities for rent-seeking. Historians can use the experience of other post-communist countries as evidence, but the comparisons are limited: Poland, the Czech Republic, and the Baltic states differed in size, industrial structure, political culture, and the prospect of membership in European institutions.

Historical Vocabulary
Shock Therapy

Definition: A term for a rapid, simultaneous introduction of price liberalization, fiscal tightening, and related measures, intended to move quickly from a planned to a market economy.

Historical context: Applied to Poland's reforms of 1990 and to Russia's program beginning in January 1992. It was used by critics and sometimes by supporters; many reformers preferred terms such as "radical reform." In practice Russia's program was less complete than the label implies: energy prices stayed regulated, monetary and fiscal control was weak, and privatization proceeded in stages.

Why it matters: The label can obscure how much policy was negotiated, delayed, or reversed. Students should ask which parts of the program were actually implemented.

Russia and the World

Advice, Assistance, and Comparison

Russia's transition was watched and advised by foreign governments, international institutions, and academic economists. Poland, Czechoslovakia, Hungary, and the Baltic states each pursued different combinations of rapid and gradual reform, with differing results, and comparisons shaped Russian debates. China, a market-oriented reform under continued party rule, offered an alternative model to Russian critics. Yet the comparisons are imperfect: each country's size, industrial structure, institutions, and political situation differed, and Russia was both a former superpower and the successor to a collapsing union. Foreign assistance was not simply imposed or simply helpful; Russian decision-makers selected, delayed, or rejected advice according to their own judgments and political needs.

Division II — Shock Therapy, Privatization, and the Creation of a New Economy

Section 67.5

January 1992: Price Liberalization and the Beginning of Shock Therapy

On 2 January 1992 the government implemented a presidential decree issued in December 1991 that freed most retail and wholesale prices. According to International Monetary Fund descriptions of the program, roughly nine-tenths of retail prices by value and a similar share of wholesale prices were liberalized, while some essential goods and services, including energy, certain basic foods, transport, and housing-related charges, stayed under regulation or were liberalized later. Reformers hoped that higher prices would draw goods out of hoarded stocks and into shops, signal what was scarce, and eliminate the "monetary overhang" of rubles that had piled up because there had been nothing to buy.

The immediate results were mixed. Goods appeared in shops and on street markets, and the queues of the late Soviet years began to disappear. But prices rose steeply: retail prices increased several-fold in the first months, and consumer prices rose by roughly 2,500 percent between December 1991 and December 1992 (an increase of the price level by a factor of about twenty-six) according to a retrospective IMF statistical table, although figures vary with the index used. Wages, pensions, and savings did not keep pace. Households lost purchasing power, and bank deposits in rubles, the main form of Soviet savings, lost most of their real value.

Price liberalization did not by itself create competitive markets. In many sectors a single enterprise or a few producers dominated the supply, so freed prices could be set by market power rather than competition. Distribution networks, transport systems, and trading institutions were inadequate, and many enterprises were still state-owned and controlled by managers who could raise prices without fear of bankruptcy. The reforms also coincided with the end of the Soviet trading system and the sharp drop in state orders, which affected output as well as prices. Historians therefore distinguish three things: the abolition of price controls, the emergence of market exchange, and the development of competitive markets with the institutions to sustain them. The first came quickly; the others came unevenly.

Before 1992 Soviet price controls had produced chronic shortages, and household savings had accumulated in rubles that could not buy goods that did not exist. This is what economists call repressed inflation or a monetary overhang. In the last months of 1991 many cities had introduced ration coupons for basic food, and producers, expecting price increases, held back goods or diverted them to barter and to privileged customers. Reformers argued that releasing prices would draw out these goods and reduce shortages. Within weeks, stocks did appear and queues shortened, which many Russians noticed and welcomed, though at prices that ended the Soviet expectation of cheap basic goods.

Liberalization was not complete. The government kept limits on prices for bread, milk, some other foods, energy, and transport at the start, and regional authorities in many areas imposed markup limits or kept controls on certain goods. The price of oil and gas for domestic users was raised in stages and remained well below world levels for years. A presidential decree of late January 1992 legalized street trade and relaxed licensing, producing the open-air markets and kiosks that became characteristic of the decade. Wages and pensions were adjusted but with delays, and the minimum wage and public sector salaries lagged behind inflation, a pattern that underlay later protest.

Historians disagree about whether it was necessary to free prices all at once. Supporters point to the near-collapse of the supply system and argue that a gradual approach would have prolonged shortages and allowed officials to profit from price differences. Critics argue that prices should have been freed after breaking up state monopolies or after establishing a stronger social safety net, and that the January shock destroyed savings and helped create the political backlash that undermined the government. Both views rest on assumptions about what alternatives were politically and administratively feasible.

Section 67.6

Inflation, Monetary Policy, Credit, and Stabilization

Reformers hoped that after an initial jump, price increases would slow once markets supplied goods and the government held down its spending and money creation. That did not happen quickly. Inflation remained very high throughout 1992 and continued at very high rates through 1993 and 1994. It was still 131 percent in 1995 (December to December), and fell below one hundred percent only in 1996 (about 22 percent), reaching about 11 percent in 1997. Understanding why requires attention to money, credit, and the budget.

The Central Bank, nominally independent but answerable to parliament, extended large credits to enterprises and to the government. Managers of large industrial firms, facing falling demand and rising costs, lobbied for subsidized loans to pay wages and keep production going, and they found sympathetic audiences among legislators and regional leaders. Enterprises also ran up debts to each other, a system of "inter-enterprise arrears" that expanded the effective supply of credit outside bank control. At the same time, the government faced demands to protect incomes and to subsidize agriculture, energy, and the northern regions, while tax revenue was weak because the old collection system had collapsed and a new one was only starting to function.

Gaidar's government attempted to tighten both fiscal and monetary policy in spring 1992 but was forced to compromise. In the summer of 1992, the Central Bank's leadership changed to Viktor Gerashchenko, who supported more generous credit to industry. Disputes between the government and the Central Bank became a central feature of policy until the Bank's role was clarified in the 1993 constitution and later legislation. Stabilization succeeded only in stages, later in the decade, when budget deficits fell, the Central Bank restricted financing of the government, and the exchange rate was used as a nominal anchor, as Section 67.36 explains.

The statistical record shows how long stabilization took. Consumer prices rose by about 2,500 percent from December 1991 to December 1992 according to a retrospective IMF statistical table, which corresponds to a roughly twenty-six-fold increase of the price level. Inflation remained several hundred percent in 1993 and above two hundred percent in 1994, then fell to 131 percent in 1995, 22 percent in 1996, and 11 percent in 1997, measured December to December. These figures are rough guides because different price indexes and methods give different results. Understanding the sequence is essential to explaining the financial structure that collapsed in 1998.

Several episodes illustrate the policy conflicts. In mid-1992, as firms accumulated unpaid bills to each other, the government and the Central Bank agreed to a program of offsetting debts and extending credit, which eased the arrears crisis but added to the money supply. The replacement of Georgy Matyukhin by Viktor Gerashchenko as Central Bank chairman in July 1992 signaled a more accommodating stance toward industry. After the government changes of December 1992 and the constitutional settlement of 1993, finance ministers and Central Bank leaders continued to disagree about the scale of credit. A sharp fall in the ruble on 11 October 1994, known as "Black Tuesday," when the currency lost more than a fifth of its value in a single day, shocked the government into tighter policy.

Real stabilization came when several conditions were met: the Central Bank ceased to finance the government's deficit directly in 1995, the government borrowed through market instruments, and the exchange rate was managed within an announced band from mid-1995. This approach lowered inflation, but it depended on high interest rates and on the ability of the government to keep borrowing, which later proved fragile. Students should note that the reduction of inflation was an achievement, and also that its method created new vulnerabilities.

Section 67.7

The Ruble Zone, Foreign Trade, Exchange Rates, and the End of Soviet Economic Integration

When the Soviet Union ended, its fifteen successor states still shared a single currency, the ruble, issued by the Central Bank of Russia, and a vast network of trade among enterprises. This arrangement was called the ruble zone. It created a problem: if Russia tightened credit, the other states could still create ruble credit through their own banks and spend the proceeds in Russia, exporting inflation. Over 1992 and 1993 states left the ruble zone and issued their own currencies, and in July 1993 the Central Bank of Russia withdrew old Soviet-era banknotes, effectively ending the zone for most members. The measure also cost many Russians who held old notes.

Trade patterns changed sharply. Within the Soviet Union, many industries depended on suppliers and customers now in different countries, and the loss of these links reduced output. Russia's exports shifted toward oil, gas, metals, and other raw materials sold on world markets, while imports of consumer goods, food, and machinery rose. Export controls and licensing, retained to prevent shortages, created opportunities for those with access to officials to buy resources at low domestic prices and sell them abroad at world prices, a source of early private fortunes. The exchange rate, once heavily controlled, was unified and allowed to float in stages, with the ruble falling steeply in the early 1990s, and the economy became more exposed to world prices for energy and metals.

The ruble zone ended in stages. Because each former republic's central bank could issue credit denominated in a common currency, the system gave an incentive to expand credit and spend the proceeds in the larger market. Several Baltic states issued their own currencies in 1992; Ukraine introduced interim coupons that autumn; and by 1993 most others had left. In July 1993 the Central Bank of Russia declared pre-1993 banknotes invalid, giving citizens a short period to exchange them, a decision made partly to prevent other states from dumping old rubles into Russia. The measure abruptly reduced the value of cash held by individuals and enterprises, particularly in rural areas and among pensioners, and it signaled the end of monetary union.

Trade with former Soviet republics fell substantially, partly because supply chains broke down and partly because Russia raised energy prices toward world levels, though more slowly than the market. Russia's exports increasingly consisted of oil, gas, metals, timber, and chemicals; the share of machinery and manufactured goods declined. Because domestic energy and raw-material prices were far below world levels in the early 1990s and exports were subject to licensing and quotas, individuals with access to licenses could buy domestically and sell abroad at large profits. These arbitrage opportunities generated many of the first large private fortunes and drew intense criticism. Exports of capital also rose, as owners moved profits abroad rather than invest at home.

Russia also inherited foreign debts of the Soviet Union and negotiated rescheduling with the Paris Club of government creditors and the London Club of commercial banks. Debt service competed with other claims on a weak budget. The government's need for foreign financing and its integration into the global economy meant that external events, such as changes in oil prices or international capital flows, increasingly affected domestic politics, a point that becomes central in the discussion of 1998.

Section 67.8

Voucher Privatization, 1992–1994

Privatization was the second pillar of reform. A basic law of 1991 and a State Program of Privatization approved in 1992 provided for the sale or transfer of state firms. Small enterprises, such as shops, restaurants, and service businesses, were sold or leased through local auctions, often to employees or local entrepreneurs, and this stage proceeded relatively quickly. For large and medium enterprises, the government chose a mass program designed to create broad ownership without waiting for scarce domestic capital to accumulate.

In autumn 1992 every citizen could obtain a voucher with a nominal value of 10,000 rubles, which could be used to buy shares in privatizing firms, invested through investment funds, or sold for cash. Nearly the entire population, roughly 144 million people, received vouchers. Because inflation had eroded the ruble, the vouchers' real value was modest, and many people sold them quickly for cash. Vouchers were used in auctions through mid-1994, by which time thousands of large and medium enterprises, on the order of fifteen thousand by commonly cited estimates, had been converted to joint-stock companies and partly or wholly transferred to private owners. Anatoly Chubais, who headed the State Property Committee, presented the program as a way to make reversal to socialism politically impossible by creating a large class of owners.

Privatization proceeded through several steps. Small privatization began in 1992, when local governments auctioned or leased retail shops, restaurants, and service outlets, often to the existing staff. Large and medium enterprises were first "corporatized," that is, converted into joint-stock companies whose shares belonged initially to the state's property funds, and then offered to employees and the public. The law gave employees choices among several arrangements. One, in which workers received a block of non-voting shares free and could buy more at a discount, was less popular than another that allowed employees and managers to purchase a majority of voting shares at a favorable price, which most collectives chose. Percentages varied and exact rules changed, but the principle was that insiders could obtain control.

The voucher itself had a face value of 10,000 rubles, and its market price fluctuated. In the early stage many citizens sold vouchers for cash worth a small amount in dollar terms, in part because they did not understand how to use them. Investment funds collected vouchers on behalf of members, and some funds failed or were fraudulent; the best-known case was the MMM pyramid scheme, whose collapse in 1994 cost many small investors their savings. Anatoly Chubais is widely reported to have said that a voucher would buy the equivalent of two cars of the Volga type, a prediction that was not borne out and was remembered by critics as an emblem of broken promises.

From July 1994 the program moved to its second stage, in which remaining state shares were sold for cash at auction and by other methods. By then the voucher phase had changed ownership for thousands of firms, although the figure depends on the definition: one IMF count gave about 13,500 enterprises privatized through voucher auctions by mid-1994, while a World Bank evaluation using a broader count reported more than 16,000. Students should distinguish enterprises corporatized, enterprises sold at auction, and total privatization, and should be cautious in citing a single number.

Section 67.9

Enterprise Managers, Workers, Insider Ownership, and Industrial Restructuring

The privatization law gave employees of an enterprise several options. Under the one most workers' collectives chose, employees and managers could acquire a majority of shares, in practice often fifty-one percent, on favorable terms. The result was that "insiders," meaning managers and workers, held a large share of equity in most privatized firms, with outside investors, investment funds, and the state holding smaller blocks. This arrangement helped win the support of directors and workers for privatization, which was politically necessary, but it also left managers entrenched.

Weak legal protection for minority shareholders, poorly developed capital markets, and the continued possibility of bureaucratic intervention complicated corporate governance. Managers could dilute outside shareholders, strip assets, or transfer profits to affiliated companies. Many firms continued to produce goods nobody wanted, since bankruptcy procedures were rarely enforced and credit and barter kept them alive. Restructuring, meaning the reorganization of production, investment in new technology, and cutting excess staff, proceeded slowly in many firms and faster in others, especially those in resource sectors or in sectors facing foreign competition. Scholars debate how far ownership transfer improved performance by itself, and how far it depended on whether new owners could secure property rights and invest.

Studies of privatized firms in the mid-1990s found that insiders held on average a large majority of equity in many firms and that outside investors, domestic and foreign, had limited influence. Managers often held proxies from employees or controlled the share registry, which was kept by the company itself. This arrangement allowed managers to defend their positions, but it also gave them incentives to protect jobs and delay restructuring, since dismissing workers would erode their base of support. Some managers diverted profits through affiliated companies, selling output cheaply to intermediaries and leaving the firm with debts, a practice known in English as asset stripping or tunneling.

Economic performance differed by sector. Firms in oil, gas, metals, and some consumer goods industries, which could export or compete with imports, adapted more quickly; defense plants, machine builders, and textile mills often shrank drastically. The reduction in military procurement left defense-dependent towns with idle capacity, and attempts at "conversion" to civilian production met with mixed success. Investment fell steeply across the economy, because profits were low, credit was costly, and owners were unsure of the stability of their rights. Foreign direct investment remained small relative to the size of the economy. Many firms survived by not paying taxes, wages, or suppliers; large energy companies such as Gazprom and the electricity monopoly tolerated nonpayment by customers, which acted as a hidden subsidy.

An often overlooked feature was the "social sphere." Soviet enterprises had provided housing, kindergartens, clinics, and holiday facilities for their employees, and these obligations continued after privatization, burdening firms and tying workers to them. Transfer of these assets to municipalities was gradual and, in a time of budget shortage, left many facilities neglected. This helps explain why many workers stayed with failing enterprises: leaving meant losing access to services that were otherwise unavailable.

Section 67.10

Agriculture, Land, Housing, and the Emergence of Private Markets

Agriculture followed a different path from industry. Soviet collective and state farms were reorganized after 1991 into joint-stock companies, cooperatives, or similar bodies, but in most cases they retained their large size, their managers, and their workforces. Land was formally distributed to members as shares, but because land transactions remained legally restricted and rural capital was scarce, relatively few households withdrew from the large farms to farm independently. Private family farms emerged but remained a small share of output. By contrast, household garden plots, which had existed in Soviet times, became more important to survival, and produced a large proportion of the country's potatoes, vegetables, and fruit. Agricultural output fell during the decade as subsidies declined and costs for fuel and equipment rose.

Housing privatization proceeded through a different mechanism. Under a 1991 law, residents could acquire the apartments they occupied, usually free or at low cost, and a large majority of urban households did so over the following years. This created widespread home ownership without a functioning housing market or reliable maintenance systems. Small private business expanded rapidly: kiosks, street markets, small shops, and personal-service businesses grew, often operating in the informal economy. Retail trade, long starved under the Soviet system, changed within a few years. Conditions differed greatly between urban and rural areas, and between large cities and small towns where there were few alternatives to the old enterprise.

The reorganization of collective and state farms followed a presidential decree of December 1991 requiring farms to choose a new legal form. Most chose to reregister as cooperatives or joint-stock companies, and members received paper shares in land or property. In practice the farm chairs, often the same officials as before, continued to direct operations. Private farmers, who left to cultivate separate holdings, faced difficulties in obtaining machinery, credit, and inputs, and in marketing produce, and many abandoned independent farming. A presidential decree of October 1993 allowed the sale of land, and the 1993 constitution recognized private ownership of land, but the Duma, in which the Agrarian Party was influential, resisted a general law permitting free sale of agricultural land, and a comprehensive land code was adopted only in the early 2000s (see Chapter 68).

Agricultural output fell substantially during the 1990s, with livestock numbers declining and many farms struggling with debts and the cost of fuel and machinery. The government cut subsidies, and imports of food, including frozen chicken from the United States popularly called "Bush legs," grew in cities. Rural households relied more heavily on personal plots, which supplied a majority of Russia's potatoes and vegetables, and on informal exchange. Rural regions also lost population as young people moved to towns.

In cities, a new commercial landscape emerged. Open markets such as Luzhniki in Moscow and thousands of kiosks sold imported goods carried by "shuttle traders," individuals who traveled to Turkey, Poland, China, and elsewhere to buy goods for resale. Small businesses were vulnerable to extortion by criminal groups and sometimes by officials, and many entrepreneurs paid for "protection" because police and courts offered little. The law on housing privatization allowed tenants to acquire their apartments, and by the end of the decade a majority of urban households owned their homes. Ownership gave families a valuable asset and a sense of security, but it did not create a functioning market for rental or mortgage housing, and many residents lived in decaying buildings with poor maintenance.

Section 67.11

Loans-for-Shares and the Concentration of Strategic Assets, 1995

By 1995 the government faced a budget shortfall and a political calendar that included parliamentary elections in December and a presidential election in 1996. Cash privatization of the most valuable enterprises, particularly oil and metals companies, had stalled. A group of Moscow banks proposed that they lend money to the government and receive, as collateral, the right to manage blocks of shares in major state-owned enterprises. If the government failed to repay by a set date, the lenders could keep or sell the shares. The program, called loans-for-shares, was implemented in auctions held from November to December 1995.

The auctions transferred control over significant parts of Russia's oil, metals, and other industries to a small group of banks and financial-industrial groups. Critics noted that the auction procedures were not competitive: some bidders were disqualified, the organizers of the auction were sometimes affiliated with the winning banks, and prices were low relative to later valuations. The government did not repay the loans, so the lenders acquired the shares. Supporters argued that the program provided financing for the budget at a critical time, helped strengthen the political position of reformers against a communist resurgence, and brought these firms under owners who might invest. The U.S. Government Accountability Office and other analysts documented the concentration that resulted.

It is important not to confuse loans-for-shares with voucher privatization. Vouchers aimed at wide distribution; loans-for-shares concentrated control by negotiated arrangement with a few bidders. Further concentration occurred afterwards as owners bought out other shareholders and merged companies.

The political background of loans-for-shares was the combination of an empty treasury and an approaching election. The Duma elected in December 1993 had refused to approve the sale of many strategic assets, and cash privatization had produced little revenue. In the spring of 1995 Vladimir Potanin, head of the Oneksimbank, proposed the loan scheme; a presidential decree of August 1995 authorized the auctions, which were run by the State Property Committee and the banks that would be bidders. Critics noted that the same banks acted as agents and bidders, creating conflicts of interest. Bids from competing groups were in several cases disqualified on procedural grounds, a practice that critics regarded as pretexts to ensure that selected groups won.

The firms involved included Norilsk Nickel, a major metals producer, and the oil companies Yukos and Sibneft, as well as others. Sibneft was created by presidential decree in 1995 from existing oil assets, and its auction was won by a group linked to Boris Berezovsky and Roman Abramovich. The winning prices were close to the starting bids, and in later years the companies' market value rose dramatically, which critics cite as evidence of undervaluation. Supporters respond that in 1995 the companies' assets were not well managed, that their valuations reflected the political risk and the state of the economy, and that later rises depended on investment and on increases in world oil prices.

The government never repaid the loans, so the banks kept the shares. The 1995 program's long-term significance lies in the group of owners it created. They had both wealth and a direct interest in the outcome of the 1996 presidential election, in which a Communist victory might have reversed the transfers. Some participants later defended the arrangement partly on these political grounds. A U.S. Government Accountability Office study and others documented the concentration of assets and its link to the government's financing needs. In 1997 the Accounts Chamber and other bodies raised questions about the auctions, but no general review followed.

Historical Debate

Did Privatization Create Markets or Concentrate Economic Power?

The question: Did the methods of Russian privatization create the basis for a functioning market economy, or did they transfer public assets to a narrow elite and weaken the legitimacy of reform?

Interpretation A: Defenders argue that rapid privatization was necessary to prevent the restoration of state control, that political circumstances left few alternatives, and that private ownership eventually improved the performance of many firms, especially in resource sectors.
Interpretation B: Critics argue that privatization occurred before institutions existed to protect property and competition, enabling insiders and well-connected banks to acquire assets at low prices, producing a concentrated and politically entangled form of capitalism that weakened trust in markets and democracy.

Evidence: Auction records, company valuations, studies of firm performance, surveys of public opinion, and analyses by international institutions and national audit bodies.

Points of agreement: Privatization changed ownership on a very large scale; loans-for-shares concentrated assets; institutions for investor protection were weak.

Points of disagreement: Whether a different sequence or design was politically feasible, and how much ownership concentration harmed long-run performance, remain contested.

Why it matters: The interpretation affects how historians judge the transition and the later political role of business elites.

Division III — Inflation, Poverty, Social Dislocation, and Changing Russian Society

Section 67.12

Industrial Contraction, Unemployment, Underemployment, and the Transformation of Work

Official statistics indicate that Russia's measured gross domestic product fell by roughly two-fifths between the late Soviet years and the trough in 1998, and industrial production fell by more. Such comparisons must be treated with caution: Soviet-era output figures included goods that were poorly made or unwanted, new private activity and the informal economy were incompletely measured, and statistical methods changed. Even with these caveats, the fall in output was severe by any measure and larger than those in most Central European economies, though specialists disagree about its causes, which include the collapse of supply networks, the fall in state demand for military goods, the loss of the Soviet market, high inflation, and weak institutions.

Formal unemployment remained relatively low in official figures for much of the decade, rarely above ten to thirteen percent by the international definition, but this understates the disruption. Many workers stayed formally employed while working short hours, taking unpaid leave, or going unpaid for months. Enterprises preferred to retain workers because they provided housing, health care, kindergartens, and other social services, and because dismissing employees was politically and socially fraught. Workers stayed because few alternatives existed and because the enterprise remained a source of benefits. Meanwhile employment shifted toward trade, services, and small business, and many people held second jobs or turned to household production. The transformation of work was therefore less a story of mass formal unemployment than of hidden unemployment, unpaid labor, and movement into the informal economy.

The pattern of contraction varied by sector. Heavy industry, machine building, light industry, and above all defense production declined steeply as state orders fell, while extractive industries and some food and consumer-goods sectors contracted less. Employment moved from industry, science, and agriculture toward retail trade, services, finance, and security, and a large share of the labor force worked in small firms or the informal economy, which official statistics captured imperfectly. Scientists and engineers who had worked in research institutes and defense enterprises were particularly affected: many left for commerce or taught part-time, and a number emigrated, a movement often called the "brain drain."

Registered unemployment, meaning those who signed up at employment offices, was small, because benefits were low and registration brought little help. Surveys using the standard international definition showed a rate that rose from single digits in the early 1990s to about thirteen percent at its peak around 1998. Alongside open unemployment, many workers were placed on "administrative leave," worked a shortened week, or were kept on the books while receiving little or nothing. Employment also became more geographically uneven, as workers in monotowns, settlements built around one plant, had few local alternatives, while those in Moscow and other large cities could find jobs in commerce.

Migration added a further dimension. In the early and mid-1990s several million people, many of them ethnic Russians but also members of other groups, moved to Russia from other former Soviet republics, particularly Central Asia, the Caucasus, and the Baltic states, driven by conflict, economic hardship, or discrimination. At the same time, populations of the Far North and the Far East declined as residents left regions where subsidies and wages no longer covered the high costs of living. The labor market of the 1990s thus involved a redistribution of people across space as well as sectors.

Section 67.13

The Destruction of Savings, Pension Insecurity, and Wage Arrears

The ruble savings accumulated by Soviet households over decades lost nearly all of their real value in the inflation of 1992. People who had saved for housing, a car, or retirement saw the purchasing power of their deposits vanish, and the government offered only limited compensation. This was among the most widely resented consequences of reform and shaped attitudes toward the government for years.

Pensioners and others on fixed incomes were especially vulnerable. Pensions were adjusted but generally lagged behind prices, and in some periods they were paid late. State employees such as teachers, doctors, scientists, and military personnel found that their salaries declined sharply in real terms and often arrived months late as budgets were short of cash. By the mid-1990s, wage arrears became widespread across industry and the public sector, as firms and governments owed workers large sums; miners, teachers, and others staged strikes and protests to demand payment. Arrears did not merely reflect lack of money: they were also a way for enterprises to continue operations without dismissing staff, and they were related to the spread of barter and non-monetary payments in which workers were paid in goods.

The loss of savings was only one part of a broader erosion of the security Soviet citizens had expected. The government proposed schemes to compensate savers, such as indexing deposits in the state savings bank, but these promises were limited by the government's finances and left many people with the sense that the state had broken its obligations. By contrast, people who held dollars, durable goods, or real estate were better protected, which contributed to the unequal effects of inflation.

The pension system, financed through a Pension Fund created in 1990, paid benefits that fell well below subsistence levels for many retirees, and in the mid-1990s payments were delayed in some regions. Pensions were indexed irregularly. Pensioners often continued to work or relied on the household economy, and in many families the pension was an essential source of cash for several generations. Disability, health, and child benefits followed similar patterns, with real values eroded and payment delays common. The weakening of the safety net was especially severe for single elderly people and for families with several children.

Wage arrears became a system. By 1997–1998 many workers in industry, agriculture, education, health, and the military were owed several months of pay, and some were paid in goods. Estimates show that a large share of enterprise transactions, perhaps a majority by value in some sectors in 1998, involved barter, promissory notes, or tax offsets rather than cash. Protests increasingly targeted arrears: teachers and doctors struck; miners in Kuzbass and Vorkuta demonstrated; and in May 1998 miners blocked sections of the Trans-Siberian Railway, demanding payment and the government's resignation. These actions were typically focused on payment and local grievances rather than on a coordinated national program, but they weakened confidence in the government's ability to govern.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Pensioner's Household in the Early 1990s

Consider a retired factory worker living in an apartment owned under the privatization law, with a monthly pension that bought a shrinking quantity of food. Such a household relied on several strategies: cultivating a garden plot or dacha plot for potatoes and vegetables, receiving help from adult children or neighbors, selling possessions, and in some cases taking occasional work. Where relatives lived nearby, intergenerational support often mattered more than state benefits. For single pensioners without family and without land, the situation could be desperate. These examples are illustrations of strategies documented in surveys and press accounts, not a claim that all pensioners lived in identical circumstances: urban and rural, Moscow and provincial, and married and widowed pensioners experienced the period very differently.

Section 67.14

Poverty, Inequality, and the Emergence of New Social Classes

Income inequality increased sharply. Soviet society had substantial differences in privilege and access but a relatively compressed official wage structure; during the 1990s, measures of income inequality rose to levels well above those of most European states, and wealth became highly concentrated at the top. Estimates of the share of the population living below the official poverty line varied from year to year and depended on the definition, but a substantial fraction, commonly estimated in a range from about one-fifth to one-third in the mid-1990s, lived in poverty. Because many households also had assets, such as apartments and garden plots, and received informal support, measured income and actual living standards did not always coincide.

New social groups emerged. Entrepreneurs and traders, often young and working in cities, built businesses from nothing or from connections; senior managers and officials turned positions into property; bankers and financiers became wealthy. At the other end, industrial workers, public-sector employees, and rural populations lost status. Professional groups such as scientists and engineers saw their social position fall, and many left for other work or emigrated. The line between entrepreneurial opportunity, inherited advantage, and political connection was often unclear: access to credit, licenses, or privatization deals depended on relations with officials, so that the new commercial elite was composed of people with differing origins and routes to wealth.

Quantitative measures illustrate the shift. The Gini coefficient, a standard measure of income inequality, rose from a level in the late Soviet years of about a quarter to a level of roughly forty percent or higher in the mid-1990s, although figures depend on the data used and underestimate top incomes. Regional differences were large: average incomes in Moscow, for example, were a multiple of those in many poor republics and regions. Differences between rich and poor were visible in consumption, with luxury goods and foreign travel for a small minority and hunger or malnutrition for the poorest.

The new commercial class was heterogeneous. Some entrepreneurs started with small capital in trade and manufacturing; others began as Komsomol officials, enterprise managers, or state officials with access to resources and contacts. A circle of bankers and financiers grew rich from state deposits, foreign-currency operations, and privatization. At the same time, the reduction of state control and the weakness of law enforcement allowed organized crime to prosper, and violence associated with business disputes, extortion, and contract killings became a feature of life in the early and mid-1990s. Citizens often distrusted police, courts, and the new rich, and these attitudes shaped later debates about order and justice.

The status of groups changed. Teachers, physicians, scientists, and engineers, who had held respected positions in Soviet society, saw their incomes fall relative to traders and bankers. Industrial workers lost the political and symbolic prominence the Soviet system had given them. Women's and young people's situations varied. Some younger, educated urban Russians adapted quickly to new opportunities; many older people found their skills and expectations devalued. Social change was therefore a matter of status and expectations as well as income.

Data Study

Measuring Hardship

Historians use several measures that do not always agree: nominal wages (not adjusted for inflation) are misleading in a period of high inflation; real wages adjust for price change but depend on the price index used; poverty rates depend on the line chosen; and unemployment can be measured by registration with employment offices or by household surveys that follow international definitions. Wage arrears, barter, and second jobs are poorly captured by official wages. Students should therefore treat any single statistic as one partial indicator. When comparing Soviet and post-Soviet figures, remember that Soviet data reflected administered prices and shortages, so a "wage" did not mean the same thing.

Section 67.15

Mortality, Public Health, Alcohol, and the Demographic Crisis

One of the most striking indicators of the transition was a steep rise in mortality, especially among working-age men. Male life expectancy at birth fell from roughly sixty-four years in the late 1980s to about fifty-eight in 1994, then partly recovered and fell again around the 1998 crisis; female life expectancy declined less. From 1992 the number of deaths in Russia exceeded the number of births, and the population began to decline, partly offset by immigration from other former Soviet republics, many of them ethnic Russians returning or relocating.

The causes are debated. Death rates from cardiovascular disease, accidents, violence, and alcohol-related causes rose, and heavy consumption of strong spirits was widely recognized as a major factor in male deaths. Researchers also point to economic insecurity, stress, unemployment, the weakening of health services, changes in diet and medical care, and social disruption. Longer-term trends in Soviet public health, which had stalled from the 1960s as discussed in Chapter 63, preceded the 1990s, and a temporary fall in mortality during the anti-alcohol campaign of the mid-1980s (Chapter 65) was followed by a rise. Scholars therefore disagree about how much of the increase was caused by the transition itself and how much reflected pre-existing health patterns; most accept that several factors interacted, with differing effects across age, gender, education, and region.

The fall in life expectancy was driven overwhelmingly by deaths of working-age men from cardiovascular disease, injuries, poisoning, violence, and alcohol-related causes. Mortality had already been unusually high for a country with Russia's level of education and urbanization since the 1960s, and the anti-alcohol campaign of 1985–1987 produced a temporary improvement that reversed after the campaign ended. This history cautions against attributing all the 1990s increase to transition policies. At the same time, the speed of the rise in 1992–1994 and the pattern across social groups suggest that the upheaval mattered: studies found that deaths rose particularly among men with lower education and those who were unmarried or unemployed, consistent with an association between stress, heavy drinking, and social disruption.

Researchers continue to debate the causal chain. Some stress psychosocial explanations, in which loss of status, insecurity, and weakened social support increased risky behavior. Others emphasize the availability and cheapness of strong alcohol and the weakness of health services. A statistical study published in 2009 argued that rapid mass privatization across post-communist countries was associated with higher mortality, a conclusion that other scholars challenged on the grounds of data and method. Because the cause of a population-level change cannot be isolated by a single comparison, historians should present such studies as contributions to a continuing debate.

The demographic crisis also involved births and migration. The fertility rate fell from about 1.9 children per woman in 1990 to around 1.2 by the end of the decade, one of the lowest levels in the world, reflecting economic uncertainty and changing family patterns. Russia's population peaked at roughly 148 million in the early 1990s and then fell gradually, with immigration from other former Soviet states offsetting only part of the natural decrease. These trends continued to concern policymakers in later decades (see Chapters 68 and 72).

Section 67.16

Women, Families, Education, and Everyday Strategies for Survival

Women had been a large part of the Soviet labor force, and they continued to work in high numbers, but their position in the labor market changed. In many sectors women were disproportionately affected by wage arrears and by layoffs in education, health, and light industry, while some found opportunities in trade, services, and small business. Many women managed a "double burden" of paid work and the unpaid labor of the household, and the decline of state-provided childcare and the rising costs of services increased the burden. Birth rates fell sharply in the early 1990s, reflecting economic uncertainty and changing family patterns, while divorce rates and the age of marriage also shifted.

Households survived through combinations of strategies: pooling income across generations, growing food, bartering, using personal networks to obtain goods and services, taking second jobs, and depending on informal exchanges of favors. Education remained highly valued, but schools and universities faced budget shortfalls and unpaid teachers, and new private institutions appeared. The experiences were not uniform: families with access to dacha plots, urban connections, or marketable skills fared differently from those without.

Gender patterns were complex. Women's formal employment remained high, and women were often better educated than men, but they were concentrated in lower-paid sectors such as education, health care, and light industry, which suffered from arrears and budget cuts. Surveys showed that women made up a majority of registered unemployed in some years and faced discrimination in hiring. At the same time, many women took the lead in household survival, working in markets, taking second jobs, and managing the family's networks of exchange. The sharp rise in male mortality left many women widowed or raising children alone. The party Women of Russia, formed in 1993 from several women's organizations, won about eight percent of the party-list vote in December 1993, a sign of political concern for family and social issues.

Family policy and services deteriorated. Municipalities and enterprises closed or reduced child care facilities, summer camps, and clinics as budgets shrank. Birth rates fell, abortion remained a widely used method of birth control, though its frequency declined over the decade, and divorce remained common. Reports of homeless and abandoned children increased, and institutions for orphans were overstretched. Education remained a priority for families, but teachers' pay was low and often late, schools lacked funds for maintenance, and universities introduced tuition-paying places. Private schools and universities appeared, offering new options for those who could pay.

A distinctive survival strategy was the family garden and dacha. A large share of urban households cultivated small plots on the outskirts of cities, growing potatoes, vegetables, and fruit that supplemented food purchases, and they preserved produce for the winter. Alongside gardens, informal borrowing, mutual help, and favors circulated goods and services outside the market. Historians and sociologists have used household budget surveys, diaries, and interviews to study these practices, but evidence is uneven and often reflects urban, educated respondents more than rural households.

Households in the Transition

Workers in a large industrial enterprise: Often retained nominal employment and benefits but faced reduced hours and delayed pay, and relied on household production.
Pensioners: Experienced the collapse of savings and falling real pensions, and relied on family support and garden plots.
Entrepreneurs and traders: Found new opportunities, but faced insecurity, extortion, and unclear legal protection.
Teachers and public-sector employees: Held socially valued jobs that paid little and irregularly, and many took extra work.

The same economic change was a loss for some, an opportunity for others, and a source of uncertainty for most.

Section 67.17

Regional Winners and Losers, Labor Protest, and Social Adaptation

Regional differences were large. Moscow and, to a lesser degree, St. Petersburg gained from finance, trade, and government functions, and attracted investment and migrants. Resource-producing regions of West Siberia and the Arctic benefited from oil, gas, and metals exports, although much of the profit went to companies and to the federal government. Single-industry towns dependent on defense plants, textiles, or machinery suffered severe decline, as did many agricultural districts and some northern settlements built around subsidized supply. The North Caucasus faced economic collapse and, in Chechnya, war.

Labor protest took place mainly around wages and unpaid arrears rather than around the wider direction of policy. Strikes by miners, teachers, and others, rail blockades, and public demonstrations occurred, particularly in 1996–1998. Trade unions, inherited from the Soviet period, had uneven power and sometimes cooperated with management. Many people responded to hardship by adaptation rather than protest: moving to cities, starting small businesses, working informally, or relying on household production.

Regions differed in more than income. The share of national output produced in Moscow, with its concentration of finance, administration, and trade, was far higher than its share of population, and the capital attracted migrants and investment. St. Petersburg, Nizhny Novgorod, and some other cities adopted reform-minded local policies that attracted attention. Regions rich in oil, gas, and metals, such as Tyumen Oblast and its autonomous okrugs, produced much of the country's export earnings, but they negotiated the share retained locally. In contrast, Russia's Far North and Far East lost population as federal subsidies for transport and supplies declined: the number of residents of Chukotka, for instance, fell by more than half over the 1990s.

Organized labor lacked a unified voice. The Federation of Independent Trade Unions of Russia, successor to the Soviet official unions, remained the largest organization and often cooperated with managers and the government, while independent unions, notably among coal miners, were smaller but better known for militant action, building on the strikes of 1989 in the late Soviet period. Protests concentrated on wages and arrears, and workers often directed their demands at federal authorities rather than at employers, whom they saw as sharing their predicament. The occasional "rail wars" of 1998, when miners blocked railways, were exceptional because of their disruption of transport.

Adaptation took many forms. Households combined wage work, side jobs, trading, and gardening; municipalities paid employees in goods or allowed offsets of local taxes; and enterprises supported workers with food, housing, and transport. These practices kept many communities functioning but also reinforced barter and informal arrangements and delayed adaptation to a monetary economy. Their persistence showed that formal market institutions coexisted with social networks and informal practices inherited from the Soviet period, a combination that scholars describe in various ways.

Regional Context

Industrial Towns and Resource Regions

Seen from a single-industry town in the Urals or the Kuznetsk Basin, privatization and price liberalization meant that the factory or mine that housed, fed, and employed the community now struggled to pay wages and maintain services. Seen from an oil-producing district of West Siberia, the same changes meant new investment and higher incomes for some, but also dependence on commodity prices and decisions by distant owners. The contrast shows why a single national narrative of the 1990s is inadequate.

Division IV — The Constitutional Crisis of 1993 and the Reconstruction of Political Authority

Section 67.18

Dual Legitimacy: President, Congress, Supreme Soviet, and the Amended Soviet Constitution

By 1992 Russia had two sources of political authority that each claimed democratic legitimacy: a president elected by the whole country in 1991, and a Congress of People's Deputies elected in 1990 that held, under the inherited constitution, the highest state power. The president headed the executive and appointed ministers; the Congress and its Supreme Soviet passed laws, approved the budget, and could amend the constitution. Because the Congress could change the rules by a two-thirds vote, it could in principle alter the president's powers at any time, and the president could appeal to the people in a referendum or issue decrees under delegated powers.

This resembled, in one respect, the "dual power" of 1917 discussed in Chapter 50, where rival institutions claimed authority, though the comparison should not be pressed, since the conditions and actors were different. The central problem was constitutional: the inherited framework offered no reliable mechanism to resolve a dispute between the two branches over the direction of economic policy, the control of government appointments, or the future constitution. Each side had ideas about the proper constitution: the president favored a strong presidency; the parliamentary leadership favored a system in which the legislature kept supremacy.

Comparative scholarship helps clarify the problem. The political scientist Juan Linz argued in 1990 that presidential systems, in which a president and a legislature are each elected for fixed terms, are prone to conflicts that cannot be resolved by constitutional means because each claims a popular mandate. Russia's circumstances went beyond that general argument, because the legislature was elected under Soviet-era rules, and because the constitutional text allowed the legislature to rewrite itself. Neighboring states made different institutional choices: Poland, after tense relations between its president and parliament, adopted a "Little Constitution" in 1992 to define their powers, while several former Soviet republics adopted strong presidential systems. Russia's choices therefore belonged to a wider regional search for suitable institutions.

The dispute also concerned who should control specific levers of power. These included appointments to key ministries, the Central Bank, the procuracy, and the media, along with the authority to call referendums, to interpret the constitution, and to dissolve elected bodies. Each side had reason to fear that the other would use these levers against it. The Constitutional Court, created to resolve such disputes, became itself a political actor as its chairman Valery Zorkin tried to broker compromises, and as its rulings were interpreted as favoring one side or the other. When courts cannot command acceptance of their decisions, constitutional disputes tend to be settled by political or military means.

Finally, the conflict was entangled with the question of reform. Opponents of the government's economic policy sought to use parliament's powers over the budget and appointments to change it; the government's defenders argued that the parliament, elected before the Soviet collapse, no longer represented the country. Both claims contained some truth, which is one reason why historians describe the situation as a genuine crisis of legitimacy rather than a simple struggle between democrats and their enemies.

Section 67.19

The Breakdown of Cooperation, 1992–Early 1993

Opposition to the government's economic policy grew in 1992 as prices rose and industry contracted. Leaders such as Ruslan Khasbulatov, chairman of the Supreme Soviet, who had been Yeltsin's ally in 1991, and Vice President Alexander Rutskoi, who criticized the reforms as destructive, became prominent opponents. They were joined by industrial managers, agrarian interests, communists, and nationalists, as well as by some reform-minded deputies who opposed particular decisions. The Congress, meeting in April and December 1992, sought to limit the president's decree powers and his control over government appointments.

At the Seventh Congress in December 1992 the deputies refused to confirm Gaidar as prime minister. After negotiation, Yeltsin accepted Viktor Chernomyrdin, an experienced industrial manager, who combined continued market reform with greater attention to industrial interests. The compromise did not settle the underlying dispute, and in early 1993 the conflict escalated, with each side questioning the other's authority.

The politics of 1992 illustrate how institutional conflict interacted with policy. In October 1992 groups of communists and nationalists formed the National Salvation Front, while industrial managers and centrist politicians formed the Civic Union to press for a slower pace of reform and greater state support for industry. These coalitions were not identical, and they cooperated only partly, but together they put pressure on the government. The Supreme Soviet under Khasbulatov used its powers over the budget and legislative agenda to challenge the government, and in the course of 1992 he moved from alliance with Yeltsin to open opposition.

At the Seventh Congress in December 1992, deputies refused to confirm Gaidar, and the Congress adopted measures reducing presidential powers. A compromise brokered with the help of the Constitutional Court's chairman provided for a referendum on a new constitution in April 1993 and a temporary arrangement for sharing power, with the Congress choosing among candidates nominated by Yeltsin for prime minister. Chernomyrdin became prime minister. The compromise broke down at the Eighth Congress in March 1993, when deputies canceled the referendum agreement and moved to limit the president's powers further. Vice President Rutskoi publicly accused officials in Yeltsin's government of corruption, allegations that were not established in court, and the political atmosphere deteriorated.

These developments show that the 1993 crisis was not a sudden event. For more than a year the two sides pursued a pattern of challenge and counter-challenge. Compromises were made and then repudiated, and each side invested in the idea that the other aimed to eliminate it. Historians use this pattern to argue both that the crisis could probably have been avoided with institutional design and that, by 1993, the political leaders on both sides had developed incentives to escalate.

Section 67.20

The March–April 1993 Constitutional Confrontation and Referendum

In March 1993 the Congress moved to cut back the president's powers and Yeltsin, in a televised address on 20 March, announced that he would introduce a "special regime" of presidential rule pending a referendum. The Constitutional Court under Valery Zorkin ruled that the announcement, even before an official text appeared, was unconstitutional; the actual decree later omitted some of the claims. The Congress then attempted to impeach the president but fell short of the necessary two-thirds majority, and it called for a nationwide referendum.

On 25 April 1993 voters answered four questions: confidence in the president; approval of his socio-economic policy; early presidential elections; and early parliamentary elections. A majority of those voting expressed confidence in Yeltsin (about 58 percent) and approved of his policy (about 53 percent). The questions on early elections received a majority of votes cast only for parliament, but the Constitutional Court ruled that these two required the support of a majority of all registered voters, which neither achieved. The result was open to interpretation: Yeltsin claimed a popular mandate; his opponents pointed to the legal thresholds. The vote did not resolve the conflict, and both sides prepared for the next stage, including a constitutional conference convened by the president in the summer to draft a new constitution that the Congress opposed.

The referendum itself reflected a divided but not hostile electorate. Turnout was about sixty-four percent. Results varied by region, with support for Yeltsin strongest in Moscow, St. Petersburg, and many cities, and weaker in agricultural areas and parts of the south and the Volga region. The vote on economic policy showed that many voters who disliked specific consequences of reform still preferred the president to the parliament. Russians were asked to choose in a situation of institutional conflict, and the outcome gave each side an argument. Yeltsin's allies emphasized the first two questions, and the Congress's leaders emphasized the legal thresholds for the second two.

Violence was already present in spring 1993. On 1 May, clashes between demonstrators and police in Moscow led to injuries and the death of a police officer, and similar confrontations occurred later. After the referendum Yeltsin convened a Constitutional Conference in June 1993, including representatives of regions, parties, and social organizations, to draft a new constitution. The congressional leadership contested the conference's authority and pursued a competing constitutional draft; the two institutions remained divided. In the summer the two sides were therefore negotiating from irreconcilable positions, and each acted as though the other might attempt a seizure of power.

Section 67.21

September 21, 1993: Presidential Decree No. 1400 and Competing Constitutional Claims

On 21 September 1993, in a televised address, Yeltsin announced Decree No. 1400, "On Gradual Constitutional Reform in the Russian Federation." The decree ordered the Congress and Supreme Soviet to cease their activity, called elections to a new lower house, the State Duma, for December, and proposed a vote on a new constitution. The existing constitution, as amended, did not give the president the power to dissolve the legislature, and a provision in force stated that the president's authority could not be used to dissolve or suspend elected bodies. The Constitutional Court, meeting the same evening, ruled that the decree violated the constitution, and the Supreme Soviet voted to remove Yeltsin from office and swore in Rutskoi as acting president.

Yeltsin and his supporters argued that the legislature had become a barrier to reform, that the April referendum showed popular backing, and that the inherited constitution was an obstacle that could only be replaced by extra-constitutional action. The legislature's leaders argued that the decree was an unconstitutional seizure of power. Mediators, including the Orthodox Patriarch, attempted to negotiate a compromise, but without success. The White House, the Supreme Soviet's building, was cordoned by police and cut off from electricity and phones, while deputies and armed supporters remained inside.

The legal arguments can be set out precisely. The constitution in force contained a provision, added in 1991–1992 amendments, stating that the president's powers could not be used to dissolve or suspend any elected body of state power, and that any such attempt would end the president's powers immediately. The Constitutional Court, by a large majority, ruled on the night of 21 September that the decree and the president's address violated the constitution and provided grounds for his removal. On 22–23 September the Congress, convened in an emergency session, voted to remove Yeltsin and confirmed Rutskoi as acting president. Yeltsin's response was to treat the legislature's acts as void, and he enjoyed the support of the army and interior ministry leadership, who stayed loyal to him as commander in chief.

Yeltsin's defenders argued that the conflict between constitutional text and political necessity justified an extraordinary measure, since the Congress would never have agreed to a new constitution and was itself acting beyond its mandate. His critics argued that the argument of necessity undermines the principle that legal authority derives from constitutional rules. Both sides claimed to defend democracy, and each accused the other of aiming at dictatorship. Regions took different stands: many regional soviets supported the parliament, while most governors, who had been appointed by Yeltsin, backed the president. After the crisis, Yeltsin dissolved soviets at the city and district level as well.

Between 21 September and 3 October the White House was progressively isolated. Electricity and telephones were cut, police cordons were established, and armed volunteers entered the building, including members of nationalist and paramilitary groups. Talks mediated by Patriarch Alexy II of the Russian Orthodox Church at the Danilov Monastery in late September and early October produced no agreement. By the weekend of 2–3 October, both sides were preparing for confrontation, though neither had a clear plan for how it would end.

Section 67.22

October 3, 1993: Street Violence, Ostankino, and the Escalation of the Crisis

On 3 October, crowds of anti-government demonstrators, including communists, nationalists, and supporters of the Supreme Soviet, broke through police lines near the White House. Rutskoi and Khasbulatov, speaking to the crowd, urged an assault on the Moscow mayor's office and the Ostankino television center, and armed men associated with the parliament's side, including a group led by General Albert Makashov, attacked the mayor's building and later moved on Ostankino. Fighting at the television center killed a number of people, including security personnel, bystanders, and journalists; the broadcast of the main channel was briefly interrupted.

In the evening Yeltsin declared a state of emergency in Moscow and called on the army to defend the government. The decisions of the Defense Ministry and other forces were uncertain for some hours; senior commanders reportedly hesitated before agreeing to act. The events show that the confrontation had moved beyond a dispute between institutions to an armed conflict in the capital, with violence by both organized and spontaneous participants.

Events on 3 October moved quickly. In the afternoon, demonstrators broke through police cordons on Crimean Bridge and Smolenskaya Square, and armed supporters took over the mayor's office on Novy Arbat, where shots were fired. At the White House Rutskoi addressed the crowd, calling for an assault on the mayor's office and the Ostankino television center; Khasbulatov's speeches were similarly provocative. In the early evening a group led by General Albert Makashov, with trucks and armed men, attacked the Ostankino complex. Interior Ministry troops defended the building, and fighting continued for hours, killing a number of people, including security personnel and civilians, and interrupting the main broadcasts. Dozens of people died around Ostankino according to official and press accounts.

That night, Gaidar appeared on television and urged Muscovites to gather near the Moscow City Soviet to defend the government; thousands came. At the Defense Ministry, Minister Pavel Grachev and the general staff debated whether to commit the army, and some senior officers reportedly asked for explicit written orders. By early morning the military leadership agreed to support Yeltsin's order, and units, including tanks, moved into central Moscow. Some elite units had been reluctant to take part in an assault on the parliament, and the reluctance of the armed forces to take part in political conflicts was one of the lasting concerns of the following years.

The violence of 3 October raises unresolved questions. Armed men on the parliament's side attacked buildings, and the leaders publicly encouraged them, which Yeltsin's supporters cite to justify the response. Critics of the government cite reports of unidentified snipers firing from rooftops during the following day, killings of unarmed people, and the fact that investigations were curtailed by the 1994 amnesty. The responsibility for specific deaths has never been fully established in a public judicial process, and students should treat claims about the events of the evening with caution.

Section 67.23

October 4: The Assault on the Russian White House and the Defeat of Parliament

On the morning of 4 October, army tanks positioned on a bridge near the White House fired on the building, and troops stormed it later in the day. Fires burned in the upper floors; deputies and defenders surrendered or were arrested, including Rutskoi, Khasbulatov, and Makashov, who were held in Lefortovo prison. The Constitutional Court's activity was suspended, and some opposition newspapers and parties were temporarily banned. Foreign governments largely supported Yeltsin, while some commentators and Russian critics described the assault as a violation of the constitution and of human rights.

The number of dead is disputed. Official figures from the Russian authorities, as reported in contemporary U.S. State Department and Human Rights Watch documents, were 147 and 143 dead respectively for the period from 21 September to 5 October, with several hundred wounded; both organizations noted that unofficial claims were substantially higher. Casualty numbers must be attributed to their sources, and no definitive independent count has been established. In February 1994, the new State Duma voted an amnesty that freed the leaders of the October events, as well as those who had been in the August 1991 coup attempt, which Yeltsin opposed, but could not prevent. Yeltsin's own decision to use tanks against a legislature shaped later assessments of the Russian state's use of force.

The constitutional consequences of resolving an institutional dispute by military force were profound. First, the legislature was removed from the political process and the Constitutional Court was suspended, so the new constitutional order was drafted by the president's side alone, and the parliament that had the legal right to approve changes played no part. Second, the opposition's parties and newspapers were temporarily banned or closed, and local soviets across the country were dissolved by presidential decree, so the executive centralized authority in the weeks before the election. Third, the constitution that resulted, adopted by referendum under these circumstances, gave the presidency exceptional powers, a feature that scholars describe as "super-presidentialism."

The episode also affected attitudes toward law and the use of force. Yeltsin, who in August 1991 had stood on a tank against the coup, now used tanks against an elected legislature, and many of his supporters in the intelligentsia, including some who had backed the reforms, justified the action as necessary. Others, including some liberals, condemned it. The restraint of the armed forces in the crisis, followed by their reluctant intervention, shaped the army's relationship with the political leadership, which was tested again in Chechnya. Because the Duma's amnesty in February 1994 freed both the leaders of the October events and those of the August 1991 coup attempt, no court determined the legal responsibility of either side, leaving the events open to competing political narratives.

The question of how to remember October 1993 remains difficult. Some Russians regard it as a defense of democracy against an armed revolt, others as the destruction of Russia's first parliament by force. Scholars generally agree that both sides contributed to the escalation and that the outcome weakened the legislature, though they disagree about whether a peaceful resolution was possible. This disagreement is the reason that the Historical Debate below does not present one side as simply right.

Section 67.24

The Constitution of December 1993 and the New Political Order

On 12 December 1993 voters were asked to approve a new constitution and to elect the State Duma and a new Federation Council. According to official results, about 58.4 percent of voters supported the constitution, on a reported turnout of about 54.8 percent; the figures are distinct and should not be confused. A legal requirement that a majority of registered voters participate was met according to the Central Electoral Commission, but some critics questioned the count, and the matter remained controversial.

The constitution defined Russia as a democratic federal state with a republican form of government and listed rights and freedoms including freedom of speech, assembly, and religion. It gave the president extensive powers: head of state and supreme commander, with authority to appoint the prime minister (subject to Duma consent), dismiss the government, issue decrees, call referendums, and, under conditions, dissolve the Duma. The Federal Assembly consisted of the State Duma, with 450 members elected half by party lists and half in single-member districts, and the Federation Council, representing the federal subjects. The Duma could pass legislation, approve the budget, and, with difficulty, impeach the president. The constitution also created a Constitutional Court, recognized local self-government, and affirmed private property and, in principle, the ownership of land.

The system combined an elected parliament with a strong presidency. Critics argued that it gave the president too much power relative to parliament and made the government answerable chiefly to him; defenders argued that a strong presidency was necessary in a period of turbulence. The constitution's meaning depended on practice, and its institutions were tested over the following years.

The election of December 1993 combined three decisions: the vote on the constitution, the election of the State Duma, and the election of the Federation Council. The Duma was chosen by a mixed system in which half of the 450 seats were filled from party lists with a five-percent threshold, and half from single-member districts. The Federation Council initially consisted of two elected members from each of the federal subjects; from 1995 a new law made the heads of regional executives and legislatures its members ex officio. Critics, including a group of researchers led by Alexei Yablokov, questioned the official turnout figure and suggested that the required participation might not have been reached, but the Central Electoral Commission certified the results, and the constitution took effect.

The constitution's institutional provisions explain why it has been described as strongly presidential. The president, elected for four years in the original text, appoints the prime minister with the Duma's consent; if the Duma rejects three candidates, the president must dissolve it and call elections. The Duma may pass a vote of no confidence in the government, but the president may disregard a single vote; if it is repeated within three months, he must dismiss the government or dissolve the Duma. Impeachment requires a complex sequence of charges by the Duma, opinions from the Supreme Court and the Constitutional Court, and a two-thirds vote in the Federation Council, which made removal highly unlikely. The Constitutional Court of nineteen judges was reconstituted in 1995, with judges nominated by the president and confirmed by the Federation Council.

The constitution also provided a chapter on rights and freedoms, local self-government separate from state power, and a federal structure in which all subjects were declared equal while republics retained their special status and a list of areas of joint jurisdiction was defined. Amendments were made difficult by rigid procedures for the main chapters. Whether the document would produce a functioning separation of powers depended on political practice. Many scholars consider that the weakness of parties, courts, and local government, rather than only the constitutional text, shaped the way the system worked in later years.

Section 67.25

The Federal Treaty of 1992 and the Reorganization of Russian Federalism

The Russian Federation inherited a complex territorial structure from the Soviet period. It included ethnically defined republics (such as Tatarstan, Bashkortostan, Sakha, and Chechnya-Ingushetia), as well as ordinary territorial units called krais and oblasts, two federal cities (Moscow and St. Petersburg), an autonomous oblast, and autonomous okrugs, which in some cases lay within larger oblasts. In 1990–1991 republican leaders had been encouraged, by Yeltsin among others, to take "as much sovereignty as you can swallow," and many declared sovereignty, claiming control over resources and taxes. When the Soviet center disappeared, Moscow faced the risk that Russia itself might fragment.

On 31 March 1992, representatives of most of Russia's republics, regions, and cities signed a Federal Treaty, which was actually three agreements: one with the republics, one with the krais, oblasts, and federal cities, and one with the autonomous oblast and okrugs. The treaties divided authority between the center and the units, and gave republics a higher formal status than regions. Tatarstan and Chechnya did not sign. The treaty did not end disputes: regions continued to bargain over taxes, property, and jurisdiction. The 1993 constitution incorporated the Federal Treaty in a revised form, declaring all subjects equal in their relations with federal authorities while retaining the distinction of republics, and left the details to be settled by laws and bilateral agreements.

The 1993 constitution listed eighty-nine federal subjects, including twenty-one republics, a number that reflected the Soviet inheritance and the inclusion of Chechnya on paper. The units differed enormously in size, population, and wealth: Moscow had a population of several million and a large share of the country's financial activity, while some autonomous okrugs had populations of only tens of thousands. Many republics, though named for non-Russian peoples, had large Russian populations; in several, the titular nationality was a minority. These facts mean that "federalism" in Russia combined ethnic and territorial principles in ways that did not map neatly onto ethnic groups.

During 1990–1993 relations between Moscow and regions were marked by what contemporaries called a "war of laws." Regions adopted legislation contradicting federal law, withheld tax payments, claimed ownership of land and resources, and demanded control of local industries. Yeltsin had encouraged these assertions of autonomy in 1990 when seeking allies against the Soviet center; he was reported to have urged regional leaders to take as much sovereignty as they could handle. In 1993, when the Sverdlovsk Oblast leadership under Eduard Rossel proclaimed a "Urals Republic" in the midst of the constitutional struggle, it demonstrated how far regional assertiveness could go, and the president dissolved the initiative. In the fiscal sphere, taxes were divided by negotiation, and regions with strong bargaining positions kept larger shares.

The Federal Treaty stabilized relations without fully resolving them. It recognized that republics had greater formal rights than other units, a distinction that some oblast leaders resented, and the 1993 constitution partly equalized subjects while allowing separate agreements. Moscow's approach thus relied on negotiation, trade-offs, and compromises with regional elites. This practice kept the federation together, but it also left central authority dependent on bargains with regional leaders and created inconsistent legal arrangements that later governments sought to change (see Chapter 68).

Section 67.26

Tatarstan, Regional Bargaining, and Unequal Federal Arrangements

Tatarstan, a republic on the middle Volga whose population included Tatars, who are a Turkic-speaking, traditionally Muslim people, and Russians in roughly comparable numbers, declared sovereignty in 1990 and held a referendum in March 1992 in which a majority of those voting supported a definition of Tatarstan as a sovereign state. Its president, Mintimer Shaimiyev, declined to sign the Federal Treaty but remained in negotiation with Moscow, and Tatarstan did not seek full independence. In February 1994 the two sides signed a bilateral treaty that recognized Tatarstan's wide autonomy in managing its property and economy while affirming that it remained part of the Russian Federation. Similar agreements were signed with other regions, including Sakha (Yakutia) in 1995 and, over the decade, dozens of other units.

These treaties produced what scholars call asymmetric federalism: different regions had different formal and informal rights, depending on their bargaining power. Resource-rich and politically assertive regions obtained exemptions or favorable terms on taxes and property, while poorer regions depended on federal transfers. Regional governors, who were elected from 1996 onward, gained political independence from Moscow and significant influence. Critics argued that the practice created legal confusion and inequality; defenders argued that it allowed Russia to remain united without force.

The Tatarstan case reveals the content of regional bargaining. In November 1992 Tatarstan adopted a constitution describing it as a sovereign state and subject of international law associated with the Russian Federation by treaty, language that conflicted with the Russian constitution. A Russian Constitutional Court ruling in 1992 had declared parts of the republic's declaration of sovereignty and referendum unconstitutional, and Tatarstan's leaders did not abandon their position. The February 1994 treaty recognized the republic's right to manage its oil and other resources and to conduct some foreign economic activity, with agreements on taxation and other matters negotiated separately. The republic's oil company, Tatneft, was a significant source of revenue, and Tatarstan's control of its property and revenue was a central part of the bargain.

Other republics sought similar arrangements. Bashkortostan signed a treaty in 1994, Sakha (Yakutia), whose diamond industry generated large revenues, in 1995, and by 1998 roughly forty regions had bilateral agreements with Moscow. These agreements varied in content: some gave regions more control over natural resources, others modified tax-sharing, and some addressed cultural matters. Their legal status was uncertain, since they sometimes conflicted with the constitution and federal law, and later governments revised or annulled many of them. Yet in the 1990s they served as instruments of accommodation that helped to avoid the kind of confrontation that occurred in Chechnya.

Politics inside Tatarstan was also complicated. President Shaimiyev had to balance different constituencies, including Tatar nationalists who sought greater independence, Russians who formed a large minority and were wary of ethnic politics, and local business and industrial interests. The republic maintained generally peaceful interethnic and interreligious relations, which later commentators used as evidence of the possibilities of negotiated federalism, although critics also noted that the arrangement entrenched the power of a regional elite and limited political competition at home.

Section 67.27

Chechnya's Declaration of Independence and the Road to War, 1991–1994

Chechnya is a mountainous region in the North Caucasus, inhabited mainly by Chechens, a people with their own language and, overwhelmingly, a Sunni Muslim tradition that includes Sufi brotherhoods. The Russian Empire conquered the region in the long Caucasian War of the nineteenth century (see Chapter 39), and Chechens resisted rule for decades. In February 1944 Stalin ordered the deportation of the entire Chechen and Ingush population to Central Asia and Siberia (see Chapter 58), and they were allowed to return only after 1957. This history left deep memories and a strong sense of grievance, though it did not determine the events of the 1990s.

In autumn 1991, Dzhokhar Dudayev, a former Soviet air force general, led a movement that overthrew the local Soviet authorities, was elected president of Chechnya in October, and declared independence on 1 November. Yeltsin's attempt to impose a state of emergency failed, and Russian forces withdrew in 1992. Chechnya did not sign the Federal Treaty and was not internationally recognized as independent. The Chechen-Ingush republic split, with Ingushetia remaining within Russia. For three years Chechnya was in practice outside Moscow's control, while internally it suffered economic collapse, crime, and conflict between Dudayev and his opponents, some of whom received support from Moscow. Negotiations were attempted but did not produce an agreement on Chechnya's status. Moscow insisted that Chechnya remained part of the Russian Federation; the Chechen leadership insisted that it was an independent state.

Chechen society was organized by extended kin groups called teips and by Sufi religious brotherhoods, and these structures influenced politics alongside the formal institutions of the Soviet period. The movement that brought Dudayev to power in 1991 drew on national feeling, resentment of Soviet and Russian rule, and the weakness of local Communist authorities after the August coup. His election in October 1991 took place under disputed conditions, and the Russian leadership refused to recognize the declaration of independence that followed. Dudayev's government soon faced difficulties: political rivals, including former Soviet officials and local commanders, opposed him, and the republic's institutions did not function well. By 1993 he had dissolved the parliament and constitutional court, and an armed opposition emerged in the north, supported by Russian agencies.

The republic's economic situation was marked by the collapse of formal institutions and the growth of illegal activity. The large refinery and oil industry around Grozny continued to operate unevenly, and criminal networks exploited the absence of customs and regulation, including in a 1992 scandal over fraudulent financial instruments that cost Russian banks heavily. Moscow imposed economic restrictions, and the republic's neighbors, such as Ingushetia and Dagestan, were affected by refugees and smuggling. Chechens were not united behind independence; many hoped for an arrangement within Russia, and relations between Chechens and the Russian-speaking population in Grozny deteriorated, with many Russians leaving.

Moscow's policy was inconsistent. Between 1991 and 1994 the Russian government alternated between attempts at negotiation, economic pressure, covert support for Dudayev's opponents, and discussions of force. A planned meeting between Yeltsin and Dudayev never took place. In late November 1994 the Russian Security Council decided to act against the Dudayev government, and on 26 November an attack on Grozny by opposition units with Russian military participation failed, with Russian soldiers captured. That failure left the government with a choice between withdrawing and escalating, and it chose escalation. Historians dispute the role of different advisers and agencies in the decision, since the relevant documents are not fully available.

Section 67.28

The Russian Invasion of Chechnya and the Battle for Grozny, 1994–1995

In autumn 1994, forces opposed to Dudayev, covertly supported by Russian agencies, attacked Grozny and failed. On 9 December Yeltsin signed a decree on measures to restore constitutional order, and on 11 December Russian forces entered Chechnya. Senior defense officials had reportedly expected a quick operation, and the Defense Minister, Pavel Grachev, was widely quoted as saying that a single airborne regiment could do the job in a matter of hours. Those assumptions proved wrong.

On New Year's Eve 1994, Russian armored columns entered Grozny and suffered heavy losses in street fighting against Chechen defenders who used the city's buildings and tunnels. Russian forces then bombarded the city by air and artillery, and in the following weeks much of Grozny was destroyed; tens of thousands of civilians, many of them ethnic Russians who had stayed in the city, were exposed to the fighting. Federal forces took control of the city center in February and March 1995, then of other towns, and Chechen forces withdrew to the mountains. The conflict was unpopular among much of the Russian public, and prominent politicians, journalists, and groups such as the Committee of Soldiers' Mothers criticized the war; media coverage of the fighting, including by independent television, was more open than in earlier wars, which affected public opinion.

The campaign exposed the condition of the Russian armed forces. Troops from the army, the Interior Ministry, and other agencies were assembled hastily, with poor coordination, inadequate maps and communication, and units whose members had not trained together. Many soldiers were conscripts with little preparation. Armored columns entering Grozny at the end of December were exposed in narrow streets, and units such as the Maikop brigade suffered heavy losses in the first days of fighting. After this setback the Russian command relied on air strikes and artillery, and Grozny was heavily damaged. The presidential palace in the center of the city was taken in January 1995, and the Chechen forces withdrew from the city by the end of February.

Civilians were the main victims of the bombardment. Many remaining residents were elderly Russians and others who could not leave, and thousands were killed or injured. Russia's presidential human rights commissioner, Sergei Kovalev, went to Grozny and publicly criticized the conduct of the war, and he was later removed from his post by the Duma. Human-rights organizations documented the killing of civilians in villages such as Samashki in April 1995. Russian television, including the independent channel NTV, broadcast reports from the war zone, and public opinion polls showed that a majority of Russians opposed the war. The Committee of Soldiers' Mothers organized visits to the front to seek the return of sons. These developments make the First Chechen War distinct from earlier Soviet and Russian wars, since it was fought under conditions of relatively open media and public debate.

The Russian government declared the main phase over by mid-1995, but control of the lowlands did not translate into control of the republic. Chechen forces, organized in small mobile units and supported by local communities, avoided open battle and operated in the mountains and villages. Russian forces established checkpoints and conducted sweeps, which contributed to local hostility. The war thus shifted from conventional combat to a prolonged struggle in which the distinction between civilians and fighters became blurred, with consequences described in the next section.

Section 67.29

Guerrilla Warfare, Hostage Crises, and the Human Costs of the First Chechen War

After the fall of Grozny, the war continued as guerrilla fighting. Chechen units, many of them local militias commanded by field commanders such as Shamil Basayev and Aslan Maskhadov, attacked federal forces and checkpoints, while federal troops conducted "cleansing" operations in villages, which human-rights organizations documented as involving killings, detention, and abuse of civilians. In June 1995 Basayev led a group that seized a hospital in the Russian town of Budyonnovsk, taking hundreds of hostages; after a failed assault by security forces and the intervention of Prime Minister Chernomyrdin, who negotiated by telephone, the hostage-takers were allowed to return to Chechnya. More than a hundred civilians died in the crisis. In January 1996 another group seized hostages in Kizlyar, Dagestan, and the siege ended with fighting in the village of Pervomayskoye.

The human costs were large. Estimates of civilian deaths differ widely, from the tens of thousands upward, depending on the source and methodology; Russian military deaths in the war are usually given as several thousand in official statements, with higher figures from non-governmental groups. Hundreds of thousands of people were displaced. Russian conscripts, poorly trained and equipped, suffered heavy losses, and many soldiers' families protested. The Chechen side also committed violations, including hostage-taking and attacks on civilians. Casualty estimates should be attributed to their sources, and the figures remain uncertain.

The Budyonnovsk hostage-taking in June 1995 showed both the weakness of Russian security forces and the willingness of Chechen fighters to attack civilians outside Chechnya. Basayev's group, numbering a few score, seized a hospital and held around fifteen hundred people. Russian special forces attempted to storm the building, and about a hundred and thirty civilians are generally reported to have died overall, though estimates vary. Chernomyrdin's telephone negotiation led to the release of hostages and the departure of the fighters to Chechnya, an outcome presented in Russia as a defeat of the government and in Chechnya as a success. The Duma passed a vote of no confidence in the government shortly afterward, though the president retained it.

Civilian casualty estimates differ widely and depend on the definition of the war's period, on methods, and on political positions. Human Rights Watch reported an estimate of at least 50,000 civilian deaths in the 1994–1996 war. A contemporary U.S. State Department report recorded sharply divergent figures from Russian officials, including fewer than 20,000 attributed to one minister and 80,000 to 100,000 attributed to Alexander Lebed. These figures do not measure the same thing and cannot be treated as interchangeable. Russian official counts of military dead are usually given as roughly five to six thousand, while soldiers' organizations suggested higher numbers. Hundreds of thousands of people were displaced, many to Ingushetia and Dagestan, and Grozny and other towns were largely destroyed.

Violations occurred on both sides. Russian forces were accused of indiscriminate bombing and shelling, killings of civilians in sweeps of villages, arbitrary detention, and abuse in "filtration" points where detainees were screened. Chechen fighters committed hostage-taking, killings of prisoners and civilians suspected of collaboration, and attacks on civilian targets. In addition, kidnapping and violence by criminal groups spread. Because organizations such as Memorial and Human Rights Watch gathered testimony under difficult conditions, their reports are an essential source, but historians must distinguish between established findings, credible allegations, and estimates.

Section 67.30

The Khasavyurt Agreements, 1996, and the Unresolved Status of Chechnya

In March 1996 Yeltsin, facing an election, announced a peace plan; in April Dudayev was killed by a Russian missile, and a ceasefire agreed in May did not hold. On 6 August, days before Yeltsin's inauguration for a second term, Chechen forces recaptured Grozny in a surprise attack, humiliating the federal army. Alexander Lebed, a former general who had come third in the first round of the presidential election and was appointed secretary of the Security Council, negotiated with Maskhadov, then Chechnya's chief of staff. On 31 August 1996, in the Dagestani town of Khasavyurt, they signed a set of agreements providing for the withdrawal of Russian forces and postponing a decision on Chechnya's status until the end of 2001.

Maskhadov was elected president of Chechnya in elections in January 1997 that international observers judged generally free, and in May 1997 he and Yeltsin signed a treaty on peace and principles of relations. The question of Chechnya's final legal status was left open, and Chechnya was left physically devastated, with no effective government control over armed groups and little reconstruction. Russian federal authorities regarded Chechnya as part of Russia; Chechen authorities regarded their state as independent; no state recognized it. The peace did not resolve these differences, and the situation after 1997 is treated in Chapter 68. The Second Chechen War, from 1999, cannot be explained simply as a continuation of the first.

The Khasavyurt settlement was a pragmatic bargain. Lebed, who had gained attention as an outspoken general and a presidential candidate, argued that the war could not be won and ought to be ended; he was supported by many in the military who wanted to leave. His opponents in the government, including officials who favored continuing the war, criticized the agreement as a capitulation, and Yeltsin dismissed Lebed in October 1996. The agreements provided for a joint commission, the withdrawal of federal forces, and elections, and they deferred the question of Chechnya's status until the end of 2001. Federal forces withdrew by the end of the year.

The elections of January 1997, monitored by the Organization for Security and Co-operation in Europe, were won by Maskhadov with about sixty percent of the vote, ahead of Shamil Basayev, who received around a quarter. The result showed a preference among voters for a moderate leader, but Maskhadov's authority was limited. The republic lacked a functioning economy, police, and courts; armed commanders retained independent forces; and kidnapping for ransom became widespread. Radical Islamist groups, some with foreign fighters, gained influence in some districts, which led to tension with Maskhadov's government and Sufi leaders. Russian financial promises for reconstruction were largely unfulfilled.

The 1997 treaty between Yeltsin and Maskhadov, signed in Moscow in May, spoke of peace and principles of relations but avoided any statement on sovereignty, and each side interpreted it according to its own views. The settlement thus stopped the fighting without resolving the underlying disagreement or building institutions able to maintain peace. Why that peace collapsed in 1999 involves events in Chechnya, Dagestan, and Moscow that are discussed in Chapter 68, and students should avoid the assumption that the second war was an inevitable result of the first.

Historical Debate

Division VI — Oligarchs, Electoral Politics, and Yeltsin's Second Term

Section 67.31

Political Parties and Parliamentary Elections, 1993–1995

The December 1993 elections to the State Duma gave a surprising result. The Liberal Democratic Party of Russia (LDPR), led by the nationalist Vladimir Zhirinovsky, won the largest share of the party-list vote (about 23 percent), ahead of the reformist bloc Russia's Choice, associated with Gaidar (about 15 percent), and the Communist Party of the Russian Federation (about 12 percent). Smaller parties included Yabloko, a liberal grouping led by Grigory Yavlinsky, the Agrarian Party, and Women of Russia. The results showed public discontent with the economic reforms and left the government without a stable majority. Gaidar resigned from government in January 1994.

The December 1995 elections confirmed the pattern: the Communist Party of the Russian Federation, led by Gennady Zyuganov, came first with roughly a fifth of the party-list vote, followed by the LDPR and Our Home Is Russia, a "party of power" associated with Chernomyrdin. The party system remained fragmented, with parties more organized around leaders than ideologies, weak ties to voters, and limited control over government. The constitution gave the Duma power to block legislation but little power over government formation, and this encouraged conflict between the two without making the executive answerable to parliamentary majorities.

Parties in the new Duma were weak and unstable. Many were created shortly before elections and depended on personalities, regional machines, or administrative support. In the 1995 election, 43 parties and blocs competed under the party-list system, and close to half of the votes went to parties that failed to cross the five-percent threshold, which left a large proportion of voters without representation in the party-list seats. Four parties passed: the Communists, the LDPR, Our Home Is Russia, and Yabloko. The Duma's composition made cooperation with the government difficult, since the Communists and their allies were strong enough to block legislation, but the government could rely on presidential decrees and its influence over single-member district deputies.

The balance of powers in practice was demonstrated in June 1995, when the Duma passed a vote of no confidence in the government after the Budyonnovsk crisis. A second vote within the next three months, which would have forced the president to dismiss the government or dissolve the Duma, did not pass, and Chernomyrdin remained. This showed that the Duma could criticize the government but, owing to the constitutional design and internal divisions, could not easily change it. Political scientists often describe the system as one in which parties competed for representation but had limited influence on the formation and policy of governments.

Voters' support for the Communists and the LDPR reflected discontent but not a single ideology. The Communist Party of the Russian Federation, a successor to part of the Soviet-era party, combined appeals to social protection and nostalgia with opposition to privatization, while accepting parliamentary politics. The LDPR's nationalist rhetoric attracted voters angry about the collapse of the USSR and about crime and disorder. Reformist parties, divided between Gaidar's group and Yavlinsky's Yabloko, struggled to combine. These patterns influenced the presidential race of 1996.

Section 67.32

The Oligarchs: Banking, Industry, Media, and Political Influence

The term "oligarchs" came into use in the mid-1990s for a small group of businessmen who controlled banks, industrial holdings, and media outlets and who had considerable influence on government. Figures such as Boris Berezovsky, Vladimir Gusinsky, Vladimir Potanin, Mikhail Khodorkovsky, and Mikhail Fridman built financial-industrial groups through banking, trade, and privatization. Their strength came from several sources: access to government deposits and credit, loans-for-shares and other privatization deals, ties to officials, and control of television channels and newspapers.

The oligarchs were not a unified group. They competed fiercely, as in the "bankers' war" of 1997 over the privatization of the telecommunications holding Svyazinvest, which divided the government's reformers and their business allies. Their relationship to the state was one of mutual dependence rather than simple control: businessmen needed political protection and access, and the government needed financing, media support, and allies. In 1996 Berezovsky claimed that a handful of bankers controlled about half of the Russian economy; this was a claim by an interested party and should not be treated as a measured fact. Historians debate whether the oligarchs "captured" the state, or whether the state and the business elite formed a mutually constraining relationship.

The institutions that gave rise to the oligarchs developed in the early 1990s. Many commercial banks were founded in 1990–1992 and prospered by serving as "authorized banks" that held government and state-enterprise accounts, earning income from delays in transferring funds while inflation reduced their real value, and by foreign-exchange operations. Industrial and resource groups used connections and privatization opportunities to expand. Media ownership gave political leverage: Gusinsky's NTV began broadcasting in 1993 and became a prominent independent channel, while the public channel ORT was controlled by the state with significant private influence associated with Berezovsky. In August 1995 a banking crisis showed the fragility of the system, and in 1996 the leading businessmen at the Davos forum agreed to coordinate support for Yeltsin.

After the election, competition among the oligarchs intensified. In 1997 the auction of the telecommunications holding Svyazinvest, won by a consortium including Potanin's group, led Berezovsky and Gusinsky, who had lost, to attack the government through their media, in what the press called the "bankers' war." A scandal over large advances paid for a book by members of Chubais's team led to dismissals of officials. These episodes showed that businessmen used media, courts, and political connections against each other and against government officials, and that the state had not established impartial rules for competition.

It would be misleading to treat the oligarchs as the only economic elite. Gazprom's management, under Rem Vyakhirev, controlled the country's gas monopoly and was a major actor; regional leaders built their own business networks; and many enterprise directors retained local power. The oligarchs themselves differed in origins, sectors, and relationship to the state. The historiographical question is whether they captured the state or whether the weakness of the state made informal bargaining the only means of governing; evidence supports elements of both views, and the later relationship between the Putin government and these businessmen is discussed in Chapter 68.

Section 67.33

The 1996 Presidential Election: Yeltsin, Zyuganov, and Competing Futures

At the start of 1996 Yeltsin's approval ratings were very low, with surveys suggesting single-digit support, and many observers expected Zyuganov, leading the largest party, to win. Yeltsin's advisers assembled a campaign that combined a change of tone on policy (increased promises on wages and pensions, an announced reduction of the draft, a ceasefire in Chechnya), the mobilization of business support and state resources, and a media campaign portraying the election as a choice between reform and a return to communism. Anatoly Chubais, who had been dismissed from government, helped lead the campaign. A group of businessmen, meeting at the Davos economic forum in early 1996, agreed to support Yeltsin against Zyuganov.

In the first round on 16 June, Yeltsin received about 35 percent, Zyuganov about 32 percent, Lebed about 15 percent, Yavlinsky about 7 percent, and Zhirinovsky about 6 percent. As none had a majority a runoff was necessary. Yeltsin then appointed Lebed secretary of the Security Council and dismissed Defense Minister Grachev, a move that brought Lebed's voters to his side. The campaign's principal themes show that the contest was seen by many Russians as a choice about the country's direction, but they also illustrate how the incumbent could use government power.

The 1996 campaign took place in a tense atmosphere. After the Communists' success in the December 1995 Duma elections and polls showing Yeltsin far behind, some figures in his entourage reportedly urged postponing the election or banning the Communist Party; accounts in memoirs and press reports describe a discussion in March 1996 of such a measure, which Yeltsin did not carry out. In February 1996 he announced his candidacy, replaced unpopular officials, appointed Yevgeny Primakov as foreign minister in January to replace Andrei Kozyrev, and began to distribute funds to meet wage and pension arrears. He announced a peace initiative in Chechnya and a plan to end conscription in the future, and he campaigned actively across the country in rallies and concerts, including the "Vote or Lose" tour aimed at young people.

Zyuganov's campaign emphasized economic protection and criticism of the government's record but was hampered by uncertainty about what a Communist government would do. He spoke of a mixed economy and respect for private property in some contexts, while using language of social justice and national revival, and he sought to reassure voters while keeping the Communist base. Among other candidates, Lebed gained support with a message of law and order, Yavlinsky offered a liberal opposition to the government, Zhirinovsky appealed to nationalist voters, and Mikhail Gorbachev, the last Soviet leader, received only about half a percent, a reminder of the unpopularity of the late Soviet reforms among many voters.

The first-round result, with Yeltsin slightly ahead of Zyuganov, made the runoff a contest between them. Yeltsin's incorporation of Lebed gave him additional votes, and the dismissal of Defense Minister Grachev and of senior security officials such as Alexander Korzhakov in June removed some of the circle around him, though it also left decision-making dependent on the shifting influence of the president's family and aides. The meaning of the first round is clear: more than half of the voters did not choose either of the two main candidates, and much of the electorate decided late.

Section 67.34

The July 1996 Runoff, Media Bias, Campaign Financing, and Democratic Legitimacy

In the runoff on 3 July 1996 Yeltsin received about 53.8 percent and Zyuganov about 40.3 percent, with the rest voting against both candidates or spoiling ballots. Observers from international organizations, including the Organization for Security and Co-operation in Europe, reported that the election was competitive and reflected the will of the voters in general, but they documented serious imbalances: most major television channels and many newspapers strongly favored the incumbent; his campaign had access to state resources and is widely reported to have spent far more than permitted limits; and officials made promises of payments timed to the vote. Yeltsin's serious heart trouble during the campaign was concealed from the public and acknowledged only later.

Historians should distinguish three things: documented unfairness in media coverage and campaign finance; irregularities in voting and counting, which some critics alleged; and claims that votes were falsified so as to change the outcome. The first is well documented. For the third, the evidence available to scholars has not demonstrated that falsification reversed the result, and the margin was large. The election can therefore be described as competitive, consequential, and unfair in important respects, without assuming that the outcome was fabricated. It reinforced alliance between the presidency and the business elite and the use of media for political ends.

Media coverage was a central issue. Studies of television news during the campaign found that coverage heavily favored the incumbent and was often negative about Zyuganov, a pattern that reflected the political preferences of major channel owners and the state's control of some outlets. Print media were more varied but also leaned toward Yeltsin. The campaign's spending is reported to have far exceeded legal limits, and the government used public resources, from announced payments of arrears to presidential trips and decrees on pensions and wages, to improve voters' views. In June 1996, two campaign aides were detained leaving the government building with a box containing a large sum of cash in dollars, an episode widely reported at the time and followed by the dismissals of several security chiefs.

Turnout in the runoff was about two-thirds of registered voters. Yeltsin's margin of more than thirteen percentage points was too large to be explained by the kinds of irregularities documented by observers, and Zyuganov accepted the result while criticizing the conditions of the campaign. The election's legitimacy rests on the combination of a real contest, genuine voter choice, and the distortions of media and money. Subsequent analysis of voting patterns, including by independent researchers, found some regional anomalies but no evidence of fraud sufficient to reverse the result. Historians should therefore avoid both the claim that the election was merely a staged affair and the claim that it was a model of fairness.

The democratic costs of the election's conduct were long-term. The partnership between the presidency and major business owners offered a template for later political management of media and finance, and the use of administrative resources became a persistent feature of Russian elections. At the same time, the competition was genuine, the opposition candidate won tens of millions of votes, and the result was accepted. Evaluating the election requires weighing these elements without reducing them to a single label.

Section 67.35

Yeltsin's Declining Health and the Instability of His Second-Term Government, 1996–1998

Yeltsin underwent heart bypass surgery in November 1996 and was frequently ill in the following years, which limited his public activity and the continuity of policy. Power was exercised through a presidential administration and a circle of advisers, including family members and close aides, and shifts among them shaped government. In 1997 Chubais and Boris Nemtsov entered the government as first deputy prime ministers to speed reform, and conflicts among businessmen and officials, such as the Svyazinvest dispute, weakened the coalition.

In March 1998 Yeltsin abruptly dismissed Chernomyrdin and the whole government. He nominated Sergei Kiriyenko, a young former banker and minister of energy, as prime minister; the Duma rejected him twice and approved him on the third vote under the threat of dissolution. The government rested on a shaky political base, faced a hostile Duma, and tried to reduce budget deficits and attract international support, while unpaid wages and falling confidence persisted. The weak capacity of the state to carry out consistent policy became more important as external shocks accumulated.

After the runoff, Yeltsin withdrew from public view for weeks. His heart surgery in November 1996 was carried out after he had been ill for much of the year, and Chernomyrdin served as acting president during the operation. Yeltsin's return to work was partial, and important decisions were increasingly shaped by those around him, notably his daughter Tatyana Dyachenko, his chief of staff Valentin Yumashev, and influential businessmen such as Berezovsky. Chubais headed the presidential administration from July 1996 and in March 1997 became first deputy prime minister, alongside Boris Nemtsov, the former governor of Nizhny Novgorod, in an effort to give the government a reformist impulse. The government introduced measures to reduce budget deficits and attempted to restructure monopolies.

Policy achievement was limited. The Duma opposed key tax and budget proposals, and the government's inability to collect taxes continued. The ruble was redenominated on 1 January 1998, removing three zeros, a change that was symbolic of stabilization. The government's finances nevertheless relied on foreign borrowing and short-term domestic debt, and unpaid wages remained a problem. Meanwhile Lebed's dismissal in October 1996 and a series of changes of defense and security officials showed the volatility of the president's circle. Defense Minister Igor Rodionov, appointed after Grachev, was replaced in 1997 by Igor Sergeyev, and the army continued to suffer from underfunding.

In March 1998 Yeltsin dismissed Chernomyrdin and his cabinet without warning and nominated the thirty-five-year-old Kiriyenko. The Duma rejected the nomination twice and approved it on the third vote in April, under the constitutional threat of dissolution. The episode showed how the constitution's rules shaped political conflict and also how limited the government's political base was. The appointment of a young technocrat reflected the president's hope for a fresh start, but it placed an inexperienced prime minister in charge when financial pressures were increasing.

Division VII — Financial Crisis, Institutional Instability, and the Legacy of the Yeltsin Years

Section 67.36

Partial Stabilization, Growth Expectations, and Persistent Economic Weakness, 1995–1997

From 1995 the government and Central Bank achieved a partial macroeconomic stabilization. Annual inflation (December to December) fell from several hundred percent in 1993 and over two hundred percent in 1994 to 131 percent in 1995, about 22 percent in 1996, and about 11 percent in 1997. The exchange rate was managed within a corridor, a band within which the Central Bank allowed the ruble to move against the dollar, which helped to anchor expectations. The government reduced its direct financing by the Central Bank and instead borrowed by selling short-term ruble bonds, known by the Russian abbreviation GKO, to banks and, increasingly, foreign investors. Financial markets developed, foreign portfolio investment grew, and in 1997 official data showed a small return to economic growth for the first time in the decade.

Beneath this improvement lay structural weaknesses. Tax collection remained poor, because many firms avoided taxes, relied on barter and non-monetary offsets, and negotiated exemptions, so the government ran large deficits. Wage and pension arrears persisted. In the mid-1990s a large part of enterprise transactions took place by barter, tax offsets, and promissory notes rather than cash, which reduced the effectiveness of monetary policy and left many firms with little incentive to restructure. Stabilization therefore reduced inflation but did not solve the underlying fiscal and institutional problems. The government's reliance on high-yield short-term borrowing created vulnerabilities that became apparent in 1998.

The financial architecture of partial stabilization deserves explanation. The market for GKOs, short-term ruble government bonds first issued in 1993, grew rapidly after 1995 as the government replaced Central Bank credit with borrowing from banks and, from early 1996, from foreign investors permitted to buy them. High yields attracted capital, and banks that had once profited from holding government deposits now earned large returns from lending to the government. Russian companies and the government also began to borrow abroad: the first post-Soviet Eurobond issue was made in 1996. The stock market rose sharply in 1996–1997, and Moscow's financial district boomed. These years produced the impression, in some circles, that Russia was on the verge of sustained recovery.

Behind this impression the real economy remained weak. Federal budget deficits were large, by some measures seven or eight percent of national output in 1996, and tax collection fell short because of weak enforcement and widespread tax avoidance. Many firms operated in what the economists Clifford Gaddy and Barry Ickes called a "virtual economy," in which goods were overvalued in barter and offsets, so that value-subtracting enterprises could appear to be viable. Energy monopolies tolerated unpaid bills, and local governments accepted goods instead of taxes. Because cash was scarce, the government's own arrears to suppliers, employees, and pensioners became a form of borrowing. Official data showed output increasing slightly in 1997, but a second collapse was approaching.

Historians therefore assess this period as one in which macroeconomic indicators improved while the underlying institutions needed for growth, notably reliable taxation and enforcement of contracts, lagged. Stabilization required high real interest rates, which discouraged productive investment and strengthened financial speculation, and the exchange-rate corridor made the economy more vulnerable to shocks in capital flows. The successes of 1995–1997 were genuine, but their sustainability depended on conditions that proved temporary.

Section 67.37

The Road to the 1998 Financial Crisis: Debt, Oil Prices, and External Shocks

In 1997 the financial crisis that began in Thailand spread across Asia and made investors more cautious about emerging markets. Russia was exposed through its large holdings of foreign portfolio investment in GKOs. At the same time the price of oil, a main export, fell sharply during 1998, reducing export revenue and tax receipts. Investors demanded higher interest rates to hold Russian bonds; yields on GKOs rose to very high levels in the spring and summer of 1998, increasing the government's debt-service costs and the size of the deficit.

The government's responses included a rise in the Central Bank's interest rates, emergency borrowing, and a package of international support in July 1998, led by the International Monetary Fund, of about twenty-two billion dollars, with an initial portion paid immediately. Efforts to pass tax and spending measures were blocked by the Duma or weakly implemented. The policy of defending the ruble's exchange rate while financing deficits through short-term borrowing reached its limit as reserves fell. The International Monetary Fund's later assessment identified deep fiscal weakness and the deterioration of external conditions as important causes; it also noted the role of delays in policy adjustment, an assessment that Russian and foreign economists have debated.

The sequence of 1998 shows how vulnerability turned into crisis. In May, after the Duma rejected key tax measures and as the Asian crisis spread and oil prices fell, investors withdrew from Russian assets. The Central Bank raised its refinancing rate from 30 to 50 percent on 19 May and then to 150 percent on 27 May, an emergency step to defend the ruble, and yields on GKOs rose above one hundred percent. Russian banks that had borrowed abroad and had made forward contracts on the ruble were exposed to losses if the currency fell. A large share of GKOs was held by foreign investors, and the Central Bank's reserves fell as it sold foreign currency to defend the exchange rate. Miners' protests in May and the continuing political uncertainty further reduced confidence.

In July an international support package of about twenty-two billion dollars was announced, led by the IMF, with funds from the World Bank and Japan, conditional on Russian measures to raise revenue. The Duma passed only part of the proposed legislation. An attempt in July to exchange short-term ruble debt for longer-term dollar bonds attracted little interest, and the first installment of IMF funds was used up quickly in defending the ruble. By early August reserves were sharply lower, and the financial authorities faced the choice of devaluing, defaulting, or both. Historians disagree about whether an earlier devaluation or a more credible fiscal adjustment in 1997 could have prevented the crisis, and whether the IMF's support delayed necessary change or gave the government a chance to act.

Both domestic and external explanations contain truth. Without the Asian crisis and the fall in oil prices, Russia might have continued to roll over its debt for a time. Without chronic fiscal weakness and dependence on volatile short-term capital, a shock of this size would not have been so damaging. The political environment, in which the president was ill, the government was new, and the Duma was obstructive, reduced the capacity to respond quickly. Explanation therefore requires attention to the interplay of institutions, markets, and decisions.

Section 67.38

August 17, 1998: Default, Ruble Devaluation, and Banking Crisis

On 17 August 1998 the government and the Central Bank announced a package of measures: the ruble's trading band was widened, allowing a devaluation; the government declared a restructuring of its domestic debt, meaning that it stopped paying on the GKO bonds as scheduled and proposed to convert them into other instruments; and a ninety-day moratorium was placed on certain payments by private banks and firms to foreign creditors. The effect was a default on domestic government debt and a major devaluation. The ruble, which stood at roughly 6.2 to the dollar at the end of July 1998, stood at about 20.7 by the end of December 1998, according to standard exchange-rate series.

Banks, many of which held large amounts of GKOs and foreign debt, faced insolvency. Depositors rushed to withdraw funds, payments systems seized up, and several large banks failed or were restructured. Foreign investors lost heavily, and the shock affected world markets. The government dismissed Kiriyenko's cabinet on 23 August. The crisis revealed the fragility of the financial system that had developed since 1992.

The announcements of 17 August came after days of tension. President Yeltsin was reported to have said on 14 August that there would be no devaluation of the ruble, and the government's decision three days later was widely seen as a reversal. The ruble's trading band, which had been set at a range of about 5.3 to 7.1 per dollar, was widened to between 6.0 and 9.5; holders of GKOs were told that the government would restructure the bonds into new instruments with longer maturities, and the trading of GKOs was effectively suspended; and commercial banks and firms were barred for ninety days from making payments on certain foreign obligations, including forward contracts. By the end of the month the Central Bank stopped defending the band, and the ruble floated and fell sharply.

The banking sector was devastated. Several of the largest private banks, which had held GKOs and foreign liabilities, were unable to meet their obligations, and some deposits were frozen; large banks such as SBS-Agro, Inkombank, and Menatep failed or were placed under administration. The payments system faced disruptions, and households and firms rushed to withdraw cash. State-owned Sberbank, backed by the government, was in a stronger position, but private depositors lost savings, and the loss of confidence in the banks lasted for years. Foreign investors suffered heavy losses, and the Russian crisis contributed to turbulence in global financial markets in the autumn of 1998, including the difficulties of the American hedge fund Long-Term Capital Management, demonstrating Russia's integration into the world economy.

The nature of the announcement matters. It was described as a package to stabilize the system, yet in effect it was a default on domestic debt and a devaluation, with a moratorium that limited creditors' rights. Historians use the term "default" for the restructuring of GKOs and the moratorium on foreign payments, while Russian officials at the time used more cautious language. The Eurobonds and other foreign-currency government debt were not restructured in 1998, a point that distinguishes the event from a general default on all debt.

Section 67.39

The Social and Political Consequences of the Crisis, 1998–Early 1999

The devaluation caused a surge in inflation, which reached about 84 percent for 1998 measured December to December (the annual-average increase was much lower, about 28 percent, because the surge came late in the year), raised the price of imports, and wiped out the savings of many households and some businesses. Real wages fell sharply, banks froze deposits, and many firms that depended on imports or foreign loans closed. Output declined by several percent in 1998. For many Russians this crisis was the second destruction of savings in a decade.

Politically, the crisis discredited the government and its liberal economic advisers. The Duma rejected Chernomyrdin's attempt to return as prime minister, and in September a compromise candidate, Yevgeny Primakov, a former foreign minister and intelligence chief, was confirmed. His government included figures from different groups, including communists, and sought to stabilize the economy with limited intervention. Contrary to some expectations, the devaluation made Russian goods cheaper compared with imports, and domestic production began to recover in 1999, helped later by rising oil prices. Analysts debate the relative weights of devaluation, commodity prices, and policy in the recovery that followed, which belongs partly to Chapter 68.

The political aftermath unfolded within weeks. Yeltsin dismissed Kiriyenko's government on 23 August and named Chernomyrdin acting prime minister, but the Duma rejected his nomination twice, partly because many deputies opposed his policies and held him responsible for the crisis, and the threat of dissolution gave way to compromise. On 11 September 1998 the Duma confirmed Primakov by a large majority, with support from the Communists and many centrist deputies. His cabinet included the Communist-affiliated Yuri Maslyukov, a former head of Soviet state planning, as first deputy prime minister, and Viktor Gerashchenko returned as Central Bank chairman. The government emphasized stability and avoided the strongest interventionist measures that some had feared, and it pursued relatively cautious budgets.

The economic effects were harsh in the first half-year. Prices of imported goods rose steeply, shelves emptied of some goods, small businesses that depended on imports or credit failed, and real wages and household incomes fell sharply; later IMF-compiled figures place the decline in average real wages in 1999 at approximately 23 percent compared with 1998, with larger year-on-year falls during some of the worst months. Poverty increased, and many middle-class households that had begun to accumulate savings and consumer debt lost them. Unlike in 1992, however, the economy had more flexible markets and a sector of private firms, and the lower ruble made domestic producers more competitive. Output began to grow in 1999, and higher oil prices later that year helped public finances.

Scholars debate the sources of the recovery, attributing it variously to devaluation and import substitution, rising oil prices, the restructuring of firms, and the fiscal discipline of the Primakov government. The recovery's sources matter for interpretation, since they bear on whether the crisis exposed a flawed model or cleared away distortions. The impeachment proceedings against Yeltsin in the Duma and Primakov's dismissal in May 1999 belong to the political narrative of Chapter 68.

Section 67.40

The Yeltsin-Era Transformation: Achievements, Failures, and Unresolved Questions

By the end of 1998 Russia had changed profoundly. Private ownership dominated most sectors; prices were set largely by markets; elections were held regularly; the constitution of 1993 had been in force for five years; regions had considerable autonomy; media were more varied than in Soviet times; and Russians could travel, trade, and organize with more freedom than before. At the same time, output had fallen dramatically; the state's capacity to collect taxes and enforce law was weak; wealth was concentrated, and poverty was widespread; the courts and many institutions lacked independence; and the war in Chechnya and the use of force in 1993 had shown the willingness of the state to use violence against domestic opponents. The relationship between the business elite and the state raised questions about the equality of political influence.

Historians disagree about how to weigh these results. Some emphasize that Russia avoided civil war and the reversion to central planning and established basic institutions, under extraordinary difficulty; others emphasize that the method of reform, rule by decree, the 1993 confrontation, and the oligarchic privatizations undermined the institutions needed for a durable democratic market order. Many scholars hold that both elements were real. The legacy of the 1990s is also contested in political memory: for some Russians the decade was a time of freedom and opportunity, for others a time of humiliation and chaos. The weakening of the president's health, the 1998 crisis, and the search for a successor led to the leadership crisis examined in Chapter 68.

Comparisons help to place the Russian experience. Among post-communist states, Poland returned to its pre-transition output level by the mid-1990s, while Russia, Ukraine, and several other former Soviet republics remained far below their 1989 levels at the end of the decade. Central European states benefited from the prospect of membership in the European Union, from stronger administrative traditions, and from smaller industrial structures that were easier to reorient. Ukraine, which did not follow Russia's reform path closely, experienced an even larger decline in measured output, which complicates simple explanations that attribute Russia's difficulties to a single policy. Historians use such comparisons carefully, since the differences among countries are too large to allow simple conclusions.

The era also changed Russian life in ways not captured by economic statistics. Independent newspapers, television, publishing, and the internet in its early stages offered a range of information that had been unavailable in the Soviet period. Citizens could travel, form associations, practice religion openly, and engage in business, and nongovernmental organizations such as Memorial and human-rights groups were active. The Russian Orthodox Church recovered property and influence, other faiths revived, and cultural life diversified. At the same time, the wars and the use of force at home, corruption, and the weakness of courts and law enforcement left many citizens skeptical about the state. Public opinion surveys in later years recorded varied assessments of the decade, with many respondents associating it with disorder and hardship, and others with freedom.

For historians, the central analytic problem is separating the effects of different causes. Some difficulties arose from the Soviet inheritance and the collapse of the union; some from decisions made by the Russian government and from the interaction between political conflict and policy; others came from global conditions. Because these influences operated together, a single moral verdict is less useful than an analysis of mechanisms. The students' task is to recognize both the possibilities that existed in 1992 and the constraints that limited them.

Chapter Review and Historical Thinking Checkpoint

This chapter has traced the first seven years of the Russian Federation. The governing question was whether the political and economic transformation of the 1990s established the foundations of a functioning market democracy, or whether the methods of transition undermined the institutions necessary to sustain one. The evidence supports a more careful answer than either alternative.

Students should distinguish four pairs of concepts. First, the existence of markets is not the same as effective competition: prices were freed in a day, but competition, bankruptcy, and regulation developed slowly. Second, private ownership is not the same as transparent corporate governance: voucher privatization created millions of shareholders, but insiders and, after 1995, a few financial groups controlled the main assets. Third, elections are not the same as equal political influence: the 1996 election was competitive but unequal in access to media and money. Fourth, constitutional rules are not the same as the capacity to enforce them: the 1993 constitution created institutions, but its adoption followed an armed conflict, and the state's capacity to collect taxes and enforce law remained weak.

Problems for reflection: (1) Identify two causes of persistent inflation after January 1992 and explain how they interacted. (2) Explain why the legality and the legitimacy of Decree No. 1400 are separate questions. (3) Compare Moscow's relations with Tatarstan and with Chechnya, identifying what differed in the political situation. (4) Explain how the 1998 crisis reflected both external and domestic causes. The chapter's lessons about institutional capacity, contingency, and the gap between formal rules and practice prepare students for Chapter 68.

A strong analysis of the chapter distinguishes among levels of explanation. At the level of structure, Russia inherited an economy organized around planning and an administrative state with weak independent institutions for law and taxation, and a constitution that did not specify how conflicts were to be resolved. At the level of decisions, the government chose rapid price liberalization, mass privatization, and a political strategy that relied on presidential decrees, while other actors made choices such as the Congress's attempt to limit the executive and the decision to use force in 1993. At the level of contingency, events such as the failed assault of November 1994, the 1996 hostage crises, Yeltsin's health, and the Asian financial crisis influenced timing and outcomes. Explaining the chapter's major events requires all three levels.

The chapter also invites caution about counterfactuals. Historians may ask whether a more gradual reform, a different privatization design, or an earlier constitutional agreement would have produced better results, but they have to support their arguments with evidence from Russia and comparable cases and acknowledge uncertainty. Equally, they should avoid drawing a straight line from the 1990s to later developments: the political and economic structures of the Russian Federation after 1999 were shaped by the decisions of later leaders and new circumstances, which Chapters 68 to 72 examine. The Yeltsin years are best understood as a period of open possibilities, some foreclosed by the methods used, and not as a preparation for a known outcome.

Chapter Summary

This chapter examined the first phase of the independent Russian Federation, from the beginning of price liberalization in January 1992 to the aftermath of the August 1998 financial crisis. Its central problem was why efforts to build a market economy and a new constitutional order produced both major institutional change and severe instability.

Russia inherited the Soviet Union's international position, debts, and nuclear arsenal, but also an economy of state enterprises, administered prices, and weak market institutions, and a constitutional framework from the late Soviet period that gave no reliable way to settle disputes between president and legislature. The reform government under Yeltsin, Gaidar, and Chubais sought rapid liberalization, stabilization, and privatization with limited administrative capacity.

Price liberalization ended shortages and queues but was followed by very high inflation, the loss of savings, and a sharp decline in output. Stabilization was delayed by disputes over credit and by weak tax collection, and it came only in stages by 1995–1997. The dissolution of the ruble zone and the end of Soviet supply networks added further disruption.

Voucher privatization transferred thousands of enterprises into private hands, largely to insiders, while the loans-for-shares auctions of 1995 concentrated control of major resource assets in a few financial-industrial groups. The distinction between these programs is central to understanding the origins of Russia's business elite.

The social consequences were uneven. Wage arrears, collapsing savings, inequality, and a rise in mortality marked the decade, while some groups and regions benefited. Households survived through informal economic activity, household production, and family networks, and regional experiences varied from Moscow's gains to the decline of single-industry towns.

The constitutional conflict of 1992–1993 arose from the dual legitimacy of the president and the Congress. After the April referendum, Decree No. 1400 of 21 September led to the armed confrontation of October and the assault on the White House. The constitution adopted in December created a strong presidency, a bicameral parliament, and a constitutional court; the legality and costs of the October events remain debated, and casualty figures require attribution.

Federal relations were reorganized through the Federal Treaty and bilateral agreements such as the 1994 treaty with Tatarstan, while Chechnya's claim to independence led to the First Chechen War from December 1994. The war caused heavy civilian and military losses and ended with the Khasavyurt agreements of 1996, which postponed a decision on Chechnya's status.

Electoral politics was fragmented, and the 1996 presidential election was competitive but marked by unequal media access and campaign resources; evidence of outcome-changing fraud has not been established. The oligarchs were rival business groups with substantial but unequal political influence.

The 1998 crisis showed the fragility of a system dependent on short-term borrowing, weak taxation, and external capital. The Yeltsin-era legacy is contested, and historians continue to debate alternatives. The chapter prepares for Chapter 68, which examines the succession crisis, the Second Chechen War, and the reconstruction of state authority from 1999.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a new state was built under extreme pressure, with inherited institutions that did not fit the tasks assigned to them. The transformation of property, prices, and the constitution changed daily life more rapidly than almost any earlier period in Russian history, and its costs and benefits were distributed unequally across groups and regions.

Many of the chapter's developments continued after 1999: a strong presidency, a business elite with political ties, a federation built partly by bilateral bargains, an unresolved Chechen question, and a public memory of the 1990s that political actors would invoke. But none of these outcomes was predetermined in 1992. They emerged from decisions, conflicts, and contingencies, some of which this chapter has examined.

Questions remain open: how far different policies would have changed outcomes, how to weigh the benefits of new freedoms against the costs of disorder and inequality, and how to measure the legitimacy of institutions created in crisis. Chapter 68 turns to the succession crisis, the Second Chechen War, and the reconstruction of the Russian state from 1999, and Chapter 72 returns to these themes in the twenty-first century.
