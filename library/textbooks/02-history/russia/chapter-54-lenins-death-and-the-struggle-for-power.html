Part IX — Revolution and the Birth of the Soviet Union

Lenin's Death and the Struggle for Power

A History of Russia: From the Eurasian World to the Russian Federation

Chapter Opening

In dictated notes of December 1922 and January 1923, Lenin warned that the party's two ablest leaders might split it, criticized several colleagues, and suggested that Stalin's rudeness made him unsuitable as General Secretary. — a paraphrase of the sense of the notes known as the "Letter to the Congress," not a direct quotation.

On 27 January 1924, in bitter cold, Vladimir Lenin's body was carried through Moscow to Red Square, where it lay in a wooden mausoleum that was soon to become the most recognizable monument of the Soviet state. Among the pallbearers was Joseph Stalin. Leon Trotsky, the man many foreigners expected to succeed Lenin, was not there: he was recuperating from illness on the Black Sea coast, and he later claimed that Stalin had misinformed him about the date of the funeral. Whatever the truth of that dispute, the scene captured a larger fact. The founder of the Soviet state had died without a recognized successor, without a procedure for choosing one, and after more than a year in which he had been unable to direct government.

This chapter studies the struggle for power that followed and, above all, how Stalin became the dominant Soviet leader. It begins in 1922, when Lenin's first stroke removed him from daily politics, because the leadership that existed in 1924 had been taking shape for two years. It ends in 1929, when the political coalition that had supported the New Economic Policy (NEP), examined in Chapter 53, had fractured and Stalin had defeated the party's Left and Right in turn. The chapter does not narrate the industrialization, collectivization, and terror that followed; those belong to Chapters 55 through 57. Its subject is the political settlement that made them possible, and the question of whether that settlement was inevitable.

The central argument is that it was not. Stalin did not inherit supreme power automatically. Between 1922 and 1929, authority was contested within a collective leadership. Stalin became dominant through control of expanding party institutions, strategic alliance-building, ideological positioning, the organizational consequences of the ban on factions adopted in 1921, the weaknesses and choices of his rivals, and successive economic and political crises. The spine of the story is a sequence of changing coalitions: Stalin, Zinoviev, and Kamenev against Trotsky; then Stalin and the party's moderate wing, led by Bukharin, Rykov, and Tomsky, against Zinoviev, Kamenev, and Trotsky; then Stalin and his organizational allies against Bukharin and the Right.

The chapter also asks students to think about institutions as well as personalities. The Russian Communist Party was not an ordinary political party but the ruling organization of a multinational state, with committees at every level, a hierarchy of appointments, and rules that restricted open dispute. Who controlled those mechanisms, and how, mattered as much as who gave the best speeches. At the same time, the party contained hundreds of thousands of members, many recruited from the working class after Lenin's death, whose attitudes and loyalties shaped what leaders could do. Students will meet these ordinary members, as well as provincial secretaries, trade-union officials, Red Army commanders, and the peasants whose grain deliveries eventually broke the NEP coalition.

The evidence is rich but must be handled with care. Stenographic records of congresses and plenums, private letters, diaries, and memoirs survive, together with the writings of the contestants themselves. Many were produced as weapons in the struggle: Trotsky's memoirs and later histories were written in exile, Stalin's speeches were edited for publication, and Soviet historiography after 1930 rewrote the conflict as a story of correct Leninism defeating deviation. Archives opened after 1991 have allowed historians to revise older accounts, including accounts that explained Stalin's victory chiefly by his personality or chiefly by impersonal bureaucratic forces.

The chapter also situates the struggle in a wider context. The Bolshevik leaders believed that their revolution was part of a world revolution, and the failure of revolutions in Germany in 1923 and China in 1927 shaped domestic arguments about what the USSR could achieve alone. Meanwhile, the USSR was a union of republics, and the Georgian Affair of 1922 (Chapter 53, §53.15) had already raised questions about the way the center treated non-Russian Communists. By the chapter's end, students should be able to explain how a collective leadership became a personal one, why that outcome was explicable but not predetermined, and how historians evaluate the evidence for competing explanations.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Trace Lenin's illness and withdrawal from government between 1922 and 1924 and explain how it created a leadership problem for the party.
  2. Interpret the "Letter to the Congress" accurately, distinguishing it from a formal will and explaining what it did and did not recommend.
  3. Explain how the party's institutions, including the Politburo, Orgburo, Secretariat, and Central Control Commission, interacted with the Soviet state.
  4. Analyze how the General Secretaryship and appointment powers grew in importance without assuming that this was obvious in 1922.
  5. Compare the strengths and liabilities of Trotsky, Zinoviev, Kamenev, Stalin, Bukharin, and other leaders as potential successors.
  6. Evaluate why Trotsky lost power, weighing prestige, organization, alliances, and mistakes without reducing the outcome to a single cause.
  7. Describe the successive coalitions of 1922–1929 and explain why each formed and broke down.
  8. Explain the debate over "Socialism in One Country" and its relationship to Bolshevik internationalism and the Comintern.
  9. Assess why the grain-procurement crisis of 1927–1928 fractured the NEP coalition.
  10. Evaluate competing historical explanations of Stalin's rise using different types of evidence.
  11. Distinguish contingency from inevitability in the succession struggle and explain the evidence for each.
  12. Connect the political outcome of 1929 to the transformations of Chapters 55 through 57 without treating those developments as predetermined.

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  2. What did Lenin's final writings and dictations actually say, and how were they used after his death?
  3. Why did control of appointments, agendas, and information matter so much in the party-state?
  4. Why was there no obvious successor to Lenin, and how did each leading figure's record help and harm him?
  5. Why did Stalin's partners of 1923–1925 become his opponents?
  6. How did disputes about history, ideology, and Lenin's legacy become instruments of political struggle?
  7. Why did the NEP coalition of 1926–1927 break apart?
  8. Which explanations of Stalin's victory emphasize structure, which emphasize individual choice, and what evidence supports each?
  9. What alternatives existed in 1924, 1927, and 1928, and why were they closed off?

Historical Context

Chapter 53 described the settlement of 1921 to 1924: the New Economic Policy, which restored markets in agriculture and trade; the 1921 ban on factions inside the Russian Communist Party (Bolsheviks); the suppression of rival socialist parties; and the creation of the USSR on 30 December 1922. By 1922 the party's political monopoly was complete in law and increasingly in practice. The party had grown from a small revolutionary organization into a mass governing body. It had perhaps half a million members in the early 1920s and, by late 1925, just over one million members and candidates, but only a minority of members had joined before 1917, and the share of those with underground experience was small.

Lenin's position within this system was unique. He chaired the Council of People's Commissars, was the dominant voice in the Politburo, and was the party's most authoritative theorist. His authority rested partly on formal office, partly on his record as founder of Bolshevism and leader of the October seizure of power (Chapter 51), and partly on the habit, among colleagues who often disagreed with him, of eventually deferring to him. When Lenin was present, he arbitrated among leaders with strong personalities and divergent views; when he was absent, nothing replaced that function. Neither the party rules nor the new Union Constitution described how a leader should be chosen, because the Bolsheviks regarded their leadership as collective and their legitimacy as flowing from the party and the working class rather than from a person.

The leadership itself was a generation of people who had worked together for two decades, often in exile or underground, and whose personal and political relationships were complicated. Trotsky, who had joined the Bolsheviks only in 1917, had led the Red Army and had enormous prestige abroad, but was distrusted by older Bolsheviks for his earlier criticisms of Lenin. Grigory Zinoviev and Lev Kamenev were among Lenin's oldest associates but had opposed the October insurrection in 1917 (Chapter 51, §51.9). Nikolai Bukharin, younger and widely liked, was the party's leading theorist of economics. Stalin, a long-serving member of the Central Committee and commissar for nationalities, was regarded as a competent organizer and troubleshooter rather than a theorist or orator.

The economic situation also framed events. NEP produced recovery but also tension: the Scissors Crisis of autumn 1923 (Chapter 53, §53.9), unemployment, resentment of the Nepmen, and a peasantry that sold grain on its own terms. Leaders disagreed about how fast the state sector should grow, how much to tax the countryside, and where capital for industrial investment would come from. Disputes about these questions were bound up with disputes about power, which makes it difficult to separate principle from ambition in the arguments of the 1920s. Students should avoid both extremes: the leaders were not simply cynical competitors, and their ideological arguments were not simply sincere statements disconnected from personal rivalry.

A note on dates and terms. This chapter uses New Style dates throughout. The party was officially the Russian Communist Party (Bolsheviks), or RKP(b), until December 1925, when it was renamed the All-Union Communist Party (Bolsheviks), VKP(b). Petrograd was renamed Leningrad in January 1924, and the chapter uses the name appropriate to the date.

Section 54.1

Lenin's Final Years: Illness, Recovery, and Withdrawal from Government

Lenin had been ill since an assassination attempt in 1918 left bullets in his body, and he suffered from severe headaches and exhaustion during 1921. On 25–26 May 1922 he had his first major stroke, which impaired his speech and the use of his right side. Over the summer he recovered partially at the estate of Gorki outside Moscow and, in the autumn, returned to work, attending meetings of the Council of People's Commissars and the Politburo and speaking at the Fourth Congress of the Communist International in November. The recovery encouraged colleagues to assume that he would resume his role, and for months the leadership's arrangements were understood to be temporary.

On 16 December 1922 a second stroke ended his regular work. Physicians restricted his activity, and the Politburo, with Stalin given responsibility for enforcing the medical regime, tried to limit his contact with political business. Lenin nevertheless continued, with permission that was sometimes granted and sometimes disputed, to dictate short notes and articles to secretaries during December 1922 and the first months of 1923. A third stroke on 9 March 1923 deprived him of speech and the ability to write, and from then until his death he was largely confined to Gorki, with occasional periods of partial recovery in which he could walk and make limited sounds. He took no part in politics after that date, though the leadership and the press continued to refer to him as the party's leader.

The leadership in these months developed gradually into a collective. In 1922 the Politburo included Lenin, Trotsky, Stalin, Zinoviev, Kamenev, Rykov, and Tomsky as full members, with Bukharin and others as candidates. Zinoviev delivered the main political report to the Twelfth Party Congress in April 1923, a task that would previously have been Lenin's. Informal consultations among Stalin, Zinoviev, and Kamenev grew into a regular practice that opponents later called the "triumvirate" or troika (see §54.7). These arrangements were not initially presented as a succession plan, and the participants did not behave as though Lenin's return were impossible: many officials hoped that he would recover, and the leaders competed to be seen as faithful to his policies.

The medical and political questions should be kept distinct. Lenin's illness limited what he could do, but it did not by itself determine who would succeed him. Historians sometimes retell the period as a drama in which the dying leader sought to warn the party against Stalin; the evidence suggests a more uneven reality, in which Lenin's views of individual colleagues shifted, his notes addressed several concerns at once, and his health permitted only fragments of intervention. The next section examines those interventions.

Section 54.2

Lenin's Final Political Interventions and the “Letter to the Congress”

In the weeks between his second and third strokes, Lenin dictated notes on several subjects that preoccupied him. One was bureaucracy. He believed that the Soviet state had inherited and reproduced the habits of the old administration, and he proposed reorganizing the Workers' and Peasants' Inspectorate (Rabkrin), which Stalin had headed until 1922, and merging it with the Central Control Commission so as to improve oversight. Another was cooperation: in an article of January 1923 he argued that cooperatives could provide a route to socialism by drawing peasants into voluntary collective commerce. A third was the national question. In notes dictated on 30–31 December 1922 he criticized the handling of the Georgian Affair and the tendency of Russian officials to treat other nationalities with arrogance (Chapter 53, §53.15), holding Stalin and Ordzhonikidze partly responsible.

The most famous document is the set of notes of 23–26 December 1922 with a postscript of 4 January 1923, which became known as the "Letter to the Congress" or, in later usage, the "Testament." Lenin dictated it for the next party congress. Its main concern was the stability of the Central Committee, and he warned that the two most able members, Stalin and Trotsky, might come into conflict and split the party. He then characterized several leaders. Stalin, he wrote, had concentrated enormous power as General Secretary and might not use it with sufficient caution. Trotsky was exceptionally capable but overconfident and too attracted to administrative methods. The conduct of Zinoviev and Kamenev in October 1917, when they had opposed the insurrection, was not an accident. Bukharin was the party's most valuable theorist but his understanding of Marxism was questionable. Georgy Pyatakov was able but too absorbed in administration to be reliable in political matters.

In the postscript of 4 January 1923, Lenin said that Stalin was too rude, a fault tolerable among Communists but intolerable in a General Secretary, and he suggested that comrades consider ways of removing Stalin from that post and appointing someone who was more patient, loyal, polite, and considerate. This is the sentence most often cited. The immediate background was a quarrel in December 1922, in which Stalin had shouted at Lenin's wife, Nadezhda Krupskaya, for allegedly allowing Lenin to dictate; Lenin learned of the incident and, on 5 March 1923, sent Stalin a note breaking personal relations. The Testament should not be read in isolation from these events, nor from Lenin's concurrent concern with the Georgian question.

Students must be careful about what the Letter was. It was not a will in a legal or Western sense, it did not name a successor, and it did not endorse any individual as leader. It made recommendations about the composition of the Central Committee, including increasing its size, and it offered critical assessments of six people, none of them wholly favorable. Its recommendation about Stalin was a suggestion for the party to consider, not an order. Its use was determined by those who held it: Krupskaya delivered the sealed text to the Central Committee after Lenin's death, and the leadership decided how it would be handled (see §54.8).

Historical Vocabulary
Letter to the Congress ("Testament")

Definition: A set of dictated notes by Lenin, dated 23–26 December 1922 with a postscript of 4 January 1923, intended for the next party congress, assessing the leadership and proposing changes to the Central Committee.

Historical context: Produced during Lenin's illness. It was withheld from the party at large after his death and read only to restricted groups at the Thirteenth Congress in 1924. It was not published in the USSR until after Stalin's death, though versions appeared abroad earlier.

Why it matters: The term "Testament" can mislead because the text is not a will and designates no heir. Its importance lies in what it reveals about Lenin's concerns and in the political uses to which it was put.

Section 54.3

How the Communist Party Governed the Soviet State

To understand the succession, students must understand the machinery of the party and its relationship to the state. In formal terms, the party's highest body was the Congress, which met annually or nearly so in the early 1920s and elected a Central Committee of several dozen members. The Central Committee met in plenary sessions every few months and delegated daily direction to three smaller organs. The Politburo, created in 1919, decided major questions of policy and had around seven full members and several candidates in the 1920s. The Orgburo (Organizational Bureau) dealt with organizational matters, including staffing and the allocation of party workers. The Secretariat, headed after 1922 by the General Secretary, supervised implementation of decisions and the party apparatus, including its records and appointments.

A further organ, the Central Control Commission, elected by the Congress, handled discipline and disputes, and could investigate and sanction party members. Below the center were provincial, district, and local committees, each with its own secretary and staff, and thousands of local party cells in factories, offices, military units, and villages. The state had a parallel structure: the Council of People's Commissars, the commissariats, the soviets at every level, and, after 1922, the union institutions described in Chapter 53, §53.16. In practice the party shaped state decisions through its members in state positions, who were expected to follow party directives, and through the principle that important state appointments required party approval.

The party's internal rules were governed by democratic centralism, a principle that combined free discussion before a decision with binding discipline after it. The ban on factions adopted in 1921 (Chapter 53, §53.4) further constrained debate by prohibiting organized groups with separate platforms and by empowering the Central Committee to expel its own members for factionalism. In theory, members could still criticize policy within party bodies. In practice, the ban meant that dissenters could be accused of factionalism, and it gave the officials who enforced party discipline a powerful instrument. The ban did not by itself determine who would win; it created a framework in which control over institutions mattered more than control over an organized following.

The central point for the succession is that political power in this system depended on more than popularity or oratory. It depended on control over appointments to party and state posts, over the flow of information to leaders and from them to members, over the agenda and composition of meetings, over selection of delegates to congresses, and over disciplinary procedures. These levers were available to those who held the relevant offices, and they were more important when the party expanded rapidly and when most members knew little about leaders beyond what the press told them. Students should also not imagine that this machinery operated flawlessly: provincial secretaries had their own interests, local organizations sometimes resisted directives, and members could and did vote against the leadership in some contexts, as the events of 1923 and 1925 show.

Section 54.4

Stalin as General Secretary and the Growth of Organizational Power

On 3 April 1922, after the Eleventh Party Congress, the Central Committee created the post of General Secretary and elected Stalin to it, with Vyacheslav Molotov and Valerian Kuibyshev as additional secretaries. The post's original purpose was to give the Secretariat a single responsible head at a time when the party's growth and administrative burdens had made coordination difficult. Lenin supported the appointment of Stalin, who was already a member of the Politburo and the Orgburo and remained People's Commissar for Nationalities until Narkomnats was wound up in 1923 as the new Union institutions replaced its functions. Stalin had also headed Rabkrin until 1922. Few contemporaries regarded the General Secretaryship as the most powerful office in the party; it was an administrative position, and some of Stalin's colleagues considered it a less prestigious post than chairing the Soviet government or the Comintern.

Its significance grew because of what Stalin did with it. The Secretariat and its Records and Assignment Department (Uchraspred) maintained files on party members and recommended them for posts, so that Stalin and his allies gained influence over thousands of appointments, from provincial party secretaries to officials in state commissariats. The process was in part a response to real needs: the party was recruiting rapidly, local organizations required competent leaders, and the leadership wished to place reliable people in key positions. It was also a source of patronage, because those appointed owed their positions to the center and tended to support the leaders who had appointed them. By 1923, a growing number of provincial secretaries were people who had been selected or promoted through the Secretariat.

Stalin's other offices reinforced this position. As a member of the Politburo, the Orgburo, and the Secretariat simultaneously, he was the only leader who sat on all three central organs, which gave him a view of the whole range of party business and an unusual ability to prepare agendas and draft resolutions. His collaborators, including Molotov and later Kuibyshev, Lazar Kaganovich, and Kliment Voroshilov, helped administer the apparatus and became his closest supporters. Stalin also cultivated an image as a practical, hard-working, undramatic leader who could be relied on to implement decisions, which contrasted with the more flamboyant styles of Trotsky and Zinoviev.

Students should resist two opposite errors. One is to assume that Lenin or anyone else consciously handed Stalin a dictatorship in 1922; the evidence shows that the office was not seen in those terms. The other is to treat the apparatus as irrelevant: control of appointments and information became a major advantage as the party grew and the leadership divided. The General Secretaryship became powerful in part because Stalin used it effectively and because the party-state itself became more bureaucratic, which increased the importance of those who administered it.

Historical Vocabulary
Nomenklatura

Definition: A system of lists of important posts in the party, state, and economy whose occupants were appointed or approved by party committees, and the people eligible to fill those posts.

Historical context: The practice developed in the early 1920s through the Secretariat and the Orgburo, which maintained records of personnel and assigned party members to positions. It later became a central feature of Soviet administration.

Why it matters: Nomenklatura gave party committees control over the careers of officials and tied administrators to the leadership. The term should not be treated as meaning simply "the elite": it refers specifically to the appointment system, although it was later also used for the group of people who held such posts.

Section 54.5

The Succession Field: Trotsky, Zinoviev, Kamenev, Stalin, and Bukharin

No formal procedure existed for choosing a successor to Lenin, and no single obvious heir. The Bolshevik tradition of collective leadership, together with the party's suspicion of personal power, meant that open claims to succession were considered improper. Students should therefore think of the succession as a competition for influence within a group, not as a race for a vacant throne. Each leader had distinct assets and liabilities, and the combinations that formed among them changed over time.

Trotsky had the greatest public prestige. He had chaired the Petrograd Soviet in 1917, organized the October insurrection through the Military Revolutionary Committee, and created and led the Red Army during the Civil War (Chapters 51 and 52). He was a brilliant orator and writer. His liabilities were his late entry into the Bolshevik Party in 1917, his earlier polemics against Lenin, his reputation for arrogance, his association with militarized labor and with coercive policies of War Communism, and his lack of a network within the party apparatus. Zinoviev headed the Leningrad party organization and chaired the Comintern, positions that gave him a major base and a platform. He was a skilled orator, but many colleagues distrusted him because of his opposition to the October insurrection and his tendency to alarmism. Kamenev, chairman of the Moscow Soviet and deputy head of the government, was an experienced administrator and an influential figure, with a reputation as moderate and conciliatory but also lacking an independent following.

Stalin's reputation was that of a reliable practical organizer. He had been among the leading Bolsheviks inside Russia during the underground years and had taken major responsibilities during the Civil War and afterward. His weaknesses included limited oratorical flair, a lack of experience abroad, and the perception among some colleagues that he was a secondary figure. Bukharin, editor of Pravda and a member of the Politburo from 1924, was admired as a theorist and popular among younger members, but he had no administrative base and had previously opposed Lenin on several issues. Aleksei Rykov, who replaced Lenin as chairman of the Council of People's Commissars in 1924, and Mikhail Tomsky, head of the trade unions, were important institutional figures who often sided with Bukharin on economic policy, though they were not simply his followers.

These assessments are, in part, retrospective. Contemporaries did not agree on which of these leaders was strongest, and many foreign observers expected Trotsky to prevail. The leaders themselves shared a strong sense that no one should be seen to be seeking Lenin's mantle, and their rivalry took the form of disputes over policy, party discipline, and interpretation of Leninism. This helps explain why arguments over doctrine and history were so intense: they were the means by which leaders claimed legitimacy.

Section 54.6

Trotsky: Prestige, Isolation, and Political Vulnerability

Trotsky's position in 1922 seemed strong. He was People's Commissar for Military and Naval Affairs and chairman of the Revolutionary Military Council, he was widely credited with victory in the Civil War, and Lenin had in December 1922 proposed that he become a deputy chairman of the Council of People's Commissars, an offer he declined. His difficulty was that his prestige derived largely from the revolutionary and military past, and the issues of the 1920s were administrative, economic, and organizational. His strengths as a commander and orator were less useful in committee politics, and the Red Army, though under his formal leadership, was not a political base that he could mobilize against the party.

Several features of his career and personality contributed to his isolation. Many older Bolsheviks remembered his criticisms of Lenin before 1917 and regarded him as an outsider who had joined the party at the moment of victory. His association with the harsh policies of War Communism, including the militarization of labor proposed in 1920, and with the use of former tsarist officers in the Red Army, aroused suspicion among colleagues. He did not build a network of followers within the party apparatus and did not take an active role in the day-to-day politics of the Politburo, preferring to present his views in writing. He also was reluctant to use Lenin's final criticisms of Stalin, for instance on the Georgian question, in open combat, which some historians interpret as misjudgment and others as reluctance to appear to be exploiting a dying leader's authority.

Historians caution against simple explanations. It is often said that Trotsky lost because he was arrogant, or because Stalin controlled appointments. Both factors mattered, but they operated within a larger structure. The ban on factions limited the forms of opposition available. The party's organization favored those who controlled the apparatus. Zinoviev and Kamenev, who also had grievances against Trotsky and feared his prestige, were willing to ally with Stalin to contain him. And Trotsky's own decisions, such as periods of absence from Moscow for illness, a reluctance to engage in factional maneuvering, and public statements that his opponents could portray as challenges to party unity, narrowed his options. His vulnerability was therefore the product of political structure, personal choices, and the shifting alliances of other leaders, rather than of any single flaw.

Section 54.7

The Triumvirate and the Crisis of 1923

The alliance among Stalin, Zinoviev, and Kamenev took shape during Lenin's illness and functioned as the principal leadership combination against Trotsky in 1923. Each had reasons for cooperating. Zinoviev and Kamenev regarded Trotsky as the main rival for leadership and as a threat to the party's collective character; Stalin valued allies with prestige and with bases in Leningrad and Moscow. At the Twelfth Party Congress in April 1923, the three coordinated their positions, with Zinoviev delivering the principal political report while Trotsky spoke on industry and did not openly challenge the leadership or use Lenin's notes on Georgia. The broader secret majority faction later known as the "Seven" or semyorka was formed in August 1924. It consisted of Stalin, Zinoviev, Kamenev, Bukharin, Rykov, and Tomsky, the six Politburo members other than Trotsky, together with Central Control Commission chairman Valerian Kuibyshev.

The autumn of 1923 brought a crisis. The Scissors Crisis (Chapter 53, §53.9) produced widespread complaints about prices and wages. A wave of strikes occurred in the summer and autumn, and independent workers' groups emerged, which the political police suppressed. In Germany, a Communist uprising planned with Comintern encouragement collapsed in October, undermining hopes of European revolution. Within the party, discontent grew about the concentration of authority in the secretarial hierarchy, the appointment of officials from above rather than their election, and the lack of discussion of economic policy.

In October 1923 Trotsky sent letters to the Central Committee criticizing the economic leadership and the "regime within the party," and a group of forty-six mostly mid-level party officials, including Pyatakov, Yevgeny Preobrazhensky, and Timofei Sapronov, submitted a statement, the Declaration of the Forty-Six, with similar criticisms and a call for open discussion (see the Primary Source feature). In December, Trotsky published a series of articles in Pravda, later collected as The New Course, arguing for party democracy and warning against bureaucratization. The Politburo adopted a resolution on party democracy in December that conceded some points in principle, but the leadership and its allies then organized a campaign against Trotsky, portraying him as a factionalist and a deviationist. In January 1924, the Thirteenth Party Conference condemned his position as a petty-bourgeois deviation, a few days before Lenin's death.

The episode shows why the triumvirate depended heavily on Zinoviev and Kamenev. Without them, Stalin lacked the standing in the Politburo to take on Trotsky, and their prestige legitimized the campaign. It also shows that the opposition of 1923 was not a coherent faction. The Forty-Six and Trotsky overlapped but were not identical, and many signatories later withdrew or recanted. The crisis revealed real discontent, but also the leadership's ability to mobilize the apparatus against criticism, an ability that would prove decisive in subsequent years.

Section 54.8

Lenin's Death, the Testament, and the Thirteenth Party Congress

Lenin died at Gorki on 21 January 1924. The leadership staged a funeral on 27 January and decided, over Krupskaya's objections, to preserve his body, which was placed in a wooden mausoleum in Red Square (a permanent stone mausoleum was constructed in 1929–1930 and completed in 1930). On 26 January the Second Congress of Soviets of the USSR, meeting to ratify the Union Constitution (Chapter 53, §53.16), heard a speech by Stalin that took the form of a solemn pledge to carry out Lenin's legacy. In the same days Petrograd was renamed Leningrad. Trotsky, absent in the Caucasus, did not attend. Stalin later delivered lectures published as Foundations of Leninism, presenting himself as a faithful pupil and interpreter. These actions began what became the Lenin cult, a complex phenomenon that combined genuine popular mourning, the party's search for legitimacy, and deliberate political construction.

In the months after Lenin's death, the party also carried out a mass recruitment known as the Lenin Enrollment, which brought in a quarter of a million or so new members, mostly workers, and reinforced the share of the membership that had no pre-1917 history and little experience of earlier controversies. The enrollment was not the only source of growth: recruitment continued afterward, and by late 1925 the party had just over one million members and candidates. This helped the leadership in two ways: new members were more likely to follow the party line, and the enrollment showed the party's continued claim to represent the working class.

The Testament was delivered by Krupskaya to the Central Committee. Before the Thirteenth Congress, held from 23 to 31 May 1924, the Central Committee met and the text was read to a restricted audience, with a further reading to the heads of delegations. It was not read to the congress as a whole or published. Zinoviev argued that Lenin's fears about Stalin had not been borne out, and Stalin offered to resign. The Central Committee voted to retain him, with Trotsky and some of his supporters not pressing the issue. The decision reflected the interests of the triumvirate, which had no wish to see the balance altered, and the desire of many members to avoid a split in the leadership.

This is an important contingency point. Had the Testament been read openly, or had the Central Committee voted differently, Stalin might have lost the General Secretaryship, though it is far from certain what would then have happened: the post might have been reassigned to a person with similar interests, or Stalin might have retained influence through other positions. What the episode shows is that Stalin's retention depended on the decisions of leaders who were his allies at the time and who later had cause to regret it. Students should therefore avoid describing the Thirteenth Congress as the moment of Stalin's victory; it was rather a moment at which a possible check on his position was not applied.

Section 54.9

Trotsky's Defeat and the Battle over October

In the autumn of 1924, Trotsky published Lessons of October, an essay prefacing a volume of his collected writings on 1917. It recounted the revolution and criticized the conduct of Zinoviev and Kamenev, who had opposed the October insurrection, drawing a parallel with the failed German Communist uprising of 1923 and suggesting that leaders who hesitated at decisive moments were a danger to revolutionary movements. The essay also implicitly emphasized Trotsky's own role in 1917 and did not give Stalin a prominent place. Zinoviev, Kamenev, and Stalin, whose reputations were directly challenged, responded with a campaign, known as the "literary discussion," that presented Trotsky as a latecomer to Bolshevism, a critic of Lenin before 1917, and a proponent of a "Trotskyism" that was alien to Leninism.

The dispute shows how history became a weapon. Each leader's claim to legitimacy depended on a reading of the revolutionary past, and leaders used archival materials, speeches, and polemical articles to contest the record. Old quotations from Trotsky's pre-1917 disputes with Lenin were republished, accounts of the October insurrection were rewritten to emphasize the role of the party as a whole, and Stalin's own contribution was highlighted. Historians still use these polemics as evidence of the participants' views, but they must be treated as arguments written for a purpose, not as neutral accounts.

The political consequences followed. In January 1925 a plenum of the Central Committee condemned Trotsky's conduct, though it did not expel him from the party as Zinoviev and Kamenev had proposed, and removed him from the post of People's Commissar for Military and Naval Affairs and chairman of the Revolutionary Military Council. Mikhail Frunze, a respected Civil War commander, replaced him in the military posts. Trotsky was assigned to economic work, including positions in the Supreme Council of the National Economy. His removal deprived him of his only institutional base outside the party apparatus. The episode also set a pattern: a leader criticized for factionalism would be removed from key positions through the machinery of the Central Committee while remaining formally within the party.

Students should note that the leadership's ability to isolate Trotsky depended on the cooperation of several people who later became his allies. It also depended on a party membership that was newly recruited and not well informed about the pre-1917 disputes. The dispute over October was therefore both a historical argument and a contest for authority within a system where the party's claim to legitimacy rested on fidelity to a revolutionary tradition.

Section 54.10

“Socialism in One Country” and the Ideological Reorientation

The Bolsheviks had come to power in 1917 expecting that their revolution would spark socialist revolutions in more industrially developed countries, particularly Germany, and that these would assist backward Russia. The failure of revolutions in Hungary, Germany, and elsewhere by 1923 raised a practical question: could socialism be built in the USSR if revolution did not occur elsewhere in the near future? Some leaders believed that the Soviet state could survive only if aided by a revolution in the West, since an isolated peasant country lacked the resources to construct a socialist economy. Others pointed out that the party had already been governing for years and needed a program for the present.

Stalin's initial position was cautious. In Foundations of Leninism (spring 1924), he wrote that the victory of socialism in one country was not possible without the combined efforts of several countries. By December 1924, in an article attacking Trotsky's theory, he argued instead that socialism could be built in the USSR, with the proletariat of the USSR overcoming its own bourgeoisie even if final victory, in the sense of guarantee against foreign intervention, required revolution abroad. The formulation was developed through 1925, notably at the Fourteenth Party Conference in April 1925, and Bukharin supported it with arguments grounded in the possibilities of NEP and cooperatives. Supporters cited statements by Lenin, including a 1915 article suggesting that socialism might first triumph in a single country and his 1923 writings on cooperation, though interpretations of these texts differ.

The opposing view was associated with Trotsky's theory of permanent revolution, developed after 1905, which held that revolutionary movements in a backward country could not stop at a bourgeois stage and that socialism could survive only as part of an international revolution. Trotsky and his supporters argued that the new formula underestimated the dangers of isolation and the need to give priority to international revolution and to heavy industrial investment. Stalin and his allies portrayed this as defeatism and a lack of confidence in the Soviet working class.

It is important not to portray the change as the abandonment of internationalism. The Comintern continued to operate, Soviet leaders continued to expect and encourage revolutionary movements abroad, and the new formula did not reject the idea that world revolution was ultimately necessary. It addressed a different question: whether the USSR should regard its own construction as a legitimate socialist project in the meantime. The political importance of the slogan was that it gave the leadership a program that appealed to many party members tired of waiting for foreign revolution and eager to see their work as significant, and that placed Stalin's critics in the position of appearing pessimistic or unpatriotic.

Historical Vocabulary
Socialism in One Country

Definition: The doctrine, formulated by Stalin in 1924–1925 and supported by Bukharin, that socialism could be built in the USSR without a prior victorious revolution in other countries.

Historical context: It emerged in the debate between Stalin's supporters and Trotsky, who defended the theory of permanent revolution and the dependence of Soviet socialism on international revolution.

Why it matters: The phrase did not mean the end of Soviet support for world revolution, but it shifted priorities toward domestic construction. It also became a test of loyalty in the party's internal disputes.

Section 54.11

Zinoviev, Kamenev, and the New Opposition

The alliance among Stalin, Zinoviev, and Kamenev did not survive Trotsky's defeat. Several factors contributed to the break. One was policy toward the peasantry. In 1925 Bukharin, Stalin's chief theoretical ally at the time, encouraged measures to make agriculture more productive by easing restrictions on hiring labor and leasing land, and in a speech in April used a phrase that urged peasants to enrich themselves, which he later retracted under criticism. Zinoviev and Kamenev, and other critics, saw this as a concession to wealthy peasants and a departure from socialist priorities. Zinoviev's writings of 1925 criticized what he called a drift toward capitalism in the village.

A second factor was power. Zinoviev controlled the Leningrad party organization and headed the Comintern; Kamenev held posts in the government and the Moscow Soviet. Stalin and his allies, with Bukharin, Rykov, and Tomsky, were building a majority in the Politburo that did not depend on them. The elevation of Stalin's allies, including Molotov, Voroshilov, and Kalinin, to full Politburo membership in 1926 reinforced this. There were also personal tensions and growing suspicion, on both sides, about the other's ambitions. Krupskaya sympathized with the dissidents, and Grigory Sokolnikov, the Commissar of Finance, also joined them.

The conflict came to a head at the Fourteenth Party Congress in December 1925, which also renamed the party the All-Union Communist Party (Bolsheviks). The Leningrad delegation, led by Zinoviev, presented a minority report criticizing the leadership's economic line, and Kamenev delivered a speech opposing the creation of a cult of a single leader, saying that he did not believe Stalin should perform the function of unifier of the party staff. The congress, whose delegates had been selected through the apparatus and which was overwhelmingly loyal to the majority, rejected the opposition by a large vote. The Leningrad organization was then taken over by Sergei Kirov, a Stalin ally sent from Azerbaijan, who replaced Zinoviev's followers with new officials.

This episode is essential because it shows Stalin defeating rivals through successive coalition shifts rather than through a permanent faction. His enemies of 1925 were his allies of 1923, and the people with whom he allied in 1925 had been among those who opposed him earlier. It also shows that the opposition was defeated, not through exclusion at first but through a congress vote and the replacement of its base. Zinoviev and Kamenev remained in the leadership for a time but were increasingly isolated, which led them to seek allies on the other side of the party, including Trotsky.

Section 54.12

The United Opposition, 1926–1927

In 1926, Trotsky, Zinoviev, and Kamenev, who had spent the previous years fighting each other, joined forces in what became known as the United Opposition. Their cooperation reflected shared opposition to the leadership's direction and recognition that separate resistance had failed. Supporters included Pyatakov, Preobrazhensky, and others, and Krupskaya was sympathetic. The group's program, set out in a platform circulated in 1927, called for faster industrialization financed in part by taxing wealthy peasants and Nepmen, restoring party democracy, controlling bureaucracy, narrowing inequality, and pursuing a more assertive policy toward international revolution. Their critics, Stalin and Bukharin among them, argued that this program would endanger the alliance with the peasantry on which NEP and the regime depended.

The opposition used methods that the ban on factions made dangerous. Its leaders spoke at party cells, circulated documents, and organized meetings, in some cases in conspiratorial conditions. The leadership responded by accusing them of factionalism and splitting, by organizing noisy meetings in which opponents were shouted down, and by using the Central Control Commission and the political police to monitor their activities. The sanctions came in stages. Kamenev had already been reduced from full to candidate member of the Politburo at the start of 1926. Zinoviev was removed from the Politburo on 23 July 1926, and Trotsky and Kamenev were removed on 23 October 1926. On 22 November 1926 Zinoviev was formally relieved as chairman of the Executive Committee of the Communist International, depriving him of his international institutional base. Each concession made by the opposition to avoid a split, such as a statement in October 1926 disavowing factionalism, weakened its credibility.

Foreign events intensified the dispute. In China, the Comintern had directed the small Communist Party to cooperate with the Nationalist Kuomintang under Chiang Kai-shek, an approach Trotsky and the opposition criticized. In April 1927 Chiang turned on the Communists in Shanghai and killed large numbers, an event that supported the opposition's critique but that the leadership blamed on other causes. In May 1927, Britain severed relations with the USSR after a police raid on a Soviet trading organization in London, and a "war scare" spread in Moscow, with leaders warning of imminent attack. Stalin portrayed the opposition's criticism as defeatism that would aid the enemy. The analytic point is that foreign setbacks intensified an existing debate about Comintern strategy and the party's priorities, and gave the leadership an argument for unity, though they should not turn this chapter into a study of foreign policy (Soviet diplomacy of the 1930s, including collective security, is treated in Chapter 58, §58.7).

The final confrontation came in November 1927, the tenth anniversary of the revolution, when oppositionists attempted separate demonstrations in Moscow and Leningrad, which were broken up. A joint meeting of the Central Committee and the Central Control Commission expelled Trotsky and Zinoviev from the party on 14 November, and the Fifteenth Congress in December expelled Kamenev and many other opposition leaders. Zinoviev and Kamenev soon recanted and sought readmission; Trotsky refused, was exiled to Alma-Ata in Kazakhstan in January 1928, and was deported from the USSR in February 1929. The outcome removed the Left from the leadership but also, as later sections show, removed a counterweight to Stalin.

Section 54.13

Bukharin, Rykov, Tomsky, and the NEP Coalition

With the Left defeated, the leadership was dominated by a coalition of Stalin and the party's moderate or "Right" wing, led by Bukharin, Rykov, and Tomsky. Rykov chaired the Council of People's Commissars, Tomsky the trade-union organization, and Bukharin edited Pravda and emerged as the leading Soviet figure in the Comintern after Zinoviev's removal; the Comintern abolished the office of chairman in December 1926, and Bukharin headed the reorganized leadership through its Political Secretariat without inheriting Zinoviev's former title. They provided much of the economic and intellectual leadership of the regime, and their cooperation with Stalin made political sense: they shared a commitment to maintaining the alliance with the peasantry, and they feared the opposition's call for pressure on the countryside.

Bukharin's views are often misrepresented. He did not advocate unrestricted capitalism. He argued that socialism could be built gradually by developing state industry, cooperatives, and trade alongside a peasant economy, with the state retaining control of the economic commanding heights, foreign trade, and credit, and using taxation and planning to guide development. He believed that sustaining peasant prosperity and the market would provide the resources for industrial growth and avoid the social conflict of coercive measures. Critics accused him of underestimating the danger of kulak power and of promoting slow growth; supporters regarded his approach as the realistic continuation of Lenin's last writings on cooperation.

The coalition had its own internal tensions, which were suppressed during the fight with the Left. Stalin's rhetoric, in these years, was often as moderate as Bukharin's, and in speeches of 1925–1927 he defended the alliance with the middle peasant. At the same time, he strengthened his own position by promoting supporters to key positions, including Molotov, Voroshilov, Kaganovich, and Sergo Ordzhonikidze, who headed the Central Control Commission and later the Supreme Council of the National Economy. These men were more attached to Stalin personally than to any economic doctrine.

The coalition initially made political sense for Stalin because Bukharin, Rykov, and Tomsky had a strong claim to represent the party's mainstream and controlled important institutions. It also made sense for them, since Stalin appeared to be a practical administrator who shared their goals. Students should avoid reading the later conflict back into the 1925–1927 period: at the time, few observers, and perhaps few participants, expected the partnership to end. The change came with the grain-procurement crisis.

Section 54.14

The Grain-Procurement Crisis and the End of the NEP Consensus

In the autumn and winter of 1927–1928, the state's grain procurements fell far short of expectations, even though the harvest was not disastrous. The reasons were debated then and are debated now. Prices offered to peasants by state agencies were low relative to the prices of other crops and to those on the private market, and the shortage of manufactured goods in the villages gave peasants little incentive to sell for money. The war scare of spring 1927 had prompted some households to hoard grain and to buy goods in case of emergency. The government and the party leadership interpreted the shortfall as a kulak strike, a deliberate withholding of grain by wealthier peasants aimed at undermining the regime, though historians note that many peasants of all categories reacted to prices and conditions in similar ways.

In January 1928 the Politburo authorized extraordinary measures, and local officials, under pressure from the center, requisitioned grain, closed private markets, and prosecuted peasants who held grain under an article of the criminal code against speculation, with a part of the confiscated grain granted to village poor. Stalin traveled to Siberia in January and February 1928 to oversee the extraordinary procurement campaign, where officials used coercive measures including prosecutions under Article 107 against alleged grain hoarding. These measures were an important stage in the radicalization of procurement policy, but they should be distinguished from the later Ural-Siberian method. The measures produced grain and relieved the immediate crisis, and the party also leaned on explanations that attributed the shortage to class enemies. At the same time, the arrests and seizures damaged peasant confidence, and in the summer of 1928 the leadership moderated the policy, only to resume pressure the following year when procurement again fell short.

The distinct Ural-Siberian method developed in early 1929. It used village assemblies and differentiated compulsory delivery quotas, placing especially heavy obligations on households classified as kulaks. The Politburo approved its broader use in March 1929, and the method was extended more widely during the spring and summer. It is mentioned here only to separate it from the 1928 measures; its consequences belong to Chapters 55 and 56.

The crisis divided the leadership. Stalin and his supporters argued that the state could not depend on a market that gave peasants the power to withhold grain, that industrialization required reliable grain supplies for cities and export, and that coercion against kulaks was justified. Bukharin, Rykov, and Tomsky argued that extraordinary measures were a return to War Communism that would alienate the peasantry, reduce sown area, and jeopardize NEP. They urged higher grain prices, imports of grain if necessary, and slower industrial growth. Stalin initially presented the measures as temporary and denied any intention to abandon NEP, which obscured the extent of the disagreement.

This is where students should see why the governing coalition fractured. The issue was not simply an economic technicality: it concerned the relationship of the state to the peasant majority, the pace of industrial development, and the use of coercion. International tensions and the party's fear of encirclement also entered, as the Shakhty trial of engineers in 1928 reinforced perceptions of domestic and foreign enemies. This chapter does not narrate the Great Break, collectivization, or the first Five-Year Plan; those are the subjects of Chapters 55 and 56. It is enough here to establish that the grain crisis ended the consensus on which the NEP coalition rested and that the disagreement was real, though it was not predetermined how it would be resolved.

Section 54.15

How Stalin Became Dominant: Alliances, Institutions, Ideology, and Contingency

Between 1928 and 1929, Stalin defeated the Right. Bukharin, alarmed by the extraordinary measures, met secretly with Kamenev in July 1928 and described Stalin in harsh terms, a conversation that was reported and used against him. Stalin's supporters in the Politburo, the Central Committee, and the apparatus portrayed the Right as a deviation that underestimated the kulak danger and opposed the party's line. The Right's defeat came in stages. The Central Committee and Central Control Commission plenum of April 1929 formally condemned the Bukharin group. During the spring and summer of 1929 Bukharin lost his leadership of Pravda and of the Comintern, and Tomsky lost control of the trade unions. Bukharin was removed from the Politburo on 17 November 1929. Rykov, however, remained chairman of the Council of People's Commissars until 19 December 1930, a forward-looking detail showing that Stalin's political dominance by the end of 1929 did not mean that every Right leader had already lost every office. In December 1929 the officially celebrated fiftieth birthday of Stalin (the official date, 21 December, differed from the archival birth date of 18 December 1878) was marked by a press and public campaign of praise. Historians treat it as an important early landmark in the development of the Stalin cult, though not as evidence that the fully developed cult of the 1930s already existed. The party had meanwhile adopted a first Five-Year Plan, which is examined in Chapter 55.

How did Stalin win? Historians identify several interacting factors. First, he controlled and used party institutions, including the Secretariat, the appointment system, and the apparatus of congresses and plenums, to ensure support. Second, he built successive alliances rather than a single fixed faction: with Zinoviev and Kamenev against Trotsky, with Bukharin and his colleagues against the United Opposition, and with his own organizational allies against the Right. Third, his rivals made mistakes and were hostile to each other, which prevented them from combining against him until too late. Fourth, the ban on factions limited the forms of organized opposition and allowed him to portray critics as splitters. Fifth, he positioned himself ideologically as defender of party unity and Leninism, using the Lenin cult and doctrinal formulas such as Socialism in One Country. Sixth, he developed personnel networks of loyal supporters. Seventh, changing economic crises, from the Scissors Crisis to the grain crisis, provided opportunities to shift alliances and policies. Eighth, he showed considerable tactical ability, patience, and skill in timing. Ninth, contingency mattered: Lenin's illness, the vote of 1924, and events abroad could have gone differently.

This analysis rejects two extreme views. One is that Stalin inevitably inherited Lenin's dictatorship, which ignores the open contest of the period and the fact that many colleagues, including Lenin, were wary of him. The other is that Stalin simply outsmarted a collection of fools, which ignores the structures created before him, including the one-party state, the ban on factions, and the bureaucratization of the party, and the real political and ideological choices made by several actors. His rise depended on structures, individual decisions, and changing circumstances together.

Students should also be careful about what Stalin's dominance in 1929 meant. He was the most powerful figure in the leadership and controlled the party apparatus, but he was not yet the unrestrained dictator of the later 1930s. The Politburo still functioned as a collective body, though with a clear leader; individuals such as Molotov and Ordzhonikidze had their own power; and the party still contained significant numbers of members who had joined before the revolution. The later development of personal rule, the cult of personality, and mass repression (Chapter 57) required further decisions and changes. The outcome of 1929 was an essential precondition for these developments, but it did not make them inevitable.

Historical Debate

Why Did Stalin Win?

The question: Why did Stalin, and not Trotsky, Zinoviev, or Bukharin, become the dominant Soviet leader?

Interpretation A (bureaucracy and institutions): Trotsky argued in exile that the bureaucratic degeneration of the revolution and the growing independence of the party-state apparatus created the setting in which Stalin could rise. The historian Moshe Lewin emphasized institutional growth, bureaucratization, and the changing Soviet state and society, rather than any simple claim that Stalin personally controlled every appointment.
Interpretation B (leadership and personality): Robert C. Tucker gave substantial weight to Stalin's personality, ambition, political skill, leadership choices, and agency, while rejecting the claim that Stalinism was an inevitable consequence of Leninism.
Interpretation C (political strategy and contingency): Historians using archival materials opened after 1991 have stressed contingent decisions, changing coalitions, the effects of crises such as the grain shortage, and the importance of ideological legitimacy, while treating both structure and personality as necessary but not sufficient.

Evidence: Historians use stenographic records of congresses and plenums, private correspondence, diaries, memoirs, appointment records, and the polemical writings of the participants, each produced for particular purposes.

Points of agreement: Most scholars agree that Stalin's control of party appointments, his skill at alliance-building, the ban on factions, and his rivals' disunity were important, and that his victory was not predetermined. They also broadly agree that no single explanation, whether bureaucracy, personality, office-holding, ideology, or rivals' mistakes, is sufficient by itself.

Points of disagreement: Scholars differ on how far structural pressures would have produced a similar outcome under another leader, on the weight to give Stalin's personality, and on whether alternative paths such as a continuation of NEP were realistic.

Why it matters: The answer shapes how historians understand the origins of Stalinism: as the product of impersonal forces, of one man's choices, or of both.

Multiple Perspectives

Experiencing the Succession

A worker who joined in the Lenin Enrollment: For many new members, the party offered status, opportunity, and a sense of participation. They knew little of the pre-1917 disputes and tended to accept the leadership's presentation of events, though some shared the discontent over wages and bureaucracy.
A provincial party secretary: Secretaries owed their positions to the center and depended on its support, but they also managed local conflicts and resource shortages. Their loyalty to the leadership was a mixture of interest, habit, and conviction.
A peasant household: Peasants experienced the succession mainly through its effects: tax and price policy, the availability of goods, and, in 1928, the seizures of grain. They were seldom aware of the arguments among leaders.
An oppositionist: Opposition members believed that they were defending the revolution against bureaucratic degeneration and economic mismanagement, and faced loss of position, expulsion, and exile.

These perspectives show that the succession was a contest within a narrow circle whose outcome affected a broad population differently.

A one-party state without a succession procedure; the ban on factions; a rapidly growing, bureaucratizing party; and a leadership that relied on Lenin's arbitration.

Intermediate Developments

Lenin's strokes and withdrawal; Stalin's control of the Secretariat and appointments; economic crises in 1923 and 1927–1928; and rivalries among leaders.

Short-Term Triggers

The decision to retain Stalin in 1924; the disputes over Lessons of October and over the peasantry; the grain crisis of 1927–1928.

Effects

The defeat of Trotsky, Zinoviev, Kamenev, and Bukharin in turn; the end of the NEP coalition; and Stalin's emergence as leader by 1929.

Historical complexity: No single factor, whether the apparatus, tactics, ideology, or luck, is sufficient to explain the outcome.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From Collective Leadership to Dominant Leader

What Changed

  • Authority moved from Lenin to a group, and then increasingly to the General Secretary.
  • The party's membership grew and shifted toward younger, less experienced members.
  • The NEP coalition broke, and policy moved toward coercive procurement.

What Remained

  • The one-party state, the ban on factions, and the institutions described in §54.3.
  • The ideological commitment to building socialism and to Leninism as a source of legitimacy.
  • The Politburo as a body in which major decisions were discussed.

Why the pattern matters: Change was gradual over seven years, with turning points in 1924, 1925, 1927, and 1928–1929; continuity in structure made the change possible.

Everyday Life and Social History

Being a Party Member in the Mid-1920s

For most members, party life centered on the local cell in a factory, office, army unit, or village. Members attended meetings, paid dues, took on assignments, and were expected to explain party policy to non-members. Membership brought obligations but also advantages, including access to posts, education, and a sense of belonging to a ruling group. Many of the newly admitted workers valued the party as a route to advancement, while others had been recruited with little choice. Evidence for this experience comes from party records, memoirs, and reports of surveys and purges, which tend to reflect the concerns of officials.

Outside the party, life in these years was shaped by the mixed economy and the persistence of shortages. Public mourning for Lenin, mass meetings, and the new rituals of the state were part of everyday life, though they were experienced differently in cities and villages. Students should not assume that the arguments of the leadership were widely known; most people learned of them through the press and through their local party organization.

Regional Context

Beyond Moscow: Leningrad, the Republics, and the Grain Regions

The struggle for power was not confined to Moscow. Leningrad was both a strategic base and a symbol of the revolution, and the replacement of Zinoviev's organization by Kirov's in 1926 showed that control of regional machines was part of the contest. In the republics, local Communists, including those in Georgia, Ukraine, and Central Asia, had their own concerns about central authority, and the Georgian Affair of 1922 had made nationality policy a part of the leadership's rivalries (Chapter 53, §§53.14–53.15). In the grain regions of Ukraine, the North Caucasus, the Volga, and Siberia, the procurement crisis of 1927–1928 brought the leadership's disputes directly into villages, where local officials were under pressure to deliver grain and peasants experienced seizures and prosecutions.

Russia and the World

The Comintern and the Limits of World Revolution

The Communist International, based in Moscow, coordinated Communist parties abroad and was a major arena of the succession struggle. Zinoviev chaired its Executive Committee until November 1926; after the office of chairman was abolished, Bukharin became its leading Soviet figure. The failure of the German uprising of 1923 and the destruction of the Chinese Communists in 1927 showed the limits of the revolutionary expectations that had shaped Bolshevik thinking. Each failure was used in internal disputes: Trotsky and his allies argued that the leadership's strategy had been mistaken, while the leadership argued that the opposition's pessimism and factionalism had weakened the movement. Soviet foreign relations also affected domestic politics: the break with Britain in 1927 and the war scare reinforced the leadership's case for unity and for industrial strength, an argument that returns in Chapters 55 and 58.

Section 54.16

Chapter Review and Historical Thinking Checkpoint

This chapter has argued that Stalin did not inherit supreme power automatically when Lenin died. Between 1922 and 1929 authority was contested within a collective leadership. Stalin became dominant through control of expanding party institutions, strategic alliance-building, ideological positioning, the organizational consequences of the ban on factions, the weaknesses and choices of his rivals, and successive economic and political crises. His victory was historically explicable but not predetermined.

The spine of the argument is the sequence of coalitions: Stalin, Zinoviev, and Kamenev against Trotsky; Stalin and Bukharin, Rykov, and Tomsky against the United Opposition; and Stalin and his organizational allies against the Right. Evidence of different kinds, from Lenin's notes to congress records and the participants' polemics, supports an account in which structure, choice, and contingency interacted. Competing interpretations differ in emphasis rather than in rejecting any of these elements. The chapter also set the stage for Chapters 55 and 56 without narrating them: the end of the NEP consensus made later policies possible, but not inevitable.

Chapter Summary

This chapter examined how a leadership accustomed to Lenin's arbitration functioned after his incapacity and death, and how Stalin became its dominant figure. Its central problem was to explain a political outcome without treating it as inevitable.

Lenin's strokes between 1922 and 1923 removed him from daily politics while the party was still adjusting to NEP and the creation of the USSR. His final writings addressed bureaucracy, cooperation, nationality policy, and the composition of the leadership. The Letter to the Congress was not a will and named no successor, but it expressed concern about conflict between Stalin and Trotsky and suggested that Stalin be removed as General Secretary.

The party's institutions shaped the contest. The Politburo, Orgburo, Secretariat, and Control Commission, together with democratic centralism and the ban on factions, meant that control of appointments, agendas, and information mattered greatly. Stalin's position as General Secretary gave him influence over personnel, and the nomenklatura system increased its importance, though contemporaries did not at first see the office as decisive.

The field of potential successors included Trotsky, with great prestige and a weak organizational base; Zinoviev and Kamenev, with major city organizations and compromised records; Bukharin, a theorist; and Stalin, an administrator. Trotsky's isolation resulted from his past, his methods, and the choices of others, and the triumvirate of Stalin, Zinoviev, and Kamenev defeated him in the crises of 1923 and the disputes of 1924–1925.

Lenin's death began the Lenin cult and a mass recruitment of workers. The Testament was read to restricted audiences and Stalin was retained, a key contingency. The dispute over Lessons of October showed how history became a weapon, and the formulation of Socialism in One Country gave the leadership a program that redefined strategy without abandoning internationalism.

The triumvirate broke over the peasantry and over power, and the Fourteenth Congress defeated Zinoviev and Kamenev. They then joined Trotsky in the United Opposition, which was defeated and expelled in 1927 amid foreign setbacks and a war scare. Stalin's alliance with Bukharin, Rykov, and Tomsky made political sense but ended in the grain crisis of 1927–1928, which produced extraordinary measures and a split over the relationship between state and peasantry.

By 1929 Stalin had defeated the Right and become the leading figure, through institutional control, successive alliances, ideological positioning, his rivals' errors, crises, and contingency. The chapter rejected both the view that he inevitably inherited a dictatorship and the view that he merely outwitted fools.

Historiographically, scholars differ on the weight of structure, personality, and strategy, though most agree that the outcome was not predetermined. The consolidation of 1929 prepared the ground for the Great Break, collectivization, and the terror, which are the subjects of Chapters 55 through 57.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it explains how a collective revolutionary leadership became a system with a dominant leader, and why that process cannot be reduced to a single cause. The political structures of the early Soviet state, especially the one-party monopoly, the ban on factions, and the control of appointments, shaped the contest, but the outcome also depended on the choices of individuals and on changing crises.

Much of what was established persisted. The party's institutions, the Lenin cult, and Stalin's position as the leading figure shaped Soviet politics in the following decades, as Chapters 55 through 57 show. Other possibilities were closed, including a continuation of NEP and a collective leadership with real checks on any one person. Later, after Stalin's death, the question of how the system had come to concentrate power became a subject of public reassessment (Chapter 61, §61.5).

The chapter reveals themes of the textbook: the relationship between institutions and individuals, the uses of history and ideology in politics, the interaction of economic crisis and political choice, and the importance of avoiding hindsight. Contemporaries did not know that collectivization and terror lay ahead, and the people who helped Stalin win later became his victims.

Questions remain open: how far another leader could have produced a similar outcome, how realistic the alternatives were, and how to weigh different kinds of evidence. These questions return in the chapters on the Great Break and collectivization.
