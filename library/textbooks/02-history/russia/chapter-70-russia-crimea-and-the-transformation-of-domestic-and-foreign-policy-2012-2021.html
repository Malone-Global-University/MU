Part XII — The Russian Federation
70

Russia, Crimea, and the Transformation of Domestic and Foreign Policy, 2012–2021

A History of Russia: From the Eurasian World to the Russian Federation

Chapter Opening

Between 2012 and the end of 2021, decisions about sovereignty, political authority and international security changed the lives of millions. The period must be studied from evidence available to contemporaries, not as a prewritten explanation of what followed.

Putin’s inauguration on 7 May 2012 began the third presidential term of a leader whose government had already reshaped Russia’s political institutions. Yet the Russia of 2021 differed substantially from the Russia of 2012. The decade witnessed changes in the rules governing opposition activity, media, regional government and presidential succession; the annexation of Crimea; the outbreak of war in the Donbas; Western sanctions; an overseas military intervention in Syria; stagnating household incomes; and a pandemic that exposed deep differences among regions. These changes did not occur on a single timetable or through one policy mechanism. They must be reconstructed through separate institutional, social, economic, military and international histories.

This chapter follows the chronology from Putin’s return to the Kremlin through 31 December 2021. It builds on the Medvedev–Putin tandem, the Georgia war and the 2011–2012 protest movement studied in Chapter 69. It ends before the full-scale invasion of Ukraine in February 2022, which is the central subject of Chapter 71. The unresolved Russian–Ukrainian conflict, military preparations and diplomatic proposals of late 2021 form the bridge between them. Explaining the conditions under which a later event became possible is not the same as claiming that contemporaries knew it would occur.

Russia was not politically uniform. Some citizens continued to support Putin’s leadership and the restoration of national prestige; others criticized corruption, economic stagnation and restrictions on civic life. Urban professionals, pensioners, public employees, small businesses, regional elites, religious communities and migrant workers experienced policy differently. Nor were Russia’s neighbors passive. Ukrainian citizens made political choices in their own constitutional system; Syrians fought over the future of their country; Belarusians protested their government; Armenia, Azerbaijan, Kazakhstan, Turkey, China, the United States and European states pursued different agendas.

The most contested events demand particular care. Crimea’s incorporation into Russia under Russian domestic law must be distinguished from its continuing international recognition as Ukrainian territory. The war in eastern Ukraine involved local grievances and armed organizations, Russian military support and involvement, Ukrainian government forces, and extensive civilian harm. Legal questions about elections, constitutional amendments and human-rights restrictions require comparison of original legal texts with evidence about how rules operated. A government’s stated reason for acting is evidence of its position, not automatic proof of the factual premise behind that reason.

Sources include Russian and Ukrainian government documents, parliamentary records, international legal texts, OSCE monitoring and election reports, United Nations human-rights investigations, statistical series, aviation inquiries, diplomatic statements, eyewitness interviews and contemporary journalism. These records are not interchangeable. A statistical series is sensitive to territorial definitions and later revisions; an intelligence assessment may rely on classified material; a court judgment resolves particular charges, and a public address expresses political arguments. Later findings can illuminate earlier events if explicitly identified as retrospective. Students are expected to evaluate these differences rather than count all published claims as equivalent evidence.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain the institutional changes accompanying Putin’s third and fourth presidencies, distinguishing formal authority from practical enforcement.
  2. Reconstruct the events of Euromaidan and the February 2014 Ukrainian political transition from Ukrainian primary records and contemporaneous evidence.
  3. Distinguish the Russian military seizure and annexation of Crimea from its internationally recognized legal status.
  4. Trace the Donbas conflict from 2014 to 2021, identifying armed actors, Russian state involvement, ceasefire arrangements and civilian consequences.
  5. Analyze the separate and interacting effects of oil-price changes, sanctions, exchange-rate policy and preexisting structural weaknesses on the Russian economy.
  6. Evaluate Russia’s intervention in Syria through military, diplomatic, humanitarian and legal evidence.
  7. Compare Russia’s foreign relations with NATO, the United States, China and post-Soviet states without erasing those states’ independent interests.
  8. Examine political opposition, internet regulation, independent journalism, law enforcement and public mobilization through specific cases and laws.
  9. Explain the 2018 pension reform and COVID-19 pandemic as social and political experiences varying across regions.
  10. Analyze the legal procedure and institutional consequences of the 2020 constitutional amendments.
  11. Interpret statistics and retrospective findings with attention to source, geographic denominator, series vintage, definitions and limitations.
  12. Summarize the principal conditions visible at the end of 2021 without assuming that the events of 2022 were inevitable.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. What changed in Russian institutions after Putin returned to the presidency in 2012, and what remained from the earlier post-Soviet system?
  2. How did Euromaidan develop from a dispute over an international agreement into a wider political crisis?
  3. What evidence establishes the distinction between control of Crimea and legally recognized sovereignty?
  4. What were the relative roles of local armed groups, Russian military involvement and Ukrainian decisions in the Donbas war?
  5. Why are sanctions and lower oil prices different explanations of economic weakness, and how did their effects interact?
  6. How should an international fact-finding report be weighed against government statements or a later criminal verdict?
  7. What goals did Moscow claim in Syria, and how can those claims be evaluated against civilian and military outcomes?
  8. How did the economy, regional inequality and the pandemic affect people differently from 2012 to 2021?
  9. What did Russia’s 2020 amendments change about presidential terms and the formal roles of other institutions?
  10. What developments in late 2021 were observable then, and which conclusions could only be reached after 2021?

Historical Context

Chapter 67 analyzed the transformation after the Soviet collapse; Chapter 68 showed the construction of a stronger presidency and the changing relationship between the state, regions and large businesses through 2008. Chapter 69 examined the Medvedev–Putin tandem, the 2008 Georgia war, the financial crisis and renewed civic activism. Together these chapters establish why the 2012 political system already possessed powerful executive institutions, a dominant parliamentary party and substantial state-connected enterprises. Chapter 70 examines how those institutions changed under further pressures rather than beginning Russian politics again from first principles.

Russia’s geography and federal system shaped the decade. Public revenues and economic opportunities differed between metropolitan centers, resource-producing regions, industrial towns and territories dependent on federal transfers. The North Caucasus had specific security and social issues; Crimea became a site of territorial conflict; Siberia and the Far East faced migration and infrastructure challenges. Central policy directives were implemented unevenly because regions possessed different administrative resources and populations.

Internationally, Moscow sought influence in the post-Soviet region and emphasized sovereignty, military capability and a place among major powers. Neighboring states, however, possessed their own institutions and electoral politics. Ukraine negotiated trade relations with the EU, the Baltic states remained members of NATO, China pursued increasingly important commercial relations with Russia, and Turkey balanced competition and cooperation. A bilateral narrative of Russia versus the West cannot explain all these developments.

The years 2014 and 2020 are significant turning points but should not determine every subject’s periodization. Economic weakness had precedents before Crimea; legal restrictions began before 2014 and continued afterward; constitutional change occurred while a pandemic disrupted daily life. To understand cause and effect, students should follow each policy through its proposal, enactment, implementation and consequences instead of treating the date of an important international event as the explanation of all domestic trends.

Division I — Putin's Return and the Consolidation of Political Authority, 2012–2013

Section 70.1

Putin's Return to the Presidency, May 2012

Vladimir Putin was inaugurated on 7 May 2012 for a term that, under a 2008 amendment, lasted six years rather than four. Dmitry Medvedev became prime minister the next day, preserving executive continuity while the presidency regained its central position.

The return to the Kremlin followed a succession process whose principal decisions had been announced in September 2011, well before voters cast ballots. Putin's official first-round victory in March 2012, and the continuing predominance of United Russia, meant that his third term began with extensive institutional leverage. Yet elections, public opinion and organized discontent cannot be collapsed into a single story of either unanimous consent or universal rejection. Many voters associated him with stability and higher living standards since the 1990s. Others regarded the exchange of offices with Medvedev as evidence that the formal electoral process was subordinate to an agreement among the leadership. This divergence is essential for explaining the political environment in which the new term opened.

The new administration could work through institutions established during the first two Putin presidencies: a strong constitutional presidency, a presidential administration influential in personnel decisions, federal districts, a parliamentary majority, and networks linking the security services and large state enterprises. Medvedev's four-year presidency had not dismantled these mechanisms. The 2008 constitutional amendment, implemented for the new six-year term, changed the interval at which presidential authority would face an electoral test. It did not, by itself, make executive decisions uniform or remove the interests of ministries, regional governments and parliament. Policies still had to be implemented through officials who possessed information, budgets and local alliances of their own.

Putin's May 2012 decrees set targets in salaries, education, health care, housing and the economy. Some objectives extended the national-project agenda associated with Medvedev; others promised new investment and improvement of public services. Governors and ministers were expected to report progress on indicators, creating incentives to demonstrate statistical fulfillment as well as to deliver substantive change. The tension between central commands and local administrative capacity recurred throughout the decade. Evaluating the decrees requires asking whether resources matched obligations, whether measures changed lived conditions and whether reported successes were independently testable.

The transfer also reshaped elite calculations. Senior officials could see continuity in the positions of figures associated with Putin, but uncertainties remained about the distribution of authority between the presidency and the new Medvedev government. Political leadership was not merely a matter of one person issuing orders: financing large projects, controlling elections, distributing investment and responding to regional crises required bargaining among ministries, the presidential administration, business groups and local administrations. Students should evaluate both the institutional resources available to the president and the social costs incurred when disagreement with the government became harder to express openly.

The six-year term also changed the rhythm of accountability: national legislative elections and presidential elections no longer followed their former intervals, altering incentives for parties and officials to build organizations independent of the presidency. Formal constitutional continuity should therefore not obscure changes in the institutional timetable. Local elections, by contrast, remained more frequent in many jurisdictions and became occasional tests of government credibility when national elections offered few opportunities for new candidates.

There is a difference between political continuity and policy immobility. The new presidency could retain familiar ministers while changing how success was measured, who received investment and which public organizations were permitted to participate in policy discussions. Comparing the personnel lists and actual regulatory decisions of 2008, 2012 and 2018 provides a more reliable answer than treating the label 'third term' as sufficient explanation.

Section 70.2

The Bolotnaya Protest Movement and the State's Response

The 6 May 2012 march ended in clashes with police at Bolotnaya Square. Prosecutions of participants (the “Bolotnaya case”) and a June 2012 law raising penalties for unsanctioned rallies followed; organizers and officials gave conflicting accounts of who caused the violence.

The May 6 gathering occurred on the eve of the inauguration and followed the much larger winter demonstrations over the December 2011 Duma election. Protest participants had varied objectives: honest elections, an end to corruption, release of political prisoners or a change in the national leadership. Organizers and police disagreed about the route, crowd control and responsibility for the ensuing violence. The contested evidence included video recordings, police reports, medical records, testimony and later judicial proceedings. A historical account should not infer responsibility for each incident merely from the fact that police officers or protesters sustained injuries.

After the confrontation, investigators opened cases against numerous participants; some remained in custody while courts heard charges of riot participation or violence against police. Rights advocates criticized the breadth of the prosecutions and the treatment of evidence, whereas authorities defended them as necessary to protect officers and public order. The prosecutions affected people beyond those charged: the possibility that participation in a demonstration might result in criminal prosecution changed calculations for students, workers and first-time activists. At the same time protests continued in various forms, revealing that the state did not eliminate dissent by one legal action.

The June 2012 amendments to the rules on public demonstrations increased administrative fines and imposed additional obligations on organizers. The distinction between permitted and unpermitted gatherings took on greater significance, and authorities gained tools to discourage repeated participation. Civil society groups challenged provisions in court, while municipal officials interpreted registration and route rules in different ways. These institutional details matter more than the simple statement that protests were 'suppressed': a rally could be authorized but constrained, a public action could be dispersed despite its peaceful character, and court proceedings might turn on formal notice requirements rather than the underlying political issue.

A second consequence involved the public sphere. National television often emphasized disorder, foreign influence or the irresponsibility of opposition leaders, while independent sites and social networks circulated footage and competing eyewitness accounts. Neither channel was an uncomplicated record; selection, editing, and audience predispositions shaped interpretation. The Bolotnaya cases thus became a focal point for disagreement about policing, courts and citizenship. They also linked the political mobilization of Chapter 69 to the legislative and institutional transformations considered in subsequent sections, without making the outcomes of later years predetermined.

The Bolotnaya confrontation also produced an archive of competing video evidence, unlike many earlier Soviet or post-Soviet episodes in which official reports dominated. Footage was fragmentary, however: it captured particular movements or confrontations, not necessarily the conditions that preceded them. Investigators, defense lawyers and journalists used overlapping but different sets of recordings. A court conviction or acquittal must be described by its precise legal basis; it should not automatically determine the political meaning of the entire demonstration.

The social effect of prosecution extended to relatives and employers of those accused. Fundraising for legal defense, public statements by prominent cultural figures and attempts to maintain solidarity converted a police event into a prolonged debate about the right to assembly. For some citizens the cases confirmed official warnings about disorder; for others they illustrated the vulnerability of constitutional liberties. Both responses formed part of the period's political history.

Section 70.3

The Presidential Administration, United Russia, and Federal Governance

Formal institutions (government, State Duma, Federation Council) operated alongside the informal influence of the presidential administration and security services. Direct gubernatorial elections, restored in 2012, were subject to a “municipal filter” on candidate registration.

The 1993 Constitution formally divided authority among the president, cabinet, parliament and judiciary, yet the actual operation of Russian politics depended substantially on appointments, partisan coordination and administrative resources. The presidential administration drafted initiatives, monitored implementation and helped coordinate relations with governors and major state enterprises. United Russia generally provided reliable legislative support, but its deputies were not identical in background or interest. Regional administrations had their own priorities involving roads, public services and financing, and national directives could create local tradeoffs. Understanding centralization therefore requires examining the chain from a presidential announcement through legislation, budget allocation and enforcement.

The direct election of regional governors was restored under the 2012 reforms, but candidates needed endorsements from local and municipal deputies under the so-called municipal filter. Supporters argued that the filter screened out unserious candidates and supported stable governance; opponents said it favored established parties and governors by making access to office dependent on networks controlled by incumbents. Other rules permitted indirect selection in some regions under later changes. The formal presence of elections did not remove the need to assess ballot access, media coverage, opposition participation and the autonomy of local legislatures.

Federal finances formed another channel of authority. Some regions collected substantial revenues from energy or metropolitan commerce, while poorer territories depended on transfers. Subsidies and national development targets could improve services, but fiscal dependence gave federal ministries leverage. Regional political leaders often advertised their ability to secure Moscow funding. This relationship was especially salient where employment relied on state contracts or where infrastructure projects exceeded municipal budgets. A balanced account should consider whether central coordination improved service delivery and whether it simultaneously reduced the ability of voters to hold a particular decision-maker accountable.

Regional Context

Federal Transfers and Regional Capacity

The same federal directive could have very different outcomes in Moscow and a subsidy-dependent republic. Compare own-source tax revenue, administrative staffing and service infrastructure. Federalism is a relationship among institutions, not simply a map of regional names.

The security services, prosecutor's office and courts operated within this system but should not be treated as a single undifferentiated actor. Agencies possessed separate jurisdictions and sometimes competing interests, and prosecutors had to present cases under formal law. Nevertheless, human-rights lawyers and opposition groups argued that selectively enforced statutes could create major inequalities between allies and critics of the executive. Students can test such interpretations by comparing cases, studying the written law, and examining the social position of defendants rather than assuming that every judgment necessarily followed direct presidential instruction.

Russian federalism includes republics, territories, regions and other constituent entities with different names and historical backgrounds. Some republics have their own official languages and distinct cultural institutions, while fiscal dependence and federal legal supremacy apply across the federation. A useful comparison examines how the municipal filter and federal transfer system functioned in a wealthy metropolitan area and in a region dependent on subsidies. Similar formal rules can have very different practical effects.

Presidential envoys, governors, local prosecutors and budget authorities could all be involved in implementation disputes. An order from Moscow might be delayed by shortages of staff or conflicting regional priorities rather than overt political resistance. Identifying these bottlenecks helps distinguish administrative capacity from the concentration of power: a highly centralized political system can still struggle to deliver health care or infrastructure evenly across a vast territory.

Section 70.4

The 2012 Legislative Turn: Demonstrations and Public Organizations

In 2012 the Duma tightened rules on rallies and passed the “foreign agent” law, requiring registration of politically active NGOs receiving foreign funding. Supporters cited sovereignty and transparency; critics, including Russian rights groups, saw stigmatization.

Two legislative developments illustrate the turning point. The demonstration-law amendments of June 2012 sharply raised penalties, while legislation adopted in July introduced the label of 'foreign agent' for certain noncommercial organizations receiving foreign funding and engaging in broadly defined political activity. The category carried reporting and public-labeling requirements, and subsequent amendments expanded and changed its reach. The government's principal argument was disclosure: citizens should know when organizations receiving support from abroad attempted to influence public policy. Critics argued that the label suggested espionage in everyday Russian language and exposed ordinary civic work to sanctions.

Section 70.5

Continuity and Change in Putin's Third Presidency

Much was inherited from 2000–2008: a strong presidency and dominant party. What was new after 2012 was a more explicit emphasis on conservative values, legal restrictions on civil society, and a narrower tolerance for organized dissent.

Continuity was visible in the presidency's dominance, the political importance of security agencies, the state role in major industries and the unequal access of opposition groups to national television. The governors' appointment system of the 2000s had been partially reversed in form, yet municipal filters and administrative networks constrained the new direct elections. Centralized economic management also persisted, through national projects, budget transfers and large enterprises. These continuities should be documented rather than inferred from a general label such as 'authoritarianism,' which can describe a political system without identifying how particular decisions were made.

The post-2012 differences were not solely legislative. Division II — Political Opposition, Civil Society, and Conservative National Identity

Section 70.6

Opposition Politics, Elections, and Alexei Navalny's Early Campaigns

Alexei Navalny, known for anti-corruption investigations, was convicted in the Kirov timber case in July 2013 (sentence later suspended). In the September 2013 Moscow mayoral election he took about 27 percent against Sergei Sobyanin's officially reported 51 percent.

Opposition politics in the early third term encompassed established parliamentary parties, liberal parties denied registration, regional organizations, nationalists and groups oriented toward specific grievances. The Communist Party and Liberal Democratic Party participated in the Duma but differed from activists advocating competitive elections and independent institutions. Alexei Navalny combined shareholder activism, corruption investigations and electoral campaigning with positions on nationalism that drew criticism from some of his allies. Historical precision requires distinguishing individual campaigns and ideological differences rather than identifying everyone outside United Russia as one movement.

The 2013 Moscow mayoral contest illustrates both the possibility and limits of electoral competition. Navalny, allowed to stand with signatures obtained through the municipal system, conducted visible street campaigning and fundraising. His officially reported share of approximately twenty-seven percent surprised some observers and demonstrated urban opposition support. Sergei Sobyanin won with just over half the reported vote, narrowly avoiding a second round. The electoral environment remained uneven in resources and media access, but the campaign provided evidence of citizens organizing for a concrete electoral alternative.

The Kirovles criminal proceedings were central to Navalny's political position. He was convicted in July 2013, sentenced to imprisonment and released pending appeal amid demonstrations; the sentence was subsequently suspended. In 2016 the European Court of Human Rights found serious procedural deficiencies in the case, and Russian judicial proceedings followed. These events should not be simplified into a judgment that no criminal allegations existed or that the initial conviction automatically established guilt under international standards. The written judgments, procedural history and political context are distinct layers of evidence.

Electoral politics outside Moscow also mattered. Communist candidates sometimes attracted protest votes, regional governors faced local opposition and some municipal campaigns dealt principally with roads, housing or corruption. At the national level, restrictions on registration and broadcast access made it difficult for new groups to convert public support into durable party organization. Comparing candidates' access to the ballot, turnout, financial support, independent monitoring and court appeals provides a better assessment of competitiveness than relying on the vote percentage of a single election.

The politics of anti-corruption were inseparable from debate about evidence. Shareholder records, corporate disclosures, property registries and publicly visible procurement could support allegations of conflicts of interest, but an allegation concerning a person's wealth is not automatically proof of a specific criminal offense. Activists drew attention to transactions that conventional television rarely examined; government representatives sometimes disputed both the interpretation of records and the legitimacy of the investigators. Students should learn to evaluate documents before reaching conclusions about motive.

The Moscow mayoral campaign also revealed organizational strengths: volunteers, street stalls, small-donor fundraising and independent monitoring helped an opposition candidacy reach citizens directly. Those capacities mattered even without an electoral victory. A candidate's vote total measures part of public support; the networks created during a campaign can influence later protests, civic education and local organization in ways that official seat counts do not capture.

Section 70.7

Civil Society, NGOs, and the 2015 Undesirable Organizations Law

The May 2015 law allowed prosecutors to declare foreign or international organizations “undesirable,” criminalizing participation. It extended the logic of the foreign-agent system and pressed independent associations and research bodies.

The 2015 law on 'undesirable' foreign and international organizations expanded the repertoire of restrictions beyond the 2012 foreign-agent framework. Prosecutorial designation could prohibit an organization's activities and subject persons associated with them to legal consequences. Authorities characterized the measure as protection against organizations threatening constitutional order and security. Critics argued that vague grounds permitted restriction of independent educational, research, democracy-assistance and humanitarian connections. Unlike a routine annual reporting requirement, an undesirable-organization designation could make ordinary collaboration difficult or dangerous.

Civic groups responded in different ways. Some stopped receiving certain donations, others moved projects abroad, and others continued under tighter monitoring. The population of independent organizations was diverse: professional associations, environmental advocates, disability-rights groups, local preservation committees and organizations documenting political repression did not have identical sources of support or relationships with government. Groups receiving state grants could perform socially useful work while facing incentives to avoid controversial public issues. The effect of law was therefore not simply the disappearance of civic life, but a redistribution of incentives among forms of participation.

International legal bodies and Russian courts considered aspects of these policies, though disagreement persisted over both constitutional principles and the authority of outside judgments. The language of sovereignty was central to the government's defense, particularly as relations with the European Union and United States worsened. Yet foreign funding, foreign coordination and foreign control are distinct concepts. The existence of one need not prove the others. Scholars should examine registration decisions, documented program activity and the stated reasons for government action in individual cases.

One consequence was a growing distance between formal consultation and critical advocacy. Government advisory bodies could invite experts to discuss social programs while organizations exposing detention abuses, election violations or corruption faced scrutiny. This uneven relationship helps explain why some citizens perceived official institutions as accessible and others saw participation as costly. The history of NGOs also connects domestic legal developments to Russia's wider diplomatic confrontation; it should not be treated merely as a technical appendix to foreign policy.

Human-rights organizations documented very different kinds of problems, from prison conditions and conscription to discrimination and interference with elections. Their independence could be valuable precisely because they criticized public institutions rather than carrying out government projects. The distinction between social service provision and rights advocacy became more important as funding and registration rules changed. A group might keep helping vulnerable families while ceasing to issue reports on police abuse.

The state's concern about foreign influence must be studied as a policy position with a legal and diplomatic context. That position gained salience after the Ukraine crisis and sanctions. Whether any particular civic organization acted unlawfully is an individual factual question. Broad suspicions cannot substitute for proof that a named organization coordinated with a foreign government, and the absence of such proof does not make disclosure of funding inherently illegitimate.

Section 70.8

Television, Journalism, and Internet Regulation

State-aligned television dominated national news, while online outlets offered more varied reporting. Regulation grew through measures such as the 2014 bloggers' registry, data-localization rules, and the 2014 dismissal of Lenta.ru's editor.

Television remained a principal source of national political news, and major channels were state-owned or affiliated with enterprises close to the government. They could devote sustained attention to official ceremonies, patriotic themes and favorable accounts of economic policy while limiting access for opposition candidates. Their reach was especially important among older viewers and in areas with less reliable internet access. At the same time radio, newspapers and online portals offered competing accounts, although each depended on advertising markets, regulatory permission and vulnerable distribution arrangements.

The rise of mobile internet made publication and political communication less dependent on conventional broadcasters. Investigative films, independent reporting and eyewitness video could reach a mass audience without occupying television airtime. The authorities responded through a mixture of platform regulation, data localization, restrictions on certain forms of content and pressure on publishers. The 2014 reshaping of the Lenta.ru editorial leadership became a prominent example in debates about independence and ownership. Such episodes should be analyzed through documented institutional and commercial decisions, not assumed to follow an identical pattern in every newsroom.

The bloggers' law and data-retention rules raised practical issues about how states regulate an environment crossing borders. Officials argued that online communication should comply with Russian law and that personal data required protection. Critics worried that registration and surveillance obligations could expose journalists, sources and political activists. Individual sites faced different kinds of interventions: some were blocked, others labeled or fined, and still others remained accessible while moderating material. These distinctions are important for explaining the selective effects of media regulation.

Public trust and media consumption varied. Someone receiving political news from state television and someone following independent investigations on social platforms could possess strikingly different accounts of the same event. That does not mean all citizens in either audience automatically accepted what they saw. Media studies, polling and interviews can help reconstruct this diversity, but sample limitations and the political cost of expressing unpopular opinions must be considered. Subsequent chapters trace how online restrictions expanded; the early years establish the institutional and technological conditions of that expansion.

A broadcaster's ownership structure is only one part of media influence. Advertising, access to officials, regulatory licensing, newsroom staffing and liability risks can affect what is reported. A comparative study might follow one event through state television, a privately owned newspaper and a social-media platform, identifying where each obtained footage and which voices were absent. Different accounts may be selective without containing direct factual falsehoods.

The development of online activism also created new vulnerabilities: a journalist might reach millions without a printing press, but could lose access to payment services, hosting or advertising. Networked communication thus redistributed power without removing dependence on infrastructure. The later sovereign-internet legislation can be understood as a response to these technical and political changes, not simply as an updated version of older television censorship.

Section 70.9

Conservatism, Religion, Family Policy, and Political Values

Authorities and the Russian Orthodox Church cooperated on “traditional values.” A June 2013 federal law banned “propaganda of nontraditional sexual relations” to minors; rights groups documented stigma and harassment of LGBT people. Russian society was not uniformly conservative: surveys and practice varied by age, region, and faith.

The conservative turn was expressed in legislation, presidential addresses, school and cultural policy and collaboration with religious leaders. The Russian Orthodox Church enjoyed public visibility through commemorations and social initiatives, but it was not identical to the state and its leaders had their own institutional priorities. Russia also contained substantial Muslim communities, Buddhists, Jews, nonbelievers and people whose religious practice was private or syncretic. A study of political identity must avoid turning an officially favored cultural narrative into a uniform description of society.

The June 2013 legislation restricting information about 'nontraditional sexual relations' to minors was defended as protecting children and traditional family life. Rights organizations argued that its broad wording chilled public discussion, contributed to discrimination and worsened conditions for LGBT people. The law initially had a stated minors-related scope; later restrictions evolved beyond that, so chronology matters. Its social effects cannot be inferred simply from the number of prosecutions because stigma, self-censorship and fear of harassment are harder to quantify.

Family policy also had an economic dimension. Incentives associated with childbirth, maternity capital, housing support and the financing of schools and clinics shaped choices in households that might or might not share official conservative rhetoric. Pronatalist arguments reflected demographic concerns, including an aging population and changing birth cohorts. Policy effects varied according to access to childcare, paid work and housing. Women were not merely subjects of demographic policy; they were workers, activists, professionals and family decision-makers whose preferences might conflict with official presentations.

Political authorities framed conservative values partly in contrast with social changes in Western countries, a contrast reinforced by international disputes. Yet the rhetoric of distinct civilizational values should not lead historians to assume a timeless Russian cultural essence. Russian religious revival, the Soviet legacy of secularism, urban individualism, regional cultures and generational change were all part of the period. The task is to explain how specific institutions and leaders mobilized particular interpretations of history and morality—and who supported, adapted or resisted them.

Religious institutions have multiple social functions: worship, charity, education, maintenance of historical sites and public commentary. Cooperation between church officials and the state therefore varied by issue, and individual clergy did not necessarily speak for all believers. Muslim communities likewise differed in institutional organization and relationship with regional authorities. Treating Orthodox identity as synonymous with Russian citizenship would erase the federation's constitutional and cultural plurality.

The political language of tradition also intersected with international human-rights law. Russian officials defended legislation through national values and parental authority, while rights advocates asked whether restrictions imposed disproportionate burdens on minorities. Students can compare these claims by separating legislative text, administrative penalties and social behavior. A law may have symbolic significance even where the number of formal prosecutions is low.

The Sochi Winter Olympics were a highly visible undertaking combining sports facilities, transportation networks, security infrastructure and an international presentation of Russia as a modern state. Construction transformed parts of the Black Sea resort area and nearby mountain communities. Official presentations emphasized engineering achievement and new tourist capacity, while journalists and activists raised concerns about procurement costs, forced relocations, labor conditions and environmental effects. Division III — Ukraine's Euromaidan Revolution and the Annexation of Crimea, 2013–2014

Section 70.11

Ukraine Between European Integration and Russian Pressure, 2012–2013

Ukraine in 2012–2013 was politically divided, with regional and linguistic differences that did not map simply onto loyalty to Russia or the West. President Viktor Yanukovych, elected in 2010, concentrated power, and the 2011 imprisonment of his rival Yulia Tymoshenko complicated relations with the European Union. Kyiv negotiated an Association Agreement with a deep free-trade component, to be signed at the Vilnius summit of November 2013.

Russia opposed the agreement, arguing it would harm trade within the Commonwealth of Independent States and its own proposed Customs Union. In summer 2013 Russian customs checks on Ukrainian goods tightened, and Ukraine depended on Russian gas and markets. On 21 November 2013 the Ukrainian cabinet suspended preparations to sign, citing trade and security concerns. In December Moscow announced a financial package and a lower gas price. Historians debate how far the decision reflected Russian pressure, Ukrainian economic calculation, or Yanukovych's domestic interests.

Ukraine's foreign-policy choices took place within a state that had experienced competitive national elections, alternating political coalitions and recurrent constitutional disputes. The 2004 Orange Revolution, the 2010 election of Yanukovych and the imprisonment of former prime minister Yulia Tymoshenko informed public debates over legal institutions and European integration. Regional identities did not determine an individual's political position: Russian-speaking Ukrainians supported different parties, and support for closer economic relations with Russia could coexist with support for Ukrainian sovereignty. Political choices were influenced by livelihoods, employment, the changing media environment and interpretations of corruption as much as by language or geography.

The proposed EU Association Agreement included an extensive trade component and commitments concerning regulation, competition and institutional reform. It was not a guarantee of immediate EU membership, and it was not the same as accession to a military alliance. Its terms nevertheless implied far-reaching economic adjustment. Ukrainian industrial producers, agricultural exporters, banks and households faced different prospective costs and gains. Russian officials worried about goods entering the Russian market through Ukraine under changed customs arrangements and about the broader political consequences of Kyiv drawing closer to Brussels. The Ukrainian government had to consider short-term financing pressures alongside longer-term claims about modernization.

During 2013 Russia used customs pressure and diplomatic warnings while European representatives encouraged Kyiv to proceed with the agreement. The cabinet's November decision to suspend preparations was therefore not made in an unconstrained environment, but it also involved Yanukovych's own assessment of economic and political survival. The December offer of financial support and lower gas prices illustrated how competing external arrangements could be used to influence domestic decisions. None of these considerations establishes that the later protests were planned or controlled by a foreign state.

The first gatherings in Kyiv were organized by Ukrainians dissatisfied with the government's retreat from the anticipated agreement. Their subsequent expansion cannot be explained solely by preference for one trading bloc. Citizens objected to police violence, judicial abuses, perceived enrichment of officials and the exclusion of ordinary people from major decisions. Students should distinguish an initial policy dispute from the transformation of that dispute into a wider movement over political accountability. The subsequent history of Crimea and the Donbas requires this account of Ukrainian agency rather than treating Ukraine as territory upon which outside actors simply competed.

The prospect of regulatory alignment with the European Union had practical implications for food standards, trade inspections, industrial certification and access to export markets. Large businesses and small enterprises were not equally equipped to handle adjustment costs, and some feared sudden exposure to competition. Ukrainian business groups therefore had reasons to disagree over the agreement even without taking a position on national identity. These economic calculations belong beside the dramatic accounts of diplomatic pressure.

Ukrainian citizens also weighed the prospect of political reform. For many, European integration suggested a hope for more predictable courts, limits on corruption and freedom of movement, even though the agreement itself could not automatically deliver those outcomes. The divergence between expectations and the precise legal commitments became central to the protests. A complete account explains the policy document and the symbolic aspirations attached to it.

Section 70.12

Euromaidan: Protest, Repression, and Political Transformation

Small protests on 21–24 November 2013 became mass demonstrations. On the night of 30 November the Berkut riot police violently dispersed students on Independence Square (the Maidan), which turned an expression of pro-European sentiment into a broader movement against police violence and corruption. Occupation of the square and nearby buildings continued through winter, supported by volunteers, churches, and opposition parties, including Batkivshchyna, UDAR, and Svoboda, as well as nonpartisan activists and some far-right groups.

On 16 January 2014 parliament passed laws restricting protest; clashes followed on Hrushevskoho Street, and the first protesters died in January. On 18–20 February, shootings and fighting in central Kyiv killed roughly a hundred protesters (later commemorated as the “Heavenly Hundred”) and a number of police. Responsibility for individual killings became the subject of Ukrainian investigations and trials; the full chain of command remains legally contested. Poland, Germany, and France mediated between government and opposition.

Euromaidan was neither an institution with a single program nor an undifferentiated crowd. Student groups, journalists, labor activists, religious organizations, opposition parties, nationalist movements, local volunteers and citizens who previously avoided politics participated for different reasons. The square became a working settlement with food distribution, medical assistance, volunteer security and public assemblies. Women and men occupied different and sometimes overlapping roles, and participants differed over how far confrontation with state authorities should go. The organizations that maintained daily life on the square help explain how a protest endured through winter despite uncertainty over its political outcome.

Section 70.13

The Fall of Yanukovych and the Crisis of Ukrainian State Authority

On 21 February 2014 Yanukovych and opposition leaders signed an agreement, witnessed by the three European foreign ministers, calling for constitutional changes and early elections. Russia's envoy did not sign. That night Yanukovych left Kyiv and then eastern Ukraine, and on 22 February parliament voted 328 of 450 that he had withdrawn from his duties and set a presidential election for 25 May. Oleksandr Turchynov became acting president and Arseniy Yatsenyuk prime minister (27 February).

Russian officials called these steps an unconstitutional coup. Ukrainian officials and many legal observers argued that the president had abandoned his post and parliament acted in a crisis, though critics noted the vote did not follow the constitution's impeachment procedure. Euromaidan was neither a foreign-directed plot nor a politically uniform revolution. It involved diverse Ukrainian participants, and the new authorities included nationalist ministers while the far right performed modestly in later elections. Petro Poroshenko won the 25 May presidential election.

The February 21 compromise envisioned constitutional changes and an early presidential election, but its implementation became impossible in the environment following the shootings and the president's departure. The Rada's February 22 resolution recorded 328 votes for the assessment that Yanukovych had withdrawn from fulfilling his constitutional duties. The language of that resolution and the failure to follow the constitution's ordinary impeachment procedure became central to later legal and political arguments. A historian should not simply import the terminology of either side without first describing what the elected legislature actually did, why it acted urgently and which procedural steps were absent.

Section 70.14

Russian Military Intervention and the Seizure of Control in Crimea

Crimea, transferred to Soviet Ukraine in 1954, was an autonomous republic with a Russian-speaking majority, a Ukrainian minority, and a Crimean Tatar community of roughly one-eighth. Russia's Black Sea Fleet was based at Sevastopol under agreements with Ukraine allowing up to 25,000 personnel. After Yanukovych's fall, pro-Russian rallies took place in Sevastopol, and from 27 February armed men in uniforms without insignia seized the Crimean parliament in Simferopol, where deputies, in a closed session, installed Sergei Aksyonov as prime minister.

Russian forces blocked Ukrainian military bases and key sites. Putin denied on 4 March that these were Russian troops; on 17 April he acknowledged their involvement. Most Ukrainian units, outnumbered and without orders to fight, were blockaded and later left. Control changed hands within weeks, with limited fatalities, and Russia's reinforcement of its existing garrison went beyond what bilateral agreements allowed.

Russian military forces already had a significant legal presence in Crimea under the Black Sea Fleet agreements, but a permitted basing arrangement did not give them authority to seize Ukrainian public institutions or determine the peninsula's constitutional status. The distinction between a garrison stationed under treaty and units taking control of roads, airfields, ports and legislative buildings matters for any legal account. In late February 2014 armed personnel without insignia secured strategically important sites, while Russian officials initially declined to identify them as Russian soldiers. Their equipment, coordination and subsequent Russian acknowledgments became major evidence concerning who controlled the operation.

The seizure of the Crimean parliament occurred amid competing local political mobilizations. Pro-Russian demonstrators advocated closer ties to Moscow, while Crimean Tatar and pro-Ukrainian groups opposed changes imposed without Ukrainian constitutional consent. Deputies met under armed conditions and appointed a new local leadership whose authority Ukrainian officials challenged. The operation did not require a conventional prolonged battlefield campaign to alter the effective balance of power: blockade of military installations, control of communications and uncertainty among Ukrainian commanders reduced opportunities for organized defense.

Section 70.15

The March 2014 Referendum, Annexation, and International Law

Crimea's parliament scheduled a referendum for 16 March 2014. The ballot offered two choices: joining Russia, or restoring Crimea's 1992 constitution, with no option to preserve the status quo. It was held under Russian military control, without invited major international monitoring. Officials reported 96.8 percent for joining Russia on 83 percent turnout; independent assessments of turnout varied, and a member of Russia's presidential human rights council cited lower figures. On 18 March Russia signed a treaty of accession.

Russia's justifications were self-determination, protection of Russian speakers, and the precedent of Kosovo's independence. Ukraine argued that its constitution requires an all-Ukrainian referendum for territorial change and that the 1994 Budapest Memorandum committed Russia to respect its borders. On 27 March the UN General Assembly adopted Resolution 68/262 (100 for, 11 against, 58 abstentions) affirming Ukraine's territorial integrity and stating the referendum had no validity. Russia thus controlled Crimea and treated it as Russian, while Ukraine and most states continued to recognize it as part of Ukraine.

Section 70.16

Crimea After Annexation: Governance, Rights, and Everyday Life

Crimea's roughly 2.4 million residents were treated as Russian citizens unless they declined within a short period, and Russian law replaced Ukrainian law. Crimean Tatar leaders, many of whom opposed the annexation, faced restrictions: in 2016 a Russian-appointed court banned the Mejlis, their representative body, and monitors documented detentions, searches, and disappearances. Ukrainian-language schooling and media shrank. Property and business ownership were reorganized, and Ukrainian authorities restricted water, power, and trade.

Western sanctions limited investment and tourism by foreign companies, while Russian federal spending and tourists increased. The Kerch Strait Bridge, opened to road traffic in May 2018, tied the peninsula to Russia's mainland. Crimea also became a larger military base. Experiences varied: some residents welcomed integration into Russia, while others, including Ukrainian and Tatar activists, faced pressure, and reliable opinion polling under these conditions is difficult.

The change in governing authority affected courts, schools, taxes, military service, public-sector employment, property registration and access to banking. Residents had to navigate new documents and rules while the Ukrainian government continued to regard the territory as its own. Some welcomed Russian pensions or infrastructure investment; others experienced loss of institutional protection, uncertainty over citizenship or pressure to accept changes. The annexation also altered connections with mainland Ukraine, including transport routes, family visits and supplies of water and electricity.

Division IV — The War in the Donbas, 2014–2021

Section 70.17

Political Unrest and Armed Separatism in Eastern Ukraine, Spring 2014

Eastern Ukraine's Donbas, a coal and heavy-industry region, had a Russian-speaking majority in many cities and was the electoral base of Yanukovych's Party of Regions. After his fall, many residents feared for jobs, language rights, and the region's influence in Kyiv; a 23 February 2014 parliamentary vote to repeal the 2012 language law, never enacted, fed those fears. Demonstrations in Donetsk, Luhansk, Kharkiv, Odesa and other cities held varied aims, from federalization to joining Russia, and many residents opposed them. On 2 May 2014 in Odesa, according to the United Nations monitoring mission, 48 people died: six in street clashes and 42 in and around the Trade Unions Building fire. Both sides used violence, and police and emergency-response failures deepened the tragedy and polarization.

In early April armed groups seized the Donetsk regional administration and the Luhansk security-service building. The “Donetsk People's Republic” was proclaimed on 7 April and a “Luhansk People's Republic” followed; referendums on 11 May, held amid fighting and without recognized monitoring, were not accepted by Ukraine or most states. Scholars agree that local grievances existed; they differ on how far the armed seizures reflected local initiative versus organization by outsiders, though evidence shows Russian citizens in leading roles.

The Donbas was economically interconnected with the rest of Ukraine and the wider post-Soviet region. Heavy industry depended on energy, transport networks, trade routes and investment decisions made in Kyiv and beyond. The decline of some industrial enterprises and the political role of local business networks created grievances that were not reducible to one national identity. Political mobilization after February 2014 involved groups favoring decentralization, groups supportive of the deposed president, advocates of union with Russia and citizens defending Ukraine's territorial integrity. Russian-language use did not reliably predict support for armed separatism.

Section 70.18

The Beginning of Armed Conflict: Sloviansk, Donetsk, and Luhansk

On 12 April 2014 an armed group led by Igor Girkin (“Strelkov”), a Russian citizen and former security-service officer, seized Sloviansk; Girkin later said publicly that he had helped start the war. Kyiv announced an “anti-terrorist operation” on 13 April. The armed formations were varied: local militias, Cossack units, battalions such as Vostok, and volunteers from Russia. Russian citizens served as early “prime ministers” of the self-declared republics. On the Ukrainian side were the army, National Guard, and volunteer battalions such as Donbas, Aidar, and Azov, whose conduct drew human-rights criticism.

Evidence of Russian state support includes documented weapons transfers, financing, and, from August, Russian regular units. Russian officials denied sending troops and described participants as volunteers; in December 2015 Putin acknowledged that people performing “tasks, including in the military sphere” were present. Ukrainian forces retook Sloviansk in July 2014.

The seizure of Sloviansk by Igor Girkin's group represented an escalation from demonstrations and brief occupations to an organized armed challenge. Girkin's earlier involvement in Crimea and statements after the event make his activities relevant to questions of organization and cross-border links. Ukrainian authorities described their response as an anti-terrorist operation, but the deployment of military and security forces also reflected the difficulties faced by a state whose command structures were disrupted after the political change in Kyiv. Police loyalties and local administrative capacity varied, and the same category of 'government forces' encompassed institutions with different training and equipment.

The armed formations on the anti-government side were heterogeneous. Some recruits lived locally, some arrived from Russia or other places, and rival commanders sometimes disagreed about authority and military strategy. Political slogans ranged from federalization to separation and union with Russia. Ukrainian volunteer battalions also appeared alongside the regular military; some filled immediate personnel gaps, while human-rights monitors reported abuses by certain units. A careful account must not treat allegations against a particular battalion as proof against every Ukrainian soldier, nor assume that all armed groups on the other side followed one command structure at every moment.

Control of transport junctions and the Russian border proved important. Access to ammunition, armored vehicles, financing and replacement personnel influenced the conflict's durability. Russian officials publicly denied formal troop deployments while acknowledging various forms of personal involvement, but later investigative material and the capture of Russian soldiers supported evidence of state military participation. Students should assess military equipment identification, satellite imagery, testimony and official statements according to their provenance. Some evidence became available only after specific events, so retrospective findings must be dated.

The fighting changed ordinary life well before the major battles of late 2014. Businesses suspended operations, families faced checkpoints, hospitals treated the wounded and schools closed or moved classes. Civilians who opposed both Kyiv and armed separatism could become vulnerable to suspicion. Local political agency did not disappear when armed actors arrived, but the costs of expressing an opinion rose sharply. The initial phase therefore joins institutional collapse, local grievances, external assistance and civilian displacement in one account of conflict escalation.

The appearance of military equipment does not by itself identify its owner or route of transfer. Open-source investigations compare photographs, serial numbers, markings and sightings at different places; testimony and official records can help corroborate them. Students should distinguish a documented transfer from allegations about a central command chain. These methods are also relevant to the later MH17 investigation and provide continuity in the chapter's treatment of evidence.

Families in Sloviansk and nearby towns experienced a dramatic shift in the meaning of ordinary spaces. Schools became shelters, road junctions became checkpoints and workplaces closed because employees could not travel safely. Such changes are evidence of state breakdown and armed control even where battle casualty figures remain uncertain. Narrating them makes it possible to understand why displacement began before the largest military operations of the year.

Section 70.19

The Military Campaign of 2014 and the Battle of Ilovaisk

In June and July 2014 Ukrainian forces regained ground, including Mariupol (13 June), and pressed toward Donetsk and Luhansk while fighting raged along the Russian border, with shelling from both sides. In August Ukraine and NATO reported Russian regular units entering the war, and Ukraine captured Russian paratroopers on 25 August; Russia denied sending its army. The separatist front advanced and the Ukrainian offensive collapsed.

At Ilovaisk, near Donetsk, Ukrainian army and volunteer units were encircled in late August. A promised withdrawal corridor was attacked on 29 August. Ukrainian official counts of dead exceeded 350, while estimates vary and many soldiers were captured or missing. Ilovaisk pushed Kyiv toward negotiations. Casualty figures for the whole 2014 campaign remain contested and should be attributed to their sources.

Ukraine's summer campaign recovered territory and threatened to separate armed enclaves from the Russian border. The reoccupation of Sloviansk and Kramatorsk and the fighting for transport routes changed the balance of the conflict. Ukrainian forces, however, operated with limitations in equipment, coordination and logistics, and several newly organized units had uneven experience. The proximity of the Russian border allowed armed groups opposing Kyiv access to a much larger strategic environment than a purely local insurgency would have possessed.

Section 70.20

Malaysia Airlines Flight MH17 and the International Investigation

On 17 July 2014 Malaysia Airlines Flight MH17, flying from Amsterdam to Kuala Lumpur, was destroyed over territory held by separatists, killing all 298 people aboard, most of them Dutch citizens. The Dutch Safety Board concluded in October 2015 that a Buk missile warhead exploded outside the cockpit. The international Joint Investigation Team, led by the Netherlands, concluded in 2016–2018 that the missile came from a Buk launcher belonging to Russia's 53rd anti-aircraft brigade, fired from a field near Pervomaiskyi.

Russia denied responsibility, offered alternative explanations including a Ukrainian launch, and vetoed a 2015 UN Security Council tribunal proposal. These responses were examined by investigators. A Dutch court in November 2022 convicted three men in absentia of murder, a retrospective finding that clarified the earlier evidence but was unavailable to contemporaries.

Flight MH17's destruction transformed an ongoing regional war into a tragedy affecting families and governments on several continents. The civilian aircraft was traveling at cruising altitude over territory under armed conflict; all 298 passengers and crew were killed. Families and national governments demanded recovery of victims, protection of the crash site and identification of the weapon and persons responsible. The initial handling of debris and bodies occurred under difficult security conditions and generated controversy. Those circumstances are part of why technical evidence preservation was central to the subsequent investigations.

The Dutch Safety Board's October 2015 investigation addressed the physical cause of the crash, identifying a Buk surface-to-air missile warhead. Its remit was aviation safety and technical explanation, not a trial establishing criminal guilt. The multinational Joint Investigation Team examined transport routes, launch location, communications and command links, and publicly identified a Buk system connected to Russia's 53rd anti-aircraft brigade. Russian authorities rejected the team's conclusions and presented other accounts, some of which investigators evaluated against physical evidence and records. The distinction among a technical inquiry, a criminal investigation and political statements must be maintained.

The downing influenced sanctions, diplomatic relations, public opinion and debates about responsibility for the conflict. An aviation safety investigation and a criminal proceeding answer different questions. The Dutch Safety Board sought the physical cause of the aircraft's destruction and issued recommendations about operating in conflict airspace. Criminal investigators sought evidence of responsible individuals under legal standards. The families of the passengers pursued remembrance and accountability across national borders. Section 70.21

Minsk I, Minsk II, and the Battles of 2014–2015

The Minsk Protocol of 5 September 2014 was negotiated in the Trilateral Contact Group (Ukraine, Russia, OSCE), with representatives of the self-declared republics also signing; a 19 September memorandum added details. It failed to hold. After Ukrainian losses at Donetsk airport and the fighting around Debaltseve, a 12 February 2015 “Package of Measures” (Minsk II) was agreed through the Trilateral Contact Group after negotiations involving the leaders of Russia, Ukraine, France, and Germany; it was endorsed by UN Security Council Resolution 2202.

Minsk II called for a ceasefire, withdrawal of heavy weapons, OSCE monitoring, local elections, special status for parts of Donetsk and Luhansk, amnesty, and Ukrainian restoration of control over its border after elections. Separatist forces took Debaltseve on 18 February, after the ceasefire began. The agreements left the order of steps disputed: Ukraine sought security and border control first, while Russia and the separatists stressed political steps. Russia described itself as a mediator, not a party.

Section 70.22

Civilian Life Along the Contact Line, 2015–2021

The front line stabilized into a “contact line” of trenches and checkpoints, with a buffer zone dotted with mines. Residents faced shelling, damaged housing, interrupted water and power, and restricted crossing; elderly people made long journeys to collect Ukrainian pensions after Kyiv suspended payments and services in non-government-controlled areas in 2014. Families were divided, and over a million people were registered as internally displaced in Ukraine. Others moved to Russia.

The UN human-rights office estimated that about 14,000 people were killed in the conflict from 2014 to 2021, including roughly 3,400 civilians (this includes the MH17 victims), with continuing civilian casualties from shelling, mines, and explosive remnants. In occupied areas, monitors reported arbitrary detention and restrictions on speech; on the government side there were also documented abuses. From 2019 Russia eased the issuance of passports to residents of the occupied areas, a step Ukraine and Western governments criticized.

Section 70.23

Negotiations, Monitoring, and the Failure to Resolve the Conflict

The OSCE Special Monitoring Mission observed the line from 2014, reporting ceasefire violations by all sides and limits on its access, especially in separatist-held territory. The Normandy format continued; Volodymyr Zelensky, elected in April 2019, pursued a settlement, and leaders met in Paris in December 2019. A formula on local elections and special status, associated with Germany's Frank-Walter Steinmeier, was contested in Ukraine, where critics feared it would legitimize separatist authorities. In July 2020 the sides agreed to additional ceasefire measures that reduced casualties for some months.

By the end of 2021 the conflict was unresolved. Negotiation stalled over sequencing, control of the border, elections under armed conditions, and the status of occupied areas, while each side blamed the other. Contemporaries could not know that the conflict would be followed by a full-scale invasion in 2022; the stalemate shows continuing hostility but not inevitability.

The OSCE Special Monitoring Mission was mandated to observe and report, not to enforce a ceasefire. Its daily reports contained counts and locations of explosions, restrictions on movement and descriptions of the humanitarian situation, but each report had geographical and temporal limits. Monitors repeatedly encountered access restrictions, especially where armed organizations controlled territory. Their observations therefore document violations while also revealing why a complete independent account was difficult. The number of recorded incidents could change because of genuine fluctuations or because monitoring technology and access varied.

Diplomatic meetings in the Normandy format involved Ukraine, Russia, France and Germany. The 2019 election of Volodymyr Zelensky brought a new Ukrainian leader who sought to reduce violence and make progress in negotiations. Prisoner exchanges and disengagement arrangements raised expectations, but disagreements continued over border security, special status and the timing of elections. Domestic Ukrainian opposition to some proposed concessions was politically significant; citizens feared that poorly secured elections might entrench armed leaders without genuine free choice.

The additional measures agreed in July 2020 reduced some types of violence for a period but did not remove weapons, resolve sovereignty or restore full freedom of movement. Ceasefire commitments and reported violations continued to be politically contested. Russia maintained that Kyiv should negotiate directly with separatist authorities, while Ukraine emphasized Russia's military role and demanded international security guarantees. These institutional disagreements are central to explaining why diplomatic progress was intermittent.

By December 2021, the conflict had lasted nearly eight years and transformed the politics of Ukraine and Russia, the region's economy and Europe's diplomatic agenda. Students can identify the causes of diplomatic stalemate without assuming that a much larger war was unavoidable. The chapter's endpoint requires attention to contemporaneous uncertainty: leaders could contemplate escalation, compromise or indefinite tension. The invasion of February 2022 belongs to Chapter 71 and should be analyzed from its own evidence rather than treated as a fact already determined by the earlier failure of Minsk.

Diplomatic frameworks operated at several levels: heads of government negotiated principles, the Trilateral Contact Group discussed operational steps and OSCE monitors observed field conditions. An announcement of progress at one level did not ensure that checkpoints opened or shooting stopped on the ground. This institutional separation explains why periodic summits generated public hope but often failed to change civilian circumstances immediately.

Negotiations also confronted questions of legitimate representation. Ukraine sought the return of its border and the organization of elections consistent with domestic law, while Russia presented some measures as obligations of Kyiv toward local authorities. Without agreement on who controlled armed groups and who could guarantee security, the terms 'autonomy,' 'election' and 'withdrawal' could carry different practical meanings. This is a core reason to teach the actual documents rather than rely on political summaries of Minsk.

Section 70.24

International Sanctions and Russian Countermeasures

Western sanctions came in stages. In March 2014 the United States and European Union imposed asset freezes and visa bans on individuals tied to the annexation of Crimea, and later banned most trade and investment with Crimea. In July 2014, after the downing of MH17, they adopted sectoral measures limiting long-term financing for major state banks and energy firms, restricting exports of some oil-extraction technology and defense-related goods. Further measures followed over other disputes, including cyber activity, the 2018 Skripal poisoning in Britain, and the U.S. CAATSA law of 2017.

Russia answered in August 2014 with a ban on many food imports from the United States, EU, Canada, Australia, and Norway, renewed repeatedly. Officials presented it as support for domestic producers; critics pointed to higher prices and, in 2015, the controversial destruction of seized food. Individual sanctions targeted named officials and businesspeople, while sectoral measures affected access to capital and technology; economists distinguish these effects from those of oil prices.

Sanctions after March 2014 were neither uniform nor introduced on one date. Initial travel restrictions and asset freezes targeted persons regarded by Western governments as involved in the annexation. Later measures restricted financing for designated banks and firms, the supply of technologies for certain energy projects and particular defense-related transactions. The legal structure mattered: a restriction on raising long-term capital abroad does not operate like a blanket embargo on consumer goods. Some firms could continue ordinary trade while confronting higher borrowing costs and difficulties obtaining specialized equipment. A complete economic analysis must specify the sanctioning jurisdiction, affected entity, transaction and period.

Russia's countermeasures similarly had distinct objectives and consequences. The ban on many Western food imports was presented as a means of defending domestic agriculture and retaliating against foreign policy pressure. Consumers faced higher prices for some goods and a narrower range of suppliers; producers in sectors such as poultry and dairy benefited from protection but also encountered rising costs of equipment and imported inputs. Changes in trade patterns occurred alongside depreciation of the ruble and shifts in consumer demand, making it difficult to infer the effect of the embargo from a price increase alone.

Sanctions affected political and business networks as well as macroeconomic indicators. Wealthy individuals facing travel and banking restrictions had incentives to restructure assets, while businesses reassessed legal risk and financial exposure. Russian authorities encouraged domestic payment systems and alternatives to some imported technology. These responses increased certain forms of resilience but also created costs and new dependencies. The sanctions regime was adjusted repeatedly, and later packages addressed issues such as cyber operations and chemical-weapons allegations, which should not be presented as consequences of Crimea alone.

Counterfactual questions require care. Without sanctions, the Russian economy would still have faced the collapse in global oil prices and long-standing productivity problems. Without the oil-price shock, restrictions on finance and technology would still have constrained selected sectors. Economists use models, sector comparisons and timing to estimate these separate effects, but estimates vary. Students should investigate which indicator is measured—output, inflation, household income, investment or access to technology—and resist single-cause explanations. The sanctions were political instruments with economic consequences, not a substitute for explaining the underlying structure of Russia's economy.

A sanctions timeline should specify whether a measure prohibited particular goods, restricted loans of specified maturity or froze named persons' assets. These categories affect different firms and households. Sectoral financial restrictions can increase the cost of borrowing without preventing an existing company from selling oil, while import bans on food have immediate consequences in grocery stores. Aggregating all measures under the single word 'sanctions' conceals their distinct mechanisms.

Responses also produced unintended effects. Some protected producers expanded output, while consumers paid more for certain categories and smaller firms struggled to buy equipment. Sanctions avoidance and legal compliance created new professional services and administrative costs. Students should connect the diplomatic purpose of imposing costs with the distribution of those costs inside both the target economy and the economies imposing measures.

Section 70.25

Oil Prices, the Ruble Crisis, and Economic Contraction, 2014–2016

Brent crude, which had traded above $100 a barrel in mid-2014, fell to about $30 by early 2016. Since oil and gas provided a large part of federal revenue and export earnings, the ruble weakened sharply. In December 2014 the Bank of Russia, led by Elvira Nabiullina, raised its key rate to 17 percent to stem a currency rout. It had already moved toward a floating ruble and inflation targeting. Capital outflows exceeded $100 billion in 2014.

Year-end inflation reached about 13 percent in 2015, real incomes fell, and GDP contracted by roughly two to three percent (official figures were revised), with almost no growth in 2016, estimated at approximately 0.2 percent in the World Bank 2022-vintage series. Households shifted spending and many cut back on imported goods. Economists disagree on how much of the downturn came from oil prices, which cut export revenues, and how much from sanctions, which restricted foreign borrowing and technology. Weaknesses in investment and productivity preceded both.

Section 70.26

Import Substitution, Agriculture, Industry, and State Enterprise

Import substitution aimed to replace foreign goods with domestic ones. Agriculture was the clearest success: grain harvests and exports rose, Russia became one of the world's largest wheat exporters, and pork and poultry output grew, helped by subsidies and the food ban. Defense procurement and some industrial sectors also expanded. Officials claimed growing self-sufficiency.

Costs and limits were also visible. Domestic substitutes were often more expensive, and critical machinery, electronics, and software still depended on imports. State-owned firms and state banks gained a larger share of the economy, partly because the Bank of Russia revoked licenses of hundreds of private banks in a clean-up of the financial sector. Supporters stressed resilience, while critics argued that protection weakened competition.

Import substitution was implemented through tariffs, public procurement, subsidies, technical standards and restrictions on purchases from abroad. In agriculture, a weaker ruble made some imported goods expensive and local production more competitive, while state support for larger producers expanded capacity. Russia became a major wheat exporter, but harvest success also depended on weather, global prices and productivity improvements that preceded 2014. In meat and dairy, domestic output increased under protection, although the price and quality experienced by consumers varied. The historian should identify which changes were driven by market incentives and which required sustained public subsidy.

Industry presented a more complicated picture. A firm assembling aircraft, telecommunications equipment or machine tools might rely on foreign components even if the final product was manufactured in Russia. Restrictions on purchasing some technologies encouraged local alternatives but could slow projects when domestic substitutes were not available. Large state-connected firms sometimes enjoyed easier access to government contracts and credit than small private enterprises. That disparity matters for evaluating whether import substitution fostered innovation or primarily redistributed market share toward established producers.

The banking system changed as well. The central bank revoked licenses from many smaller institutions in an effort to address poor capitalization, dubious lending and regulatory violations. This strengthened supervision in some respects but also increased concentration, with major state banks holding a large share of deposits and loans. Regional entrepreneurs could encounter fewer local financing options even as official indicators of bank stability improved. A centralization of finance can therefore have different consequences for systemic risk and the competitive conditions facing new firms.

The social symbolism of self-sufficiency was important. Government communications celebrated domestic cheese, machinery and agriculture as evidence that foreign pressure could be overcome. Critics pointed to higher consumer prices, constrained choice and continued dependence on imported pharmaceuticals, electronics and specialized industrial components. Both claims can be investigated through trade statistics and firm-level data. Import substitution was not one unified success or failure: outcomes differed by industry, technology, region, investment needs and exposure to export markets.

Wheat exports are an imperfect proxy for the success of import substitution because grain competitiveness developed through investment, land use and global market demand before sanctions. The effects of the 2014 food embargo were more direct in certain dairy, fruit and meat markets. The historian should identify the timing of each industry's gains and compare production with prices and consumer choice, not simply celebrate a higher aggregate harvest.

Technological dependence was particularly difficult to overcome. A domestically branded aircraft or computer could still use imported parts, software or specialized machinery. Government procurement could create demand for substitutes but also reduce competitive pressure if contracts were allocated politically. Comparing sectors helps reveal when state coordination built capability and when it reproduced dependence in a different institutional form.

Section 70.27

Economic Stagnation, Investment, and Productivity

After the recession, growth was modest: about 1.8 percent in 2017, 2.8 percent in 2018, and 2.2 percent in 2019, in a World Bank 2022-vintage series, compared with the rapid growth of the early 2000s. A fiscal rule, introduced in 2017, set aside energy revenues, building reserves and protecting the budget. Real household incomes stagnated through much of the period, and investment remained low as a share of GDP.

Explanations differ. Analysts have emphasized an economy dependent on commodity exports, state ownership, uncertain property rights, corruption, an aging workforce, and sanctions-related caution among investors. The government's May 2018 “national projects” promised spending on infrastructure, health, and education, with uneven implementation. These assessments should rely on Rosstat, Bank of Russia, IMF, and World Bank series rather than on political claims.

After the first recessionary shock, growth remained substantially slower than in the early 2000s. Sluggish productivity, uneven investment and weak household income growth affected the perceived success of public policy. The national economy could display low inflation, a substantial foreign-reserve cushion and prudent public debt management while offering limited opportunities for young people seeking higher wages. Macroeconomic stability and dynamic growth are different objectives. Examining one without the other can produce a misleading account of the decade.

The 2018 Pension Reform and Public Opposition

On 14 June 2018, the day the football World Cup opened in Russia, the government announced a plan to raise the retirement age, then 60 for men and 55 for women, to 65 for men by 2028 and 63 for women by 2034. Officials argued that the system faced demographic and fiscal strain. The idea had long been regarded as politically dangerous, and surveys showed overwhelming opposition. Protests took place in many cities, including rallies called by the Communist Party and by Alexei Navalny.

On 29 August Putin appeared on television, endorsed the reform, and reduced the increase for women to 60, with other protective measures. The law was signed on 3 October. Putin's approval ratings fell substantially from the high levels after 2014, and in September 2018 regional elections the ruling party's candidates lost or were forced into runoffs in several regions, including Primorye, where results were annulled. The episode links economic policy to political legitimacy.

Retirement policy lies at the intersection of demography, fiscal planning and social expectation. The previous ages—sixty for men and fifty-five for women under the general rules—were familiar institutional features inherited from the Soviet period. Because life expectancy varied greatly by sex and region, however, a uniform rise had unequal implications. Critics emphasized that some men, particularly in poorer or industrial areas, had relatively limited healthy years after retirement, while supporters stressed population aging and the financial burden on the pension system. Special categories of employment and transitional arrangements complicated the headline figures.

The June 2018 announcement coincided with the opening of the football World Cup, generating criticism over political timing. Public opposition cut across ordinary party divisions; trade unions, communists, independent activists and local groups protested for different reasons. Putin's later televised intervention reduced the proposed increase for women and added limited adjustments, showing that opposition could modify legislation without stopping it. The political lesson was not that public pressure had no effect, but that the extent of responsive change remained controlled by the leadership.

Section 70.29

Inequality, Demography, Migration, and Regional Economic Life

Russia's population, about 146–147 million after counting Crimea, had grown slightly in 2013–2015 as births held up and deaths declined, but natural decrease returned after 2016 as the birth rate fell and fewer women entered peak childbearing years. Immigration, notably from Central Asia, partly offset the decline; many migrants worked in construction, trade, and services in Moscow, St. Petersburg, and other cities, often in precarious legal conditions.

Living standards varied widely: incomes and services were far higher in Moscow and large energy regions than in many rural areas, small towns, and parts of the North Caucasus. Housing costs, mortgage borrowing, and consumer credit rose, and the share of households in debt grew. Official poverty rates of about 12–13 percent hid broader insecurity among the working poor. Experiences also differed by gender, age, and region, and the Far East and Siberia faced out-migration.

Demographic analysis requires separating total population, natural increase, migration and the effects of disputed territorial accounting. Russian official figures incorporating Crimea after 2014 are not directly comparable to some international datasets that use internationally recognized borders. Trends also reflect changes in the size of the female cohorts reaching childbearing age, mortality by age and sex, and migration flows. A temporary improvement in the birth rate does not necessarily indicate that a family-policy initiative caused it, just as an annual fall in population may combine several different mechanisms.

Migrant labor was central to major cities, construction, transportation, retail and some agricultural sectors. Workers from Central Asian states encountered employment opportunities unavailable at home but also complicated permits, police checks, language barriers and discrimination. Remittances connected Russian economic conditions to household welfare in Tajikistan, Kyrgyzstan and other countries. Migrants were not simply a labor supply: they were people with families, schooling needs, professional aspirations and differing legal statuses. Their economic experiences changed with the ruble's value and the pandemic's border restrictions.

Division VI — Russia's Intervention in Syria and the Projection of Military Power

Section 70.30

Syria Before Russian Military Intervention, 2011–2015

Protests against Bashar al-Assad's government began in March 2011 and, after violent repression, became an armed conflict involving government forces, many opposition factions, Kurdish-led forces, and jihadist groups, including the Islamic State, which proclaimed a “caliphate” in 2014. Millions of Syrians were displaced. Russia had long ties with Damascus: a naval supply facility at Tartus, arms sales, and diplomatic support. From October 2011 it vetoed several UN Security Council resolutions critical of the Syrian government, and in 2013 it brokered the plan for removing Syria's declared chemical weapons.

By mid-2015 government forces were losing ground to opposition coalitions, notably in Idlib. Russian motives are debated: keeping an ally and its bases, preventing regime change by outside powers, countering militants from Russia and Central Asia, restoring great-power status, and shifting attention from Ukraine. Scholars give differing weight to each.

Russian intervention in Syria cannot be understood without the local chronology beginning in 2011. Demonstrations against the Assad government were met with arrests and violence, helping produce a wider armed uprising. Armed groups developed diverse political and ideological programs, and outside governments supported different factions. The rise of the Islamic State altered the conflict, but it did not turn all armed opposition to Assad into members of that organization. Kurdish-led forces, local councils, Islamist factions and foreign-backed militias pursued different objectives. The Syrian government also mobilized loyalist forces and external allies, and millions of civilians fled their homes.

Moscow maintained longstanding diplomatic and military ties with Damascus, including arms transactions and access to Tartus. Russian leaders opposed Western-backed regime change, recalling Iraq and Libya in discussions of the danger of state collapse. Officials also expressed concern about extremist fighters traveling between the Middle East, Russia and Central Asia. These motives were not mutually exclusive, nor is it possible to infer their relative importance merely from public statements. Preserving military access, supporting an allied government and reasserting diplomatic influence may have converged in the decision to intervene.

By the summer of 2015 the Syrian government's military position had deteriorated in several important areas. Iranian advisers, Hezbollah and other allied formations had already provided support, but Moscow's air power offered capabilities they lacked. The decision to intervene was therefore made in an environment of battlefield pressure, international concern over Islamic State attacks and continuing disagreement about Assad's political future. Russia portrayed its operations as lawful assistance to the recognized government, while critics questioned the methods and political objective of sustaining that government.

The scale of human suffering should frame the military narrative rather than follow it as an afterthought. Civilians under different armed authorities faced sieges, detention, disrupted schools, destroyed homes and shortages of medicine. The difficulty of obtaining reliable casualty totals is not evidence that civilian harm can be ignored; it requires specifying sources and methodology. This background establishes why Russia's operations had implications extending far beyond military prestige, and why the Syrian perspectives of local residents and displaced people belong in a chapter centered on Russian policy.

Syria's internal political conflict preceded major Russian air operations by more than four years. The intervention can be understood as an external decision made within an already fragmented war, not as its origin. Recognizing this chronology prevents the narrative from erasing earlier Syrian political protests or the many armed factions whose actions changed the country's trajectory.

The description of terrorism also requires precision. The Islamic State held territory and perpetrated mass violence, but other armed opponents of Assad ranged across different religious and political positions. To describe every non-government-held district as terrorist territory would reproduce a wartime government claim rather than established classification. Source analysis must ask which organization held or attacked a particular location.

Section 70.31

The September 2015 Intervention and Russian Military Operations

On 30 September 2015, after the Federation Council authorized the use of force and Assad requested help, Russian aircraft began strikes from Hmeimim air base near Latakia. Russia stated that it targeted the Islamic State and other terrorists. U.S. officials, monitoring groups, and journalists documented that many early strikes hit areas held by other armed opposition groups, which Moscow rejected. Russian ships in the Caspian Sea launched cruise missiles in October 2015.

Russia's air power, advisers, and special forces supported Syrian government and allied ground forces, including Iranian-backed militias. The government recaptured eastern Aleppo in December 2016 after a siege and heavy bombing, and Palmyra changed hands more than once, the Russian side presenting a concert there in May 2016. The campaign relied on air power and the ground forces of others, and stated aims differed from observed targeting.

Section 70.32

Civilian Casualties, Destruction, and International Humanitarian Law

The UN Commission of Inquiry on Syria documented attacks on hospitals, markets, schools, and residential areas, and displacement of civilians, particularly during the sieges of Aleppo and later in Idlib. It attributed most documented violations to the Syrian government, including use of chemical weapons, and identified, in some cases, Russian airstrikes causing civilian deaths, noting limits in identifying aircraft. Russia denied targeting civilians and rejected many allegations as unsupported.

Monitoring groups such as Airwars and Syrian organizations provided casualty estimates for Russian strikes, which vary widely by method and should be attributed to their sources. Humanitarian law prohibits indiscriminate attacks and attacks on medical facilities. Evidence strongly supports that civilians suffered heavily from the combined campaign of the Syrian government and its allies, though attribution of individual incidents requires case-by-case documentation.

Humanitarian law distinguishes lawful attacks on military objectives from deliberate attacks on civilians and indiscriminate or disproportionate attacks. The classification depends on what commanders could reasonably know, the target, the weapon, foreseeable civilian harm and precautions taken. The general fact of civilian death is not sufficient to determine the legal status of every incident, but documented patterns of attacks on hospitals, markets or residential areas require serious investigation. The UN Commission of Inquiry and other monitoring bodies used interviews, satellite imagery, photographs, medical records and available military information, often under severe access constraints.

The Commission investigated specific operations in Idlib and other areas and, for some incidents, found reasonable grounds to attribute unlawful attacks to Syrian government or Russian forces. The strength of attribution differs by event; it is not accurate to assume that every strike in a joint campaign can be assigned to one state. Russia disputed many allegations and questioned investigators' access or impartiality. The appropriate historical response is not to suspend judgment about all events, but to report the strongest findings accurately, distinguish them from unverified claims and acknowledge what the record cannot show.

Section 70.33

Russia, Iran, Turkey, Israel, and the United States in Syria

Russia's military cooperation with Iran, which had supported the Syrian government with militias and advisers, was a partnership of convenience, with differing aims. Relations with Turkey, a backer of opposition groups, were strained when a Turkish aircraft shot down a Russian Su-24 on 24 November 2015, and later repaired. In 2017 Russia, Turkey, and Iran began the Astana process, which created “de-escalation zones,” and in March 2020 Putin and Erdogan agreed a ceasefire in Idlib after clashes in which Turkish soldiers were killed.

Russia and the United States agreed on a deconfliction channel to avoid accidents, though in February 2018 a clash near Deir ez-Zor involved pro-government fighters, reportedly including Russian private contractors, and U.S. forces. Israel and Russia coordinated to avoid clashes during Israeli strikes on Iranian-linked targets; in September 2018 a Russian Il-20 aircraft was shot down by Syrian air defenses during an Israeli raid, killing 15 Russians.

The Syrian theater brought into contact governments with divergent strategic interests. Iran sought to preserve an allied government and maintain regional connections; Russia prioritized its own military and diplomatic position. Their forces cooperated but did not share every objective. Turkey supported parts of the opposition and was concerned about Kurdish armed groups near its border. Its November 2015 shootdown of a Russian aircraft produced a sharp confrontation before diplomatic and economic relations were repaired. This sequence illustrates how tactical incidents could threaten broader bilateral relationships even while states maintained reasons to negotiate.

The Astana process, organized by Russia, Turkey and Iran beginning in 2017, attempted to manage fighting through ceasefire arrangements and de-escalation areas. Yet some agreements helped separate armed groups temporarily while the Syrian government and its allies later mounted offensives. Diplomatic vocabulary should not be mistaken for a guarantee of enduring peace. Decisions about checkpoints, patrols and airspace required ongoing bargaining, and local communities rarely controlled those arrangements. The 2020 Idlib ceasefire reflected a particularly tense negotiation after Turkish military casualties.

Section 70.34

Strategic Outcomes of the Intervention Through 2021

By 2021 Russia had secured long-term arrangements for its Tartus naval facility and the Hmeimim air base (the Hmeimim aviation-group agreement originated in 2015, while Tartus arrangements were expanded in 2017), tested weapons and tactics, and become an indispensable participant in Middle Eastern diplomacy. Putin announced a partial withdrawal in December 2017, though the Russian presence remained. Russian state media presented the intervention as a counterterrorism success, and officials acknowledged few Russian casualties, the extent of which, including those among private contractors, is not fully established.

The military achievements did not produce a political settlement. A UN-backed Constitutional Committee met from 2019 with little progress, large parts of Syria remained outside government control, including Idlib and the northeast, and the economy was devastated. Costs to Russia were modest compared with those to Syrians. This section ends at 2021 and does not address later developments.

Russia's basing arrangements provided strategic access to the eastern Mediterranean and the Levant. The agreement permitting its aviation group at Hmeimim dated to 2015, while the later expansion of the Tartus naval arrangement included a 2017 agreement. These instruments should be distinguished rather than treated as one pair of identical contracts concluded at the same time. Their provisions concerned facilities, jurisdiction, movement of personnel and long-term access, and they supported deployments that continued after occasional announcements of partial withdrawal.

Politically, Russia established itself as an actor that could influence the Syrian government's military survival and participate in negotiations involving Iran, Turkey, Israel, Western governments and Arab states. This diplomatic importance did not mean that Moscow controlled all participants. Iran possessed independent influence through ground forces and networks; Turkey occupied or supported areas beyond Damascus's control; Kurdish-led administrations held territory in the northeast; and Islamic State activity persisted in reduced form. The landscape by 2021 remained fragmented.

Division VII — Russia's Foreign Relations and the Changing International Order

Section 70.35

Russia, NATO, and the Transformation of European Security

On 1 April 2014 NATO suspended practical civilian and military cooperation with Russia, while keeping channels for political dialogue. At its 2014 Wales summit and 2016 Warsaw summit the alliance strengthened its eastern members' defenses, deploying four multinational battalions to Estonia, Latvia, Lithuania, and Poland in 2017. Russia held large exercises such as Zapad 2017, strengthened forces in Kaliningrad and Crimea, and criticized NATO missile-defense sites in Romania and Poland. The Black Sea and Baltic became areas of heightened military activity.

The two sides interpreted these steps differently. NATO governments described them as defensive responses to the annexation of Crimea. Russian officials described NATO enlargement and deployments as threats to Russian security. Russian threat perceptions were real as political facts, but the principle, which Russia accepted in OSCE documents such as the 1999 OSCE Charter for European Security, that states may choose their own security arrangements means that perceptions did not give Russia a legal right to determine its neighbors' alliances. The question of alliance membership for Ukraine and Georgia, raised in 2008, remained unresolved.

Russia's relationship with NATO changed after the annexation of Crimea and military involvement in eastern Ukraine. NATO suspended practical cooperation in April 2014 while seeking to maintain certain political channels, and alliance members strengthened forces and contingency planning along their eastern flank. Russian officials characterized these measures as threatening encirclement, while alliance governments described them as reassurances to states worried about the use of force against Ukraine. The distinction between perceived danger and an established legal entitlement is crucial: states may form judgments about threats without acquiring the right to dictate another state's foreign-policy choices.

Section 70.36

Relations With the United States: Diplomacy, Cyber Operations, and Electoral Interference

Relations with the United States deteriorated after the 2009 “reset.” Irritants included the 2012 Magnitsky Act and Russia's retaliatory ban on U.S. adoptions, Edward Snowden's asylum in Russia (2013), Syria, and Ukraine. In January 2017 the U.S. intelligence community assessed that Russia, under orders from Putin, conducted a campaign to influence the 2016 presidential election, including hacking and disclosure of Democratic Party emails and online disinformation. Russia denied it. The 2019 report of Special Counsel Robert Mueller described Russian interference as systematic, and indicted Russian military intelligence officers and employees of the Internet Research Agency, but did not establish a criminal conspiracy between the Trump campaign and Russia.

Sanctions, including the 2017 CAATSA law, and expulsions of diplomats followed; after the 2018 Skripal poisoning, Washington expelled sixty Russian diplomats. President Trump's own statements about Russia were mixed with his administration's sanctions. In 2021 President Biden met Putin in Geneva in June, after new sanctions and expulsions in April and after U.S. accusations of Russian responsibility for the SolarWinds cyber intrusion, which Russia denied. Intelligence assessments, court indictments, and political claims are different kinds of evidence.

The relationship with the United States deteriorated through overlapping conflicts over human rights, intelligence activities, Ukraine, Syria, arms control and the rules governing cyber operations. The Magnitsky Act and Russia's retaliatory ban on adoption by U.S. citizens established a pattern in which decisions on one issue could produce political consequences in another. Edward Snowden's arrival in Russia and subsequent asylum added a dispute about surveillance, secrecy and diplomatic trust. These episodes did not follow one predetermined sequence, but each narrowed the room for cooperation when new controversies emerged.

The January 2017 U.S. intelligence-community assessment concluded that Russia conducted an influence campaign concerning the 2016 presidential election, including cyber and information operations, and attributed direction to senior Russian leadership. Russia denied responsibility. The Mueller investigation subsequently described extensive Russian interference and issued charges against specified persons and organizations, while not establishing a criminal conspiracy between the Trump campaign and Russia. These are different propositions. The existence of interference cannot be equated with proof that foreign actors determined the election result, and the absence of an established conspiracy does not negate independently documented interference operations.

Cybersecurity cases present distinctive evidentiary problems. Technical indicators may connect malware, servers or methods to a particular operation, but attribution to a state also requires intelligence, financial, organizational or communications evidence. Public indictments name defendants and allegations under legal standards; intelligence assessments express judgments with specified confidence; court verdicts resolve particular charges. Students should be taught to distinguish those evidentiary forms, especially when political leaders publicly reject the findings or when intelligence sources cannot be disclosed.

Arms Control, Missile Defense, and Strategic Competition

Arms control, a pillar of Cold War stability, eroded. The United States accused Russia of violating the 1987 INF Treaty with the 9M729 cruise missile; Russia denied this and raised objections to U.S. missile-defense launchers in Europe. The United States suspended its obligations and announced withdrawal on 2 February 2019; the withdrawal took effect and the treaty ended on 2 August 2019. In 2020 the United States withdrew from the Open Skies Treaty, and Russia followed in 2021. In February 2021 the two countries extended New START, which limits strategic nuclear forces, for five years.

Both sides modernized nuclear forces. In March 2018 Putin described new systems, including the Avangard glide vehicle and Sarmat missile. Strategic stability talks were held in Geneva in 2021. Deterrence policy concerns weapons and signaling; it differs from military action, and the existence of arsenals does not by itself show an intention to use them.

The arms-control architecture inherited from the Cold War and its aftermath relied on negotiated definitions, inspections, limits and channels for resolving disputes. When an agreement became contested, the legal status of allegations mattered. The United States accused Russia of developing and deploying a missile system prohibited by the Intermediate-Range Nuclear Forces Treaty; Moscow rejected the accusation and made counterclaims concerning U.S. missile-defense launchers. The U.S. suspension of obligations and announcement of withdrawal in February 2019 preceded the final withdrawal and termination in August. This sequence must be stated precisely.

The Open Skies Treaty permitted specified unarmed observation flights intended to increase transparency and trust. Disagreements about restrictions on routes and reciprocal obligations became part of its collapse. The United States withdrew in 2020, and Russia completed withdrawal in 2021. These measures reduced one means by which states could observe military activity and potentially correct misunderstandings. That does not demonstrate a specific plan for aggression, but it altered the institutional environment in which military forces operated.

New START remained important because it imposed measurable limits and verification procedures on strategic nuclear forces. Its extension in February 2021 for five years showed that even adversarial governments could preserve an agreement when both judged the constraints beneficial. The existence of modernization programs and new weapons did not automatically breach the treaty; compliance depended on definitions, declared capabilities and inspection results. Policymakers debated whether arms control should cover additional systems, missile defenses or other nuclear states, but those questions were not settled in 2021.

Russian presentations of advanced weapons in 2018 mixed strategic doctrine, technical development and political messaging. Students should distinguish systems actually deployed from prototypes, planned programs and promotional imagery. Deterrence depends partly on the adversary's beliefs about capabilities and political will, which makes public claims themselves historically relevant even when technical performance is uncertain. At the end of 2021, arms control had become narrower, but it had not disappeared, and the risk of misunderstanding remained a central theme of European security.

The withdrawal process under a treaty is itself a historical event. Suspension of obligations, notification of intent and the eventual legal end of the agreement may fall on different dates. Treating February and August 2019 as interchangeable obscures the chance for last-minute diplomacy and the procedural mechanics of arms control.

Verification institutions are valuable precisely because they allow parties with little trust to check some claims. When an observation or inspection regime ends, governments face greater reliance on intelligence estimates. This creates new uncertainty even if neither side immediately increases its deployed arsenal.

Section 70.38

China, Eurasian Integration, and Economic Realignment

The Eurasian Economic Union, based on a 2014 treaty among Russia, Belarus, and Kazakhstan, began on 1 January 2015; Armenia and Kyrgyzstan joined in 2015. Moscow had hoped to include Ukraine, and the rival Association Agreement was part of the background to the Ukrainian crisis. Members valued market access but resisted political integration. Trade among them did not grow as rapidly as Russian leaders had hoped.

After 2014 Russia turned more toward China. A 2014 gas agreement led to the Power of Siberia pipeline, opened in December 2019, and Russian-Chinese trade rose to about $110 billion in 2019. Both states belonged to the Shanghai Cooperation Organisation. China's economy was far larger, and the partnership was asymmetrical: Beijing, which was cautious about sanctions risks, pursued its own Belt and Road projects in Central Asia, where governments such as Kazakhstan sought to balance among partners.

The Eurasian Economic Union began operating in 2015 as a framework for customs, regulation and market integration among Russia, Belarus and Kazakhstan, with Armenia and Kyrgyzstan subsequently joining. Membership offered access to a larger market and simplified some cross-border trade, but partners also worried about dependence on the largest economy. Rules concerning tariffs, agricultural products, migration and technical standards required negotiation; political integration was not the same thing as economic coordination. Each member state pursued its own domestic interests and maintained external commercial relationships.

China became a more important market and diplomatic partner for Russia after 2014, especially as Western financing and technology became less accessible in particular sectors. The Power of Siberia pipeline began gas deliveries in late 2019, reflecting years of negotiation and enormous infrastructure costs. Oil, gas, agricultural products and machinery formed different parts of the relationship. Bilateral trade around $110.8 billion in 2019 was significant, but a headline total does not show which country held greater bargaining leverage or how prices and supply commitments were distributed.

Eurasian partner states pursued bargaining strategies of their own. A Kazakh or Armenian government could value access to Russia's market while resisting restrictions on political independence. The organization should therefore be evaluated by examining trade rules and disputes among members rather than assuming that every Russian proposal became common policy.

Section 70.39

Russia in the Middle East, Africa, and the Arctic

Beyond Syria, Russia expanded arms sales and diplomacy in the Middle East, including a contract to supply Turkey with S-400 air-defense systems, delivered in 2019, and ties with Egypt and the Gulf states. In Africa it held its first Russia–Africa summit in Sochi in 2019 and signed security and mining agreements. UN experts and journalists reported that Wagner, a private military company linked to a businessman close to Putin, operated in Libya, the Central African Republic, and elsewhere; the Russian government denied official ties, and private military companies were formally illegal in Russia.

In the Arctic, Russia revived Soviet-era bases, expanded the Northern Sea Route, and developed energy projects, notably the Yamal LNG plant, opened in 2017 with foreign partners. Russia presented a claim to extended continental shelf to a UN commission and held the Arctic Council chair from 2021. These activities reflected both economic aims and a wish to restore a global role, and they were limited by sanctions and finances.

Russia's relations beyond its immediate neighborhood included arms sales, energy projects, military training, diplomatic partnerships and participation in multilateral forums. The delivery of S-400 systems to Turkey illustrated the complexity of international alignments: Ankara remained a NATO member while purchasing Russian equipment, producing disagreement with the United States. Relations with Gulf governments also combined energy coordination and diplomatic contact despite differences over regional conflicts. The lesson is that strategic relationships were issue-specific rather than permanent blocs.

The 2019 Russia-Africa summit in Sochi displayed Moscow's ambition to increase trade and influence on the continent, but African states pursued their own goals. Some governments sought arms, training or diplomatic support; others sought investment, mining agreements or international recognition. Russian and foreign reporting identified private military actors in several countries, including the Central African Republic and Libya. Attributing particular acts requires distinguishing reported contractor deployment, business ownership, state authorization and operational direction. The term Wagner should not be used as shorthand for every Russian citizen participating in an overseas conflict.

In the Arctic, new infrastructure and maritime routes attracted investment as warming temperatures and technological improvements changed access. Russia expanded port facilities and icebreaker capabilities and modernized military installations. Economic arguments centered on liquefied natural gas, shipping and mineral resources, while environmental concerns included fragile ecosystems, industrial accidents and the consequences of climate change for local communities. Indigenous peoples and regional residents had interests that differed from those of state energy corporations, an important corrective to maps that portray the Arctic as empty strategic space.

The Northern Sea Route illustrates the difference between potential and immediate reality. Shorter distances on a map do not guarantee economical year-round navigation: ice conditions, insurance, specialized vessels, port availability and geopolitical risk shape commercial use. International legal disputes concerning maritime rights and continental shelves require precise reference to treaties and administrative procedures. Russia's global role during this period grew in visibility, but the material capacity to sustain initiatives varied greatly among regions and sectors. Diplomatic ceremony and long-term commercial success should not be treated as equivalent measures.

The legality and structure of private military activities require scrutiny. Reports that contractors operated abroad do not alone establish which Russian officials controlled every action, but repeated financial, transport and military connections can substantiate broader patterns. Names, years and particular missions should be recorded whenever possible.

Arctic climate change created both new shipping ambitions and severe risks to infrastructure and ecosystems. Russian economic development claims should be assessed alongside the lives of local populations and the long-term cost of environmental damage. An account focused exclusively on maps of military bases would miss much of the region's social history.

Section 70.40

Belarus, the South Caucasus, and Regional Security in 2020–2021

After the disputed Belarusian presidential election of 9 August 2020, in which Aleksandr Lukashenko was officially declared the winner with about 80 percent, mass protests took place and were met by arrests and violence documented by human-rights monitors. The European Union and United States did not recognize the result. Russia supported Lukashenko with a loan and political backing, and spoke of a reserve force. Belarusian citizens were agents of their own protest movement, whose leaders were arrested or fled.

In the South Caucasus, a war over Nagorno-Karabakh between Azerbaijan, supported by Turkey, and Armenia lasted from 27 September to 9 November 2020. Azerbaijan regained territory and the town of Shusha. A ceasefire statement signed by the Armenian and Azerbaijani leaders and by Putin deployed about 2,000 Russian peacekeepers to the region. The war killed thousands. Russia kept its base in Armenia but did not intervene militarily, and the settlement increased Turkey's role and left many issues unresolved.

Belarus's 2020 presidential election triggered a profound domestic crisis after authorities reported approximately eighty percent support for Aleksandr Lukashenko. Many Belarusians rejected the reported outcome and organized demonstrations, strikes and neighborhood actions. Women, factory workers, students and journalists played prominent roles, and participation extended beyond a single opposition organization. The government used arrests, beatings and prosecution against protesters, as documented by human-rights monitors. Explaining Moscow's policy without first explaining Belarusian public mobilization would confuse the state's external relations with the political agency of its neighbor's citizens.

Russia's leadership backed Lukashenko politically and offered financial assistance, while expressing concern about outside intervention and instability on its western border. The relationship also involved bargaining: Belarus depended on Russian energy and markets but its leaders had periodically resisted proposals perceived as diminishing sovereignty. The crisis strengthened Lukashenko's reliance on Moscow without eliminating disagreements over integration. The extent of that dependence should be assessed against economic and diplomatic evidence rather than from statements alone.

Internet Sovereignty, Media Restrictions, and the Digital Public Sphere

By the late 2010s a large majority of Russians used the internet, and platforms such as YouTube, Telegram, and the domestic network VKontakte became major sources of information alongside state-aligned television. Regulation expanded in steps: a 2012 blocklist run by the regulator Roskomnadzor, a 2014 law on popular bloggers, 2015 rules requiring personal data of Russian users to be stored in Russia, and the 2016 “Yarovaya” laws on data retention. An attempt to block the messaging service Telegram in 2018 largely failed and was dropped in 2020.

A law signed on 1 May 2019 and effective from November, the “sovereign internet” law, required providers to install equipment allowing authorities to filter traffic and to route it through state-supervised exchange points, and created a national domain-name system. Officials presented it as protection against external disconnection; critics saw a tool for censorship and surveillance. Independent outlets, some of which were labeled “foreign agents,” and opposition figures used the internet for reporting and mobilization, including Navalny's investigative films, which drew tens of millions of views.

The expansion of internet use created a layered public sphere rather than replacing television. Government-aligned broadcasters continued to reach large audiences, while Telegram channels, YouTube videos, local news sites and social networks circulated competing accounts of political events. Internet use varied by age, income, location and technical access. People could consult several media sources without trusting them equally, and a high number of views did not automatically indicate support for a commentator's political conclusions.

The regulatory framework evolved through several legal instruments. The 2012 blocking system, later registration requirements for popular bloggers, data-localization rules and the Yarovaya legislation imposed different obligations. The 2019 sovereign internet law concerned infrastructure and routing as well as content moderation. The government's declared objective was resilience against disruption and compliance with national law; critics emphasized the power to monitor and restrict communications. It is important to differentiate the design of a law from whether the technical systems it prescribed worked as intended at a given date.

Attempts to block Telegram in 2018 illustrated implementation difficulties in a networked environment. Users employed workarounds, technical interventions affected unrelated services and authorities ultimately changed their approach. This episode demonstrates that state regulation was neither powerless nor mechanically effective. Other restrictions affected providers, advertisers and application stores indirectly, shaping access even without an individual website being permanently blocked. The specific mechanisms explain how political communication became less predictable for independent publishers.

The internet was also a site of commercial opportunity, entertainment, education and ordinary social interaction. Most use was not electoral activism, yet digital tools could become political during an emergency or controversy. Investigative videos concerning corruption and public services reached audiences outside conventional opposition organizations, sometimes prompting local action. Students should avoid both technological determinism—the idea that platforms automatically produce democracy—and the assumption that internet regulation eliminated all independent communication. The period is best understood as a continuing contest over attention, infrastructure and rules of access.

A central technical distinction is between blocking a particular site and creating infrastructure for broader traffic control. The 2019 legislation addressed network architecture, making it an institutional change even when ordinary users could not immediately observe the effects. The enforcement of content laws and the functioning of network-control equipment are separate empirical questions.

The experience of Telegram demonstrates that technical resistance can coexist with regulatory vulnerability. Efforts to block communications sometimes disrupted unrelated services, while changes in law and pressure on businesses still influenced platform behavior. A comprehensive account should avoid both the claim that control became perfect and the claim that technological circumvention made regulation meaningless.

Civil Society, Independent Journalism, and the Risks of Public Dissent

Boris Nemtsov, a former deputy prime minister and opposition leader, was shot dead on a bridge near the Kremlin on 27 February 2015. A Russian court in 2017 convicted five men from Chechnya, including the man identified as the shooter, but those who ordered the killing were not identified, and Nemtsov's family and associates criticized the investigation. Journalists and activists also faced risk: in 2019 the arrest of reporter Ivan Golunov on drug charges that were then dropped after public protest showed both pressure and limits.

Memorial, founded in 1989 to document Soviet repression, ran archives, a database of victims, and a human-rights center that monitored political prisoners. Its units were designated “foreign agents” in 2014 and 2016. In late December 2021, the Supreme Court ordered the liquidation of International Memorial, and a Moscow court ordered the closure of the Memorial Human Rights Center. Prosecutors cited violations of foreign-agent labeling rules and described the group's work as distorting history; supporters described the case as political.

Boris Nemtsov's murder shocked opposition activists because he was a prominent former government official and critic of the Kremlin killed close to its central institutions. Investigators secured convictions against five men in 2017, but questions about who organized the crime remained unresolved. That distinction matters: a conviction for participation in murder does not necessarily identify all those who commissioned or financed it. His family, colleagues and rights advocates challenged aspects of the investigation, while state authorities pointed to the convictions as evidence that perpetrators had been held accountable.

The investigation also affected the broader climate of public speech. Journalists and activists assessed risks when publishing sensitive material, and some people reduced public activity while others became more committed. The case cannot be used to infer automatically that every threat followed the same command structure. Historical analysis requires specific evidence of persons involved, institutional failures, responses by police and prosecutors, and the consequences for public organizing.

Memorial's activities connected the history of Soviet repression with contemporary human-rights defense. Its archives, memorial projects and databases preserved evidence about individuals imprisoned or killed by Soviet authorities, while associated groups documented current political prisoners and abuses. The organization's significance was therefore intellectual, civic and institutional. Critics of Memorial's work could disagree with historical interpretations, but the government's legal action should be analyzed through the actual claims advanced in court rather than through vague references to a national argument about memory.

In December 2021 courts ordered the liquidation of International Memorial and the Memorial Human Rights Center in separate proceedings. Prosecutors emphasized foreign-agent labeling rules and, in argument concerning historical memory, allegations about the organization's presentation of the Soviet past. Supporters described the judgments as a severe restriction on historical research and civic freedom. The orders were issued near the chapter's endpoint, making them important evidence of the changing relationship among legal institutions, historical interpretation and independent civil society.

Memorial's preservation of victims' names and archival records gave it a significance extending beyond contemporary opposition politics. Its work connected family memory, Soviet history, legal accountability and public education. The 2021 court orders therefore affected a research institution and a human-rights institution, which were related but not identical in legal status.

The unresolved question of who ordered Nemtsov's murder is distinct from the fact that five men were convicted. Similarly, allegations concerning why a particular prosecutor or judge acted cannot be inferred from the mere existence of a conviction. Source-supported attribution is especially important when discussing politically charged crimes.

Section 70.43

Opposition Mobilization and the Protest Movements of 2017–2019

Navalny's Anti-Corruption Foundation published investigations of officials' wealth, including a 2017 film on Prime Minister Medvedev. On 26 March 2017 protests were held in dozens of cities, with more than a thousand detained in Moscow, and Navalny was jailed for fifteen days. He was barred from the 2018 presidential race. In September 2018 pension protests, local discontent and opposition calls combined, though participants had varied aims.

In summer 2019 authorities refused to register many independent candidates for the Moscow city council, and mass demonstrations in July and August led to about a thousand detentions and several criminal prosecutions. In the September vote, United Russia lost seats, helped by the opposition's “Smart Voting” tactic. Other protests were local, as over garbage disposal in northern regions or a church construction in Yekaterinburg, and were not part of a single national movement.

The 2017 anti-corruption protests spread through cities of very different sizes, aided by the circulation of an investigative film concerning the prime minister's alleged wealth. Demonstrators included young people who had not taken part in the 2011–2012 movement. Some were motivated by official misconduct, others by broader dissatisfaction with restricted elections or poor public services. Authorities disputed key allegations and treated many demonstrations as violating public-assembly rules. Arrest records and participant interviews provide evidence of the scale and social composition of the movement, but the same events should not be described as having one universally shared objective.

The 2018 pension controversy joined socioeconomic grievances with political organization. Opposition parties and nonpartisan groups criticized the announced retirement-age increases, while officials defended the measure as a demographic necessity. Protests were not solely a product of Navalny's organizing and included constituencies whose views on foreign policy or national leadership differed sharply. This diversity is essential for understanding why public disagreement sometimes affected government decisions without becoming a coherent national opposition movement.

In Moscow during 2019, denied candidate registrations sparked repeated demonstrations. Many protesters focused on the practical right to stand for local office rather than an immediate change of national leadership. The municipal election disputes illustrated how signature verification, election administration and police practices could become politically explosive. Authorities defended some disqualifications as enforcement of legal requirements; rejected candidates and independent monitors alleged inconsistent or politically motivated procedures. The historian should examine particular registration decisions and distinguish authorized gatherings from those dispersed under assembly rules.

Outside the capital, protests over waste disposal, development projects, local elections and environmental protection did not necessarily align with national opposition organizations. Their significance lies partly in demonstrating that political participation could emerge around a concrete material concern without a unified ideological platform. Some campaigns achieved concessions, while others encountered prosecution or administrative obstacles. Comparing the outcomes can help identify the conditions under which authorities bargained, ignored demands or used coercive power.

The protest movements' diversity was an important political fact. A citizen opposing a landfill, an elderly worker protesting a pension change and a young activist criticizing election registration might share dissatisfaction without sharing a national program. Their organizational networks could overlap during particular events, but their objectives remained distinct.

The 2019 Moscow disputes also show why municipal electoral procedures deserve attention. Signature verification and access to local office may seem technical until they decide whether a community can choose among candidates. Studying the actual rules and their application reveals mechanisms of political competition more clearly than broad statements about authoritarianism.

Navalny's Poisoning, Return, Arrest, and the Protests of 2020–2021

On 20 August 2020, Navalny fell ill on a flight from Tomsk and was treated in Omsk before being flown to the Charité hospital in Berlin on 22 August. German authorities said on 2 September that laboratory tests showed a nerve agent of the Novichok group, and the OPCW confirmed this in October. Russian officials denied involvement and disputed the diagnosis. Investigative journalists, including Bellingcat and The Insider, identified a team of Federal Security Service personnel who had followed him; the Russian government rejected their findings.

Navalny returned to Moscow on 17 January 2021 and was detained at the airport. A film alleging a luxurious palace for Putin gained over 100 million views, and nationwide protests on 23 and 31 January led to thousands of detentions. On 2 February a court replaced his suspended sentence in the Yves Rocher case with imprisonment of about two years and eight months. In June 2021 a Moscow court declared his organizations extremist, and associates left the country or were prosecuted. The poisoning is established by forensic findings; responsibility remains a matter of attribution and Russian denial.

Navalny's sudden illness in August 2020 first became a medical emergency and then a major political and diplomatic dispute. He was treated in Omsk before being transferred to Berlin. German laboratories reported evidence of a Novichok-group nerve agent, and the OPCW's October findings confirmed a related cholinesterase inhibitor in biomedical samples. These forensic findings substantiate exposure to the agent but do not by themselves establish who administered it. That latter question requires investigative evidence of persons, access, motive and organizational direction.

Section 70.45

The COVID-19 Pandemic, Public Health, and Social Disruption

Russia reported its first COVID-19 cases in January 2020. Authorities declared a national non-working period from 30 March to 11 May, postponed the Victory Day parade and the constitutional vote, and then largely left restrictions to regional governors. Moscow and some regions imposed strict rules, others did not. Health systems, already uneven, were stretched, and medical workers faced shortages.

Russia approved the Sputnik V vaccine in August 2020, before large-scale trials were complete; later published data supported high efficacy. Vaccination began in December 2020, but uptake was slow due to public mistrust, with approximately 45.8 percent of the population fully vaccinated by 31 December 2021 in one historical series. The government's official COVID death count was far below Rosstat's broader mortality data, which, together with independent analyses of excess deaths, indicated much higher mortality, an estimated 1,029,181 excess deaths during 2020–2021 in one peer-reviewed demographic model; this is not a certified COVID-19 death count. Methods differ, and schooling, small businesses, and regional economies were disrupted unevenly.

Russia's pandemic response combined national directives with considerable regional variation. Health policy was therefore also an economic and federalism question.

The vaccine program became a matter of both scientific assessment and public trust. Russia approved Sputnik V in August 2020 before completion of large-scale clinical trials, drawing criticism about the timing of authorization; later published trial results supported substantial efficacy. Availability, distribution, government messaging and skepticism varied by region. By December 31, 2021, approximately forty-six percent of the population was fully vaccinated according to historical series, not an unequivocal majority. International comparisons require consistent definitions of full vaccination and comparable population denominators.

Everyday Life and Social History

School, Work, and Health During COVID-19

Remote schooling required equipment and a reliable internet connection; small retailers faced uncertain opening rules; hospital workers encountered shortages and long shifts. Regional governments imposed different restrictions, so the pandemic produced overlapping but not uniform experiences. Compare urban service workers with remote-region households.

Evidence to seek: dated interviews, local newspapers, public-service statistics, household budget studies and regional government records.

Officially recorded COVID-19 deaths and excess mortality answer different questions. The former depend on diagnostic and reporting rules; the latter compare observed deaths with an expected baseline constructed from past trends and demographic assumptions. An estimate on the order of one million excess deaths across 2020–2021 has been published in demographic research, but it is model-dependent and not identical to a verified count of deaths caused exclusively by the virus. Researchers should disclose the baseline and statistical uncertainty when presenting such comparisons.

The pandemic affected schooling, health access, social interaction and work. Distance education exposed inequalities in internet connections and household equipment; service workers and small businesses faced interrupted income, while some employees could work remotely. Migrants experienced border and permit disruptions. Elections and constitutional voting proceeded under special arrangements that authorities said were designed partly for safety and that monitors criticized for limiting oversight. The pandemic thus interacted with pre-existing institutions instead of being an isolated public-health episode.

Division IX — Putin's Fourth Term, Constitutional Reform, and Political Continuity

Section 70.46

The Presidential Election of 2018 and the Fourth Putin Presidency

The presidential election of 18 March 2018 was held on the fourth anniversary of the annexation of Crimea. Official results gave Putin about 77 percent on a turnout of about 68 percent; the Communist candidate Pavel Grudinin received about 12 percent, Vladimir Zhirinovsky about 6 percent, and Ksenia Sobchak, Grigory Yavlinsky and others smaller shares. Alexei Navalny was barred because of a criminal conviction that the European Court of Human Rights had found unfair. International observers from the OSCE assessed that the vote lacked genuine competition, though candidates could campaign, and Russian monitors such as Golos reported irregularities.

Putin's fourth term began on 7 May 2018, and Medvedev was reappointed prime minister the next day. A presidential decree set national goals, including raising Russia into the top five economies, reducing poverty, and increasing life expectancy. Earlier, in a March address, Putin had presented new strategic weapons. Analysts differ on whether the high official vote reflected genuine support, administrative mobilization, or both; evidence suggests both elements.

The March 2018 election occurred in an environment shaped by Crimea, the weakening of alternative political organizations, national television and official claims of economic and international recovery. Putin's reported large majority was significant as a political fact, but it did not independently establish that all campaigning was equal or free of administrative pressure. The OSCE's assessment of limited genuine competition should be considered alongside actual voting patterns and polling. These lines of evidence address different questions: legal access, visibility, formal counting and the preferences of voters.

Navalny's exclusion from the ballot followed the Kirovles conviction and subsequent legal proceedings; a full account should identify the distinction between domestic eligibility rulings and the European Court of Human Rights' earlier criticism of the trial. Other candidates varied in political program and ability to reach voters. Some ran as representatives of parliamentary parties, while others had comparatively small independent organizations. Treating the election as a contest between only Putin and a unified 'opposition' would erase this range of positions.

The inauguration of the fourth term preceded a renewed national-project agenda with goals for health, infrastructure, education and demography. Officials argued that large coordinated investments could improve public services and economic competitiveness. Critics questioned financing, implementation, and the incentives for local administrations to report achievement. The later pension reform provides a test of the leadership's ability to combine large social promises with measures perceived by many voters as reductions in benefits.

Public priorities also changed between 2018 and 2021. Stagnant living standards, currency and price concerns, demographic decline and the pandemic complicated the political appeal of international prestige. Polling results are informative but need attention to question wording, response environment and sampling. A comprehensive treatment of the fourth presidency should therefore not make the election itself the endpoint: it was the beginning of a new term whose institutional significance culminated in the 2020 constitutional amendments and 2021 parliamentary election.

A political election should be studied through several indicators: the official result, ability to register candidates, public-media access, independent monitoring and expressed voter preferences. A landslide can coexist with real popularity and an unequal field. No single indicator resolves all the questions.

The national-project agenda raised a separate problem of implementation. Ministries could announce spending and governors could report completed facilities, but students should ask whether services improved and whether the results endured. The pension reform later in 2018 showed that economic decisions could reshape public support despite a strong electoral mandate.

Section 70.47

The Constitutional Amendments of 2020

On 15 January 2020 Putin proposed constitutional amendments in his annual address. The Medvedev cabinet resigned the same day, Medvedev moved to a Security Council post, and Mikhail Mishustin, head of the Federal Tax Service, became prime minister on 16 January. A working group drafted more than 200 amendments, covering social guarantees, a reference to God and to marriage as a union of man and woman, the status of Russian as the language of the “state-forming” people, and the priority of Russian constitutional law over decisions of international bodies.

On 10 March a deputy proposed resetting presidential term counts; Putin agreed on condition that the Constitutional Court approve, and it did on 16 March. A vote planned for April was postponed because of the pandemic and held over several days from 25 June to 1 July, with online voting in some regions. Officially it was called a “nationwide vote,” not a referendum, since the law governing referendums did not apply, and it had few of the legal safeguards of one. The Central Election Commission reported 77.92 percent approval and approximately 67.97 percent turnout; these are official figures, not an independent verification of the ballot. Independent monitors reported pressure on voters and irregularities, and statistical studies suggested inflated numbers; the extent is debated.

The January 2020 constitutional proposals initially emphasized institutions and social guarantees, including parliament's role in government formation and the status of the State Council. Medvedev's cabinet resigned, Mikhail Mishustin became prime minister and a working group drafted amendments that reached far beyond the original public announcement. Some provisions concerned wages, pensions, cultural identity, marriage, language and the relationship between Russian constitutional authority and international adjudication. The package was therefore heterogeneous; voting for or against it could reflect preferences on several unrelated subjects.

The proposal to reset the counting of presidential terms changed the political stakes. Although the amended constitution described a two-term limit, a transitional provision excluded terms served before its entry into force for the incumbent and certain past officeholders. This made future Putin candidacies constitutionally possible without requiring him to run in 2020. The Constitutional Court approved the amendments through a special review process, and critics argued that this undermined the purpose of the term-limit rule. Supporters invoked stability and the need for continuity during national and international uncertainty.

Multi-day collection, voting outside polling stations and electronic procedures required safeguards different from those used in ordinary one-day elections. Official claims about safety and participation must be compared with independent monitors' accounts of access and ballot security.

Section 70.48

Presidential Term Limits, State Institutions, and the Constitutional Settlement

The central change concerned presidential terms: the text limited a president to two terms but allowed the count to restart for current and previous office-holders, making it possible for Putin to run again in 2024 and 2030. Other provisions let the president nominate senators, remove judges of the highest courts in consultation with the Federation Council, reduced the Constitutional Court from 19 to 11 judges, gave constitutional status to the State Council, and placed local self-government within a unified system of public power. Candidates were required to have lived in Russia for 25 years.

Officials defended the amendments as ensuring stability, social protection, and sovereignty. Critics, among them opposition figures and many legal scholars, argued that they weakened checks on presidential power and the independence of courts. The Venice Commission of the Council of Europe, in March 2021, criticized the way the amendments were adopted and the concentration of power, and warned about giving national constitutional rulings precedence over international court judgments; it also noted social provisions that raised no concerns. The formal constitutional order remained in place, but its balance of powers shifted.

Section 70.49

The 2021 State Duma Elections and the Political System at the End of the Period

Elections to the State Duma took place on 17–19 September 2021 under a mixed system, with half the seats by party lists and half in single-member districts. Officially United Russia won about 50 percent of the list vote and 324 of 450 seats, the Communist Party about 19 percent (57 seats), and LDPR, A Just Russia, and the new party New People smaller shares. Turnout was about 52 percent.

The campaign took place under restrictions. A June 2021 law barred people associated with “extremist” organizations from running, which affected supporters of Navalny; Apple and Google removed his “Smart Voting” app under pressure; several media outlets and the monitoring group Golos were labeled foreign agents; and the OSCE did not send an observation mission after Russia limited its size. Electronic voting in Moscow delayed the count and reversed the results in some districts, and the Communists protested. Officials called the election legitimate. By the end of 2021, United Russia retained a large majority and the presidency's institutional position had been reinforced.

The 2021 Duma election used a combination of national party lists and single-member districts, allowing the dominant party to turn organizational advantages into a larger share of seats than its party-list vote alone might suggest. United Russia's officially declared 324 seats constituted a supermajority. The Communist Party remained the largest parliamentary opposition group, while LDPR, A Just Russia and New People won representation. These categories should not imply that every deputy in an opposition-branded party behaved identically on every vote; institutional incentives and policy positions varied.

The campaign was marked by restrictions on people connected to groups designated extremist, including organizations associated with Navalny. Independent media and election monitors faced foreign-agent designations and legal pressure, while applicants to the ballot encountered registration procedures. The OSCE's election bodies announced that Russian authorities' numerical limits on monitors prevented credible observation, so they did not send a mission. This absence is not proof of the exact number of altered votes; it is evidence of an important limit on independent assessment of election administration.

Division X — Historical Assessment and the Russia of 2021

Section 70.50

Russia's Domestic Transformation, 2012–2021: An Institutional Assessment

By 2021 legal pressure on opposition, media, and NGOs had grown, and constitutional change had strengthened presidential continuity. Elections, courts, and markets continued to operate, and everyday life varied widely.

A comparison between 2012 and 2021 reveals continuity in the constitutional prominence of the presidency, the importance of large state-linked firms and the influence of security agencies. It also reveals consequential change. Legal categories governing civic organizations expanded, protest participation became riskier in many settings, and the constitutional amendments created new options for continued presidential tenure. The political system did not cease to possess courts, elections or parliamentary parties; rather, the terms under which they operated changed. A historical assessment must make those institutional mechanisms visible instead of using a general label as a substitute for analysis.

Economic conditions complicated the narrative of state strength. Governments maintained substantial financial reserves and the ability to direct resources toward major infrastructure and strategic projects, but productivity growth remained weak and real incomes stagnated for many households. Sanctions, lower oil prices, demographic pressures and the pandemic exposed different vulnerabilities. Some sectors benefited from import substitution and public spending, while small firms, migrants and pensioners faced different costs. The coexistence of military capability and economic insecurity is one of the central paradoxes of the period.

Cultural and political identity also changed unevenly. State institutions promoted patriotic commemoration, conservative family rhetoric and a narrative of sovereignty challenged by outside governments. Many citizens accepted some of these ideas; others maintained secular, liberal, socialist, regional or otherwise different perspectives. Online investigations and local activism demonstrated that public disagreement persisted, even as legal restrictions increased. Regions did not experience these processes in identical ways. The histories of Moscow, the North Caucasus, industrial towns, Siberia and Crimea belong in the same national narrative without being flattened into one average experience.

Section 70.51

Russia's International Position at the End of 2021

Relations with Ukraine, NATO, and the U.S. were hostile, ties with China had deepened, and Russia had a role in Syria and the post-Soviet space. A troop buildup near Ukraine and diplomatic confrontation marked late 2021; Chapter 71 covers what followed.

At the end of 2021 Russia remained a major nuclear power, an exporter of energy, a permanent member of the UN Security Council and a central actor in post-Soviet security. Its military intervention in Syria and deployment of peacekeepers in the South Caucasus showed an ability to project influence beyond its borders. Yet cooperation depended on partners with their own objectives, while sanctions and limited economic diversification constrained some forms of investment. China had become increasingly important commercially and diplomatically, but ties with European countries and the global financial system had not disappeared.

Russia's relationship with Ukraine was severely damaged by the annexation of Crimea, the war in the Donbas and the continued presence of Russian-supported armed forces in parts of eastern Ukraine. Ukraine's government and society pursued political and security choices of their own, including strengthened relations with Western partners. Negotiations under the Minsk framework had failed to produce a durable settlement. European governments disagreed over energy, sanctions and the right balance between deterrence and dialogue, while NATO reinforced its eastern members and maintained a formal commitment to respect state sovereignty.

In late 2021 Russian forces assembled in substantial numbers near Ukraine, and Moscow presented security proposals seeking changes in NATO deployments and enlargement policy. Western governments warned of the risk of major military action and pursued negotiations while expressing support for Ukraine's sovereignty. Public intelligence, military movements and diplomacy created an atmosphere of acute tension, but the full meaning of these signals could not be known with certainty by citizens or analysts at the time. A chapter ending on December 31 should record the developments and competing interpretations without narrating later decisions as if they had already occurred.

Chapter Summary

Putin’s return to the presidency in 2012 began a period in which the formal constitutional framework remained recognizable but the conditions of political competition and civic organization changed markedly. A strong presidency, dominant parliamentary party, state-connected industries and centralized administrative networks were already present. After 2012, laws affecting rallies and NGOs, expanded regulation of digital media, legal proceedings against opposition groups and the 2020 constitutional amendments altered the opportunities for political participation and the prospects for presidential turnover. Support for the leadership and dissatisfaction with it coexisted across different populations and regions.

Ukraine’s political crisis developed through decisions taken by Ukrainian citizens and institutions, including the refusal to sign an anticipated agreement with the EU, mass protests, police violence, the parliamentary response to Yanukovych’s departure and a subsequent national election. Russia’s intervention in Crimea in early 2014 and its annexation of the peninsula transformed regional relations. Russia established effective control and incorporated Crimea under its domestic law, but Ukraine and most states continued to recognize it as Ukrainian territory. The legal distinction is indispensable to any accurate account.

The conflict in eastern Ukraine began in 2014 with armed seizures, Ukrainian government operations, local grievances, organized separatist forces and documented Russian military support and involvement. Major military episodes included Ilovaisk and Debaltseve; the destruction of Flight MH17 made the human consequences internationally visible. The Minsk agreements set out security and political measures but did not resolve the conflict, while OSCE and UN monitoring recorded continuing violence and abuses. By 2021 the conflict was protracted rather than settled.

Economic changes interacted with the political confrontation. Falling oil prices, selected international sanctions, exchange-rate adjustment, structural weaknesses and investment constraints produced a recession and then modest growth. Import substitution benefited some agricultural and industrial producers while increasing costs or preserving dependence in other sectors. The pension reform of 2018 demonstrated the political sensitivity of fiscal and demographic policies. Across Russia, the experience of households varied by age, occupation, region and access to public services.

Russian intervention in Syria began in September 2015 and helped change the balance of the Syrian civil war. It strengthened the government of Bashar al-Assad and gave Moscow military access and diplomatic influence, while civilian harm and the lack of a national settlement remained serious consequences. Russia also pursued regional and global relations with China, Iran, Turkey, African states and Arctic partners. At the same time its relations with NATO, the United States and European governments became increasingly strained, although limited diplomatic and arms-control cooperation continued.

Domestically, the expansion of internet access created both new possibilities for independent journalism and new instruments for regulation. Protests arose around corruption, local elections, public services, pension policy and individual political cases. The poisoning and imprisonment of Alexei Navalny, the prosecution of activists and the 2021 court orders against Memorial illustrated changing relationships among state institutions, independent organizations and historical memory. Citizens nonetheless pursued diverse political and civic goals rather than forming a single uniform opposition.

The COVID-19 pandemic disrupted health care, work and education and exposed large regional differences. Official deaths and modeled excess-mortality estimates are distinct measures; the latter indicated an exceptionally severe human cost across 2020–2021. The 2020 constitutional amendments reset presidential-term counting, changed institutional provisions and were approved in an officially reported nationwide vote contested by some independent observers. The 2021 parliamentary election occurred under significant restrictions on opposition and election monitoring.

At the close of 2021 Russia combined substantial military and diplomatic capacity with slower economic dynamism, institutional centralization and increasing tension with Ukraine and NATO. Military deployments and diplomatic demands were visible, but contemporaries could not know with certainty which decisions would follow. Chapter 71 examines the subsequent invasion of Ukraine as a distinct historical event requiring its own chronology and evidence.

Chapter Reflection

The years 2012–2021 were not one continuous crisis. They were a succession of domestic decisions, international confrontations, economic adjustments and social experiences that changed the institutions and expectations inherited by Russia at the start of 2022. The history remains incomplete unless it includes not only the policies of the Russian president, but the independent actions of Ukrainian and Syrian societies, neighboring governments, political opponents, workers, families and civilians affected by war.

Historical thinking means identifying what an actor knew at the time, what an official document actually establishes and how later evidence changes the evaluation of earlier events. That method is especially necessary here. Chapter 71 begins with the subsequent full-scale war in Ukraine; it does not replace the history established in this chapter or make the choices of 2012–2021 inevitable.
