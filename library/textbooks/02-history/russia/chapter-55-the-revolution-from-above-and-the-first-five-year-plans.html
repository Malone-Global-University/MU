The Revolution from Above and the First Five-Year Plans

A History of Russia: From the Eurasian World to the Russian Federation

Chapter Opening

Between 1928 and 1937 the leadership of the Soviet Union attempted to remake a largely agrarian economy into an industrial one within a single decade, and to do so by administrative command rather than by market exchange. The attempt created steel mills, hydroelectric stations, and entire cities. It also created shortages, overcrowded barracks, and a system of labor control whose costs fell unevenly on millions of people.

In the late 1920s, in the southern Urals near the Ural River, there stood a mountain of iron ore with almost nothing built beside it: open steppe, a few settlements, and the dry wind that blows across the plains of what is now the southern part of the Russian Federation, close to the border with Kazakhstan. Beginning in 1929 the Soviet government committed itself to constructing a vast iron and steel complex at this site, which became known as Magnitogorsk, after Magnitnaya Gora, the Magnetic Mountain. Within a few years tens of thousands of people had arrived to build it, living at first in tents, dugouts, and hastily built barracks. In early 1932, according to the standard accounts, the first blast furnace was put into operation. The mill and the city that grew around it became one of the most famous symbols of Soviet industrialization, celebrated in newspapers, poetry, film, and fiction, and later studied by historians as a laboratory for examining what that industrialization actually involved.

This chapter studies the economic and social transformation that Soviet historians called the "revolution from above" and that many later scholars have called the Stalin revolution. Its central question is how the Soviet state, within roughly a decade, converted a NEP economy of small peasant farms, partly private trade, and a state-owned industrial sector into a centrally directed command economy oriented toward heavy industry. The chapter examines the political decision known as the Great Break, the institutions of planning, the financing of investment, the role of foreign technology, the physical construction of new industries and cities, the formation and disciplining of a new industrial working class, the participation of women, the training of technical specialists, and the effect of all these changes on ordinary living standards.

The chapter's central argument is a balanced one. Soviet industrialization produced real increases in the capacity to make coal, steel, electricity, machinery, and weapons, and it created new industrial regions, new skills, and unusual opportunities for social advancement. It did so, however, through an exceptionally coercive and distorted developmental model: consumption was suppressed, housing and sanitation lagged badly, waste and poor quality were endemic, workplaces were dangerous, and the human costs were enormous. The outcome was neither simply an economic miracle nor simply meaningless waste. Students should be prepared to hold both conclusions at once and to examine the evidence that supports each.

The evidence for this period presents particular difficulties. Official Soviet statistics were produced by a state with strong incentives to report success, and they were often calculated in ways that exaggerated growth. Newspapers and films were instruments of mobilization. Memoirs by foreign engineers, by Soviet workers, and by later émigrés each carry the purposes of their authors. Since the opening of Soviet archives after 1991, historians have been able to compare public claims with internal reports, factory records, police summaries, and letters of complaint, and this chapter draws on the resulting scholarship. Even so, much remains uncertain, especially the precise numbers of people who moved, who died, and who gained or lost materially.

The chapter must also keep its geography straight. The Union of Soviet Socialist Republics was a multinational federal state, and industrialization took place across it, not only in the Russian Soviet Federative Socialist Republic. The coal and steel of the Donbas and the Dnieper hydroelectric station lay in the Ukrainian SSR; the Turkestan-Siberia Railway crossed Kazakh lands and Central Asia; the oil of Baku lay in the Transcaucasian republics; and the great new Ural-Kuznetsk combine linked the Urals to western Siberia. Workers came from Russian villages, but also from Ukrainian, Belarusian, Tatar, Kazakh, Jewish, and many other communities, and some, as later chapters will show, came as prisoners or as deported peasants. A history of Soviet industrialization is therefore not only a history of Moscow's planners.

Soviet industrialization was also part of a wider world. The decade began with the Great Depression, which devastated Western economies and made Western machinery, engineers, and credit available on unusual terms. American firms designed Soviet factories, German and British firms sold machine tools, and thousands of foreign specialists worked on Soviet construction sites. At the same time the Soviet leadership justified its tempo by reference to a hostile international environment, and the end of the decade, as Chapters 58 and 59 explain, brought exactly the kind of great-power war that the leadership said it was preparing for. Students should be careful, however, not to read the 1941 invasion back into the decisions of 1928 as if it proved those decisions necessary.

Finally, this chapter has strict boundaries with its neighbors. Chapter 54 explained how Stalin defeated his rivals and the Right; this chapter begins with the policy that his dominance made possible. Chapter 56 owns the grain crisis, collectivization, dekulakization, peasant resistance, and the famines of 1932-1933; this chapter explains only why the countryside mattered to industrial accumulation. Chapter 57 owns the NKVD, the show trials, the Great Terror, and the Gulag; this chapter discusses coercion in the workplace but does not substitute for that analysis. By the chapter's end students should be able to explain how a command economy was built, who built it, who paid for it, and why historians continue to argue about whether it could have been built differently.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why the Great Break of 1928-1929 should be understood as a political choice among alternatives rather than an automatic result of Russian economic backwardness.
  2. Distinguish industrialization in general from the specific Stalinist choice of extremely rapid, heavily coerced industrialization.
  3. Analyze how Gosplan, VSNKh, material balances, physical targets, and administrative bargaining functioned, and why Soviet planning was not a single flawless plan transmitted downward.
  4. Distinguish planned targets, reported output, and estimated actual performance in the First Five-Year Plan.
  5. Trace the mechanisms by which investment was financed, including price control, taxation, restricted consumption, exports, and foreign trade.
  6. Assess the role of foreign technology, engineers, and firms and explain why Soviet industrialization was not autarkic.
  7. Describe the principal heavy-industrial sectors and construction projects, separating their propaganda value from their economic importance.
  8. Evaluate the social effects of mass urbanization, including new industrial cities, housing shortages, and the formation of an industrial working class from recent peasants.
  9. Compare the coercive and mobilizing methods of labor control, including shock work, socialist competition, penalties for absenteeism, and Stakhanovism.
  10. Analyze the experience of women as participants in industrialization and the expansion of technical education and social mobility.
  11. Evaluate competing interpretations of the achievements and costs of Soviet industrialization, recognizing the limits of counterfactual reasoning.
  12. Connect the industrial transformation to the Stalinist state examined in Chapters 56 and 57 and to the wartime mobilization examined in Chapters 59 and 60 without treating later outcomes as inevitable.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did Stalin and his supporters conclude in 1928-1929 that the NEP framework was inadequate, and what alternatives were available?
  2. How did the Soviet planning system actually work in practice, as opposed to how it was presented in official statements?
  3. How was a rapid increase in industrial investment financed in an economy with low incomes and few foreign credits?
  4. What did foreign technology, firms, and engineers contribute, and how does this change the image of Soviet industrialization as a purely domestic effort?
  5. How did millions of villagers become industrial workers, and how did the state try to discipline and train them?
  6. How did industrialization change the lives of women, of young people seeking education, and of the urban population living in new and overcrowded cities?
  7. What did the industrial drive achieve, and what distortions, wastes, and human costs accompanied it?
  8. What kinds of evidence can historians use to evaluate Soviet industrial performance, and what are the limits of each?
  9. Could the USSR have industrialized rapidly with less coercion and social sacrifice, and what can historians responsibly say about that question?

Historical Context

The Soviet economy of the mid-1920s, examined in Chapter 53, was a mixed economy. The state controlled what Lenin had called the commanding heights: large industry, banking, foreign trade, and transport. Small-scale trade, handicraft production, and, above all, agriculture remained largely in private hands, and the great majority of the population still lived in the countryside. According to the census of 1926, only about one in six inhabitants of the USSR lived in towns. Industrial output had recovered, by about 1926-1927, to roughly the level of the last years before the First World War, but recovery had mostly meant putting existing plants back to work after the destruction of the First World War, the Civil War, and War Communism (Chapters 48 and 52). It did not by itself mean the construction of new capacity, and the economy remained far behind Western Europe and the United States in output per person.

This industrial base was the legacy of an earlier period of state-guided industrialization. As Chapter 44 explained, the late imperial state under Sergei Witte had promoted railroads, heavy industry, and foreign investment, and by 1914 the Russian Empire possessed major industrial regions in the Donbas, the Urals, St. Petersburg, Moscow, and Baku. But the empire's industrial development had been regionally concentrated, dependent on foreign capital and expertise, and small relative to its population. The Bolshevik government inherited this uneven base, nationalized it during the Civil War, and then, under the NEP, allowed it to operate partly through commercial accounting while keeping political control.

During the 1920s, Bolshevik leaders and economists argued intensely about how fast to industrialize and who should bear the cost. The NEP economy suffered recurrent imbalances, including the shortage of manufactured goods in the countryside, the problem of unemployment in towns, and the difficulty of financing new factories when peasants could withhold grain from the market. Evgenii Preobrazhensky, a leading figure of the Left Opposition, argued that a socialist state needed a process of "primitive socialist accumulation" in which resources would be extracted from the private sector, including the peasantry, to finance industry. Nikolai Bukharin argued instead for balanced growth that preserved the peasant market and avoided open conflict with the countryside. These debates were connected to the factional struggles examined in Chapter 54, but they were also genuine disagreements about economic development, and many economists, planners, and party officials took positions between the extremes.

The immediate trigger for change was the grain procurement crisis of 1927-1928 and the political defeat of Bukharin, Aleksei Rykov, and Mikhail Tomsky, discussed in Chapter 54. By 1928 Stalin's group held a growing advantage in the party apparatus and had begun to redirect economic policy, but Bukharin, Rykov, Tomsky, and their allies still constituted significant institutional opposition. During 1929 that opposition was progressively defeated, leaving Stalin's group with a decisive political advantage. Internationally, the late 1920s also brought a perceived increase in military risk. The war scare of 1927, the Sino-Soviet conflict over the Chinese Eastern Railway in 1929, and the Japanese seizure of Manchuria in 1931 reinforced a view among Soviet leaders, whether sincerely or instrumentally held, that the USSR was dangerously exposed. Within this setting the First Five-Year Plan, formally begun on 1 October 1928, became the vehicle of a much larger transformation than its drafters had at first imagined.

A note on dates: because the Soviet government adopted the Gregorian calendar in 1918, the dates in this chapter are given in New Style and no Old Style conversion is required. Soviet planners, however, used a variety of fiscal and plan years, and some dates, such as the announcement that the First Plan had been completed in four years, are administrative claims rather than measurements of economic fact.

Section 55.1

The Great Break: From NEP to Forced Industrial Transformation

The phrase "Great Break" (velikii perelom) entered Soviet political language in 1929, most prominently in a Pravda article by Stalin published for the twelfth anniversary of the October Revolution in November of that year, which described the preceding twelve months as a year of decisive change on the industrial and agricultural fronts. The phrase named a real rupture. Between 1928 and 1930 the Soviet leadership abandoned the assumptions that had governed the NEP, namely that the state would negotiate with a private peasant economy through markets, that industrial growth would be paced by what that bargain could finance, and that private trade would continue to play a significant role. In their place came a policy of rapid, state-directed investment in heavy industry, administrative allocation of resources, and the progressive elimination of private commerce.

The break did not occur on a single date or by a single decision. The Fifteenth Party Congress in December 1927 had already directed planners to draft a five-year plan and had spoken of the gradual collectivization of agriculture. The grain procurement difficulties of the winter of 1927-1928, discussed in Chapter 54, led the leadership to authorize emergency measures against grain holders. Over the next two years the defeat of Bukharin and his allies removed the main institutional defenders of the NEP, and the planners' targets, as Section 55.4 shows, were repeatedly raised. By the end of 1929 the economic strategy of the USSR had changed, and it had changed through a sequence of political choices made in response to perceived crises, rather than through the unfolding of a plan that was fully conceived in advance.

This sequence matters because it undercuts a common explanation, namely that Russian backwardness made forced industrialization inevitable. Backwardness, in the sense of low industrial output per person and a predominantly rural population, was a real condition that set problems for any government. But it did not dictate a single solution. Economists and party leaders in the 1920s proposed several different strategies: a slower, balanced program built on the NEP, a faster program financed by pressure on the private sector, and mixtures of the two. Within the Gosplan apparatus itself the first drafts of the Five-Year Plan offered a "baseline" variant and a more ambitious "optimal" variant, and the leadership's choice of the latter, and then its later insistence on raising even those targets, reflected political judgments about tempo, risk, and priorities. The Great Break was therefore a choice, made by identifiable people with identifiable alternatives before them.

It was also a choice with a social base. Many party members, Komsomol activists, young workers, and technical specialists welcomed a program that promised construction, rapid advancement, and the overcoming of what they regarded as a stagnant or compromised NEP. The same period saw a "cultural revolution," discussed in Section 55.15, in which activists denounced older specialists and promoted workers into higher education. For others the break meant risk and hardship, as private traders were driven out of business, peasants faced growing pressure, and wage earners confronted rationing and rising prices. Historians continue to disagree about the relative weight of top-down direction and grassroots enthusiasm in producing the break, a question Section 55.11 returns to.

This chapter treats the Great Break in its industrial dimension. The agricultural side of the transformation, namely the drive to collectivize peasant agriculture and eliminate the so-called kulaks, was inseparable from it in the minds of Soviet leaders and in its effects on the economy, but it was a distinct set of events with its own victims and consequences, and it is the subject of Chapter 56. Here it is enough to note that the industrial and agricultural breaks were two parts of a single political decision, and that students should read this chapter and the next together.

Section 55.2

Why Industrialize So Fast? Security, Ideology, and Economic Development

Almost every Soviet leader agreed that the USSR needed to industrialize. The disagreement concerned speed, method, and cost. To understand why the Stalinist leadership chose an unusually rapid and coercive path, it helps to distinguish four kinds of motive, which reinforced one another but were not identical.

The first was the perceived gap with the advanced industrial powers. Soviet writers constantly compared Soviet output of steel, coal, machinery, and electricity with that of the United States, Germany, and Britain, and the comparison was unfavorable. Stalin gave the argument its most quoted form in a speech of February 1931, examined in this chapter's Primary Source Window, in which he said that the country was fifty to a hundred years behind the advanced countries and had ten years to make up the distance. The exact figures were rhetorical rather than measured, but the underlying claim, that the USSR could not rely on the existing level of industry to protect or develop itself, was widely shared among Bolsheviks. Notice, however, that the claim of backwardness is an argument about ends; it does not by itself determine the means.

The second motive was security. The war scare of 1927, the break in relations with Britain, the conflict with China over the Chinese Eastern Railway in 1929, the Japanese occupation of Manchuria from 1931, and, after January 1933, the Nazi government in Germany all strengthened the case for military-industrial capacity. Military planners such as Mikhail Tukhachevsky pressed for large increases in the production of tanks, aircraft, and artillery, which required steel, machine tools, engines, and chemicals. Historians generally accept that security concerns were genuine and influential, while disagreeing over how much they explain the specific tempo and methods chosen; one cannot infer from the existence of real external dangers that the particular strategy adopted was the only one that could have addressed them.

The third motive was ideological. Marxist-Leninist doctrine assumed that socialism required an advanced industrial base and a large industrial working class, and that the elimination of private capital was a goal in itself. Marx's schemas of reproduction distinguished producer goods (Department I) from consumer goods (Department II), and Soviet planners treated the former, meaning machinery, metals, and energy, as the foundation for expanding all other production. The view that heavy industry should grow faster than consumer industry became a doctrinal commitment, defended as the condition for later abundance. At the same time hostility toward private accumulation, and toward the market relations that the NEP had tolerated, made a state-directed strategy politically attractive for reasons that were not strictly economic.

The fourth was the political dynamic of the leadership itself. A program of rapid transformation gave Stalin and his supporters an issue on which their opponents on the Right appeared timid and their opponents on the Left had already been defeated. Once targets were publicly proclaimed, retreat became costly, and officials at every level had incentives to promise and report more than the previous year's totals. Planners who argued for lower and more realistic targets, including economists associated with the earlier "genetic" approach to planning, which began from observed economic trends, were marginalized, and some were later prosecuted in the trials of 1930 and 1931 on charges of wrecking, a subject that belongs to the political history of Chapter 57.

The intellectual debate of the 1920s provides a frame for these motives, but should not be mistaken for a choice between two neat programs. Preobrazhensky had argued that the state should extract resources from the private economy at prices favorable to industry. Bukharin had argued for sustaining peasant demand and incremental growth. The policy adopted after 1928 resembled neither. It used instruments of administrative compulsion and a tempo that Preobrazhensky himself had not advocated, and it eliminated the market relations on which Bukharin's program depended. Preobrazhensky was expelled from the party in 1927 as an oppositionist, later recanted, and was executed in 1937; Bukharin met the same fate in 1938, as Chapter 57 recounts. Students should therefore distinguish three questions: whether to industrialize, how fast, and by what means. The Stalinist answer to the last two was a choice, and it is on those choices that the historical debate in Section 55.17 turns.

Section 55.3

Gosplan, VSNKh, and the Construction of a Command Economy

A command economy, in the sense used by historians of the USSR, is an economy in which the central state determines what is produced, in what quantities, and often by whom and for whom, through administrative directives rather than through prices determined in markets. The Soviet command economy was not created in a single act, and it was not complete even at its height. It was assembled between 1928 and the mid-1930s out of institutions that already existed, supplemented by new ones, and it always coexisted with informal practices that its designers had not foreseen.

Two institutions were central at the beginning. Gosplan, the State Planning Commission, had been established in 1921 and, under leaders such as Gleb Krzhizhanovsky and, from 1930, Valerian Kuibyshev, produced the annual "control figures" and the multi-year plan drafts. VSNKh, the Supreme Council of the National Economy, had administered state industry since 1917 through a hierarchy of industrial associations and trusts. VSNKh ceased to exist in January 1932, when it was divided into separate People's Commissariats for heavy industry (Narkomtyazhprom), light industry, and the timber industry, so that for the Second Plan period the central industrial authority was a set of branch commissariats rather than a single council. The reorganization, with Ordzhonikidze at the head of Narkomtyazhprom, reflected the leadership's wish for tighter and more specialized control over industrial branches, and it further multiplied commissariats over the following years as industrial branches were subdivided.

The planning process combined physical and financial instruments. Planners set output targets in physical units, such as tons of steel, kilowatt-hours of electricity, or numbers of tractors, and then tried to ensure that the targeted production of one sector could be supplied by the targeted production of others. The method used to check this consistency was the material balance, described in the Vocabulary box below. Investment resources were allocated by the center to priority projects, and prices for most goods were set administratively rather than by supply and demand. Enterprises received plans specifying output, costs, labor, and supplies, and their managers were judged largely by whether they met the output target.

Historical Vocabulary
Material Balance

Definition: A material balance is a planning table that lists, for a given product such as steel or cement, the planned sources of supply (production, stocks, imports) and the planned uses (consumption by other industries, construction, export, reserves), and checks that the two sides match.

Historical context: Soviet planners used material balances increasingly from the late 1920s as the principal technique for coordinating physical output targets. Because they covered only a limited number of key commodities, they could not achieve complete consistency across the entire economy, and many products were left to rough allocation.

Why it matters: The method explains both the power and the weakness of Soviet planning. It allowed the center to direct resources toward priority sectors, but it required enormous information, and the balances could be wrong or quickly outdated. Students should not confuse it with a market price system, in which relative scarcity is signaled automatically.

A key lesson of recent scholarship is that Soviet planning was not one perfect mathematical plan transmitted smoothly downward. Plans were drafted in several variants, revised repeatedly during the year, and bargained over between enterprises, commissariats, regional authorities, and planners. Enterprise managers had incentives to obtain generous input allocations and modest output targets, to hoard scarce materials and skilled labor, and to resort to informal arrangements and brokers to obtain supplies. Planners, for their part, often set demanding targets deliberately in order to elicit maximum effort, and then adjusted them when reality intervened. Bottlenecks in transport, fuel, or a single component could halt a whole production chain. Campaigns of "storming" at the end of planning periods, in which plants worked at frantic pace to meet output figures, produced uneven quality and wasteful scheduling. Moshe Lewin stressed the hurried and improvised character of this planning and the proliferation of administrative intervention, and Alec Nove analyzed its characteristic inefficiencies, though neither argued that the resulting industrial growth was fictitious.

The system also relied on political as well as administrative authority. Party committees at every level supervised enterprises, mobilized workers, and pressed managers, and senior leaders intervened directly in particular projects. It would therefore be a mistake to describe the Soviet economy as purely bureaucratic or as purely political; it was a hybrid in which planning offices, industrial commissariats, party organs, and informal networks all played roles. Section 55.17 returns to the strengths and limits of this arrangement.

Section 55.4

The First Five-Year Plan, 1928–1932

Work on a comprehensive five-year plan began in the mid-1920s, and Gosplan and VSNKh produced several drafts between 1927 and 1929. The plan period began with the 1928/29 economic year, conventionally dated from 1 October 1928, but the final plan was still being formulated in early 1929: Gosplan approved its variants in March 1929, the Sixteenth Party Conference endorsed the plan on 29 April 1929, and the Fifth All-Union Congress of Soviets formally approved it on 28 May 1929. The conventional First Plan period ends in December 1932, after the regime proclaimed early completion. The approved version was the "optimal" variant, which assumed unusually favorable harvests and efficiency gains and set targets noticeably above those of the "baseline" variant. Even before it was formally adopted, the plan had begun to inflate, since local officials, industrial leaders, and party activists competed to propose higher targets.

In 1929 and 1930 the leadership repeatedly revised the goals upward, and "counterplans" proposed by factories and construction sites were encouraged. Slogans such as "the five-year plan in four years" expressed the expectation that the plan could be completed ahead of schedule. At the Sixteenth Party Congress in 1930, speakers called for pig-iron production far above even the optimal variant. These upward revisions were based on enthusiasm and political calculation rather than on a sober assessment of capacity, and they gave planners little room to coordinate: when one sector's target was raised, balanced supply from other sectors was not assured.

The plan's priority was heavy industry and associated infrastructure. Investment was concentrated in coal, iron and steel, machine building, electric power, chemicals, and transport, and in the construction of new plants in the Urals, western Siberia, Ukraine, and elsewhere. Light industry, housing, and urban services received far less. The plan also assumed substantial change in agriculture, which Chapter 56 treats separately; here it is enough to recognize that the plan and the assault on private agriculture were designed to work together.

The result must be assessed carefully, and students should distinguish three things: planned targets, reported output, and estimated actual performance. In January 1933 Stalin announced that the plan's main goals had been achieved in four years and three months. That claim was a political statement. Output of many heavy-industrial products had risen substantially, but fell well short of the plan's revised targets and, in several cases, of the original ones. The historian's conclusion is that the First Plan produced a major but uneven expansion of heavy industry that was both less than the leadership claimed and, at the same time, substantial.

Alongside the successes, the plan years brought severe dislocation: sharp inflation in the unregulated market, falling real wages for many urban workers, supply breakdowns, and, in the countryside, the catastrophe examined in Chapter 56. The leadership responded in 1931 and 1932 by modifying some policies, including a more tolerant attitude toward technical specialists and a decision to moderate the plan's growth targets for the following period, which Section 55.12 takes up.

Section 55.5

Financing the Industrial Drive

Every industrializing economy must find resources to build factories rather than consume goods, and the Soviet state faced this problem with unusual constraints. It could not borrow substantially abroad, since the USSR had repudiated tsarian debts and Western credit was limited; it lacked a large class of private savers; and the one large sector with potential surplus, agriculture, was dominated by smallholders who could respond to low prices by selling less. The financial system devised between 1928 and 1932 solved the problem by giving the state control over prices, wages, trade, and credit, and by channeling resources to priority investment through administrative means.

The central financial mechanism was the control of prices and incomes. The state set wholesale and retail prices for most goods, and it deliberately kept the prices it paid for agricultural products low and the prices it charged for manufactured and processed goods high. The credit and tax reforms of 1930 simplified the tax system and made the turnover tax, a levy on the sale of goods that could be set at different rates for different products, the principal source of budget revenue. Because the tax was applied at the point where goods passed from enterprises to the trade network, it allowed the state to capture much of the difference between low procurement prices and high retail prices and to direct the proceeds toward investment. Profits of state enterprises were a second source.

Consumption was suppressed to make this possible. Wages rose in money terms, but the supply of consumer goods did not keep pace; the government expanded the money supply to pay for construction, producing what economists describe as repressed inflation, in which official prices stay low but shortages, rationing, and illegal market prices appear. Rationing of bread was introduced in some cities in 1928-1929 and was extended in 1930 and 1931 to a wide range of goods. Most scholarly reconstructions conclude that real wages and per capita consumption for many urban workers fell in the early 1930s and recovered only partly by the middle of the decade, although the estimates depend heavily on how prices are measured and on how privileged access to goods is treated.

The countryside mattered to this system because it was the largest source of surplus and of exports. The state's procurement of grain at low fixed prices provided food for the growing towns and goods for export, and the broader assault on private agriculture was in part intended to ensure that this supply would be reliable. How this extraction operated, how peasants resisted it, and what it cost in lives is the subject of Chapter 56. For present purposes the essential point is that agricultural extraction was an economic link in the industrial strategy, and not a separate or incidental policy.

The state also paid for imported machinery through exports and through the sale of assets. Grain, timber, oil, furs, and other raw materials were exported in large quantities, and the Soviet leadership continued to export grain at the beginning of the 1930s even as world prices collapsed during the Great Depression, which meant that more physical goods were needed to pay for the same imports. The government also sold works of art from state museums, including pieces from the Hermitage, to Western buyers, and in 1931 established the Torgsin network of stores that sold scarce goods to citizens in exchange for gold, silver, and foreign currency, drawing hoarded valuables from the population. These measures yielded modest sums in relation to total investment but provided precious hard currency in the years of heaviest machinery imports.

The financing system therefore combined state control of the whole price structure with deliberate restrictions on consumption and with exports and asset sales abroad. It permitted an extraordinary rate of capital investment, which rose to a very large share of national income by the early 1930s, but it also embedded permanent features of the Soviet economy: priority for investment over consumption, administrative rather than market rationing of scarce goods, and chronic shortage.

Section 55.6

Foreign Technology, Engineers, and Soviet Industrialization

Soviet industrialization was not an isolated, self-sufficient achievement. In the early 1930s the USSR purchased a very large share of world exports of machinery and industrial equipment, hired thousands of foreign engineers and skilled workers, and relied on foreign firms to design some of its largest plants. This dependence was widely acknowledged at the time within technical circles, though the public rhetoric of the period emphasized Soviet self-reliance, and it has often been forgotten in later popular memory.

The Great Depression shaped the opportunity. As Western industrial economies contracted after 1929, firms facing collapsing domestic demand were eager for Soviet orders, and engineers who had lost work in the United States, Germany, Britain, and elsewhere sought employment abroad. The USSR used its purchasing agencies, including Amtorg in the United States, to buy machine tools, turbines, rolling mills, and other equipment. It signed technical-assistance agreements under which Western companies provided designs, supervised construction, and trained Soviet staff. In some cases these agreements were with the largest firms in the industry, and they were an important channel for the transfer of industrial method.

Several cases illustrate the pattern. The Detroit architectural firm of Albert Kahn, which had designed Ford's plants, received its first Soviet contract, for the Stalingrad tractor plant, in May 1929, followed by a much broader agreement to consult on Soviet industrial construction; the firm's Soviet-related work is often described as involving a large number of industrial buildings. Ford Motor Company concluded a technical-assistance agreement in May 1929 under which it supplied designs and training for what became the Gorky automobile plant at Nizhny Novgorod, which began production in 1932. The Arthur G. McKee company of Cleveland advised on the design of the Magnitogorsk steel works. An American engineer, Hugh Lincoln Cooper, served as chief consulting engineer on the Dnieper hydroelectric project. General Electric supplied major generators, while Newport News Shipbuilding and Dry Dock supplied the large water turbines; Soviet industry subsequently produced additional equipment. Tractor plants at Stalingrad, Kharkov, and Chelyabinsk were built on the basis of American designs and equipment, although Soviet engineers modified them.

The Soviet side was not simply a recipient. Soviet engineers, many of whom had received their training before 1917 and some of whom had studied abroad, adapted foreign designs to local conditions, copied and reproduced imported models, and sent large numbers of their own specialists to study Western factories. Production methods associated with American mass production, including the assembly line, standardized parts, and the organization of work in large plants, were studied closely and often admired by Soviet planners, who were also interested in earlier Soviet efforts, such as Aleksei Gastev's Central Institute of Labor, to systematize work. The relationship was thus one of exchange and adaptation, even though the terms of exchange were set by the Soviet state's need for equipment and the West's need for orders.

The importation of technology had limits, and the Soviet leadership reduced foreign purchases sharply after 1932 as repayment obligations grew and hard currency ran short. Many imported plants ran far below capacity because of shortages of skilled labor, spare parts, and compatible inputs. Foreign specialists also frequently encountered friction with Soviet officials and with workers, in part because of language and culture, in part because of differences in expectations about discipline and pay. Their numbers fell in the mid-1930s, and during the Terror, examined in Chapter 57, many foreign and Soviet engineers with foreign connections were suspected and some were arrested. The significance of the episode is lasting nonetheless: Soviet heavy industry was built in part on Western designs, equipment, and expertise, and any account of its achievement must include that global context.

Section 55.7

Heavy Industry: Coal, Steel, Machinery, and Electric Power

The core of the First and Second Plans was the expansion of what Soviet planners called Group A: the branches of industry producing means of production. These included fuel and energy, metals, machine building, and chemicals, and they were given strategic preference over Group B, the branches producing consumer goods. The preference was justified by the doctrine that producer goods were the foundation of all further growth, and it was supported by military considerations. It also meant that the population's immediate living standards were deliberately subordinated to the expansion of capacity.

Coal and fuel. Coal was the basic energy source for railways, power stations, and metallurgy. The old Donbas basin in the Ukrainian SSR remained the largest producer, but the plans invested heavily in the Kuznetsk basin (Kuzbass) in western Siberia and, later, in Karaganda in Kazakhstan and in northern fields such as Vorkuta, where forced labor was employed, as Chapter 57 explains. Oil output grew more slowly in the early 1930s, centered on Baku and the North Caucasus, with new regions in the Volga-Urals area developing later. Coal output rose from about 35.5 million tons in 1928 to roughly 64–65 million in 1932 and about 128 million in 1937, though the 1932 total fell short of the plan.

Iron and steel. Metallurgy was the emblematic sector. The pre-1914 industry was concentrated in southern Ukraine, where iron ore from Krivoi Rog was combined with Donbas coking coal, and in the Urals. The plans called for the construction of an additional metallurgical base in the east, built around the Ural-Kuznetsk combine, which was to link Ural iron ore, notably from Magnitogorsk, with Kuznetsk coal over a distance of roughly two thousand kilometers by rail, with each region's trains carrying one raw material to the other and returning with the second. Pig iron output rose from about 3.3 million tons in 1928 to about 6.2 million in 1932 and about 14.5 million in 1937, and steel from roughly 4 million tons to about 5.9 million and then about 17.7 million, but the First Plan's targets were far from being met.

Machine building. The creation of a domestic machine-building industry was central to the goal of reducing dependence on imports. New plants such as Uralmash in Sverdlovsk (heavy machinery), the Kramatorsk heavy machinery works in the Donbas, tractor works at Stalingrad, Kharkov, and Chelyabinsk, and automobile plants at Nizhny Novgorod and Moscow produced equipment that earlier had been purchased abroad, together with an expanding range of machine tools. Soviet machine-tool output grew rapidly from a low base, although quality and variety remained limited.

Electric power. Electrification, associated since 1920 with the GOELRO plan, continued as a national goal. Electricity output rose from about five billion kilowatt-hours in 1928 to roughly 13.4 billion in 1932 and about 36 billion by 1937, with the construction of large regional power stations and the Dnieper station, which began generating in 1932 and was among the largest hydroelectric plants in Europe at the time. Industrial electrification supported the shift from steam-driven to electrically powered machinery.

Chemicals. The chemical industry, which produced fertilizers, explosives, synthetic materials, and industrial inputs, received investment at sites such as Berezniki in the Urals and, in the far north, the apatite mines at Khibiny on the Kola Peninsula. Some chemical development had strategic as well as agricultural purposes.

The interdependence of these sectors created persistent bottlenecks. A steel mill required coking coal, ore, transport, electricity, and refractory materials, and its output was then needed by machine builders, railways, and construction. Shortage in any link disrupted the whole chain. The railway network, in particular, was strained by the demand to move coal, ore, grain, building materials, and people over long distances, and transport shortages became one of the principal reasons why the Second Plan gave particular attention to railways. The result was an economy in which priority sectors could be built quickly while support sectors lagged, and in which managers often responded by building their own subsidiary workshops and supply operations, further reducing efficiency.

Section 55.8

The Great Construction Projects

Soviet industrialization acquired its public face through a series of major construction projects, often called by Soviet writers the "great construction sites of socialism." They served three purposes simultaneously: they added productive capacity, they gave propagandists concrete symbols of progress, and they concentrated scarce labor, materials, and technical talent on priority goals. Historians must separate these functions, because a project's symbolic value could exceed, or differ from, its economic contribution.

Magnitogorsk was the most famous. Construction of the steel complex began in 1929 and the first blast furnace was in operation in early 1932. The plant relied on American design advice, imported equipment, and a large, shifting workforce that included volunteer workers, recruited laborers, Komsomol members, foreign workers, and people deported to special settlements during the campaign against the "kulaks," whose forced labor is part of the story told in Chapter 56. The site experienced severe shortages of housing, food, and materials, accidents, and high turnover, and it reached design capacity later than the plans had foreseen. It nevertheless became one of the largest steelmaking centers of the USSR.

Kuznetsk, in western Siberia, was the eastern partner in the combine. Its steel works and the new city built beside it, renamed Stalinsk in 1932 and later Novokuznetsk, drew on the coal of the Kuzbass. Dneprostroi, the Dnieper hydroelectric station near Zaporizhzhia in the Ukrainian SSR, was designed to supply power to the metallurgical and chemical industries of southern Ukraine. Construction began in the late 1920s and the station was formally opened in 1932. Its dam and generators were among the most visible products of Soviet-American technical cooperation, and the project displaced villages and altered the Dnieper rapids, a landscape long familiar in Ukrainian history and Cossack memory.

Other projects made industrial output visible in consumer-facing form. The tractor works at Stalingrad (opened in 1930), Kharkov (1931), and Chelyabinsk (1933) were intended to supply the mechanized agriculture that the leadership associated with collectivization. The Gorky automobile plant and the reconstructed Moscow plant began to produce trucks and cars in volume. The Turkestan-Siberia Railway (Turksib), completed in 1930, joined Central Asia to the Trans-Siberian line and was celebrated in a well-known documentary film. In the mid-1930s the Moscow Metro and the Moscow-Volga Canal, the latter built in part with prison labor, displayed the prestige side of the program, again as Chapter 57 notes in relation to forced labor.

Writers, filmmakers, and poster artists were mobilized to celebrate these sites. Valentin Kataev's novel Time, Forward! (1932) set a fictional race to break a concrete-pouring record at a Magnitogorsk-like construction site, and the press presented construction as heroic collective effort. These representations were deliberately designed to inspire, and historians must read them as sources for the propaganda of the period, not as neutral descriptions.

Their economic reality was more mixed. Projects were frequently started before designs were complete, were delayed by shortages of cement, steel, and skilled labor, and were completed with considerable waste. Safety standards were low and accidents were common. Some plants operated well below capacity for years. At the same time, the physical result was substantial: a set of large, modern, if unevenly performing, industrial plants located in regions that previously had little heavy industry. The responsible assessment is that the construction campaigns were both genuine achievements of construction and instruments of political mobilization, and that their economic return was real but smaller and more costly than the propaganda suggested.

Section 55.9

New Industrial Cities and Mass Urbanization

The most visible social consequence of Soviet industrialization was urbanization on an extraordinary scale. According to the census of 1926, the urban population of the USSR was 26.3 million, 17.9 percent of the total; by the census of 1939 it had risen to 55.9 million, 32.8 percent. A census taken in January 1937 was suppressed after producing politically unwelcome demographic results and was not published at the time; the 1926 and published 1939 figures are used here as the principal census benchmarks. This growth resulted from migration from villages, from the reclassification of growing settlements as urban, and from natural increase, and in the early 1930s it was driven by the collapse of rural living conditions as well as by the attraction of industrial wages and rations. Historians continue to debate the precise breakdown, but all agree that the pace of urban growth was exceptional by the standards of any society.

The state could not build housing, water systems, transport, and food supply fast enough. Industrial investment went into factories, not into dwellings, and many new workers arrived before any permanent housing existed. In new construction sites, people lived in tents, dugouts, hastily built wooden barracks, and unheated shacks. In older cities such as Moscow, Leningrad, Kharkov, and Sverdlovsk, the influx led to overcrowding in existing apartments, which were subdivided into communal apartments (kommunalki) in which several families shared a kitchen and bathroom. Estimates of average urban living space per person fell during the decade to approximately 4.5 square meters per person by 1939, down from roughly 5.9 in 1926, though such averages conceal wide differences by city and social group.

Many new cities were company towns in the sense that the enterprise provided a large share of local services. Enterprises ran housing, canteens, shops, clinics, and sometimes farms through departments of workers' supply, and a worker's access to goods and housing often depended on employment in a favored enterprise. Because workers' needs were met largely through the enterprise rather than through municipal government, local authorities were weak, and sanitation, water supply, and transport were often poor. Epidemic disease, accidents, and high infant mortality were persistent problems in the early years of the new settlements.

Urban planning became a subject of intense debate. Soviet architects and planners in the late 1920s and early 1930s argued about the nature of the "socialist city," with some advocating dense collective housing and others favoring dispersed settlement that would erase the distinction between city and country. A group of German architects and planners led by Ernst May worked in the USSR between 1930 and 1933 on plans for several new industrial towns, including a plan for Magnitogorsk, though the results were limited by budgets and political shifts. By the mid-1930s, with the adoption of the Moscow master plan of 1935, official taste favored monumental avenues and a more traditional urban form.

The authorities also tried to control urban growth. In December 1932 the government introduced internal passports for urban residents and a system of residence registration (propiska) that restricted who could live in major cities, although the system was imperfectly enforced and many people lived without permits. Passports were not issued to most collective farmers, which tied them to the countryside and reflected the effort to control mobility, a subject treated more fully in Chapters 56 and 57. Magnitogorsk can serve as a case study: from an almost empty site it became a city of well over one hundred thousand people within a few years, with a planned "socialist" residential district on one side and the original barracks and shanty settlement on the other. But it was one case among many, including Kuznetsk, Karaganda, Komsomolsk-on-Amur in the Far East, Kirovsk in the far north, and rapidly expanded older cities in the Donbas and the Urals, each with its own mix of voluntary migrants, recruited workers, and forced labor.

Section 55.10

Creating an Industrial Working Class

The expansion of industry required an enormous increase in the labor force. Statistical categories matter here. In the narrower category of large-industry workers and apprentices, employment rose from about 2.5 million in 1928 to about 5.3 million in 1932; across the economy as a whole, estimates of all wage and salary employees suggest a rise from roughly 11 or 12 million to roughly 22 million over the same years, with further growth to the end of the decade. The unemployment that had troubled the NEP economy, a major concern of the 1920s, disappeared as an official category at the end of 1930 when labor exchanges were closed, and by the early 1930s the chief problem was not a surplus but a shortage of workers, especially skilled ones.

Most of the new recruits were former peasants. Some came voluntarily, drawn by wages, food rations, and the prospect of escaping the deteriorating conditions of the village; others were sent by collective farms under contracts with industrial enterprises or were recruited through organized campaigns. Some had been pushed out by collectivization and dekulakization (Chapter 56), and some were deported people and prisoners assigned to construction projects or industries (Chapter 57). The resulting workforce was heterogeneous: Russians, Ukrainians, Belarusians, Tatars, Jews, Kazakhs, and others, many with limited literacy and little familiarity with machinery or factory discipline. In some regions, language barriers and cultural differences between rural migrants and the existing industrial workforce complicated training and supervision.

Older, skilled workers of the prerevolutionary and NEP periods, the so-called cadre workers, often found themselves working alongside, and sometimes supervising, large numbers of novices. Tensions arose over wages, skill recognition, housing, and status, as the state's rhetoric celebrated workers as the ruling class while the actual workplace was increasingly governed by managers and by production targets. The historian Moshe Lewin described the resulting social environment as a "quicksand society," in which large numbers of people were mobile, unsettled, and only partly integrated into the institutions of the industrial order. The term has been widely used, though some scholars argue that it understates the formation of new communities and identities among workers.

Labor turnover was enormous. In many industries and construction sites, a high proportion of workers left their jobs each year, and in some sectors annual turnover exceeded one hundred percent, meaning that the whole workforce was replaced more than once. Workers moved in search of better housing, food, wages, or conditions, or to escape harsh discipline. Absenteeism, lateness, and poor workmanship were persistent complaints in managerial reports. Managers, short of labor, competed for workers by offering better housing or supplies, and workers learned to exploit this competition.

Out of this turbulent process emerged a new industrial working class that differed from that of the 1920s. It was larger, younger, more recently rural, and more dependent on the state and the enterprise for housing, food, and advancement. It also contained rapid upward mobility for some individuals, who moved into skilled jobs, technical training, or administrative positions, as Section 55.15 shows. The formation of this working class is one of the most important social transformations of the Stalin period, with consequences extending to the wartime and postwar decades examined in later chapters.

Section 55.11

Labor Discipline, Socialist Competition, and Shock Work

The state's second challenge was to control and motivate this workforce. It used a combination of material incentives, moral mobilization, and penal sanctions, and the proportions shifted over time. Historians have debated how far the resulting system rested on enthusiasm and how far on pressure, and the best answer is that both were present, often in the same workplace and sometimes in the same person.

Material incentives were a major instrument. In speeches in June 1931 Stalin criticized what he called wage leveling (uravnilovka) and called for wages to reflect skill and output, and enterprises extended piece rates, progressive piece rates, and bonuses. Wage differentials widened between skilled and unskilled workers, between industries, and between workers and managerial staff. Production norms were set, and then raised, to extract more output for a given wage. Access to housing, scarce goods, and canteens could also reward favored workers.

Moral mobilization took the form of socialist competition and shock work. Beginning in 1929 the party and trade unions promoted competition among workers, brigades, and enterprises to exceed their targets and to complete the plan ahead of time, accompanied by "counterplans" in which workplaces pledged to surpass assigned targets. Workers who exceeded norms were designated shock workers, as the Vocabulary box below explains, and honored through publications, banners, and prizes. Young Communist League members and party activists were encouraged to set examples. For some participants the movement expressed real commitment to the building of socialism, while for others it brought material advantages or was simply a requirement of workplace life.

Historical Vocabulary
Udarnik (Shock Worker)

Definition: An udarnik was a worker, or a member of a "shock brigade" (udarnaia brigada), who consistently exceeded production norms or took on especially demanding tasks and was recognized for doing so.

Historical context: The term had been used in the Civil War period for volunteer labor, but it was revived and widely promoted from 1929, when shock brigades became a standard feature of factories, mines, and construction sites. By the early 1930s a large proportion of industrial workers was nominally classified as shock workers, which suggests that the label was in part an administrative one.

Why it matters: The category shows how the state combined moral appeal with material reward and managerial pressure. Students should distinguish it from the later Stakhanovite movement (Section 55.13), which emphasized individual record-setting and led to a different set of workplace and wage consequences.

Coercive instruments grew over time. In 1929 the government introduced the five-day continuous production week (nepreryvka), under which enterprises operated continuously while workers received staggered rest days. The arrangement disrupted family and social life and created coordination problems. In late 1931 it was replaced in most workplaces by a six-day cycle with common rest days, although continuous-shift arrangements survived in some industries. In June 1940 the government restored the seven-day week with Sunday as the regular rest day. A decree of November 1932 made unexcused absence of even a single day grounds for dismissal, which in practice could also mean loss of ration cards and, where tied to the enterprise, housing. Internal passports and residence registration, introduced in December 1932, strengthened control over movement. Labor books, recording each worker's employment history, were made compulsory in December 1938, and in 1940 further laws criminalized unauthorized job changes and repeated lateness. These measures belong to a trend toward greater state control of labor, although each had its own context and enforcement was uneven.

Management also used the language of class struggle and "wrecking" to explain failures. When production fell short, managers and specialists could be accused of sabotage, as in the trials of engineers in 1928 and 1930, and workers could be denounced for slacking. The political consequences of this practice, which became murderous in the later 1930s, are examined in Chapter 57. In the workplace of the early 1930s the effect was to place managers, specialists, and workers alike under pressure from above and to encourage the falsification of reports and the concealment of problems.

Hiroaki Kuromiya has treated the First Plan as a political and social mobilization, driven by class-war rhetoric, in which real worker and activist support coexisted with severe hardship, resistance, and coercion, while Donald Filtzer has emphasized labor discipline, turnover, absenteeism, working conditions, and the antagonism between workers and the state and management. The two views are less incompatible than they may appear, since enthusiasm was real for some groups and some periods, while exhaustion, deprivation, and fear were the experience of others, and many workers shifted between them. Evidence from workers' letters, police summaries, and enterprise records suggests a complex mixture of acceptance, complaint, bargaining, and evasion.

Section 55.12

The Second Five-Year Plan and the Consolidation of Industrialization, 1933–1937

The outline's reference to the "First Five-Year Plans," in the plural, reflects the fact that the industrial drive did not end in 1932. The Second Five-Year Plan, which covered the period 1933 to 1937 and was adopted at the Seventeenth Party Congress in early 1934, continued the industrial program under somewhat different conditions. The leadership had concluded, after the disruptions of the first plan period, that more moderate rates of growth were appropriate and that the main task was now to master the plants that had been built. Planned annual growth in industrial output was set lower than in the First Plan, and the plan stressed technical competence, productivity, and the efficient use of existing capacity.

Heavy industry remained the priority. The plan envisioned further expansion of metallurgy, machine building, chemicals, and electric power, and particular emphasis was placed on the development of the eastern industrial base around the Urals and western Siberia and on railway reconstruction. Lazar Kaganovich, who became People's Commissar of Transport in 1935, led a campaign to improve rail performance, which had been a major bottleneck. Major prestige projects of the period included the Moscow Metro, opened in May 1935, and the Moscow-Volga Canal, completed in 1937.

The international situation influenced priorities. The Japanese position in Manchuria and the rise of the Nazi regime in Germany after January 1933 led to a steady increase in the share of military production in the economy, and the defense industries benefited from the heavy-industrial base built in the First Plan. The scale of this shift should not be exaggerated, since a much larger increase in military spending came toward the end of the decade, but the Second Plan period saw a significant growth in the output of tanks, aircraft, and artillery.

Living conditions improved from the trough of 1932-1933, at least in comparison with that low point. Agricultural conditions stabilized after the famine years, as Chapter 56 explains, and the government abolished card rationing in stages: bread, flour, and cereals on 1 January 1935, the remaining centrally rationed foods on 1 October 1935, and industrial consumer goods on 1 January 1936, replacing it with a system of unified, though still high, state retail prices. Shortages and informal restrictions on access continued after formal rationing ended. Official propaganda emphasized that life had become "better and more joyous," and workers' wages rose in money terms. Yet consumer goods remained scarce, housing remained overcrowded, and quality was poor. Official figures claimed that the plan had been fulfilled in four years and three months, but actual performance, as with the First Plan, was uneven: heavy industry grew strongly, consumer goods lagged, and some targets were missed.

Industrial output continued to rise through 1937, and productivity improved in some sectors as workers and managers gained experience. But the Second Plan years also overlapped with the mounting political violence examined in Chapter 57, as arrests of managers and engineers began to disrupt industry from 1936 and especially in 1937 and 1938. This chapter's account of the Second Plan therefore stops at the point where industrial history and the history of the Terror begin to intertwine, and students should read the two chapters together. The Third Five-Year Plan, begun in 1938, was increasingly overtaken by rearmament and then by war.

Section 55.13

Stakhanovism and the Politics of Productivity

In the night of 30 to 31 August 1935, a coal miner named Aleksei Stakhanov at the Central-Irmino mine in the Donbas was reported to have cut 102 tons of coal in a single shift, many times the standard norm of about seven tons. The feat was publicized rapidly in the national press and was soon taken up as the basis of a movement. Within weeks workers in other industries, among them Aleksandr Busygin at the Gorky automobile plant, were reported to have set similar records, and in November 1935 a national conference of Stakhanovites was held in Moscow, at which Stalin spoke approvingly of the movement and of the improvement in living conditions.

It is easy, and mistaken, to reduce the story to the question of whether the records were "fake." The historian Lewis Siegelbaum, in his study of the movement, showed that the original record was organized: the party organizer at the mine, Konstantin Petrov, arranged for Stakhanov to work a favorable seam with support workers handling timbering and haulage so that he could concentrate on cutting. This was not simply a fraud; it demonstrated that a reorganization of work, with division of labor and better supply, could raise output far above existing norms. But it also showed that the record depended on conditions that could not be generalized to the whole mine.

The movement had several purposes. It was a propaganda campaign associating the regime with heroic labor and with a rising standard of living. It was a managerial instrument, since it provided a pretext to challenge existing production norms, which many engineers and managers defended as technically grounded, and to demand better organization of work. And it was a wage policy, because Stakhanovites received high pay, housing, and privileges, widening the gap between elite workers and the rest. The leadership, and especially Ordzhonikidze's commissariat, used the movement to push enterprises beyond the plan.

The consequences in workplaces were mixed. In some factories Stakhanovite methods led to genuine improvement in organization and productivity. In others, managers manipulated conditions to produce records for show, diverting materials and labor from the rest of the plant and disrupting production, and ordinary workers resented both the favored status of the Stakhanovites and the increases in norms that often followed. There were instances of hostility and violence against Stakhanovite workers, which authorities attributed to class enemies and wreckers. When norms were revised upward in 1936, many workers saw their pay per unit of output decline.

Stakhanovism thus turned exceptional production into a political and managerial instrument. Its significance lies less in the individual records than in what the movement shows about the Soviet system: the use of exemplary figures for mobilization, the tension between planning and campaigns, the role of the press, and the interaction between worker initiative, managerial interest, and political direction. It also overlapped with the onset of the Terror, since failures to meet Stakhanovite expectations were increasingly interpreted as sabotage, a connection treated in Chapter 57.

Section 55.14

Women and the Industrial Workforce

The mobilization of labor for industrialization transformed the position of women in the Soviet workforce. In the 1920s women had been a minority of wage workers and were disproportionately affected by urban unemployment. During the First and Second Plans, the demand for labor, together with falling real wages that made a single income inadequate for many families, drew women into paid work on a large scale. The historian Wendy Goldman's data show women rising from 28.6 percent of large-industry workers and apprentices in 1928 to 32.9 percent in 1932 and 35.5 percent in 1933; a broader series for industrial workers puts the female share at about 42 percent by 1935. These figures use different categories and should not be read as a single continuous series.

Women's employment was concentrated in particular sectors. They formed the majority of workers in textiles and clothing, large shares in food processing, education, health care, and trade, and growing numbers in heavy industry and construction, where they often performed heavy unskilled labor as well as skilled work. Recruitment campaigns encouraged women to take up industrial and technical jobs, and propaganda celebrated female tractor drivers, such as Pasha Angelina, and women shock workers and Stakhanovites. Technical and vocational courses admitted growing numbers of women, and some advanced into skilled posts, engineering, and management, though the higher levels of administration remained heavily male.

Equality in principle did not translate into equality in practice. Women on average earned less than men because they were concentrated in lower-paid occupations and lower grades even within the same industry, and protective legislation, which restricted women's employment in some hazardous jobs, was unevenly applied. Promotion was slower. Attitudes in the workplace and in party and union organizations often assumed that women's primary responsibilities lay in the home. The women's department of the party, the Zhenotdel, had been abolished in 1930 on the grounds that its main tasks had been accomplished, removing a dedicated institutional advocate.

The burden on women was heavy, and scholars have described it as a "double burden." Women worked full shifts and were still expected to manage the household, which in the conditions of the 1930s meant long hours of queuing for food, cooking without modern appliances, laundering by hand, and caring for children in overcrowded housing. The state expanded nurseries, kindergartens, canteens, and laundries, but facilities lagged far behind need, and in many places childcare depended on relatives or older children. Pregnancy and childbirth were managed under maternity leave provisions that existed on paper but were often inadequately supported by actual services.

Women's experience also varied by region and community. Industrial work drew rural Russian and Ukrainian women into towns, while in Central Asia and the Caucasus Soviet campaigns to bring women into public life and paid work encountered resistance from families and local authorities, and met with violence in some cases, as will be discussed in connection with nationality policy in Chapter 58. This section focuses on women as participants in industrialization. The broader changes of the mid-1930s in family law, including the restriction of abortion in 1936, belong to Chapter 58.

Section 55.15

Education, Technical Training, and the Creation of Soviet Specialists

Industrialization required engineers, technicians, skilled workers, and managers in numbers far exceeding those available, and the regime sought to produce them quickly through the expansion of technical education. Higher technical institutes and vocational schools multiplied during the First Plan, enrollments in higher education rose several-fold between the late 1920s and the mid-1930s, and factory schools trained young workers on the job. A four-year primary education requirement was introduced in 1930, and literacy rose substantially, with the census of 1939 recording a literacy rate around 80 percent among those aged nine and above, compared with about half in 1926.

A central feature of this expansion was the promotion of workers into positions of expertise and responsibility. Workers' faculties (rabfaki), established earlier to prepare adults of worker or peasant origin for university entry, were expanded, party and Komsomol members were mobilized into higher technical schools, and thousands of workers were promoted directly from the shop floor into administrative and technical posts.

Historical Vocabulary
Vydvizhenets (plural: vydvizhentsy)

Definition: A vydvizhenets was a person "promoted" or "advanced" (vydvizhenie) from a worker or peasant background into higher education or into a technical, managerial, or political position.

Historical context: The practice began in the 1920s but grew sharply during the cultural revolution of 1928-1931 and the industrialization drive. As the historian Sheila Fitzpatrick has shown, it opened real avenues of social mobility for hundreds of thousands of people from humble backgrounds, who often became the generation of Soviet administrators and engineers of the Stalin period and after.

Why it matters: The concept helps explain why the regime had real support among some groups despite the hardships of the period, and it complicates any picture of Stalinist society as simply a population of victims. Many later Soviet leaders were vydvizhentsy: Nikita Khrushchev studied at the Industrial Academy, Leonid Brezhnev trained at a metallurgical institute, and Aleksei Kosygin at a textile institute in the early 1930s, as later chapters on the post-Stalin period recount.

The "red specialists," as the new technical cadres were called, were meant to replace or supplement the older engineers and technical experts who had been trained before the revolution and whose loyalty the party distrusted. The 1928 Shakhty trial, in which engineers in the Donbas were accused of sabotage, and the 1930 trial of the so-called Industrial Party, in which prominent engineers were accused of conspiracy with foreign powers, signaled a campaign against "bourgeois specialists," and many old specialists faced dismissal, harassment, or arrest. The political aspects of these trials belong to Chapter 57; for industry the effect was to deprive some enterprises of experienced personnel at the very moment when demand for expertise was greatest.

By 1931 the leadership had reversed course. Stalin's speech of June 1931 called for a more conciliatory attitude toward the old technical intelligentsia and for their better treatment, and the following years saw the combination of older engineers and newly trained cadres in working teams. The resulting technical class was therefore a mixture of prerevolutionary specialists, often conservative in outlook but technically proficient, and young Soviet-trained engineers, often promoted quickly and eager to advance, and the relationship between them mixed cooperation with mutual suspicion.

Education and promotion also carried costs and limits. Rapid expansion strained teaching staff and facilities, graduates sometimes lacked adequate preparation, and the pressure to train quickly produced uneven quality. Social origin remained a political criterion in admissions through the early 1930s, favoring children of workers and poor peasants and disadvantaging those of "class alien" backgrounds. Even so, for hundreds of thousands of workers and their children, the expansion of schooling and promotion was a real and valued opportunity, and it formed part of the social base of the system.

Section 55.16

Living Standards, Consumption, Housing, and Everyday Urban Life

The investment strategy examined in the previous sections had a direct counterpart in the household: resources devoted to producer goods could not be devoted to consumer goods, housing, or services. The effects were felt most sharply between 1929 and 1933, when the supply of food and manufactured goods in the cities deteriorated badly, and they continued in modified form through the decade. For most urban residents, the everyday meaning of industrialization was scarcity.

Rationing, introduced for bread in 1928-1929 and extended widely in 1930 and 1931, determined access to basic foods and goods through cards tied to employment and workplace. The ration system was differentiated: workers in priority industries received larger rations than office employees and dependents, and party and managerial elites had access to special distribution outlets with better goods. Beyond the rationed supply, citizens bought what they could in collective-farm markets, where prices were far higher, or in the Torgsin shops for those with gold or foreign currency. Time spent in queues, often hours daily, became a routine part of urban life and was a particular burden on women. Card rationing was abolished in stages between January 1935 and January 1936 (Section 55.12), but shortages and informal restrictions persisted.

Housing was the most visible deficiency. The investment priority given to industry meant that new housing lagged badly behind urban growth, and the available space was subdivided, shared, and often informal. Typical urban residents lived in a single room for a family in a communal apartment, in a factory barracks, or in a self-built hut on the edge of the city. Water, heating, and sanitation were inadequate in many places. Higher-status groups, including managers, specialists, party officials, and Stakhanovites, could obtain better housing, and the differences in living standards became a source of resentment even as official language celebrated equality.

Enterprise-based provisioning softened but also reinforced inequality. Factory canteens provided subsidized meals, and enterprises often ran their own food supplies, from vegetable plots to livestock, to supplement the state distribution. Workers in large, favored factories could be relatively better provided for than those in smaller or lower-priority enterprises, and a job in a plant with good supply networks was valuable. Health services, clinics, and sanatoria, along with sports and cultural facilities, expanded, and mass literacy, newspapers, cinema, and radio reached a larger audience. Such services were real, if unevenly distributed.

Most historical assessments conclude that average real consumption and real wages for urban workers fell sharply in the early 1930s and recovered only partially by the later 1930s, although the figures are disputed and depend on how prices are measured, on whether free services are counted, and on how much weight is given to differences between privileged and ordinary consumers. Whatever the precise numbers, the contrast between investment in industry and the household economy is central to understanding the period. Memoirs, such as the account by the American welder John Scott of his years at Magnitogorsk, offer vivid detail, but like all memoirs they reflect the author's vantage point, and should be read alongside police summaries, enterprise records, and statistical evidence.

Section 55.17

What Did Industrialization Achieve? Growth, Distortion, Mobility, and Human Cost

Assessing Soviet industrialization requires holding together several kinds of evidence. On the side of achievement, the USSR in 1937 possessed a far greater capacity to produce coal, steel, electricity, machinery, and munitions than in 1928. Industrial centers had been created in the Urals, western Siberia, Kazakhstan, and the Far East, in regions that had been sparsely industrialized. The country had built a domestic machine-tool and tractor industry, trained large numbers of engineers and skilled workers, and provided avenues of upward mobility for many. These developments laid foundations for the wartime mobilization of 1941-1945, examined in Chapters 59 and 60, though how much they contributed, and how much the same resources might have contributed under a different strategy, are separate questions.

On the side of distortion and cost, the picture is equally clear. The command economy produced chronic shortages, poor quality, and large-scale waste, since enterprises were rewarded for fulfilling output targets rather than for efficiency, innovation, or meeting consumer needs. Statistics were exaggerated, sometimes deliberately, sometimes through the pricing methods used. Workplace safety was poor and accidents frequent. Consumer goods, housing, and services were starved of resources, and living standards for a large part of the population fell below the levels of the late 1920s before partially recovering. Coercive labor practices, from penalties for absenteeism to the use of special settlers and prisoners on some construction sites, were integral to the system. Inequality widened among workers and between workers and the new managerial and technical elite. The industrial strategy also depended on the agricultural policies examined in Chapter 56, whose human costs, including the famine of 1932-1933, are not reflected in the industrial statistics.

A fair assessment therefore avoids two simplifications. One is the claim that the plans were an unqualified economic triumph, which rests on official figures that overstate growth and ignores the deprivation and loss of life with which the strategy was bound up. The other is the claim that the industrialization was simply wasteful or meaningless, which ignores the real increase in capital goods production and the creation of an industrial base that persisted for decades. The system created real industrial power through an exceptionally coercive and distorted developmental model.

The key historiographical question is whether the USSR could have industrialized rapidly without the degree of coercion and social sacrifice imposed under Stalin. Some historians and economists argue that a more balanced strategy, drawing on the peasant market and the NEP framework, as Bukharin and others proposed, could have achieved substantial growth at lower human cost, possibly more efficiently. Others argue that the leadership faced severe constraints, including limited savings, external threat, and the political and social structure of the USSR, which made alternatives less feasible than they appear in retrospect. A third group emphasizes that the Stalinist strategy, whatever its cost, achieved high rates of capital formation and rapid structural change, and asks how to weigh these results against the costs. The Historical Debate feature below sets out these positions. Because the alternative paths were never tried, any claim about what they would have produced is an inference from comparison and modeling, and historians should state it as such.

For students, the more productive approach is to ask what each type of evidence can and cannot show. Production statistics can show direction and magnitude but are unreliable at the level of detail. Archival records of enterprises, the police, and the party show conflicts and shortcomings that official publications concealed. Memoirs and letters convey experience but not aggregates. Comparison with other late industrializers, such as Japan, Germany, or later developing economies, suggests that rapid industrialization has taken many forms, some far less coercive, but that the Soviet case also differed in its institutional setting. The answer to the question, in short, is that industrialization produced real power at very great cost, and that the limits of the evidence prevent a confident verdict on the necessity of that cost.

Primary Source Window

Joseph Stalin, “The Tasks of Business Executives,” Speech to the First All-Union Conference of Leading Personnel of Socialist Industry (4 February 1931)

Creator: Joseph Stalin, General Secretary of the Communist Party · Date: 4 February 1931 · Type: Political speech, published in Soviet newspapers and later in Stalin's collected works

Context: The speech was delivered to the First All-Union Conference of Leading Personnel of Socialist Industry in Moscow at a time when the First Plan's targets were falling short and the leadership was pressing for greater effort. It responded to calls from some managers and planners for slower tempo. It was a public statement of policy, addressed to managers but intended for a wider party and public audience.

Paraphrase of the argument (not a verbatim translated quotation):

Stalin argued against slowing the pace of industrialization. He said the country had a history of being beaten for its backwardness, listing earlier defeats by foreign powers, and he declared that the USSR lagged behind the advanced countries by fifty to a hundred years. He concluded that this gap had to be closed within about ten years, and that either the USSR would do so or it would be crushed. He also urged managers to master technology and to take responsibility for the industrial tempo.

Analysis: The speech is a classic statement of the argument from backwardness and security, and it illustrates how the leadership framed tempo as a matter of national survival, as well as of socialist construction. Historians note its rhetorical features: the specific figures of fifty to a hundred years and of ten years were not derived from economic calculation. The speech was later widely interpreted as prophetic, since the German invasion came in June 1941, just over ten years later, but this retrospective reading is a product of later memory and of the Soviet and Russian narrative of the war and should not be treated as evidence that the policy was necessarily the only response. The source also tells us about the intended audience, namely managers under pressure, and it cannot tell us what the managers thought, what the actual economic constraints were, or how the policy was received by workers and peasants.

Questions for students:

  1. What does the source say about the leadership's justification for rapid industrialization?
  2. Who created it, and for what possible purpose and audience?
  3. What can historians learn from it about the political language of the early 1930s?
  4. What additional evidence, such as economic data, managerial reports, or foreign assessments, would help evaluate the claim that the USSR could not slow its tempo?
Historical Debate

Was This Degree of Coercion Necessary?

The question: Could the USSR have industrialized rapidly without the extreme tempo, suppressed consumption, and coercion of the Stalin revolution, and what can historians responsibly say about an unrealized alternative?

Interpretation A — Constraint and necessity: Official Soviet historiography, and some later writers, argued that the USSR faced a narrow window before war, lacked foreign credit, and had to extract resources from agriculture and consumption to build a defense-industrial base. In this view the speed and methods adopted were forced by circumstances. Scholars who stress structural constraints, including limited savings and international isolation, make a more qualified version of the argument without necessarily endorsing the specific policies.
Interpretation B — Alternatives existed: Stephen Cohen's work on Bukharin, Moshe Lewin, and Alec Nove, whose essay "Was Stalin Really Necessary?" gave the question its classic form, argued that more moderate strategies were available and that the extreme tempo and the break with the peasantry were political choices that magnified costs and inefficiency. Robert Allen's economic modeling points in a related direction: it finds that Soviet growth was real and rapid, but that much of it could have been achieved under an NEP-style economy.
Interpretation C — Constraints, costs, and measurement: R. W. Davies, Mark Harrison, Stephen Wheatcroft, and collaborators emphasize forced mobilization, the difficulty of measuring Soviet economic performance, and the link between industrialization and military-economic power. The article by S. G. Wheatcroft, R. W. Davies, and J. M. Cooper, “Soviet Industrialization Reconsidered: Some Preliminary Conclusions about Economic Development between 1926 and 1941” (1986), rejects both the claim that the NEP could not have sustained any industrialization and the claim that the same very high rate of the 1930s could simply have been achieved under the NEP unchanged. Their work cannot be reduced to a verdict of success or failure.

Evidence: Historians use production statistics and the debates over index numbers, enterprise and planning archives, party and police records, memoirs, comparative data from other industrializing economies, and modeling of alternative investment paths.

Points of agreement: Most historians agree that industrial capacity grew substantially, that official statistics overstated growth, that consumption and living standards suffered severely, that coercion was extensive, and that the strategy depended on policies in agriculture with catastrophic human costs.

Points of disagreement: Historians differ on the feasibility and likely results of alternatives, on how far external threat explains the tempo, on how to weigh growth against suffering, and on how much the industrial base contributed to wartime survival compared with other possible strategies. The question of what would have happened under a different policy cannot be settled by evidence alone, since the alternative was not tried.

Why it matters: The debate bears on how later societies remember the period, on whether Stalinist methods are seen as the necessary price of modernization, and on how historians use counterfactual reasoning responsibly.

Student task: Identify two kinds of evidence that bear on this debate and explain what each can and cannot show. Then explain why the claim "there was no alternative" is difficult to establish.

Multiple Perspectives

The Construction Site

A young peasant migrant and shock worker: For a young villager arriving at a site such as Magnitogorsk, the new job could mean wages, rations, training, and a way out of a countryside under severe pressure, along with harsh housing and heavy labor. Some embraced the movement of shock work as a path to advancement; others left within months, which helps explain the high turnover.
A skilled cadre worker from the pre-1928 workforce: Older skilled workers often valued their craft and status and were frustrated by the influx of untrained newcomers, rising norms, and the leveling of workplaces. Some gained as foremen or trainers, while others resented the changes in discipline and pay.
A plant director or planner: Managers faced contradictory demands: to meet ambitious output targets, to economize, and to maintain labor in conditions of scarcity. Many hoarded supplies, bargained for easier targets, and relied on informal connections, while knowing that failure could be attributed to sabotage.
A foreign engineer or skilled worker: Foreign specialists, some attracted by the pay and the chance to work during the Depression and some by sympathy with the Soviet project, encountered both impressive construction and frustrating shortages, language barriers, and bureaucratic obstacles.
A special settler or prisoner assigned to construction: For people deported or imprisoned and sent to work on some sites, labor was compulsory, conditions were often harsh, and their experience was part of a system of forced labor whose full history belongs to Chapters 56 and 57. Their presence reminds us that the same projects celebrated in propaganda depended in part on coercion.

These perspectives show why the same construction site could be a place of opportunity, exhaustion, and compulsion at once, and why no single description captures its history.

Continuity and Change Over Time

State-Led Industrialization from Witte to Stalin

What Stayed the Same

  • The state played a leading role in industrial development, as under Witte (Chapter 44), with heavy industry, railways, and foreign technology prominent.
  • Key industrial regions, notably the Donbas, the Urals, Moscow, and Leningrad, remained central, even as new ones were added.
  • Dependence on foreign machinery and expertise recurred, though through different channels.

What Changed

  • Private ownership of industry and most trade was eliminated, and state planning replaced market allocation.
  • The scale and speed of investment, and of urbanization, were far greater than in the late empire.
  • Foreign capital investment gave way to purchases and technical contracts, and the state itself financed accumulation through price and tax control.
  • A system of labor control and a very large state-trained technical class took shape.

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both continuity and change helps students avoid two errors: treating Soviet industrialization as simply a continuation of imperial development, and treating it as a complete break with everything before it.

Cause and Effect

The Great Break and the Command Economy

Long-Term Conditions

Low industrial output per person compared with the leading powers; the inherited uneven industrial base; the experience of war and civil war; the mixed NEP economy and its recurring imbalances; an ideology prioritizing state ownership and heavy industry.

Intermediate Developments

The industrialization debates of the 1920s; the stagnation of investment capacity under the NEP; the war scare of 1927; the consolidation of Stalin's control over the party and the defeat of the Right (Chapter 54).

Short-Term Triggers

The grain procurement crisis of 1927-1928; the adoption of the First Plan in 1928-1929; the decision to raise targets; the availability of foreign equipment during the Great Depression.

Immediate Consequences

A command economy with priority for heavy industry; rapid construction; severe shortages; mass urbanization; new labor controls; the agricultural upheaval treated in Chapter 56.

Long-Term Consequences

An industrial base for later wartime and postwar mobilization; a lasting model of planning and its inefficiencies; a large technical class and new industrial regions; and the social and demographic costs examined across Part X.

Historical Complexity

No single factor explains the outcome. Backwardness, security fears, ideology, political competition, and economic constraints all mattered, but the specific tempo and methods were choices, made among alternatives, by people responding to crises of their own and others' making.

Why one cause is insufficient: Backwardness alone cannot explain the policy, since other strategies were possible; ideology alone cannot, since leaders disagreed within the same ideology; and security alone cannot, since the specific methods imposed costs that did not obviously serve security.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Barracks, a Queue, a Canteen

For a typical urban worker in the early 1930s, the day began early and was organized around the shift, the ration card, and the line. A family might share one room in a communal apartment or a barracks with others; heating came from a stove, water from a shared tap, and cooking from a shared kitchen where relations with neighbors could be cooperative or tense. Children might be left with a relative or in a crowded nursery. Mothers, who often worked a full shift, spent hours more in queues. The factory canteen offered a subsidized, if monotonous, meal and was often the most reliable source of hot food.

Differences mattered. A Stakhanovite or a manager might have a separate apartment and access to a closed store; a recent migrant might sleep in a barracks bunk and send part of his wages to the village. In new cities such as Magnitogorsk, the distance between the barracks district and the planned residential district illustrated the gap between the aspiration and the reality. The evidence for these lives comes from memoirs, letters, police reports, and surveys, each biased in its own way, and historians should resist generalizing from the best-documented cases to the whole of the Soviet population.

Regional Context

Beyond Moscow: Ukraine, the Urals, Siberia, Kazakhstan, and the Caucasus

Viewed from outside Moscow, industrialization looked different. In the Ukrainian SSR, the Donbas coal and metallurgical region and the Dnieper station made Ukraine one of the USSR's industrial heartlands, while the Ukrainian countryside experienced the upheavals discussed in Chapter 56; Kharkov was the republic's capital until 1934, when the capital moved to Kyiv. In the Urals and western Siberia, the new combine transformed sparsely settled areas into urban industrial regions, with a workforce that was largely migrant and often compelled.

In Kazakhstan and Central Asia, railways such as Turksib, new mines, and the demand for cotton and raw materials integrated the region into the Soviet economy, but the Kazakh pastoral population experienced severe disruption, a subject treated in Chapter 56. In Azerbaijan, the Baku oil industry, with its long history, remained a key national resource, and in Transcaucasia and elsewhere local industrialization combined republic-level institutions with central commissariats. The republics were thus affected differently, as participants, as sources of resources and labor, and as sites of disruption, and their experiences cannot be reduced to those of the Russian center.

Russia and the World

The Depression Decade

Soviet industrialization took place against the background of the global Great Depression. While Western economies suffered mass unemployment, the USSR proclaimed the abolition of unemployment, and this contrast attracted attention, including from some Western intellectuals and workers who traveled to the USSR or sought employment there. At the same time the Depression lowered world prices for Soviet exports such as grain and timber, increasing the burden of paying for imports, and encouraged Western firms to seek Soviet contracts.

The exchange was not one-directional. Soviet planners studied Western production methods, and Western observers studied Soviet planning, which influenced debates about economic management in the 1930s. The United States extended diplomatic recognition to the USSR in November 1933, partly in the hope of trade, though trade expectations were largely unmet. Soviet industrial policy was thus both a response to the international environment and a participant in it, shaped by Western technology and by Western crisis, and in turn influencing perceptions of planning abroad.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the Soviet "revolution from above" in its industrial and urban dimensions, from the decision to abandon the NEP in 1928-1929 to the consolidation of the Second Five-Year Plan in the mid-1930s. Its central problem was how a largely agrarian NEP economy was converted into a rapidly industrializing command economy, and with what consequences.

The chapter began with the Great Break, which it presented as a political choice. Backwardness, security fears, ideology, and political competition all contributed, but alternatives existed, and the specific tempo and coercive methods adopted were decisions made by identifiable actors. The chapter distinguished industrialization itself, which almost all Bolsheviks supported, from the particular strategy of extremely rapid and heavily coerced industrialization.

It then examined the institutions of the command economy: Gosplan, VSNKh and its successor commissariats, material balances, physical targets, administered prices, and the informal bargaining and hoarding that shaped practice. The chapter stressed that planning did not operate as a single flawless plan transmitted downward, but as a contested, improvised, and frequently inconsistent process.

The First Five-Year Plan was traced as a sequence of escalating targets, with growth that was substantial but well below the revised goals, and a declaration of completion that exceeded measured results. The financing of the drive depended on control of prices, the turnover tax, suppression of consumption, exports, asset sales, and the extraction of resources from agriculture, a link that points to Chapter 56. Foreign technology, engineers, and firms played a major role, which gave the project a global dimension.

The chapter described the principal heavy-industrial sectors and the great construction projects, separating their propaganda value from their economic importance, and showed how interdependence created bottlenecks. New industrial cities and mass urbanization transformed the country's demography, but housing, sanitation, and services lagged badly.

Social analysis focused on the creation of an industrial working class from recent peasants, on high turnover, and on the layered system of incentives, mobilization, and penalties used to discipline labor. Shock work and socialist competition, and later Stakhanovism, were examined as instruments that combined real enthusiasm, material reward, and managerial and political pressure. Women entered paid employment in large numbers and bore a double burden, while the expansion of technical education and the promotion of workers created real avenues of social mobility and a new technical class.

The Second Plan period consolidated industrial gains under somewhat more moderate targets, with greater attention to technique, transport, and defense, and with a partial improvement in living conditions after 1933, before industrial history began to intersect with the political violence of the later 1930s. Everyday life under this system was marked by shortages, rationing, crowded housing, and unequal access to goods, with differences by region, enterprise, and status.

The chapter's assessment held that industrialization produced real industrial power and new regions, skills, and mobility, but did so through an exceptionally coercive and distorted model with severe inefficiency, suppressed consumption, and enormous human costs, including those examined in Chapters 56 and 57. The historiographical question of whether less coercive paths were possible remains contested and cannot be settled by evidence alone, because the alternatives were never tried.

These lessons prepare students for Chapter 56, which examines the agricultural transformation that was the other side of the Great Break, and for Chapter 57, which examines the political institutions and violence of the Stalinist state. They also prepare students for Chapters 59 and 60, where the industrial base built in these years was tested in war, and for the later chapters on the Soviet economy, where the strengths and limits of the planning system recur.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Great Break (velikii perelom)
The Soviet term for the radical policy turn of 1928-1930 away from the NEP toward rapid industrialization and the transformation of agriculture.
Revolution from above
A historians' term for the state-initiated transformation of the economy and society under Stalin, as distinct from a revolution arising from popular uprising.
Command economy
An economy in which the state allocates resources and sets output through administrative directives rather than market prices.
Gosplan
The State Planning Commission, founded in 1921, which drafted economic plans and control figures.
VSNKh
The Supreme Council of the National Economy, the central body for state industry until its division into industrial commissariats in 1932.
Five-Year Plan
A multi-year economic plan setting targets for investment and output; the first covered 1928-1932 and the second 1933-1937.
Control figures
Annual planning estimates issued by Gosplan to guide economic activity.
Material balance
A planning table matching planned supply and use of a commodity.
Primitive socialist accumulation
Preobrazhensky's term for the extraction of resources from the private economy to finance state industry.
Turnover tax
A tax on the sale of goods, adjustable by product, that became the main source of state revenue after 1930.
Heavy industry (Group A)
Branches producing means of production, such as fuel, metals, machinery, and chemicals.
Group B
Branches producing consumer goods, treated as secondary in investment priority.
Counterplan
A pledge by a workplace to exceed its assigned plan targets, promoted in 1929-1930.
Socialist competition
Organized rivalry among workers and enterprises to exceed output targets, combining moral appeal and material reward.
Udarnik
A shock worker recognized for exceeding norms; also a member of a shock brigade.
Stakhanovism
A movement from 1935 celebrating record-setting workers, used to raise norms and as a political and managerial instrument.
Storming (shturmovshchina)
The practice of frantic, end-of-period efforts to meet output targets, often producing waste and poor quality.
Labor turnover
The rate at which workers leave and are replaced; in some Soviet industries it exceeded 100 percent annually.
Nepreryvka
The five-day continuous production week introduced in 1929, with staggered rest days; replaced in most workplaces by a six-day cycle in late 1931 and by the seven-day week in 1940.
Propiska
The residence registration system, introduced with internal passports in 1932, restricting residence in major cities.
Torgsin
A chain of stores established in 1931 that sold goods in exchange for gold and foreign currency.
Vydvizhenets
A person promoted from worker or peasant background into education or responsibility.
Rabfak
A workers' faculty preparing adults of worker or peasant origin for higher education.
Kommunalka
A communal apartment in which several families shared kitchen and bathroom facilities.
Double burden
The combination of paid employment and responsibility for housework and childcare borne by women.
Ural-Kuznetsk combine
A planned industrial complex linking Ural iron ore with Kuznetsk coal, with Magnitogorsk and Kuznetsk at its ends.
Cultural revolution (1928–1931)
A campaign of promoting workers, attacking "bourgeois" specialists, and reshaping education and culture.
Special settlers
People deported during the campaign against so-called kulaks and sent to remote areas, often used as forced labor.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has examined the creation of a command economy and the first great wave of Soviet industrialization, a transformation that reshaped the geography, institutions, and social structure of the USSR. It matters because the system it created, with its priorities, its planning methods, and its patterns of labor and consumption, framed the Soviet economy and society for decades, through the war, the postwar reconstruction, and the later reforms and stagnation examined in Parts X and XI.

The chapter also matters because it shows how to think about a transformation with both substantial achievements and severe costs. The industrial capacity, new cities, and new skills were real; so were the shortages, the coercion, and the loss of life with which the strategy was bound up. Neither a celebratory nor a dismissive account is adequate, and the historian's task is to explain how the system worked, who experienced it in which ways, and what the evidence can and cannot establish.

The themes of this chapter recur across the textbook: the role of the state in economic development, the relationship between center and regions, the experience of different peoples within a multinational state, the interaction of enthusiasm and coercion, and the role of memory in shaping how later generations view the past. The industrial legacy would be tested in the Second World War, remembered in Soviet and Russian public life, and reconsidered after 1991, when the privatization and market reforms of the post-Soviet period, examined in Chapter 67, confronted the inherited industrial structure.

Questions remain open, among them how much alternative strategies could have achieved, how reliable the surviving statistics are, and how the experiences of the many regions and peoples of the USSR should be weighed against the story told from Moscow. Students should carry forward both the evidence and the caution into Chapter 56, where the agricultural side of the Great Break reveals costs that industrial statistics alone cannot show, without assuming that later outcomes were determined by the choices made here.
