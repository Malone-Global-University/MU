Part VIII — The Late Imperial Crisis
48

The First World War and the Collapse of Imperial Russia

A History of Russia: From the Eurasian World to the Russian Federation

Chapter Opening

"By the autumn of 1916, leading members of the State Duma were asking publicly whether the failures of the government reflected incompetence or something worse." — a paraphrase of the sense of the criticism voiced in the Duma in November 1916, which this chapter examines as political rhetoric rather than as proof of treason.

In the cold of January 1917, a woman in Petrograd might rise before dawn to join a line outside a bakery or a shop selling flour, kerosene, or sugar. The city had borne that name only since the first weeks of the war, when the government replaced the Germanic-sounding "St. Petersburg" with the Russian-language "Petrograd." Her husband, if she had one, was probably in uniform, perhaps at the front, perhaps in a hospital, perhaps in a prisoner-of-war camp, perhaps dead. She might work in a metalworking plant that had expanded enormously to produce shells, rifles, or railway equipment, where women and adolescents now stood at machines once operated by men. Wages had risen, but prices had risen faster for many of the things she needed. The line moved slowly. Rumor traveled along it: about hoarding, about speculators, about ministers, about the Empress, about the man known as Rasputin, who had been murdered a few weeks earlier. A police informant might note the mood in a report that would reach officials who were already struggling to manage the city's food supply, its transportation, and its restless factories.

Some 600 miles away, in the Belarusian provincial town of Mogilev, Emperor Nicholas II spent much of the war at the headquarters of the Russian armies, known as Stavka. He had assumed the post of Supreme Commander in September 1915, after the army's disastrous retreat from Poland and Galicia. The decision tied the prestige of the monarchy more closely to the army's fortunes, while removing the emperor for long periods from the capital, where ministers, Duma politicians, and the Empress competed to influence the conduct of government. This chapter asks how the state described in Chapters 43 through 47, an autocracy that had survived the Revolution of 1905, accepted a limited Duma, expanded its industry, and attempted agrarian reform, came by early 1917 to face an unusually deep crisis of confidence.

The central argument of the chapter is that the First World War did not automatically destroy the Russian Empire, and defeat was not inevitable from the moment of mobilization in 1914. Russia mobilized millions of soldiers, won significant victories against Austria-Hungary and the Ottoman Empire, recovered from the severe shortages of munitions in 1915, and mounted in 1916 one of the most successful offensives of the war on any front. Yet the prolonged conflict strained transportation, public administration, public finance, and the relationship between the monarchy and a society that had organized itself in unprecedented ways to support the war. Military losses and mass displacement disrupted communities; inflation and transport failure damaged urban food supply; politicians who had rallied to the government in 1914 lost faith in its competence; and the emperor's refusal to grant a ministry that commanded the confidence of the Duma left the regime increasingly isolated.

The chapter treats the war as an experience of the whole empire rather than of ethnic Russians alone. The Great Retreat of 1915 uprooted millions of people from Poland, Lithuania, Latvia, Belarus, and Galicia. Jews, Germans, Poles, Latvians, Ukrainians, Armenians, Muslims of the Caucasus and Central Asia, and many others experienced mobilization, occupation, suspicion, expulsion, or revolt in different ways, depending on where they lived and how the state classified them. Their experiences are not a sideshow to the story of Petrograd and the court. They show how the war tested the empire's capacity to govern a vast and diverse society, a theme introduced in Chapter 45.

The evidence for these developments is abundant but uneven. Historians use the stenographic reports of the State Duma, the reports of the Okhrana (the security police) on public mood, the reports of military censors who read soldiers' letters, ministerial correspondence, statistics on production, prices, and railway loadings, newspapers and their censorship, memoirs of generals and politicians, the private letters of Nicholas II and Empress Alexandra, and, increasingly, studies of refugees, women, and soldiers. Each source has limitations. Memoirs written after 1917 are often shaped by the need to explain or defend what happened. Police reports record what officials feared and wished to report. Rumor, which was politically important in 1916, is itself evidence about public belief but not necessarily about the facts the rumors claimed to describe.

The chapter also continues the problem of historical contingency that runs through this textbook. Historians have long argued over whether Russia's collapse reflected military failure, economic breakdown, political mismanagement, social conflict, or a loss of legitimacy; the Historical Debate feature in this chapter examines that question. Students should be wary of any account that makes the collapse appear inevitable, and equally wary of one that treats it as an accident. Total war placed extraordinary pressure on every belligerent state. Germany, Austria-Hungary, France, Britain, and the Ottoman Empire all experienced casualties, inflation, shortages, and political conflict. The question is why the Russian political system proved less able than most to absorb those pressures.

This chapter is the culminating chapter of Part VIII. It ends in the winter of 1916–1917, before the demonstrations, strikes, and military mutiny that began in Petrograd in late February (Old Style) 1917. Those events belong to Chapter 49. Here the task is to explain how an imperial government that still possessed an army, a bureaucracy, a police force, and formal sovereign authority had by early 1917 lost the cooperation and confidence of much of the political elite and of large parts of the population. Crisis, as the chapter will repeatedly stress, is not yet revolution.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Describe the alliance system and Balkan and Straits interests that shaped Russian policy in 1914, and distinguish the Triple Entente from a single integrated military alliance.
  2. Analyze Russia's decisions in the July Crisis without reducing them either to sole responsibility for the war or to helpless reaction.
  3. Trace the major campaigns of 1914–1916, distinguishing defeats such as Tannenberg and the Great Retreat from successes in Galicia, the Caucasus, and the Brusilov Offensive.
  4. Evaluate competing explanations for the military crisis of 1915, including industrial capacity, logistics, command, and the strength of the Central Powers.
  5. Explain how mass displacement, deportation, and suspicion of minorities affected the empire's borderlands and its multinational population.
  6. Compare the army of 1914, 1915, and early 1917 in social composition, leadership, and morale.
  7. Assess the role of Zemgor, the War Industries Committees, and other public organizations in wartime mobilization and political conflict.
  8. Distinguish food production from food distribution and explain how inflation, fuel, and railway congestion produced urban shortages.
  9. Interpret Nicholas II's assumption of supreme command, the Progressive Bloc, and the Rasputin affair as parts of a developing crisis of political confidence.
  10. Evaluate competing historical explanations for the monarchy's weakening by early 1917, recognizing the limits of any single-cause account.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did a monarchy that had survived 1905 and entered the war with substantial economic and military resources lose the confidence of so much of its political elite by 1917?
  2. Did Russia choose war in July 1914, accept the risk of war, or become trapped by alliance commitments and mobilization timetables?
  3. Was the military crisis of 1915 primarily a failure of industry, logistics, command, or operational circumstances?
  4. How did the war's effects differ among soldiers, refugees, factory workers, peasants, townspeople, and the peoples of the borderlands?
  5. Why could organized society, through Zemgor and the War Industries Committees, show great capacity for mobilization while confidence in the imperial government declined?
  6. Why could bread shortages occur in Petrograd while substantial grain was produced elsewhere in the empire?
  7. How did Nicholas II's assumption of supreme command change the relationship between the monarchy, the army, and the government?
  8. What was the difference between the actual political influence of Rasputin and the political consequences of belief in that influence?
  9. What evidence supports the claim that by early 1917 Russia faced a crisis but not yet a revolution?

Historical Context

Chapters 43 through 47 described a regime that was authoritarian but adaptable. Alexander III and Nicholas II had maintained autocracy against revolutionary movements while promoting state-led industrialization (Chapter 44) and governing a multinational empire through a mixture of accommodation and Russification (Chapters 43 and 45). The Russo-Japanese War and the Revolution of 1905 (Chapter 46) forced the monarchy to issue the October Manifesto and accept an elected State Duma. Yet the Fundamental Laws of 1906 preserved the emperor's autocratic prerogatives, retained for him control of foreign policy and the army, and made ministers answerable to him rather than to the legislature (Chapter 47). After the dissolution of the first two Dumas and the electoral law of June 1907, a Duma more favorable to the government cooperated with ministers, most notably in the era of Pyotr Stolypin, but the unresolved question remained whether the government would ever become accountable to representative society.

By 1914 the Russian economy had expanded rapidly for more than a decade. Industrial output, railway mileage, banking, and urban populations had grown; the countryside, though still poor and burdened by rural land shortage, was changing after the abolition of redemption payments and the agrarian reforms of 1906–1911. A wave of strikes in 1912–1914, including a large strike in St. Petersburg in July 1914 during the visit of the French president, showed that labor conflict had not disappeared. Historians therefore disagree about how stable the empire was on the eve of the war, but few now describe it as an obviously collapsing power. Its economy was growing, its army was being expanded under the Great Programme of 1913, and its alliance with France gave it both loans and strategic support.

The empire's geography and administration shaped its capacity to wage war. Russia fought on a very long front, from the Baltic to the Black Sea and, against the Ottoman Empire, in the Caucasus. Its railway network was less dense than those of Germany or France, especially west of Moscow, and a large share of its manufactured and military goods had to be moved over great distances. Its principal outlets to the sea were quickly constrained: the Baltic was blocked by the German fleet and the Black Sea outlet through the Straits by Ottoman closure after Ottoman entry into the war in late 1914, leaving Archangel (icebound in winter), Vladivostok (thousands of miles from the front), and, from 1916, the new northern port of Murmansk for contact with the Allies. Exports and imports, including grain exports and imported machinery, were severely disrupted.

The state also governed a diverse population, a large part of which lived in the western territories that would become war zones. The Kingdom of Poland, the Baltic provinces, the western provinces with large Jewish, Ukrainian, Belarusian, Polish, and Lithuanian populations, the Caucasus, and Central Asia all entered the war under different legal regimes and with different relationships to the imperial center. Dates in this chapter follow a consistent rule: because Russia used the Julian calendar until 1918, which ran thirteen days behind the Gregorian calendar in this period, dates are given in New Style (N.S., Gregorian) with the Old Style (O.S., Julian) date in parentheses where it matters. Contemporary Russians, including the Duma and the government, used Old Style.

Section 48.1

Russia and the European Alliance System

Russia entered the war of 1914 through a network of diplomatic and military commitments built over two decades. After the lapse of the Reinsurance Treaty with Germany in 1890, Russia and France moved toward a military convention (concluded in 1892 and confirmed in 1894) that bound each to support the other if attacked by Germany or its allies. The alliance served both states: France gained a counterweight to German power on the continent, while Russia gained access to French capital markets. French loans financed Russian railways and industry, including strategic lines in the western provinces, and thus connected the industrial expansion described in Chapter 44 to great-power politics.

The second pillar of Russian policy was a rapprochement with Britain. After the Russo-Japanese War and the Revolution of 1905 (Chapter 46), the Russian government sought to reduce its imperial rivalries. The Anglo-Russian Convention of 1907 settled disputes over Persia, Afghanistan, and Tibet, and combined with the Anglo-French Entente of 1904 to produce what contemporaries called the Triple Entente. Students should note that this was not a single integrated military alliance. Russia and France had a formal alliance with detailed military obligations. Britain, by contrast, had understandings and military conversations with France and a diplomatic agreement with Russia, but not a binding treaty requiring it to fight alongside either. The opposing Triple Alliance of Germany, Austria-Hungary, and Italy was also less solid than its name suggests: Italy remained neutral in 1914 and entered the war on the Allied side in 1915.

Russian interests in the Balkans and at the Straits shaped the specific crisis that produced war. Russia had cultural, religious, and political ties to Orthodox and Slavic peoples in the Balkans, and a vocal nationalist and Pan-Slavic press urged the government to support Serbia. The Bosnian annexation crisis of 1908–1909, when Austria-Hungary formally annexed Bosnia-Herzegovina and Russia, still weakened by defeat in Asia, was unable to resist, left a legacy of resentment. The Balkan Wars of 1912–1913 altered the regional balance, left Serbia larger and more assertive, and heightened Austro-Hungarian fears of Serb nationalism. Foreign Minister Sergei Sazonov and many Russian officials believed that another humiliating retreat would damage Russia's status as a great power and its influence in the Balkans.

The Straits of the Bosporus and Dardanelles, which connect the Black Sea to the Mediterranean, mattered for economic as well as strategic reasons. A substantial share of Russian exports, especially grain from the southern ports, passed through them, and the Ottoman closure of the Straits during the Italo-Turkish War of 1911–1912 disrupted trade. The arrival of a German military mission under General Liman von Sanders in Constantinople in 1913–1914 heightened Russian fears that a major rival would gain influence over the waterway. Historians debate how far Russian officials pursued the actual conquest of Constantinople, a goal later promised in secret wartime agreements among the Allies; before 1914 the evidence points more strongly to concern about control, access, and the Ottoman state's alignment than to a settled plan of annexation.

Military planning and rearmament also shaped Russian expectations. After the defeats of 1904–1905, the army was reorganized under War Minister Vladimir Sukhomlinov, and in 1913 the government approved an expanded armament program, the Great Programme, intended to produce larger forces and better equipment in the following years. German strategists were aware of this growth and feared that Russian strength, supported by French investment in railways, would make a war more dangerous for Germany after about 1917. Russian leaders for their part knew that the army was not yet fully prepared, a belief that could encourage caution or, in different circumstances, a determination not to appear weak. Chapter 47 showed a state that was economically expanding and politically troubled rather than visibly on the verge of collapse; this section's alliance structure helps explain why a Balkan crisis could escalate into a general war, and does not imply that Russia's later collapse was already visible in 1914.

Section 48.2

The July Crisis of 1914

On 28 June 1914 N.S., a young Bosnian Serb nationalist, Gavrilo Princip, assassinated Archduke Franz Ferdinand, heir to the Austro-Hungarian throne, and his wife in Sarajevo. Austria-Hungary, with German assurances of support, prepared a stern response aimed at Serbia. On 23 July N.S., after the departure of the French president, Raymond Poincaré, from a state visit to St. Petersburg, Austria-Hungary delivered an ultimatum to Serbia whose terms were widely seen as designed to be difficult for any sovereign state to accept in full. Serbia accepted most of the demands but not all, and on 28 July N.S. Austria-Hungary declared war.

The Russian government considered its options in an atmosphere of alarm. Sazonov and many ministers believed that allowing Austria-Hungary to crush Serbia would destroy Russian influence in the Balkans. Nicholas II hoped to avoid war and exchanged telegrams with his cousin, Kaiser Wilhelm II, but he also approved preparatory military measures. On 29 July N.S., after Austro-Hungarian shelling of Belgrade, Russia announced a partial mobilization of military districts facing Austria-Hungary. Military leaders, including the Chief of the General Staff, Nikolai Yanushkevich, then argued that no workable plan existed for a partial mobilization and that improvising one could disrupt a general mobilization if war with Germany followed. Under pressure from Sazonov and the generals, Nicholas, after hesitating and briefly canceling the general order, approved general mobilization on 30 July N.S., and it was announced on 31 July N.S.

Germany treated Russian mobilization as a direct threat. German war plans, shaped by the Schlieffen Plan, assumed a rapid defeat of France before Russia could mobilize fully, which meant that delay carried military costs for Berlin. Germany demanded that Russia halt mobilization; when Russia did not, Germany declared war on 1 August N.S. (19 July O.S.), then on France on 3 August N.S. Austria-Hungary declared war on Russia on 6 August N.S. Britain entered the war on 4 August N.S. after the German invasion of Belgium. In Russia, crowds gathered in the capital, and the Duma, convened for a one-day session, expressed its support for the war effort. Contemporary accounts describe large patriotic demonstrations, although historians caution that enthusiasm varied by class and region and that many peasant recruits reacted with resignation or anxiety. In late August the capital was renamed Petrograd, replacing the Germanic ending of St. Petersburg.

The key interpretive question is whether Russia chose war, accepted the risk of war, or became trapped by alliances and mobilization timetables. Older works, including Fritz Fischer's influential studies, placed primary responsibility on German aims. Christopher Clark's The Sleepwalkers emphasized the interaction of decision-makers in several capitals and the weight of Balkan politics, while Sean McMeekin's The Russian Origins of the First World War argued that Russian decisions were more central than earlier accounts allowed. Dominic Lieven has stressed that Russian leaders did not seek a general war but accepted the risk of one in defense of their position in the Balkans. Most scholars today distinguish among the responsibility of different governments for different decisions. Austria-Hungary's ultimatum, Germany's support and its own war plan, and Russian and French willingness to escalate each contributed. The sound conclusion is agency without monocausal blame: Russian leaders made choices under constraints they helped create.

Section 48.3

Mobilization and the Opening Campaigns

Russian mobilization, on a scale unmatched in the empire's earlier history, brought millions of reservists and recruits into uniform within weeks and eventually drew some fifteen million men into the armed forces over the course of the war by commonly cited estimates. Mobilization was conducted by rail, village by village, with a combination of efficient administration and local disorder; contemporary observers noted that it proceeded more smoothly than many expected. Peasants made up the great majority of the soldiers, and the loss of men and horses from villages began immediately to affect agricultural households. Not every population was treated in the same way: Muslims of Central Asia and parts of the Caucasus, and some other groups, were exempt from regular conscription under prewar law, a distinction that would have consequences in 1916.

Russian strategy was shaped by an obligation to France and by competing priorities. French leaders pressed Russia to attack Germany quickly in order to draw German forces eastward, while the Russian General Staff also saw Austria-Hungary as a more vulnerable adversary. The result was offensives in two directions. In East Prussia, the Russian Northwestern Front under General Yakov Zhilinsky sent the First Army under Pavel Rennenkampf from the east and the Second Army under Aleksandr Samsonov from the south. The invasion began in mid-August N.S. and at first threw the German Eighth Army into disarray. At the Battle of Tannenberg (26–30 August N.S.; 13–17 August O.S.), however, German forces commanded by Paul von Hindenburg and Erich Ludendorff encircled and destroyed most of Samsonov's army. Tens of thousands of Russians were killed or wounded, and by common estimates roughly ninety thousand were captured; Samsonov took his own life. At the First Battle of the Masurian Lakes in early September, German forces drove Rennenkampf's army back out of East Prussia. Poor coordination, inadequate communications, and mistakes by commanders contributed to these defeats, while the Germans also benefited from railway mobility, effective use of intelligence, and exact knowledge of Russian radio messages sent without coding.

At the same time, Russia won major victories against Austria-Hungary. In the Battle of Galicia in August and September 1914, the Southwestern Front inflicted heavy losses on Austro-Hungarian armies, captured the Galician capital of Lemberg (Lviv) on 3 September N.S., and besieged the fortress of Przemyśl, which surrendered in March 1915 with a large garrison. Austro-Hungarian losses in the first months, especially among its professional officers and non-commissioned officers, were severe and reduced the army's quality for the remainder of the war. In the Caucasus, the Ottoman Empire entered the war in late October 1914. Ottoman commander Enver Pasha's winter offensive collapsed at Sarikamish in December 1914 and January 1915, with great losses attributable to cold, hunger, and Russian resistance. The opening phase of the war, then, was not a simple sequence of Russian defeats. It combined catastrophe at Tannenberg with real operational success against two other opponents.

By the end of 1914 the war had become longer and more costly than anyone had expected. The Russian army had suffered large casualties and consumed more ammunition than peacetime planning had anticipated, while fighting continued in Poland around Warsaw, Łódź, and the Vistula without decisive result. Peacetime stocks of artillery ammunition were quickly exhausted, and the state's munitions industry was not designed for the scale of consumption that the new warfare required. These shortages became critical in 1915, but their roots lay in decisions about peacetime planning and production made before the war, and in the unexpected duration of the conflict.

Section 48.4

The Military Crisis of 1915 and the Great Retreat

In the spring of 1915 Germany, having failed to win a quick victory in the west, shifted its principal effort to the eastern front. On 2 May N.S. (19 April O.S.), German and Austro-Hungarian forces under August von Mackensen launched the Gorlice-Tarnów offensive in western Galicia. After an intense concentrated artillery bombardment, the Russian Third Army's line broke. The Carpathian winter campaign had already exhausted Russian reserves and ammunition, and Russian units found themselves outgunned in firepower at the point of attack. The defenders were driven back, and Przemyśl was retaken in June and Lemberg in late June. By early August the Russians had been forced to evacuate Warsaw and the Polish salient, and in the following weeks they abandoned the fortresses of Novogeorgievsk and Brest-Litovsk, as well as Kovno, Grodno, and, in September, Vilna. By the autumn the front had stabilized on a line running roughly from the Gulf of Riga south through the Dvina region and the Pripet marshes to the Romanian border.

The human cost was enormous. Total Russian casualties for 1915, including killed, wounded, and captured, are commonly estimated in the range of two to three million, and prisoners in particular numbered in the hundreds of thousands. Exact figures remain uncertain because wartime record keeping was incomplete and because different sources count the missing and the wounded differently. The retreat also involved the loss of territory that included major industrial and agricultural regions in Poland and the western provinces, and the destruction and evacuation of many cities, factories, and railway lines. The Stavka ordered a policy of removing or destroying resources that might help the advancing enemy, including forced evacuations of civilians. Students should not confuse the policy with the Russian withdrawal of 1812 (Chapter 36); the circumstances, the scale of civilian displacement, and the political context were very different.

The "shell shortage" or "shell famine" was the most widely discussed cause. Peacetime stocks of artillery shells were small relative to wartime use, domestic production was limited, and imports were constrained by blockaded ports and the long distances from Archangel and Vladivostok. Rifles were also in short supply in some units, especially newly formed and reserve units. Historians caution, however, against the old image that Russian soldiers as a whole fought without weapons throughout the war: the shortages were serious, uneven, and concentrated in 1915, and they were substantially reduced in 1916. The Russian Special Councils created in August 1915 (on defense, fuel, food, and transport), the dismissal of Sukhomlinov as Minister of War in June 1915 and the appointment of Aleksei Polivanov, and the expansion of private and public industrial production marked a significant effort at correction.

Why did the front collapse in 1915? The question admits several partial answers. First, industrial capacity was a real limit, though not a simple one. Second, the Central Powers' ability to concentrate heavy artillery and the best German units at one point of the long line gave them tactical superiority that Russia could not match. Third, the Russian command, including Supreme Commander Grand Duke Nikolai Nikolaevich, Chief of Staff Yanushkevich, and the Stavka, was criticized by contemporaries and later historians for poor intelligence, rigid defense of fortresses, and weak coordination. Fourth, logistics and railway capacity limited Russia's ability to shift reserves. Finally, the scale of the empire gave Russian forces room to retreat without surrender. Though costly, the retreat saved the army from encirclement and allowed the front to stabilize. The most defensible conclusion is that 1915 was a crisis produced by the interaction of these causes rather than by any single one.

The political consequences were as important as the military ones. The retreat produced a search for scapegoats: ministers, generals, and, increasingly, minorities and foreigners were accused of treachery or incompetence. In 1915 Colonel Sergei Myasoyedev was hanged for alleged espionage on evidence now regarded as flimsy; Sukhomlinov was later arrested, charged with treason and abuse of office, and tried. The result was a climate of suspicion that fed both public criticism of the government and anti-German and antisemitic campaigns, as the next section shows.

Section 48.5

Refugees, Deportations, and the Imperial Borderlands

The Great Retreat was also a human catastrophe for civilians. As armies moved and fortresses fell, millions of people left their homes in Poland, Lithuania, Latvia, Belarus, and Galicia, some fleeing the fighting, some ordered out by military authorities, some driven by the scorched-earth practices of the retreat. Estimates of the number of refugees range from roughly three million in 1916 to well over six million by 1917, depending on whether one counts only registered refugees or also the deported and informally displaced. They moved eastward into Russian provinces, packing railway stations and cities and placing new demands on housing, food, and public health.

The Russian state classified and governed these populations in ways that reflected wartime suspicion. Under the Regulations on Field Administration (1914), military commanders in the theater of operations held extensive authority over civilians, and Stavka exercised it aggressively. Some of the policies targeted Jewish communities, whom military and police officials, drawing on long-standing prejudice, often accused of disloyalty or espionage. In 1915 large numbers of Jews, in numbers that some accounts place at 150,000 or more, were expelled on short notice from Courland and other frontier districts, hostages were taken from Jewish communities, and soldiers committed pogroms and acts of violence. Hundreds of thousands of Jews in all were displaced during the war by various estimates. In the summer of 1915 the government, under pressure from the practical need to house refugees and from political criticism, relaxed the Pale of Settlement restrictions and allowed Jewish refugees to settle in many interior cities, though not in the capitals. This shows the state acting in contradictory ways: persecuting and then partly accommodating the same population.

Other groups were also targeted. Anti-German feeling, already present, intensified during 1915. "Liquidation laws" from 1915 onward restricted or ended land ownership by Russian subjects of German descent and by citizens of enemy states in many regions, and tens of thousands of ethnic Germans, including colonists in Volhynia and elsewhere, were deported to the interior. In Moscow in late May (O.S.) 1915, anti-German riots devastated shops and homes. Poles experienced the war in a contradictory way. In August 1914 the Supreme Commander issued a proclamation promising Poles national unification under the Russian crown, but the actual wartime policies of the state fell short, and the German-occupied Kingdom of Poland became the object of competing promises: in November 1916 Germany and Austria-Hungary announced a nominally independent Polish state. In occupied eastern Galicia, Russian authorities, hoping to integrate the region and weaken its Ukrainian national movement and its Greek Catholic Church, arrested the Greek Catholic Metropolitan Andrei Sheptytsky in 1914 and pursued Russifying policies among local populations.

On the Caucasus front, Armenians were among the most affected. In 1915 the Ottoman government ordered the mass deportation of Armenians, accompanied by massacres; the great majority of scholars and many governments recognize these events as genocide, a conclusion contested by the Turkish state. Several hundred thousand Armenian refugees fled to the Russian Caucasus. Armenian volunteer units fought alongside the Russian army, while Russian policy toward Armenian, Georgian, and Muslim populations remained shaped by imperial interests and suspicion. Muslim populations in the same region also experienced displacement and violence. The Caucasus theater, where Russian forces took Erzurum in February 1916 and Trebizond in April 1916, was far from the capital, but it was linked to it by the same state and economy.

Much of the practical care of refugees fell to civil society rather than the state. The Tatiana Committee, established under the patronage of the emperor's eldest daughter, Zemgor, local committees, and national organizations such as Jewish, Polish, Latvian, and Armenian relief societies provided shelter, food, and employment, while the state's own capacity to process millions of displaced people was limited. Refugees strained urban housing and food supply, and local residents often resented their arrival. These experiences extended the multinational perspective of Chapter 45: the war was experienced differently depending on where within the empire a person lived and how officials classified that person.

Section 48.6

The Russian Army After the Great Retreat

The disasters of 1915 did not lead directly to the collapse of 1917. The army that held the line in the autumn of 1915 was battered but intact, and over the following year it was substantially rebuilt. Industrial production of artillery shells, rifles, and other equipment rose several-fold between 1914 and 1916 by standard estimates, aided by the expanded use of private factories, the Special Councils, and orders placed abroad. Imports from Japan, Britain, France, and the United States supplemented domestic output. The main route for Allied supplies remained difficult: Archangel was icebound for part of the year, the Trans-Siberian route from Vladivostok was extraordinarily long and capacity-constrained, with important single-track sections, bottlenecks, and limited rolling stock, and the new Murmansk railway, completed in 1916, opened an ice-free northern route only at the end of the year. The army was also reorganized, trained, and reequipped. By 1916 artillery supply was far better than in 1915, though shortages of heavy guns and of some categories of ammunition persisted.

Command also changed. Mikhail Alekseev, an experienced and careful officer, became Chief of Staff to the Supreme Commander in 1915 and directed the daily conduct of operations. The Stavka, while not free of rivalries, was more professional than before. Operations in 1916 began with a Russian attack at Lake Naroch in March to relieve German pressure on the French at Verdun, which achieved little and was costly. The decisive operation of the year was planned for the summer. At an Allied conference, Russia promised to attack in coordination with British and French offensives, and the main effort was initially assigned to the Western Front under General Aleksei Evert.

The Brusilov Offensive began on 4 June 1916 N.S. (22 May O.S.). General Aleksei Brusilov, commander of the Southwestern Front, proposed attacking at multiple points rather than in a single concentrated thrust, relying on careful reconnaissance, short and accurate artillery preparation, hidden assembly of troops close to the enemy lines, and the use of specially trained assault units. The Austro-Hungarian defenders, believing that a Russian attack on this front was unlikely to be large, were caught unprepared. Russian forces broke through in several places, captured Lutsk and Czernowitz, and took many prisoners. Austro-Hungarian losses were extremely heavy; estimates of casualties for the Central Powers commonly range from roughly one million upward, including several hundred thousand prisoners, though the numbers differ by source. The offensive forced Germany to shift divisions from the west and helped relieve pressure on Italy and Verdun.

The offensive, however, was not decisive. Brusilov's success was not matched on the neighboring fronts, as Evert's attack in the north failed, and Russian troops, supply lines, and reserves could not exploit the breakthrough. German reinforcements and renewed Austro-Hungarian resistance halted the advance, and by autumn the offensive had ground to a halt, with heavy Russian casualties, again estimated in the hundreds of thousands to over a million depending on the source. The best units, including Guards formations committed to the fighting around Kovel, suffered particularly severe losses. The entry of Romania into the war on the Allied side in late August 1916 briefly raised hopes of a decisive shift but quickly produced a new burden: German and Austro-Hungarian forces overran much of Romania, including Bucharest in December, and Russia had to extend its line southward by hundreds of kilometers to defend its ally.

The Brusilov Offensive prevents a misleading picture of continuous Russian military decline. In 1916 the army demonstrated that it could plan, supply, and conduct a complex offensive, and that its soldiers could fight effectively under competent leadership. At the same time, the cost was devastating. The victory strengthened the sense among many officers and politicians that victory was possible and that the war must be won, but it also removed hundreds of thousands of trained men and further changed the army's composition, a transformation examined in the next section.

Section 48.7

Soldiers, Officers, and the Experience of Mass War

To understand the army's condition in the winter of 1916–1917, historians study not only operations but military society. The overwhelming majority of the soldiers were peasants, many of them literate only minimally or not at all, taken from villages with strong local loyalties and attachments to family land. Mobilization drew successive age cohorts into service, and by 1916 the state was drafting older men and men previously exempted. The army of 1914, with its large core of regular soldiers and prewar reservists, was not the same as the army of 1915, which had lost many of its best-trained men, and neither was the same as the army of the winter of 1916–1917, which was dominated by wartime recruits and a largely new officer corps.

The officer corps was transformed by casualties. Prewar regular officers, drawn largely from the nobility and from career military families, died or were wounded in disproportionate numbers, and the army expanded rapidly by training junior officers in accelerated schools of ensigns (praporshchiki). These wartime officers were often drawn from townspeople, clerks, teachers, students, and educated peasants, and by 1917 they formed a significant portion of company-level leadership. Their social background and wartime experience could bring them closer to their men than prewar officers had been, but could also leave them less committed to the traditional hierarchy. Relations between officers and soldiers varied greatly by unit. Sources such as military censors' reports on soldiers' letters, which summarize what soldiers wrote home, reveal grievances about food, leave, harsh treatment, and the sense that soldiers were suffering while others profited, but they also show persistent patriotism and religious faith in many writers.

The daily experience of soldiers combined routine, boredom, danger, and weather. Trench life in the east was often less elaborately fortified than in the west because the front was longer, but artillery and machine guns brought heavy casualties. Wounded soldiers were evacuated to hospitals, many run by public organizations, and sometimes returned to the front. The army also contained millions of men in the rear, in depots and reserve units, whose experience was different again: crowded barracks, uncertain supplies, and the prospect of being sent forward. Russian prisoners of war, numbering in the millions across the whole conflict, endured captivity in Germany, Austria-Hungary, and the Ottoman Empire, with varying treatment and high mortality in some camps, and their families at home faced uncertainty and hardship. Muslim and Jewish soldiers, Poles, Ukrainians, and other nationalities served in a multinational army whose officers did not always trust or understand them, and Jewish soldiers in particular were often suspected of disloyalty despite their service.

Religion remained important, with military chaplains, icons, and services accompanying the troops, though practice varied. Desertion, self-inflicted wounds, unauthorized absences, and occasional refusals of orders increased in the second half of the war, but historians stress that the army did not enter 1917 in a state of uniform mutiny. Fraternization with the enemy was sporadic. War weariness, resentment of officers, and the growing belief that those in power were incompetent, or worse, coexisted with continued obedience. A major concern was the enormous reserve garrisons in cities, including Petrograd, where newly recruited soldiers, kept in poor conditions with little training and no prospect of going to the front under favorable terms, were a very different group from soldiers at the front. This chapter does not narrate what happened to those garrisons in February 1917; that is a subject of Chapter 49. Here it is enough to note that the army of early 1917 was socially and psychologically very different from the one that had mobilized in 1914.

Section 48.8

The Home Front and Wartime Mobilization

Total war changed the economy and society far from the front. Industrial plants, particularly in Petrograd, Moscow, the Urals, and the southern coal and metallurgical regions, were redirected to military production, and new factories were built or expanded. Petrograd's metalworking works, such as the Putilov plant, became vast war enterprises, and after a major labor conflict in February 1916 the government sequestered the Putilov works and placed it under state control. The urban working population grew as peasants, refugees, and women entered factories. Women and adolescents took on a larger share of industrial and clerical work as men were drafted, although skilled workers in essential industries were often exempted or reserved. Working hours lengthened, overtime became common, and discipline in war industries tightened, with the military often involved in labor supervision.

Public life became patriotic and militarized. Hospitals multiplied across the country, supported by the state, local governments, and private charity, and the Empress and her two eldest daughters, Olga and Tatiana, trained and served as wartime nurses, while Maria and Anastasia supported hospital work and visited wounded soldiers. Wounded soldiers returned to towns and villages, bringing the war home. Propaganda in newspapers, posters, popular prints, films, and school lessons urged support for the war and portrayed the enemy in harsh terms. Charitable campaigns, flag days, and fundraising drives mobilized women, students, and associations, and expanded the public sphere even as censorship remained strict.

The government's fiscal policy created serious problems. In 1914 it prohibited the sale of vodka, first as a mobilization measure and then for the duration of the war. The state's spirits monopoly had been a very large source of revenue, commonly estimated at roughly a quarter of ordinary state income, and its loss removed a major pillar of the budget. Prohibition reduced some social problems and was supported by many temperance advocates, but it also encouraged illicit distilling and further strained state finances. The government financed the war mainly through borrowing, from domestic markets and from Allied governments, and through issuing paper money rather than through large increases in taxation. The gold standard was suspended at the outset of the war. The expansion of the money supply, combined with limited supplies of consumer goods, contributed to inflation, as examined in Section 48.10.

The countryside also changed. Men were drafted, horses requisitioned, and women, older people, and children carried a larger share of farm labor. The government provided allowances to soldiers' families, and the wives of soldiers, known as soldatki, were a recognized social group and a source of local protest when allowances were late or inadequate. Peasants who produced grain faced fixed prices and a scarcity of goods to buy with their earnings, such as cloth, metal tools, and sugar, which affected their willingness to bring grain to market. For many rural households the war meant labor shortage, loss of family members, and burdens from requisitions, but some also benefited from higher prices for certain goods and from wartime employment and savings. The home front thus experienced both new organization and new strain, and the strain was concentrated in the cities and in particular among the poor.

Section 48.9

Industry, Zemgor, and the War Industries Committees

The early failures of military procurement led to an extraordinary mobilization of society's own organizations. At the start of the war the zemstvos and municipal governments (institutions created in the Great Reforms of Chapter 42) formed the Union of Zemstvos, headed by Prince Georgy Lvov, and the Union of Towns, with the Moscow mayor Mikhail Chelnokov among its leaders, to care for the wounded. In 1915 these bodies combined their activities in a joint committee, known as Zemgor, which expanded into supplying uniforms, boots, medical equipment, and other goods for the army. By 1916 Zemgor employed large numbers of workers and administered vast sums and had become, in effect, a parallel structure alongside the state's own bureaucracy.

A second movement came from industry. In the spring and summer of 1915, as the shell shortage became public, industrialists, politicians, and engineers founded the War Industries Committees, with a Central War Industries Committee headed by the Octobrist leader Aleksandr Guchkov and prominent businessmen such as Aleksandr Konovalov and members of the Moscow merchant elite. Regional and local committees organized the redirection of small and medium enterprises to war work, coordinated orders, and sought to overcome the bureaucratic slowness and corruption that critics saw in ministries. Many committees also created a Workers' Group so that labor representatives could participate, an unusual step that gave moderate socialists a legal platform. The government ambivalently accepted these organizations. In August 1915 it created Special Councils, which included representatives of the Duma, industry, and public organizations alongside officials, to supervise defense, fuel, food, and transport.

The record of these organizations is a mixed one, but it suggests a paradox. On one hand, they were highly effective in many areas. Production of shells, rifles, uniforms, and medical services increased dramatically, and the army that fought in 1916 was far better supplied than in 1915, although that recovery also depended on state arsenals, the Special Councils, private industry, foreign purchasing, and imports, not on public organizations alone. On the other hand, the organizations were also political. Their leaders, many of whom were liberal and conservative critics of the existing government, believed that the bureaucracy was too rigid and the court too closed to run the war effectively; the government and its supporters, for their part, feared that these bodies were building an alternative power base. Some officials accused them of political ambitions. The Okhrana and ministers monitored them, and in early 1917 members of the Workers' Group of the Central War Industries Committee were arrested.

The paradox is central to this chapter: Russian society demonstrated considerable capacity for wartime organization precisely while confidence in the imperial government was declining. This is a more sophisticated argument than the older claim that the empire could not produce enough weapons. The weakness lay less in an absence of talent, resources, or public energy than in the political arrangements for using them. The Fundamental Laws of 1906 (Chapter 47) did not provide a mechanism by which the organized public, which was providing so much of the war effort, could hold ministers accountable or share responsibility for national policy. The result was a growing political demand for institutional change by precisely the people whose cooperation the war effort required.

Section 48.10

Inflation, Food Supply, and Transportation

The most direct way the war reached ordinary urban households was through prices and shortages. Students must distinguish between food production and food distribution. Russia did not simply "run out of food." Grain harvests in the empire's agricultural regions, including the Volga region, Siberia, and the southern provinces, were substantial throughout the war, and the loss of grain exports because of the closure of the Baltic and the Straits increased domestic supply. The crisis was in the movement of food to cities and the army, in the incentives for peasants to sell, and in prices.

Inflation was the first factor. Wartime government spending, financed largely by borrowing and the printing of money, increased the amount of currency in circulation several-fold between 1914 and 1917, while the supply of manufactured goods fell as industry shifted to war production and as imports dwindled. Prices rose sharply, and by early 1917 retail prices in major cities were several times their prewar levels by contemporary estimates. Wages in many industries also rose, but for many workers and salaried employees not as fast as the prices of food, fuel, and housing, and families with soldiers' allowances or irregular employment were especially vulnerable. The difference between nominal and real income was understood by contemporaries and became a source of political resentment.

Transportation was the second factor. The railway network, designed for the pre-war economy, was overloaded by troop movements, military supplies, the evacuation of industry and refugees, and the need to move food and fuel. Locomotives and freight cars wore out faster than they could be replaced, and imported replacements and parts were limited. Military priorities took precedence over civilian goods. Congestion at junctions, long delays, and unpredictable deliveries were common. The harsh winter of 1916–1917 made matters worse, with heavy snow and extreme cold stalling locomotives and disrupting schedules. Fuel was a related problem: Petrograd had relied before the war on imported British coal, a source cut off by the war, and now depended on coal and wood from distant regions that had to travel on the same overstretched railways. Petrograd, far from the grain-producing regions and dependent on rail deliveries, was in a particularly vulnerable position.

Third, the government's policies on procurement and prices created incentives that did not always work. The state fixed prices for grain and other products in an effort to restrain inflation and secure army supplies. Peasants, who could not buy many goods with their earnings, often held back grain or sold it unofficially; merchants and intermediaries were accused of speculation and hoarding, and sometimes were guilty of it. A Special Council on Food Supply attempted to coordinate purchases, and in late 1916 the Minister of Agriculture, Aleksandr Rittikh, introduced a system of compulsory grain delivery quotas assigned to provinces, which was only partly implemented before the revolutionary crisis. Students should resist reading this later as an inevitable forerunner of Soviet policy; it was a wartime emergency measure devised by a tsarist minister in specific conditions.

The consequence was the urban queue, known in Russian as the khvost (tail). By the winter of 1916–1917, queues for bread, sugar, and other goods lengthened in Petrograd, Moscow, and other cities, and rumors spread about hoarding, impending rationing, and official indifference. Bread shortages in Petrograd could coexist with substantial grain production elsewhere because the problem was one of distribution, transport, price, and confidence rather than aggregate output. This distinction is essential for understanding Chapter 49, in which food supply, labor unrest, and political anger converged in the capital.

Section 48.11

Nicholas II Takes Supreme Command

At the outbreak of the war the emperor had appointed his cousin, Grand Duke Nikolai Nikolaevich, as Supreme Commander. The Grand Duke was tall, forceful, and popular with many officers and with segments of the public, and the Stavka under his authority, with Yanushkevich as Chief of Staff, directed operations from Baranovichi. The retreat of 1915 exposed the weaknesses of this arrangement. In the summer of that year, as the army fell back, Nicholas decided to dismiss the Grand Duke and take command himself, sending him to the Caucasus as viceroy and commander of the Caucasus front.

The decision reflected several motives. Nicholas was deeply conscious of his duties as sovereign and of the symbolic role of the monarch in wartime, and he regarded the army's leadership as his own responsibility. The Empress, supported by Rasputin in her correspondence, urged him to act firmly, and she distrusted the Grand Duke's popularity. Nicholas may also have wished to prevent the emergence of a rival center of authority at headquarters. His ministers viewed the plan with alarm. Most of the Council of Ministers, including Sazonov and Krivoshein, argued in August 1915 that the emperor's absence from the capital and his personal association with defeat would be dangerous at a moment of political tension; Nicholas rejected their advice. He assumed command on 5 September 1915 N.S. (23 August O.S.) and moved to the new headquarters at Mogilev, where General Alekseev, as Chief of Staff, directed the daily conduct of operations.

Students should avoid the caricature that Nicholas personally directed battlefield tactics. In practice, the Supreme Commander approved strategy, appointments, and major decisions, but the professional generals, led by Alekseev, planned and conducted operations. The emperor spent his time at Stavka reviewing reports, meeting with commanders, visiting troops, and, as the surviving diaries and letters show, living a quiet and regular routine, often with his son, the Tsarevich Alexei, at his side. Some officers welcomed his presence; others noted that his absences from Petrograd, and his reluctance to change course under pressure, left political management to others.

The political consequences were significant. First, the emperor's personal authority was now directly tied to the army's fortunes, so that future defeats could be attributed to him in a way that was less easily deflected onto ministers or a Grand Duke. Second, Stavka and the government in Petrograd became two centers of authority, and the Empress, who corresponded daily with the emperor, became an important informal political channel between them, alongside the formal governmental and military channels that continued to function. Third, the emperor's long absence from the capital gave the Empress, some ministers, and informal advisers a larger role, which in turn fed suspicion of a "court party." The decision is therefore best understood as one that connected the prestige of the monarchy to the outcome of the war while removing the emperor from the day-to-day political negotiations that might have kept a broader coalition together. Whether that was fatal depends on the other developments of 1915–1917, to which we now turn.

Section 48.12

The Duma, the Progressive Bloc, and the Crisis of Government

The war began with an apparent political truce. In a one-day session on 8 August 1914 N.S., the Fourth State Duma voted support for the war effort. The Kadets under Pavel Milyukov, the Octobrists, and other groups declared their patriotic cooperation, while Social Democratic and Trudovik deputies voiced their opposition in various ways, with the Mensheviks and Trudoviks refusing to vote for war credits and some socialists calling for a peace without annexations. In November 1914 the government arrested the Bolshevik deputies of the Duma, who were later tried and exiled for opposition to the war. For many liberals the war offered a chance to demonstrate loyalty and to ask for reform in return.

Expectations were disappointed in 1915. The Duma met only briefly during the early part of the war, and the Council of Ministers under the elderly Ivan Goremykin regarded it with suspicion. The military crisis made criticism more urgent. When the Duma reconvened in the summer of 1915, most of its groups demanded investigation of the causes of the military failures and reform of the government. In the late summer of 1915 (August O.S.), a majority of the Duma and a group in the State Council formed the Progressive Bloc, an alliance of Kadets, Progressists, Octobrists, moderate nationalists, and centrists. Its program called for a "government of public confidence," meaning a ministry whose members enjoyed the trust of the legislature and the public, as well as limited reforms, including some amnesty for political prisoners and a relaxation of discrimination against certain minorities. It excluded the socialist left and the far right.

Students should distinguish between a "government of public confidence" and a fully "responsible ministry." The first would have been a cabinet of figures acceptable to the Duma majority but still appointed by the emperor; the second would have made ministers formally accountable to parliament, as in Britain or France. The Bloc's leaders generally sought the first as a practical wartime step, and many of them, including Milyukov, were willing to preserve the monarchy. Even this limited demand collided with the emperor's view of his prerogatives under the Fundamental Laws and with his belief that concessions in wartime would weaken his authority. Several ministers, including Sazonov, Krivoshein, and Polivanov, supported cooperation with the Duma; Goremykin and others opposed it. In September 1915, shortly after taking command, Nicholas prorogued the Duma, and the ministers who had urged compromise were dismissed or resigned in the following months.

Over the next year the government changed repeatedly. Goremykin was replaced in early 1916 by Boris Stürmer, a man of limited reputation who was also given the foreign ministry, and then, in November 1916, by Aleksandr Trepov, and on 9 January 1917 N.S. (27 December 1916 O.S.) by Prince Nikolai Golitsyn. The post of Minister of the Interior, central to relations with the Duma and the police, changed hands several times, and in September 1916 it was given to Aleksandr Protopopov, a former Octobrist and vice-president of the Duma whose erratic behavior and personal connections to the court circle made him widely distrusted. The Ministry of War likewise changed several times. Contemporaries called this pattern "ministerial leapfrog" (ministerskaya chekharda), and it deprived the government of continuity at a moment when coordination of food supply, transport, and industry was essential.

By late 1916 the Duma's leaders, including the chairman Mikhail Rodzianko, repeatedly warned the emperor that the combination of military strain, economic hardship, and political isolation was dangerous. The question left over from Chapter 47, whether ministers would become accountable to representative society, had become more urgent in war. The monarchy repeatedly refused to concede responsible government, and the refusal increased the sense among many politicians that the regime was unable to learn. The Duma's restricted, unequal, multistage franchise sharply underrepresented peasants and workers, although both peasant and worker curiae participated in the electoral system; its majority represented many of the educated, propertied, and organized groups on which the war effort depended, and their alienation was therefore politically serious.

Section 48.13

Alexandra, Rasputin, and the Politics of Mistrust

Empress Alexandra Feodorovna, born Princess Alix of Hesse and by Rhine, was a granddaughter of Queen Victoria who had converted to Orthodoxy on marriage. She was devoted to her husband and to the monarchy, and deeply anxious about the health of her only son, Alexei, heir to the throne, who suffered from hemophilia, a condition the court kept private. In this context she turned to Grigory Rasputin, a Siberian peasant and wandering holy man (strannik) who had gained the family's trust because he seemed able to calm the boy and alleviate his suffering during crises, and who was regarded by the family as a man of genuine faith and a representative of the common people.

Historians distinguish carefully between Rasputin's actual influence and the political consequences of belief in that influence. His closeness to the imperial family was real, and during the war Alexandra, who had more influence over her husband when he was away from the capital, sometimes passed on his opinions in her letters about church appointments and about ministers. A number of ministerial changes in 1915–1916 reflected the Empress's and sometimes Rasputin's preferences, although the emperor made the decisions and had his own reasons. At the same time, many rumors were exaggerated or false: that the Empress was a German agent or sympathized with Germany, that she and Rasputin were lovers, that the court was working for a separate peace. There is no reliable evidence for such claims, and the letters between Nicholas and Alexandra show a determination to continue the war until victory. Alexandra's German birth, her reputation for reserve, and the secrecy surrounding her son's illness made her an easy target in a time of anti-German hysteria. Attacks on her also reflected gendered assumptions about women's role in politics.

The rumors mattered because they were widely believed, including among elites. The phrase "dark forces" (temnye sily) came to describe the supposed hidden influences around the throne. Caricatures, pamphlets, and drawing-room gossip portrayed the emperor as weak, the Empress as domineering, and the government as in the hands of a disreputable peasant. Police reports, diplomats' dispatches, and memoirs all recorded the spread of such stories. The consequence was that political critics, including patriots who wanted the war won, increasingly believed that government was being conducted through an incompetent and disreputable court circle. The historical significance of Rasputin thus lies less in the details of his influence than in the way the belief in it eroded the legitimacy of the monarchy.

In December 1916, a group of conspirators including Prince Felix Yusupov, a relative of the imperial family by marriage; the Grand Duke Dmitry Pavlovich, the emperor's cousin; and the right-wing Duma deputy Vladimir Purishkevich murdered Rasputin in Petrograd on the night of 29–30 December N.S. (16–17 December O.S.). His body was found in the Neva river system a few days later. The murder was committed by members of the aristocracy and a conservative politician rather than by revolutionaries, a sign of how widely the court's critics had spread. It did not change imperial policy. The emperor punished the Grand Duke by sending him to a post in Persia, and the Empress, who was devastated, became more determined to resist political concessions. For the regime the murder was thus both a symptom of elite alienation and an event that left the underlying conflict unresolved.

Section 48.14

The Crisis of the Winter of 1916–1917

The autumn and winter of 1916–1917 brought together the strands examined in this chapter. In November 1916 the Duma reconvened, and on 14 November N.S. (1 November O.S.) Pavel Milyukov delivered a speech that listed the government's failures and asked repeatedly whether they reflected "stupidity or treason." The speech, examined in this chapter's Primary Source Window, was circulated in copies despite censorship and became a symbol of the public break between the Duma and the ministers. Other speakers, including the monarchist Purishkevich and the Octobrist Vasily Shulgin, delivered sharp criticism of their own. Stürmer was dismissed within weeks, but his replacement by Trepov and then Golitsyn did not satisfy the Duma, and Protopopov remained at the Ministry of the Interior.

Outside the Duma, talk of conspiracy became common. Groups of politicians, officers, and members of the grand ducal family discussed removing the Empress's influence, forcing political concessions, or even replacing the emperor, though historians find the evidence about actual plans fragmentary and the plots largely unrealized. Allied representatives met in Petrograd from 29 January to 21 February 1917 N.S. to coordinate strategy, supplies, transportation, and war aims. Separately, Allied diplomats and visiting delegates heard increasingly urgent warnings about Russia's political instability. The monarchy still possessed a functioning government, an army of millions at the front, an extensive police apparatus, and formal sovereign authority. What it lacked was a coalition of support.

Meanwhile, material conditions in the cities deteriorated. Prices rose, queues lengthened, fuel was scarce, and the unusually severe winter disrupted transport. Police reports from the autumn and winter warned of growing discontent among workers and in the barracks and predicted disorder. Strikes increased. On 22 January N.S. (9 January O.S.), the anniversary of Bloody Sunday, large strikes and demonstrations took place in Petrograd and other cities, with well over a hundred thousand workers participating in the capital according to police and other estimates. In February the authorities arrested the members of the Workers' Group of the Central War Industries Committee, whom they suspected of political agitation. The ranks of discontented workers were joined by refugees, soldiers' wives, and the urban poor.

Throughout this period Nicholas II remained firmly opposed to political concessions. He treated warnings from the Duma chairman Rodzianko, from relatives, and from some of his generals as unwelcome pressure. His wife encouraged him to hold firm, and his own conception of autocracy, strengthened by his sense of duty as Supreme Commander, led him to see constitutional change as a threat to the war effort and to the dynasty. Historians differ over whether a timely concession, such as a ministry acceptable to the Duma, would have stabilized the situation or whether the crisis had by then gone beyond remedies available to the court. What is clear is that the emperor chose not to try.

By February 1917 the monarchy still possessed an army, a bureaucracy, a police force, and formal sovereign authority. What it increasingly lacked was confidence, from political elites, organized society, workers, and parts of its own governing apparatus, that the existing leadership could manage the war and the state. Crisis did not yet equal revolution. Chapter 49 explains how, within days, that crisis became one.

Historical Vocabulary
Zemgor

Definition: Zemgor (from zemstvo and gorod, "town") was the joint committee of the All-Russian Union of Zemstvos and the All-Russian Union of Towns, voluntary public organizations formed in 1914–1915 to care for the wounded and later to supply the army and assist refugees.

Historical context: The zemstvos were elected local government bodies created in the Great Reforms (Chapter 42), and city governments were their urban counterparts. During the First World War they became a large parallel structure, with their own staff, funds, and procurement networks, working alongside, and sometimes in tension with, the state bureaucracy.

Why it matters: Zemgor shows that Russian society mobilized itself for war on a large scale. It should not be mistaken for a revolutionary body: its leaders were mostly liberal or moderate figures who wished to strengthen the war effort. Its political significance lay in the fact that it demonstrated the ability of non-governmental institutions to do what the state was failing to do, and so deepened the argument for greater public participation in government.

Historical Vocabulary
Stavka

Definition: Stavka was the general headquarters of the Supreme Commander of the Russian armed forces during the war. The word derives from an older Russian term for the headquarters of a prince or commander.

Historical context: From 1914 until 1915 the Stavka at Baranovichi served Grand Duke Nikolai Nikolaevich; from August 1915 it was located at Mogilev under Nicholas II, with General Alekseev as Chief of Staff. Under wartime regulations, military authorities at the headquarters and the fronts held wide powers over civilians in the war zone.

Why it matters: Stavka was not simply a military office. Its authority over the war zone, its role in refugee and deportation policy, and the emperor's presence there made it a political center that rivaled the government in Petrograd. The word was revived in the Second World War (Chapter 59) by the Soviet High Command, though the institutions were different.

Historical Vocabulary
Government of Public Confidence

Definition: A "government of public confidence" (ministerstvo doveriya) was the formula used by the Progressive Bloc for a cabinet whose members would be acceptable to the Duma and the educated public.

Historical context: The demand emerged in 1915 and remained central through 1916. It was a more limited demand than a "responsible ministry," in which ministers would be formally accountable to the legislature, though in practice the two ideas could shade into each other.

Why it matters: The distinction shows that the Duma majority was not seeking to overthrow the monarchy in 1915–1916. The emperor's refusal of even this limited concession is an important part of the explanation for the political isolation of the regime.

Primary Source Window

Pavel Milyukov, Speech in the State Duma (1 November O.S. / 14 November N.S. 1916)

Creator: Pavel Milyukov, historian and leader of the Kadet party and the Progressive Bloc · Date: 14 November 1916 N.S. · Type: Parliamentary speech, preserved in Duma records and in circulated copies

Context: Milyukov spoke when the Duma reconvened after a long recess, at a moment of widespread frustration over the government's conduct of the war. The speech criticized the Stürmer government and the influence of the court circle, drawing on foreign newspapers and rumors to argue that the government was not worthy of public confidence. The speech was censored in the press but circulated widely in typed and printed copies, including to the army. Students should note that texts in circulation sometimes contained stronger passages than the official record, so it is necessary to know which version is being read.

Brief paraphrase and short quoted phrase (the wording of translations varies):

Milyukov listed a series of government measures and failures and, after each, asked whether the cause was stupidity or treason: "Is this stupidity or is it treason?" He argued that the government had lost the confidence of the Duma and of the country and that cooperation with it for victory was no longer possible.

Analysis: The speech reveals how far confidence between the Duma's leaders and the government had eroded by late 1916, and shows that opposition leaders framed their critique as patriotic and aimed at winning the war. It was also an exercise in political rhetoric. Milyukov himself later indicated that he lacked proof of actual treason by the ministers, and historians do not treat it as evidence that Alexandra or the government committed treason. The speech tells us about elite perceptions, parliamentary strategy, and the political culture of rumor, but little about the actual conduct of ministers. Missing from the source are the perspectives of workers, soldiers, refugees, and the government's own defenders.

Historical Debate

Why Did the Romanov Monarchy Approach Collapse?

The question: By early 1917 the monarchy faced a crisis it could not manage. Historians ask which factors were decisive: military failure, economic breakdown, political mismanagement, social conflict, or loss of legitimacy.

Interpretation A: military and economic overload. Some accounts emphasize that the scale of the war exceeded the capacity of the Russian state and economy, pointing to the casualties of 1915, the shortages of munitions, transport breakdown, and inflation. Norman Stone's study of the eastern front is often cited for a revisionist version of the argument: the Russian army and industry performed better than older accounts allowed, but the transportation system and administrative structures were unable to keep up with the demands of the war.
Interpretation B: political failure and loss of legitimacy. Others stress the regime's failure to build a durable partnership with organized society, pointing to the refusal of a government of public confidence, the ministerial turnover, the Rasputin affair, and the emperor's isolation. Richard Pipes, among others, emphasized the weakness of the monarchy's political foundations, while many historians stress that the politicians who criticized the government were largely patriotic and wished to continue the war.
Interpretation C: social crisis and class conflict. Soviet historiography and many social historians have emphasized the growth of labor unrest, peasant discontent, and soldiers' war weariness, together with the effects of inflation and shortages on urban households. Peter Gatrell's work on the wartime economy and state is influential for analyzing how institutional limits and social strain interacted.

Evidence: Historians use military and production statistics, price and wage series, railway records, Duma stenographic reports, police and censors' reports, memoirs, diaries, and correspondence. Each type has limits, such as the unreliability of wartime statistics and the retrospective bias of memoirs.

Points of agreement: Many historians agree that the war was essential to the crisis, that the Russian state coped less well than some others with its demands, that the 1915 crisis was followed by significant recovery in military supply, and that political confidence had collapsed by early 1917. Many also reject the notion that the outcome was inevitable.

Points of disagreement: Historians differ over the relative weight of each factor, over whether reform in 1915–1916 could have saved the monarchy, and over how far the crisis of early 1917 was rooted in long-term structural problems rather than wartime contingencies.

Why it matters: The answer shapes how historians understand both the February Revolution and the question of contingency in 1917.

Student task: Choose two causes discussed in this chapter and evaluate how they interacted, citing at least three specific pieces of evidence rather than choosing a single winner.

Multiple Perspectives

Six Experiences of the War, 1915–1917

A peasant soldier: For a conscripted peasant, the war meant separation from family and land, danger, hardship, and obedience to officers. Censors' reports suggest that many soldiers wrote about food, leave, news from home, and the length of the war, while continuing to serve; his village, short of labor, depended on the labor of women and older relatives.
A junior wartime officer: A graduate of a school of ensigns, often from a townsman or clerical background, led soldiers who might trust him more than a prewar noble officer. He was exposed to heavy casualties and knew the war's costs at close range, and many such officers criticized the competence of the high command.
A Petrograd factory woman: A woman working in a war plant earned wages but lost time and energy to long hours and queues, while managing a household with scarce fuel and rising prices; she might have a husband or son at the front and receive uncertain allowances.
A refugee from the western borderlands: Whether Polish, Jewish, Latvian, Lithuanian, Belarusian, or ethnic German, a refugee faced displacement, loss of property, and suspicion, with experiences that differed sharply according to how the state classified each group; some were expelled or deported by order, others fled on their own.
A Duma deputy of the Progressive Bloc: A deputy typically supported the war but believed that the government's incompetence and its refusal to cooperate endangered victory, and sought a government enjoying public confidence while remaining loyal to the monarchy.
A government official: An official in the ministries faced overloaded railways, competing demands, short-term ministers, and public organizations that seemed to challenge state authority, and may have regarded the Duma's critics as presumptuous, or privately shared their concerns.

These perspectives show that the war was not a single national experience. Power, coercion, and exclusion were distributed unequally: refugees and minorities faced state suspicion, workers faced discipline, soldiers faced danger, and elites faced political frustration.

Cause and Effect

The Loss of Confidence in the Imperial Government, 1914–1917

Long-Term Conditions

Autocracy preserved by the Fundamental Laws of 1906 alongside an elected Duma; an expanding but uneven economy; a thin railway network; a multinational empire with fragile relations between the state and many of its subjects.

Intermediate Developments

Casualties and the retreat of 1915; wartime inflation and fiscal strain; public organizations taking on tasks of supply and relief; the formation of the Progressive Bloc; ministerial turnover.

Short-Term Triggers

The decision to assume supreme command; the refusal of a government of public confidence; the Rasputin rumors; the transport and food crisis of winter 1916–1917.

Immediate and Long-Term Consequences

Alienation of the Duma, parts of the elite, and organized society; a regime isolated at the moment of acute strain; conditions in which a sudden crisis in Petrograd could escalate, as Chapter 49 shows.

Historical complexity: No single cause suffices. Military defeat alone does not explain the crisis, because Russia was still fighting effectively in 1916, and economic strain alone does not explain it, because other states bore similar burdens. The key was the interaction of these pressures with a political system that did not provide a way for state and society to share responsibility.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From Patriotic Mobilization to Crisis of Confidence

What Stayed the Same

  • The monarchy, the Fundamental Laws, the bureaucracy, the police, and the army remained institutionally intact until the revolutionary crisis.
  • The emperor retained control over ministers and foreign policy, and refused to make ministers accountable to the Duma.
  • Peasants remained the large majority of the population and of the army.

What Changed

  • The mood of 1914, marked by patriotic mobilization and a political truce, gave way in 1915 to military crisis and in 1916 to political conflict.
  • The army was transformed in its composition and leadership, and its industrial supply improved markedly after 1915.
  • Public organizations assumed functions that had been the state's, and millions of people were displaced.
  • Inflation, transport strain, and queues transformed urban life.

Why the pattern matters: Change was uneven and not uniform in direction. The army was stronger in some respects in 1916 than in 1915, even as political confidence declined. Those who experienced the greatest change were refugees, workers, and wartime soldiers, while the monarchy's formal structures changed least.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Petrograd Household in Wartime

Consider a composite household of the type described in police reports, factory records, and the later studies of historians: a woman, perhaps a former peasant or a long-time resident of the capital's working-class districts, working in or near a metal plant, with her mother-in-law and two children in a rented room or a corner of a crowded apartment. Her husband has been drafted. She receives a soldier's family allowance, which does not match rising rents or food prices. She works long shifts and spends hours queueing before or after work for bread, kerosene for lamps, and firewood. Children may be left in the care of relatives or neighbors, and the family relies on cheaper foods, such as bread and potatoes, as sugar, meat, and butter become costly or hard to find.

Social position and region mattered. Skilled male workers in some industries earned relatively high wages, while unskilled workers, women, and refugees were more vulnerable. Better-off households could buy at higher prices or through personal connections. Refugees arriving in the capital often had no work and no network. The evidence is imperfect: police and factory sources reflect what officials monitored, and memoirs mostly come from the educated, so the voices of ordinary women are often heard indirectly. What the sources show is a city in which rumors about hoarding, ministers, and the court spread through the queues, and in which hardship and political anger were beginning to reinforce each other.

Frontier Context

The Borderlands at War: Poland, the Baltic, the Caucasus, and Central Asia

From the perspective of the borderlands, the war looked quite different from the view in Petrograd. In Poland, the front crossed the country repeatedly, German occupation began in 1915, and Polish political leaders weighed competing promises from Russia, Germany, and Austria-Hungary, while Warsaw's industries and institutions were evacuated eastward. In the Baltic provinces, the retreat displaced Latvians and Lithuanians, and in the Latvian case refugees and Latvian riflemen became a powerful political force by 1917. Finland, an autonomous grand duchy, was not a battlefield but hosted Russian garrisons and war industry, and some Finnish activists sought German help for independence.

In the Caucasus, the war against the Ottoman Empire and the Armenian genocide shaped the experience of Armenians, Georgians, Azerbaijani Muslims, and others. In Central Asia and the Kazakh steppe, the government issued a decree in the summer of 1916 conscripting men from groups previously exempt, for labor behind the front. The decree prompted a widespread revolt, particularly in Semirechye and among Kazakh and Kyrgyz communities, which was met with severe repression and the flight of large numbers of people toward China, with heavy loss of life; estimates of deaths vary and remain debated. The revolt shows that the war's burdens were not restricted to the empire's Slavic core, and that the relationship between state and peoples in Central Asia, discussed in Chapters 39 and 45, was changing.

Russia and the World

Total War Compared: Russia, Germany, Austria-Hungary, France, and Britain

The pressures that Russia experienced were not unique to Russia. All belligerent states suffered huge casualties, inflation, shortages, and labor and political unrest. Germany faced severe food shortages, especially in the winter of 1916–1917 (often called the "turnip winter"), as the Allied naval blockade and wartime agricultural problems reduced supplies. Austria-Hungary experienced food shortages, nationality tensions, and military exhaustion. France and Britain mobilized economies through state intervention, rationing, and political coalition governments. Britain created a wartime coalition in 1915 and later a new government under David Lloyd George, and France's political life also underwent reorganization and crisis in 1916–1917.

Comparison therefore sharpens rather than removes the central question. The distinguishing feature of Russia was not that it suffered strain, but that its political system, built around an emperor who retained control over ministers and rejected parliamentary responsibility, proved less able than most to convert public mobilization into a stable partnership between state and society. Russia was also not a passive recipient of foreign models: it was part of an Allied coalition, depended on Allied finance and supplies, and influenced Allied strategy through its offensives, as in 1916.

Chapter Review and Historical Thinking Checkpoint

This chapter has argued that the First World War weakened the Romanov monarchy not simply by defeating its army but by overwhelming the mechanisms through which state and society cooperated. The central checkpoint question is this: Why did the First World War weaken the Romanov monarchy so severely by early 1917 even though Russia remained militarily in the war and had expanded its capacity to produce weapons? A strong answer resists the simple formula that Russia lost the war and therefore faced revolution.

Causation. Military casualties, the retreat of 1915, inflation, transport breakdown, fiscal strain, and administrative weakness all contributed, as did political decisions: the refusal of a government of public confidence, the emperor's assumption of command, and the pattern of ministerial turnover. None is sufficient alone.

Change over time. The sequence runs from patriotic mobilization in 1914, to military crisis in 1915, to partial recovery and political conflict in 1916, to a crisis of legitimacy in the winter of 1916–1917.

Continuity. The monarchy, the bureaucracy, the Duma, the army, and the police remained institutionally intact up to the revolutionary crisis. The regime still held substantial coercive resources.

Evidence. Historians draw on military and production statistics, Duma debates, price data, railway records, letters and diaries, and police and censors' reports, and must weigh the limits of each.

Competing interpretations. Explanations emphasize military collapse, socioeconomic crisis, political failure, or loss of elite confidence; the strongest recent work stresses their interaction.

Contingency. The war made collapse possible but did not make the events of February 1917 inevitable. Different decisions by the emperor, ministers, and the Duma in 1915–1916 might have produced a different political outcome, although historians disagree about how much difference such decisions would have made.

Broader significance. The case shows that total war could destabilize a modernizing empire by overwhelming the institutions linking state and society, not merely by defeating its army. Students should now apply these lessons to four short problems before moving to Chapter 49.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how the First World War brought the Romanov monarchy to a crisis of governance by the winter of 1916–1917. Its central problem was why a state that had survived the Revolution of 1905, adopted a limited constitutional order, and entered the war with considerable resources came to lose the confidence of much of its political elite and population while remaining a belligerent with an army in the field.

Russia entered the war through the Franco-Russian alliance and the looser Triple Entente, driven by interests in the Balkans and the Straits and by concern for great-power status. In the July Crisis, Russian leaders mobilized in support of Serbia, accepting the risk of war and facing the consequences of mobilization timetables and German war plans. Historians assign responsibility among several governments rather than a single one.

The opening campaigns combined disaster at Tannenberg with victories over Austria-Hungary in Galicia and over the Ottoman army at Sarikamish. In 1915 the Gorlice-Tarnów offensive led to the Great Retreat, enormous casualties, loss of the Polish salient and much of the western borderlands, and a severe shortage of munitions. The causes of the crisis lay in a combination of industrial limits, logistics, command weakness, and the concentrated power of the Central Powers.

The retreat also displaced millions of civilians and led to deportations of Jews and Germans, anti-German violence, and wartime ethnic suspicion, while refugees strained cities and public organizations. The empire's multinational character shaped how the war was experienced, from Galicia and Poland to the Caucasus and Central Asia, where the labor decree of 1916 provoked revolt.

The army recovered after 1915. Arms production expanded, command improved, and in June 1916 the Brusilov Offensive achieved a major success against Austria-Hungary, though at heavy cost. Meanwhile the army's social composition changed as casualties and mobilization replaced the prewar cadre with wartime recruits and officers, and war weariness grew without producing a uniformly mutinous army before February 1917.

On the home front, industry expanded, women and adolescents entered factories, hospitals and charities multiplied, and public organizations, notably Zemgor and the War Industries Committees, demonstrated great capacity. The paradox was that society's capacity for organization grew while its confidence in the government declined, because institutions offered no way for the organized public to share responsibility.

Economic and fiscal strain compounded the problem. Prohibition, deficit finance, and the expansion of the money supply, together with transport congestion and fixed prices, produced inflation and urban shortages that were failures of distribution rather than an absolute lack of food. Petrograd, dependent on distant supplies, was especially vulnerable.

Politically, Nicholas II's assumption of supreme command tied the monarchy's prestige to the army and removed him from the capital. The Progressive Bloc's demand for a government of public confidence was refused, ministers changed rapidly, and rumors about the Empress and Rasputin eroded the monarchy's legitimacy. The murder of Rasputin in December 1916 reflected alienation within the elite without changing policy.

Historians disagree about the weight of military, economic, political, and social causes, and the best recent work emphasizes their interaction. The chapter has avoided treating the outcome as inevitable: by February 1917 the monarchy still had an army, a bureaucracy, a police force, and formal authority, but lacked confidence from elites, organized society, workers, and parts of its own apparatus.

These developments set the stage for Chapter 49, which examines how the crisis of the capital in late February (Old Style) 1917 became a revolution, and for Chapter 50, which examines the competing institutions that emerged. They also complete Part VIII, showing how the contradictions examined in Chapters 43 through 47 were transformed by total war.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a state with real resources and demonstrated military capacity could nonetheless be brought to a crisis of governance. Russia's mobilization of soldiers, workers, public organizations, and industries was extensive; its failure lay in the political and administrative arrangements linking these efforts to the monarchy. The chapter therefore changes the way students should think about 1917: the war did not simply defeat Russia, but transformed the conditions under which the existing political order could survive.

What continued afterward was the interplay of war, state capacity, and social strain. Many of the problems examined here, including transport, food distribution, inflation, the status of refugees, and the relationship of public organizations to the state, were inherited by the governments that followed in 1917, which are examined in Chapters 49 through 52. They also shaped debates about wartime requisition and state control that recur in later Soviet history. Students should avoid assuming that later outcomes were already visible in the winter of 1916–1917; those outcomes depended on events and decisions that lay ahead.

The chapter also reveals broader themes of this textbook: the multinational character of the empire, the problem of political representation, the importance of ordinary people's experience, and the need to read evidence critically. The war was experienced differently by Russians, Poles, Jews, Germans, Armenians, Kazakhs, and many others, and the historical record includes both official sources and the traces of ordinary lives.

Questions remain unresolved: how far a different political course in 1915–1916 might have altered the outcome, how to weigh structural and contingent causes, and how to interpret the many gaps in the evidence. As Chapter 49 begins, students should carry forward the lesson that crisis is not yet revolution, and that explaining the next stage requires attention to the actions of workers, soldiers, women, politicians, and officials in the days of late February (Old Style) 1917.
