Part VIII — The Late Imperial Crisis
45

Peoples, Minorities, Nationalism, and the Late Imperial State

A History of Russia: From the Eurasian World to the Russian Federation

Chapter Opening

In January 1897 the Russian Empire attempted, for the first and only time, to count all of its subjects at once. The census asked each person's age, sex, estate, religion, and native language. It did not ask for "nationality."

The omission was deliberate, and it reveals the subject of this chapter. In the winter of 1897 (the census date was 28 January according to the Old Style calendar then in use), tens of thousands of enumerators went from household to household, in cities such as Vilna, Warsaw, Riga, Tiflis, and Tashkent and in villages from the Baltic coast to the Pacific. In a single Vilna street an enumerator might record a Catholic who spoke Polish at home, a Jewish shopkeeper who named Yiddish as his native tongue, an Orthodox official who named Russian, a Lutheran or Catholic family who spoke Lithuanian, and a Belarusian-speaking servant who was told, or chose, to be entered under one of several categories. The census asked about the things the state's own institutions recognized: religion, language, and legal estate. Whether those answers added up to "a nation" was a different question, and one that the government, the activists, and the subjects themselves answered very differently.

The total counted was about 125.6 million people. By native language, Great Russian accounted for roughly 44 percent of the whole, Little Russian (Ukrainian) about 18 percent, Polish about 6 percent, Belarusian about 5 percent, and the census category "Jewish" (principally Yiddish) about 4 percent, followed by Kazakh (then called "Kirghiz"), Tatar, German, Finnish, Latvian, Lithuanian, Estonian, Georgian, Armenian, Uzbek, and dozens of other languages. These are language categories, not measures of nationality, and the census itself did not ask about nationality. In other words, the empire was not a Russian nation-state with a few minorities at its edges. It was a multinational continental polity in which those who spoke Russian as a first language were a plurality, not a majority. Many of the language communities later described as minorities were local majorities in their own regions: Polish speakers in the Vistula provinces, Finnish speakers in Finland, Estonian and Latvian speakers in the Baltic countryside, Kazakh speakers on the steppe, Georgian speakers in much of western Georgia.

This chapter examines how that empire was governed and how its peoples understood themselves in the decades between the 1860s and the eve of the Revolution of 1905. Its central question is whether the Russian Empire could create greater political and administrative unity without destroying the regional, religious, linguistic, and historical distinctions through which many of its subjects defined themselves. The question is deliberately framed to avoid two errors. One is the simple story of "Russia oppressing its minorities," which treats imperial administration as a single, consistently hostile project and treats every subject population as a modern nation waiting to be freed. The opposite error treats imperial government as culturally neutral, concerned only with efficiency and security. Neither description fits the evidence.

The chapter's governing argument is that the late Russian Empire was governed through historically accumulated differences of law, religion, language, estate, and regional privilege. During the late nineteenth century the government increasingly sought administrative uniformity, a stronger place for the Russian language, and tighter political integration. These policies sometimes increased the state's capacity, but they also collided with local institutions, religious communities, expanding schools, print culture, social mobility, and emerging national movements. The result was neither uniform Russification nor inevitable national liberation. It was a widening struggle over what membership in the empire meant and how much diversity imperial rule could accommodate.

The chapter's persons are therefore many and various. They include Polish landowners and factory workers in Łódź, a Ukrainian-speaking schoolteacher in a Kyiv hromada (a cultural association), a Finnish senator defending a constitution, an Estonian choir singer and a Baltic German pastor, a Jewish tailor, a yeshiva student, and a Bundist organizer, an Armenian priest and a Georgian poet, a Tatar publisher who reformed Muslim schooling, and a Russian governor-general in Tashkent who ruled with a handful of officials and a great deal of local intermediaries. The evidence for their experiences is equally varied: the 1897 census, laws and circulars, petitions, newspapers, school curricula, folklore collections, memoirs, police reports, and the literary and scholarly works of the new national movements.

The chapter builds directly on the preceding ones. Chapter 39 examined how the empire acquired and governed its frontier territories, including Poland, the Caucasus, and Central Asia. Chapter 43 introduced Russification as a policy strategy of Alexander III's government and described the pogrom crisis of 1881–82. Chapter 44 described how railways, factories, cities, and migration were transforming the empire's economy and society. Chapter 45 asks what those political and economic processes meant inside particular societies, and how identities and political movements developed within them. Chapter 46 will then show how war and revolution in 1904–1905 mobilized many of the grievances examined here. The chapter should not be read as a story in which nationalism automatically destroyed the empire; the empire survived until 1917, and its collapse then depended on a world war and a revolutionary crisis (Chapters 48–52).

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why the late Russian Empire is better described as a multinational polity governed through differentiated rule than as a Russian nation-state with minorities.
  2. Distinguish among administrative centralization, linguistic Russification, religious policy, cultural nationalism, and political loyalty as separate dimensions of imperial policy.
  3. Trace the transformation of the Kingdom of Poland after 1863 and the changing forms of Polish national politics.
  4. Distinguish language, ethnographic identity, cultural activism, and mass political nationalism in the Ukrainian and Belarusian cases.
  5. Compare Finland and the Baltic provinces as regions with strong historical privileges that experienced centralization differently.
  6. Analyze Jewish life in the Pale of Settlement as a history of law, economy, religion, culture, migration, and politics rather than only of discrimination and violence.
  7. Contrast the post-conquest Caucasus and Central Asia as colonial and imperial regions governed under different administrative logics.
  8. Evaluate how state recognition of Islamic institutions both integrated Muslims into the empire and provided frameworks for Muslim organization.
  9. Assess how railways, schools, print, and urbanization could simultaneously strengthen imperial integration and the tools of national movements.
  10. Interpret a petition from a non-Russian regional society as evidence, distinguishing its claims from later nationalist outcomes.
  11. Evaluate competing historiographical explanations of Russification as modernization, nation-building, security policy, or an inconsistent combination.
  12. Connect the multinational society described here to the crisis of 1905 without treating imperial collapse as inevitable.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Could the Russian Empire create greater administrative unity without destroying the distinctions through which its subjects understood themselves?
  2. Why did the 1897 census ask about language and religion but not nationality, and what does that choice reveal about how the state saw its population?
  3. How did the regional experiences of Poles, Finns, Baltic Germans, Jews, Georgians, and Central Asians differ, and why?
  4. What is the difference between speaking a language, cultivating it as a cultural project, and mobilizing politically on its behalf?
  5. In what ways did the same modernizing institutions (railways, schools, newspapers, cities) both bind the empire together and equip movements that questioned it?
  6. Why did Islam, religion more generally, and legal estate matter as much as ethnicity in shaping how imperial subjects were governed?
  7. How should historians evaluate Russification given that policies were uneven, contradictory, and applied very differently across regions?
  8. How does this chapter help explain which society was entering the crisis of 1904–1905, without implying that the empire's collapse was predetermined?

Historical Context

The Russian Empire's diversity was an accumulation, not a design. Over three centuries Muscovite and imperial rulers had incorporated territories by conquest, treaty, dynastic arrangement, and partition: the Volga khanates and Siberia (Chapters 23 and 28), eastern Ukraine and Smolensk (Chapter 30), the Baltic provinces and Livonia from Sweden (Chapter 31), much of the former Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth (Chapter 34), Finland and Bessarabia (Chapter 36), the Caucasus (Chapter 39), and Central Asia (Chapters 39 and 42). Each acquisition came with its own elites, laws, religions, and expectations. Rulers usually preferred to keep local arrangements that worked and to win over local elites, as long as taxes were paid, soldiers were supplied, and order held. The result was a patchwork. Baltic German nobles kept their Landtage and Lutheran church; Finland kept its Lutheran church, its Diet, and its legal order; the Kingdom of Poland received a constitution in 1815 that was later curtailed; Muslim subjects were allowed their own clerical structures; Jews were governed by a distinct set of residence and occupational rules; Cossack hosts held land in return for military service.

Two long-term pressures cut across this patchwork in the nineteenth century. First, the idea of the state as an instrument of uniform administration and the idea of the nation as the natural basis of political community spread across Europe. The Great Reforms of the 1860s (Chapter 42) created new institutions, such as courts, zemstvos, and universal military service after 1874, that were in principle empire-wide, though many were not extended to the western borderlands or to the non-Russian periphery in the same form. Second, the Polish risings of 1830–31 (Chapter 39) and 1863–64 convinced many officials that regional particularism threatened security, especially in the western provinces. After 1863 a generation of officials, journalists, and conservative intellectuals argued for a stronger "Russian" character to imperial government. They were never unified in what they wanted, and the emperors themselves were often ambivalent, but the tendency is visible across the 1860s to the 1890s, and especially under Alexander III (Chapter 43).

At the same time, the empire's society was changing in ways that governments did not fully control. Literacy was rising, though unevenly; newspapers, journals, and cheap printed books multiplied; railways carried people, goods, and ideas (Chapter 44); cities such as Warsaw, Łódź, Riga, Baku, and Odesa grew rapidly; and universities, gymnasia, and professional associations created new educated strata who could imagine and organize communities in new ways. In Europe generally, intellectuals were codifying vernacular languages, collecting folklore, writing national histories, and creating cultural societies. Similar activity developed in the Russian Empire among Ukrainians, Estonians, Latvians, Finns, Georgians, Armenians, Jews, Tatars, and others, though in widely different conditions. The word "nation" meant different things to different people: a historic political community with institutions (as for Polish nobles or Finnish constitutionalists), a cultural and linguistic community (as for many Estonian or Ukrainian activists), a religious community, or an imperial citizenry.

Students should keep three analytical distinctions in mind throughout this chapter. First, identity in the late empire could be religious, linguistic, regional, dynastic, estate-based, municipal, imperial, ethnic, national, or several of these simultaneously; a person might be a Lutheran, a Latvian speaker, a subject of the emperor, a resident of Riga, and a member of a guild. Second, a population that spoke a given language was not automatically a politically mobilized nation. Third, the word "minority" has limited value for the empire's peoples, since many were majorities in their home regions; this chapter generally uses terms such as peoples, communities, confessions, and regional populations. These distinctions are essential, because the evidence for a given group, such as a government circular, a newspaper, or a petition, usually reflects the views of a particular person or institution and not of an entire "people."

Finally, the chronology matters. Many of the policies examined here are associated with the 1880s and 1890s, but several of the major confrontations, including the assault on Finland's autonomy, the closure of Armenian church property, and the large-scale peasant resettlement to Central Asia, belong to the reign of Nicholas II after 1894 and sit at the edge of the period examined in Chapter 46. This chapter therefore follows the long arc to approximately 1904, noting where the story passes into the revolutionary crisis.

Section 45.1

Governing a Multinational Empire

A common mental model treats the Russian Empire as essentially modern Russia with peripheral minorities. That picture misleads in at least three ways. The empire's core and its peripheries were not clearly separated: Ukrainians and Belarusians lived in regions that were geographically central to the European part of the empire. Large cities such as Odesa, Vilna, Riga, and Baku were ethnically mixed. And the legal structure of the empire did not treat "Russians" and "everyone else" as the two basic categories. Subjects were distinguished by estate (nobles, clergy, townspeople, peasants, Cossacks), by religion, and by region, and these distinctions overlapped in complicated ways. A Polish Catholic noble, a Russian Orthodox peasant, a Jewish merchant, and a Muslim Kazakh herder lived under different rules about land, taxation, military service, education, courts, and residence.

Historians sometimes describe this as differentiated rule. Finland was not governed like Poland, Poland was not governed like Bukhara, and Bukhara was not governed like the Baltic provinces. Jewish legal status resembled neither Muslim institutional arrangements nor Armenian church organization. Even the "Caucasus" had no single administrative system. This variety was practical as much as principled. The empire was huge, its bureaucracy was small relative to its territory, and communications were slow. Officials needed local intermediaries (nobles, clergy, village elders, qadis, headmen, and merchants) and often found it cheaper and safer to work through existing institutions than to replace them. Differentiated rule thus helped a modestly staffed state hold together an enormous and varied territory.

Historical Vocabulary
Differentiated Rule

Definition: A historians' term for the imperial practice of governing different populations and regions under different laws, institutions, and privileges rather than a single uniform system.

Historical context: The Russian Empire accumulated its territories over centuries and typically left in place many local elites, religious bodies, and legal customs. By the late nineteenth century this produced such contrasts as Finland's Diet and constitutional order, the Baltic nobles' Landtage, Muslim spiritual boards, Jewish residence restrictions, and military-run governance in Turkestan.

Why it matters: The concept explains why late nineteenth-century policies of uniformity were controversial: they threatened arrangements that many subjects, and sometimes imperial officials themselves, regarded as legitimate. It also prevents students from treating "the empire" as a single, uniform machine. The term is analytical; contemporaries did not use it.

The state pursued several objectives that did not always fit together: security (especially in border regions and after the Polish risings), taxation and recruitment (the universal conscription law of 1874, though applied with exemptions for some groups), administrative efficiency, political loyalty, defense of the Orthodox Church's privileged position, and, increasingly in the late century, cultural integration, meaning the extension of the Russian language and of Russian institutions as a unifying element. Officials differed among themselves about how to balance these goals. Some, influenced by the conservative press, believed that a Russian national core must be strengthened for the empire to survive. Others, more cautious, believed that provoking regional elites would be dangerous and that stability required tolerance of difference. Under Alexander III and Nicholas II, ministers and governors-general could pursue divergent policies in neighboring regions at the same time.

This produces the chapter's central tension. Imperial government had long survived by recognizing difference, and late nineteenth-century centralization increasingly sought to reduce it. The tension was not simply between a "Russian" state and "non-Russian" subjects. It also divided those who favored integration, who sometimes included members of local elites and the educated groups, from those who defended particular local arrangements. Baltic German nobles resisted Russian administrative control, but their own order stood above the Estonian and Latvian peasantry. Polish landowners resented Russian governance, but Polish industrialists in Łódź depended on the Russian market. Finnish elites defended a constitution that had limited effect on social equality. In each case, the state's actions interacted with divisions inside the local society.

The census of 1897 illustrates the state's categories. It recorded the population by religion, native language, and estate. It did not offer a nationality question, and published tables grouped languages in ways that reflected official conceptions: Great Russian, Little Russian, and Belarusian were separate language categories, but officials commonly viewed them as branches of a single Russian people, a view contested by Ukrainian and Belarusian activists. The census cannot tell us how individuals felt about their identities, which were often multiple and situational. It does tell us how the state wished to describe them.

Religion was probably the single most important category of legal difference. The Orthodox Church held a privileged position. Conversion from Orthodoxy was illegal, and the children of mixed marriages involving an Orthodox spouse were to be raised Orthodox. Lutheran, Catholic, Armenian Apostolic, Muslim, Jewish, Buddhist, and Old Believer communities existed under varying degrees of recognition, restriction, and supervision. The state recognized several religious hierarchies and funded some of them, but it also treated religious affiliation as a marker of reliability and, for some groups, of foreignness. Since religion, language, and region frequently overlapped, religious policy and nationality policy were intertwined and often indistinguishable in practice.

A final feature of the legal order deserves emphasis. The category inorodtsy (literally "those of other origin," usually translated "aliens") classified some populations, including Siberian indigenous peoples, Kazakhs, and, in a distinct legal sense, Jews, as separate from the majority population for purposes of administration. It did not mean the same thing everywhere, and it did not track what we would call ethnicity. The vocabulary of the imperial state was legal and administrative, and its categories could be inconsistent with how peoples saw themselves.

Historical Vocabulary
Inorodtsy

Definition: A legal and administrative category in the Russian Empire, literally "people of other origin," applied to certain non-Slavic populations who were governed by special statutes and often exempt from certain obligations (such as conscription in some cases).

Historical context: The category was formalized in the early nineteenth century (Speransky's Siberian reforms of 1822 are a major landmark) and later extended and modified. Its groups included many Siberian peoples, Kazakhs and other nomads, Samoyeds, and, under separate provisions, Jews. The meaning shifted over time and was applied unevenly.

Why it matters: It shows that imperial classification was based on law and administration rather than a modern concept of nationality. Students should avoid translating it as "minorities" or "ethnic groups"; the term was a tool of governance, and later acquired pejorative connotations.

Section 45.2

Poles and Polish Nationalism

The partitions of Poland and the Polish risings of 1830–31 were treated in Chapters 34 and 39. The turning point for this chapter is the January Uprising of 1863–64, which broke out in the Kingdom of Poland after a forced conscription targeted at young urban men, and spread into Lithuania, Belarus, and parts of Ukraine. It was a guerrilla war fought by small partisan bands, led by an underground National Government, and it was suppressed after about eighteen months. The consequences included executions, mass deportation to Siberia, confiscation of estates, and heavy fines on participants. The empire also took a decisive step against the Polish gentry: the decree of 1864 gave Polish peasants the land they worked, with terms more favorable than in the Russian emancipation of 1861 (Chapter 42), in an effort to win their loyalty and weaken the landowners who had led the rising.

In the following decades the Kingdom's remaining distinct institutions were reduced. The separate Polish administration was folded into the imperial system; the old name "Kingdom of Poland" fell into official disuse in favor of "Vistula Land" (Privislinsky krai) in the second half of the 1880s. Russian became the language of government and of instruction in state schools, with Polish confined largely to the teaching of religion, and the University of Warsaw was reorganized as a Russian-language institution in 1869. In the Kholm (Chełm) region the Uniate Church, a Greek-rite church in union with Rome, was suppressed in 1875 and its members were administratively assigned to Orthodoxy, a policy that left bitter memories and, after 1905, saw many return to Catholicism. The Catholic Church, whose clergy were suspected of fostering Polish identity, was subjected to supervision and restriction.

It would be wrong, however, to describe Polish society as simply crushed or as unanimously hostile. Several things were happening at once. Industrialization transformed Warsaw and especially Łódź, where textile production grew rapidly within the empire's tariff-protected market (Chapter 44). Polish industrialists, merchants, and bankers benefited from access to Russian and Asian markets, and a Polish working class and a socialist movement emerged. Peasants, newly landed after 1864, developed political interests different from those of the gentry. Cultural life, though restricted, continued in private education, secret "flying universities," publishing, and the Catholic Church, and Polish-language literature could be published in Warsaw within censorship limits. Polish intellectuals also drew on Galicia, the Polish-inhabited province of Austria-Hungary, where after 1867 Poles enjoyed substantial autonomy and Polish-language universities in Kraków and Lwów (Lviv).

One of the most important analytical points is that Polish nationalism itself changed. Students should not imagine a single revolutionary doctrine passing unchanged from 1830 to 1905. After the failure of 1863, many Polish intellectuals rejected insurrection. The Warsaw positivists of the 1870s argued for "organic work" and "work at the foundations": economic development, education, science, and social modernization as the real basis of national survival. Conservative elites pursued accommodation with the imperial authorities in hopes of gaining concessions. Socialist movements, beginning with the "Proletariat" party of the early 1880s and the Polish Socialist Party (PPS, founded 1892, Józef Piłsudski among its leaders), combined national and class goals and disputed whether independence or revolutionary solidarity with Russian workers should come first. The National League (1893) and the National Democratic movement associated with Roman Dmowski developed a more ethnically defined nationalism, increasingly hostile to Jews and Germans and, in the Kingdom, increasingly anti-socialist. These movements did not agree on tactics, goals, or even on who belonged to the nation.

Class mattered at every point. Polish landowners, peasants, workers, Catholic clergy, intellectuals, Jews (many of whom lived in the Kingdom's towns and were an important part of its economy), and industrialists did not respond identically to imperial policy. Jewish residents of the Kingdom, for example, were often regarded by Polish nationalists as outsiders and by Russian officials as a separate administrative category, which complicated any picture of a simple confrontation between "Poles" and "Russians." The experience of Polish peasants, many of whom saw the 1864 land settlement as a benefit, differed from that of dispossessed gentry families. By the 1890s Polish society was more urban, more industrial, more literate, and more politically differentiated than in 1863, and the imperial authorities had not found a stable basis on which to relate to it.

Section 45.3

Ukrainians and Belarusian Identity

This section requires terminological care, because modern national categories can easily be projected backward. In the nineteenth-century empire, officials and many educated Russians used the term "Little Russians" (malorossy) for the East Slavic population of what is now much of Ukraine, and often regarded Great Russians, Little Russians, and Belarusians as three branches of a single Russian people. Activists, especially in the south, increasingly argued that Ukrainians (the term used by many in Galicia and increasingly in the empire) were a distinct people with their own language, history, and rights. Both views existed, and many peasants used local or religious identities (people from a village, region, or "Orthodox") rather than either label. The task of the historian is to follow how these categories developed, not to decide which was "correct."

Ukrainian cultural activity in the empire had earlier landmarks. The poet Taras Shevchenko, a former serf whose collection Kobzar first appeared in 1840, became a cultural symbol after his death in 1861. The short-lived Brotherhood of Saints Cyril and Methodius in Kyiv (1845–47) was suppressed. In the 1860s and 1870s, educated Ukrainians formed hromady (communities or societies) in Kyiv and other cities. They collected folklore, produced ethnographic and historical studies, ran Sunday schools in the vernacular, and promoted Ukrainian publishing. The Kyiv section of the Imperial Russian Geographical Society, which carried out major ethnographic expeditions in the 1870s, became an important center of such scholarship.

Historical Vocabulary
Hromada

Definition: Ukrainian for "community" or "society." In the nineteenth century it denoted informal and semi-formal associations of educated Ukrainians who studied and promoted Ukrainian language, folklore, and history, and sometimes engaged in political discussion.

Historical context: The best-known was the Kyiv Hromada of the 1860s–1870s. Similar circles existed in Kharkiv, Poltava, Chernihiv, and Odesa. A "hromada" was also the Ukrainian term for a village community, and later political groups in Belarus and elsewhere borrowed the name.

Why it matters: Hromady show that the first stage of the Ukrainian movement was cultural and scholarly rather than mass political. They were networks of professionals, landowners, teachers, and students, not parties with peasant followings.

Imperial authorities responded with restrictions. The Valuev Circular of July 1863, issued by the Minister of Internal Affairs Petr Valuev at a moment of fear about Polish influence, restricted the publication of Ukrainian-language books for religious and popular educational purposes while permitting belles lettres. It was justified in part by the claim that a separate Ukrainian language did not exist and that Ukrainian publications were a pretext of Polish intrigue. The Ems Ukaz of 1876, signed by Alexander II at the German spa of Bad Ems after a commission examined alleged Ukrainian separatism, went further: it prohibited the import of Ukrainian publications from abroad, banned most new printing in Ukrainian apart from historical documents and some literary works under censorship, and forbade Ukrainian theatrical performances and public readings. The Kyiv section of the Geographical Society was closed, and the scholar and political thinker Mykhailo Drahomanov lost his university post and emigrated to Geneva, where he published Ukrainian political writing for readers in the empire.

These measures were consequential but also uneven. Enforcement varied by governor, by censor, and over time. Restrictions were eased somewhat in the 1880s and 1890s, and Ukrainian-language literature continued to appear within limits. Much activity moved to Galicia, in the Austrian Empire, where Ukrainians (then often called Ruthenians) could use their language in schools and public life. Organizations such as the Prosvita educational society (founded in Lviv in 1868) and the Shevchenko Scientific Society (1873, reorganized and expanded in the 1890s) developed an institutional base and a literary standard that circulated across the border and influenced activists in the Russian Empire. Galicia thus functioned as an alternative center of Ukrainian print and politics, a clear illustration of how state borders did not contain cultural movements.

A crucial point of method: language restriction does not prove that every Ukrainian-speaking peasant was already a political nationalist. Historians distinguish at least four levels: the use of a language in daily life; ethnographic identity, meaning awareness of shared customs; cultural activism, meaning the deliberate cultivation of the language and its literature by educated people; and mass political nationalism, in which large populations support a political program in the name of the nation. In the late nineteenth-century empire, the first was nearly universal in rural Ukraine, the second was widespread but locally defined, the third involved a small educated minority, and the fourth emerged only gradually, largely after the 1905 revolution loosened censorship and then, more decisively, during the revolution of 1917 (Chapter 50, Section 50.8). The first explicitly political Ukrainian party in the empire, the Revolutionary Ukrainian Party (RUP), was founded in Kharkiv in 1900 and remained small. Ukrainian-speaking peasants, workers in the Donbas and in southern cities, Jews, Poles, Russians, and Cossack-descended communities each had different relations to these movements. Cities in Ukraine were largely Russian-, Yiddish-, or Polish-speaking in everyday life; Ukrainian speakers were overwhelmingly rural. Social mobility often meant adopting Russian.

Belarus deserves separate treatment rather than being treated as an appendix. The Belarusian-speaking lands, part of the Grand Duchy of Lithuania until the partitions (Chapters 18 and 34) and known in official usage as the Northwestern Region after 1863, had a population divided by religion (Orthodox, Catholic, and Jewish communities, plus former Uniates) and by language and class. Polish gentry culture, Russian official culture, and local rural dialects all competed. After the 1863 uprising, which was crushed in these provinces under Governor-General Mikhail Muravyov (nicknamed "the Hangman" by his critics), the state pursued policies intended to reduce Polish and Catholic influence, including restrictions on Polish and on the use of the Latin script for Belarusian and Lithuanian publications. Many officials considered Belarusians to be Russian peasants, and considered the Belarusian language a dialect.

Belarusian cultural activity was smaller, and its institutional resources were thinner than those of Polish, Finnish, or even Ukrainian movements. It nevertheless existed. Ethnographers collected folklore and songs; writers such as Francišak Bahuševič, whose poems were published abroad in the 1890s, wrote in Belarusian and affirmed its dignity; and in the early twentieth century activists formed the Belarusian Revolutionary Hromada (1902) and later socialist organizations, with a Belarusian-language newspaper, Nasha Niva, appearing in 1906. Students should not measure Belarusian identity solely by whether it had become a mass nationalist movement before 1914. A more productive question is how identities were shaped when language, religion, class, and region cut across one another and when state policy and Polish and Russian cultural influence were both strong.

Section 45.4

Finland and the Baltic Provinces

Finland and the Baltic provinces make a valuable comparison because both regions possessed strong historical institutions and privileges, and both experienced late nineteenth-century centralization. But the outcomes differed, because the social structures differed, and because each experienced the empire differently.

Finland. Taken from Sweden in 1809 (Chapter 36), the Grand Duchy of Finland was not incorporated into the Russian state as a province. Alexander I confirmed its Lutheran religion and the existing laws, and the emperor ruled as Grand Duke. The Grand Duchy had its own Senate, its own administrative and judicial system, its own currency (the markka was introduced in 1860), and, after a long interval, a revived four-estate Diet that met in 1863 and thereafter regularly. A Finnish army was created under a conscription law of 1878. Language politics within Finland was itself an arena of change. Swedish had long been the language of the educated elite, though only a minority of the population (roughly one in eight in 1900) spoke it as their first language. The Fennoman movement, associated with figures such as J. V. Snellman and inspired by Elias Lönnrot's compilation of the Kalevala, promoted Finnish as a language of administration, education, and culture. A language decree of 1863 began a process by which Finnish gained official equality with Swedish. Thus Finnish nationalism was, in its first phases, directed less at Russia than at the Swedish-speaking elite within Finland.

The major confrontation with the imperial government belongs primarily to the reign of Nicholas II, not Alexander III. Officials and journalists in St. Petersburg argued that Finland's special status was an anomaly at a time of growing strategic concern about the Baltic and the capital's security. The appointment of Nikolai Bobrikov as governor-general in 1898 prepared the ground. The February Manifesto of 1899 (3 February O.S., 15 February N.S.) asserted the emperor's right to legislate for matters affecting the empire as a whole without the consent of the Finnish Diet, and thus put in question the interpretation of Finland's constitutional position. A language manifesto of 1900 made Russian the language of administration for higher offices, and a conscription law of 1901 abolished the separate Finnish army and imposed service in the imperial forces. Finns responded in 1899 with a mass petition, the Great Address, discussed in this chapter's Primary Source Window, and later with widespread passive resistance, including refusal to appear for conscription. Bobrikov was assassinated in 1904 by a young Finnish civil servant, Eugen Schauman. This conflict was exceptionally intense, but its resolution lies in the events of 1905, when the general strike in Finland and the October Manifesto led to the suspension of many measures (Chapter 46).

The Finnish case illustrates something important: a region with a legalistic political culture, high literacy, a functioning representative institution, and a defensive constitutional argument could resist through petitions, legal reasoning, and passive resistance. It was also true that many Finnish politicians, including some conservative Swedish-speaking elites, were willing to cooperate with the empire in exchange for the maintenance of local institutions. Most Finnish political actors before 1905 sought to preserve autonomy within the empire, not to leave it.

The Baltic provinces. The provinces of Estland, Livonia, and Courland (roughly modern Estonia and Latvia) were incorporated into the empire in the eighteenth century (Chapter 31) and kept a social order dominated by the Baltic Germans, a German-speaking Lutheran landowning and urban elite making up well under a tenth of the population. Their Landtage (provincial diets of the nobility), the German-language administration, municipal self-government in towns such as Riga and Reval (Tallinn), the German-language University of Dorpat (Tartu), and the Lutheran church gave them wide autonomy. Their privileges had been confirmed by successive emperors in return for loyal service, and many Baltic Germans served in the imperial army and bureaucracy at the highest levels.

Beneath this elite, the peasants and growing urban populations were Estonian and Latvian speakers. Their national movements took shape in the second half of the century. Estonian activists such as Jakob Hurt promoted the Estonian language and folklore; Friedrich Reinhold Kreutzwald's epic Kalevipoeg (published 1857–61) became a national literary symbol; and the first national song festival was held in Tartu in 1869. Latvian developments paralleled these: the "Young Latvians" of the 1850s–60s, the collection of folk songs (dainas) by Krišjānis Barons, and a song festival in Riga in 1873. These movements were cultural, educational, and economic at first. They often cooperated with, rather than opposed, the imperial government in their opposition to German landowning privilege.

From the 1880s the imperial government began introducing administrative and judicial measures to integrate the Baltic provinces: a senatorial inspection in 1882–83 recommended reforms; the judicial system was restructured in 1889 on the imperial model, replacing local German-language courts with Russian-language proceedings; police administration was reorganized; Russian became the language of administration and, increasingly, of secondary and higher education. The University of Dorpat was renamed Yuryev in 1893 and became Russian-language; Orthodox religious proselytism, which had produced waves of peasant conversions in the 1840s with expectations of land, was again promoted. The Lutheran church, a pillar of Baltic German identity, was constrained in its relations with Orthodoxy.

This case shows why Russification cannot be reduced to "Russians versus Latvians." The policy undermined an old privileged elite, the Baltic Germans, whose power Estonians and Latvians had themselves resisted. But the same measures reduced the use of Estonian and Latvian in schools, impeded their national movements, and imposed Russian as the language of advancement. Some Estonian and Latvian activists welcomed the weakening of German dominance but resented the imposition of Russian. In the 1890s a new generation, including socialists such as the Latvian "New Current" (Jaunā strāva) circle, linked national aspirations to social democracy, and many in the Baltic provinces were to play prominent roles in the revolutionary events of 1905, which saw intense violence against manor houses and German landowners. The relations among Baltic Germans, Estonians, Latvians, and the imperial state were therefore triangular rather than binary.

Section 45.5

Jews and the Pale of Settlement

Chapter 43 described the pogroms of 1881–82 and the restrictive laws that followed. This section places Jewish life in the empire in a wider frame. The 1897 census recorded about 5.2 million Jews, roughly 4 percent of the population and by far the largest Jewish community in the world at that date. The great majority lived in the Pale of Settlement, a belt of fifteen western and southwestern provinces, plus the ten provinces of the Kingdom of Poland, where Jewish residence was legally permitted. Residence outside the Pale was allowed only to certain categories (merchants of the first guild, those with higher education, some craftsmen, and some veterans). Within the Pale, Jews were concentrated in towns and small market towns (shtetlekh), where they sometimes formed a majority or near-majority of the population; in large cities such as Odesa, Vilna, Warsaw, Kyiv (where residence was restricted to certain districts), and Białystok; and in rural areas, though after 1882 the May Laws restricted new rural residence and landholding.

The legal framework went far beyond residence. Quotas introduced in 1887 limited Jewish enrollment in secondary schools and universities to a percentage of the total (10 percent inside the Pale, 5 percent outside, and 3 percent in the capitals), which pushed many young Jews to study abroad. Jews were excluded from certain state and municipal posts, limited in some professional fields (admission to the bar was restricted in practice), and, with the state liquor monopoly of the 1890s, were removed from the liquor trade in which many had worked. Expulsions from Moscow in 1891–92 removed many longtime Jewish residents. Jews were subject to military conscription, which was unpopular and often evaded, and few were promoted to officer rank. These rules were enforced unevenly and could be bent through bribery, special permissions, or changes of status, but they shaped life profoundly.

Economically, the Jewish population was occupationally distinctive but diverse. Many worked as artisans (tailors, shoemakers, carpenters), as petty traders, innkeepers, and peddlers, and in the grain, timber, and textile trades. Smaller numbers were bankers, railway contractors, factory owners, and professionals. Large numbers were poor, and many were underemployed. The idea that Jews were all merchants or all financiers ignores both the artisan and laboring majority and the exclusions that pushed so many into precarious trade. Industrialization (Chapter 44) both created opportunities, as in the textile industry of Łódź and Białystok and in sugar and tobacco production, and displaced traditional occupations.

Religion and communal life were central. The Pale's Jews included followers of Hasidism, a pietistic movement whose courts under charismatic rebbes drew communities across the southern and central regions; their opponents (mitnagdim), strongest in Lithuania with Vilna as an intellectual center; and growing numbers of the secular and the reform-minded. Religious institutions included synagogues, heders (elementary schools), yeshivas (advanced talmudic academies, for example at Volozhin), and charitable and burial societies. Official "crown rabbis" were appointed by the state for civil registration, an example of how the government tried to administer the community. Communal self-government had been weakened by earlier reforms, but private associations and philanthropy continued, including the schools founded by the Society for the Promotion of Enlightenment among the Jews of Russia and the Jewish Colonization Association established in 1891 by Baron Maurice de Hirsch to assist emigration and agricultural settlement.

Culturally, the period saw an expansion of Jewish creativity in several languages. The Haskalah, the Jewish Enlightenment, promoted secular education, Hebrew literature, and a reform of Jewish life, and in the empire it had advocates who hoped that the state would reward acculturation, including learning Russian. The Russian-Jewish press, which included the newspaper Voskhod, debated Jewish affairs in Russian. Yiddish literature flowered with writers such as Mendele Mocher Sforim, Sholem Aleichem, and I. L. Peretz, giving literary standing to the language spoken by the majority. Hebrew revival also began. Many Jews entered Russian-language schools and universities where they could, and some became prominent in law, medicine, journalism, music, and science.

The political and ideological responses to the crisis of the 1880s were diverse. The pogroms led some, such as Leon Pinsker, whose pamphlet Auto-Emancipation appeared in 1882, to argue that Jews needed a territorial national home, and the Hibbat Zion ("Love of Zion") societies organized small settlements in Palestine. In 1897 Theodor Herzl convened the First Zionist Congress in Basel, and the movement drew substantial support in the empire. At about the same time, in October 1897, the General Jewish Labor Bund was founded in Vilna: a Jewish socialist party that organized workers in Yiddish, advocated civil equality and, increasingly, national-cultural autonomy within a democratic state, and for a time played a major role in the Russian socialist movement (Chapter 46). Other Jews joined the Russian revolutionary movements as individuals, often moving away from Jewish communal identity. Others, especially religious traditionalists, rejected both Zionism and socialism and sought to preserve communal institutions. And many people, perhaps millions, chose or were driven to leave: on the order of two million Jews left the empire between 1881 and 1914, chiefly for the United States but also for Britain, Argentina, South Africa, and Palestine.

An interpretive question follows: did discrimination reinforce separate Jewish communal identity, encourage assimilation, stimulate emigration, promote revolutionary politics, strengthen Jewish nationalism, or all of these among different people? The evidence points to all of them, in varying proportions by region, generation, gender, and class. This is why Jewish history in the empire cannot be reduced to a sequence of pogrom, restriction, and victimization. Those were indispensable parts of the story, and the violence of the early 1880s and of the early 1900s (Chapter 46) was real and terrible. Yet Jewish communities were also actors who built institutions, literature, commerce, religious movements, political parties, philanthropic networks, and migration strategies. They did so while facing an official antisemitism that also drew on popular prejudice and on stereotypes in the press, and while treated, in the state's categories, as a distinct and suspect population.

Section 45.6

The Caucasus

Chapter 39 examined conquest in the Caucasus, including the long war with the North Caucasian imam Shamil, which ended in 1859, and the subsequent subjugation of the western Caucasus in 1864. This section turns to the imperial Caucasus after the conquest. The word "Caucasus" covers a region of extraordinary variety, and the first task is to avoid treating it as a single people or a single administrative unit.

The South Caucasus (Transcaucasia) was divided among several societies. Georgians, mostly Orthodox Christians but with their own church whose autocephaly had been abolished by Russia in 1811, had an old nobility, a peasantry emancipated in the 1860s on terms that left many landowners economically strained, and a cultural revival led by figures such as the poet and publicist Ilia Chavchavadze, who promoted Georgian language, schooling, and the Society for the Spreading of Literacy among Georgians (1879). Armenians, belonging largely to the Armenian Apostolic Church with its center at Echmiadzin, formed a prominent merchant and professional class in Tiflis, Baku, and other cities, and a peasant population elsewhere; they were also divided across the empire's border with the Ottoman Empire, where Armenians suffered the massacres of the 1890s. Azerbaijanis, whom contemporaries commonly called "Caucasian Tatars" or simply Muslims, were mostly Shia Muslims, along with a Sunni minority. Their intellectuals, including the playwright and philosopher Mirza Fath Ali Akhundov (died 1878) and the educator Hasan bey Zardabi, who founded the newspaper Akinchi in 1875, promoted modern education and language reform. The North Caucasus held Chechens, Ingush, Dagestani peoples, Circassians (Adyghe), Ossetians, Karachays, Balkars, and others, as well as Cossack communities (Terek and Kuban) and Russian and other settlers.

The conquest of the western Caucasus in the 1860s was accompanied by ethnic cleansing: Russian military operations destroyed Circassian villages and expelled or drove into flight, toward the Ottoman Empire, very large numbers of Circassians and other Muslim mountaineers (commonly estimated at roughly half a million from the western Caucasus in 1862–64), with heavy mortality from violence, disease, and hardship. Exact figures are debated. Whether these events should also be classified as genocide is contested among scholars and is a matter of public controversy among Circassian communities, but the coercive character of the displacement is not seriously in doubt. The northwestern Caucasus was demographically transformed, with Cossack and other settlers moving into vacated lands.

Administratively, the Caucasus was ruled for decades by a viceroy (namestnik) with wide powers, a position held from 1844 until 1882. In 1882, after the retirement of the last viceroy of that period, Grand Duke Mikhail Nikolaevich, the viceroyalty was abolished and the region was governed by a governor-general, a change that integrated it more closely into the imperial system; the viceroyalty was restored in 1905 and lasted until 1917. Tiflis (Tbilisi) was the administrative center and a major cultural city, where Armenians, Georgians, Russians, and others formed a multilingual urban society. Government in the region was a mixture of military administration in the mountains and civil institutions in the lowlands. Local Muslims had Spiritual Boards created in 1872, one for Sunnis and one for Shia, to oversee religious affairs. The state recruited local nobles into the officer corps and bureaucracy, a form of cooptation common across the empire.

Economic change was dramatic. As Chapter 44 explained, Baku became one of the world's leading oil-producing centers after the 1870s, with the Nobel brothers and other firms (including the Rothschild interests) investing heavily. By 1897 Baku had more than 100,000 inhabitants, and the oil fields drew workers from Azerbaijan, Persia, Dagestan, Russia, and Armenia. Armenians were prominent among oil entrepreneurs, Azerbaijani Muslims among the workers and, increasingly, among a new Muslim oil-owning elite, and Russians among administrators and skilled workers. Railways (the Transcaucasian line connecting Baku, Tiflis, and the Black Sea port of Batum) integrated the region into imperial and global markets. Baku thus became what we may call a multilingual and multiconfessional imperial city, a place where economic competition, class conflict, and ethnic and religious differences overlapped.

State policies in the Caucasus were not those of a uniform Russification. The government restricted the Georgian church's autonomy and promoted Russian in schools, but it also relied on Georgian nobles. It was suspicious of Armenian political and clerical networks, in part because of fears about links to Armenian communities abroad and revolutionary organizations. In the 1880s Armenian parish schools were closed or placed under state control, and in 1903 a decree ordered the confiscation of Armenian church property, which provoked widespread resistance, including armed protest, and led the government to retreat in 1905. At the same time, the region saw growth of Georgian and Armenian printing, schooling, theater, and political organization, including Georgian social democrats of the Mesame Dasi ("Third Group") from the 1890s. The violence between Armenians and Azerbaijanis in 1905–06 belongs to the revolutionary crisis (Chapter 46) and should be examined there rather than read back into the earlier decades.

Section 45.7

Central Asia

Chapter 39 described the early expansion into Central Asia, and Chapter 42 touched on its later phases. This section asks what imperial rule looked like afterward. The key distinction is between territories ruled directly and territories ruled as protectorates. The core of directly governed Central Asia was the Turkestan Governor-Generalship, created in 1867 with its capital at Tashkent and its first governor-general, Konstantin von Kaufman, a powerful figure who governed until 1882. Its provinces included Syr-Darya, Samarkand, and Fergana (the last created after the abolition of the Khanate of Kokand in 1876). To the north the nomadic Kazakh lands (called "Kirghiz" in imperial usage) were governed as the Steppe region, formally organized as a separate governor-generalship in 1882, with provinces such as Akmolinsk and Semipalatinsk. The Transcaspian region was conquered in 1880–81 after the capture of Geok-Tepe. The Emirate of Bukhara (1868) and the Khanate of Khiva (1873) became protectorates in which local rulers retained their titles and internal administration under Russian oversight, a system that endured until 1917.

Central Asian rule is useful evidence against the idea that Russification was one uniform empire-wide program. Officials in Turkestan generally did not attempt to assimilate the native population. They governed through existing local institutions, because they lacked personnel and feared disruption. Under the Turkestan Statute of 1886 the native population (which included sedentary Uzbeks, Tajiks, and others) kept village and district officials chosen locally, and Islamic judges (qadis) and customary courts (biys) continued to hear civil cases among natives. Local elites, including the aksakals (village elders), volost and district heads, and religious notables, were intermediaries in tax collection and order. Kaufman's policy has been described by historians as one of noninterference with Islam, partly out of prudence and partly out of a belief that Islam would weaken under the influence of Russian civilization. Natives of Turkestan were largely exempt from military conscription until 1916, when the attempt to mobilize them for labor precipitated a major uprising.

That did not make the system benign. The colonial regime was run by military officers and officials who held the native population in condescension and often disregarded its interests. It extracted taxes and land, in the steppe especially: the 1891 Steppe Statute treated lands occupied by nomads as state property held for their customary use and authorized the state to appropriate land it classified as "surplus" to nomadic needs, making such land available for Russian and Ukrainian peasant settlement, and the Resettlement Administration (established in 1896) organized migration. The much larger waves of settlers arrived after 1906 (Chapter 47), though earlier migration was already changing land use and conflicts over land, water, and pasture, to the disadvantage of Kazakh nomads. Russian towns and garrison quarters grew adjacent to old cities such as Tashkent, Samarkand, and Kokand, creating a visible spatial division between "Russian" and "native" city.

The economy changed under the influence of railways and cotton. The Transcaspian Railway, built to supply military operations from the 1880s, reached Samarkand in 1888, and connections to European Russia were extended in the following decades (the Orenburg–Tashkent line opened in 1906). Cotton cultivation, particularly of American upland varieties introduced from the 1880s, expanded rapidly in Fergana and elsewhere to supply the Russian textile mills, which were protected by tariffs from foreign competition (Chapter 44). As cotton replaced grain on many irrigated lands, Central Asia became dependent on grain imports by rail from Russia, an example of market integration that connected regional livelihoods to imperial and global prices. Merchant intermediaries, often local, profited; many cultivators became indebted.

Resistance and accommodation both occurred. A revolt in Andijan in 1898, led by a Sufi leader, Dukchi Ishan, attacked a Russian garrison and was suppressed with executions and exile; it showed Russian fear of religious networks. Yet Muslim notables also cooperated, sent children to the new Russo-native schools that began to appear in the 1880s, and used Russian courts when they saw advantage. Intellectuals within the Muslim community, discussed in the next section, began to debate reform. Students should avoid saying that modern Uzbek, Kazakh, Kyrgyz, Tajik, or Turkmen national identities already existed in their later forms. In the late empire people identified through combinations of locality, oasis, tribe or clan, religion, language, and dynasty, and the national categories that later became union republics were substantially shaped by Soviet policy in the 1920s (Chapter 53, Section 53.7).

Section 45.8

Muslims and Imperial Government

Muslims cannot simply be folded into Central Asia. The 1897 census counted roughly 14 million Muslims, about one in ten of the empire's population, living in the Volga-Ural region, Crimea, the North and South Caucasus, Central Asia, Siberia, and in cities throughout the empire. They included Tatars and Bashkirs, Kazakhs, Uzbeks, Azerbaijanis, Chechens, Dagestanis, Circassians, and many others, who spoke Turkic, Caucasian, Iranian, and other languages and who were divided between Sunni and Shia traditions, among different legal schools, and among Sufi brotherhoods. "Muslim" was a religious identity that overlapped with, but did not replace, ethnic, linguistic, and regional identities. Islam should not be treated as an ethnicity.

The empire had centuries of experience administering Islam. Since Catherine II's toleration policies of the 1770s–80s (Chapter 34), the state had created and funded clerical structures. The Orenburg Muslim Spiritual Assembly, established in 1788 and later based in Ufa, supervised mosques, clergy, and religious schools across much of the Volga-Ural region and, for a time, Siberia and the Kazakh steppe; a separate Taurida board in Crimea and the Transcaucasian boards created in 1872 covered other regions. The state appointed a mufti and approved clergy, recognized Islamic law in certain spheres such as family law and inheritance, and licensed mosques. This system carried obligations: clergy were expected to keep registers, support imperial authority, and refrain from "fanaticism." It created recognized institutions, but also gave the state leverage over them.

Imperial policy toward Muslims in the late century combined tolerance with missionary and educational pressure. Orthodox missionary work among Muslims in the Volga region, the educational ideas of the Kazan orientalist Nikolai Ilminsky (who promoted native-language Orthodox schooling intended to Christianize non-Russian populations and bind them more closely to the imperial order, an approach known as the Ilminsky system; it was not identical to immediate linguistic Russification), and regional supervision of Muslim clergy, schools, and religious institutions all reflected a desire to limit Islamic influence, though policy differed considerably across Muslim regions. Some baptized Tatars (Kräshens) and others who had been counted as Orthodox sought to return to Islam; many could do so only after the toleration decree of April 1905. Muslim schools (maktabs and madrasas) operated largely free from state supervision in many areas, though the Russian-language "Russo-native" schools were created in the Volga and Central Asia as alternatives.

Muslim societies were not passive. The Tatars of the Volga region, with a substantial merchant class and with access to a printing and educational network centered on Kazan, were among the most active. The Crimean Tatar educator and publisher Ismail Gasprinski (Gaspıralı) founded the newspaper Tercüman ("The Interpreter") in 1883 in Bakhchisarai, printing in a simplified language intended to be understood by Turkic readers across the empire and beyond, with Russian columns as well. He also promoted the "new method" (usul-i jadid) of teaching reading and writing, which gave its name to Jadidism: a movement of Muslim educational and cultural reform that advocated modern curricula, schooling for girls in some versions, and critical engagement with European knowledge while remaining within Islam. Jadids met opposition from conservative clergy (the "old method," usul-i qadim advocates) as well as from the authorities, who suspected them of Pan-Islamic or Pan-Turkic tendencies. Their ideas spread through Kazan, Orenburg, Ufa, Crimea, Baku, Bukhara, and Tashkent in the 1890s and early 1900s.

Students should distinguish several strands often confused. Islamic reform concerned the interpretation and practice of religion and education. Pan-Islamic ideas emphasized solidarity among Muslims across state borders, and were viewed with suspicion by the authorities, partly because of the Ottoman sultan-caliph's claims. Turkic linguistic and cultural movements stressed commonalities among Turkic-speaking peoples, as in Gasprinski's slogan of unity in language, thought, and action. Political nationalism, in the sense of demands for territorial autonomy or independence for a given people, emerged later, chiefly after 1905 and especially in 1917. Many Muslim intellectuals combined elements of these strands, but they were not identical, and the Muslim community had its own disagreements over them.

A good analytical question is whether state recognition of Islamic institutions integrated Muslims into the empire or created durable institutions through which Muslim society could organize independently. The answer is, plausibly, both. Recognition provided a framework of legal status, offered a degree of security, and gave Muslim elites an interest in the imperial order; at the same time, it preserved clerical authority, schools, publishing networks, trade connections, and pilgrimage routes (including the hajj to Mecca, often by sea from Black Sea ports), all of which linked Muslims to wider Islamic worlds. During 1905–1906, Muslim activists from across the empire held congresses and formed an empire-wide political organization, the Union of Muslims (Ittifaq al-Muslimin), building on networks developed in the preceding decades (Chapter 46).

Section 45.9

Russification and the Limits of Imperial Integration

Chapter 43 introduced Russification as a strategy of Alexander III's government. This section asks a harder question: what did Russification actually mean? The Russian word obrusenie was used by contemporaries, usually by critics, though some conservative nationalists also employed it, and historians have used "Russification" for a bundle of different measures that were not always implemented together. They included administrative centralization (reducing special regional institutions and applying imperial law), Russian-language bureaucracy (requiring Russian in offices and courts), Russian-language education (making Russian the language of instruction in state schools), restrictions on other languages (in print, schooling, and public performance), extension of Russian law, Orthodox missionary activity and favoring of Orthodoxy in civil life, reduction of regional privileges (of nobles, diets, and churches), appointment of Russian officials to posts formerly held by locals, and symbolic assertions of imperial culture in architecture, ceremony, and naming. A policy might include some elements and not others. For example, administrative centralization in the Baltic provinces did not require conversion of Lutherans, while the repression of Ukrainian print was not accompanied by a campaign to impose Orthodoxy on a population that was already largely Orthodox. In Central Asia, none of these programs was applied in comparable form.

It is therefore wrong to regard Russification as a single coordinated plan. Different ministries pursued different goals, governors-general sometimes acted on their own, emperors changed course, and policy was often reactive to perceived crises: the Polish rising, fears of Ukrainian separatism, anxiety about German influence, suspicion of Jewish and Armenian networks. Scholars have also stressed limitations of capacity. The state lacked enough Russian-speaking teachers and officials to impose Russian everywhere, village schools were few, and in many rural areas the state's presence was thin. Policy statements often exceeded what could be carried out on the ground.

Outcomes differed by region and social group. In some regions administrative integration strengthened control, as with the judicial reform in the Baltic provinces or the incorporation of Polish institutions. In other places, language and school policies provoked cultural nationalism, as with Estonian and Latvian responses, Polish clandestine schooling, or Finnish resistance. Elsewhere, local elites adapted and cooperated, as with many Georgian nobles who served in the imperial army or Polish and Baltic industrialists dependent on the Russian market. In still other areas, policy barely reached rural society: in Central Asia and among many Siberian peoples, daily life continued largely within local institutions. Among Jews, discriminatory legislation pushed some toward acculturation and others toward emigration or politics; Russian schooling attracted many who were denied access to it.

The deepest paradox of late imperial integration is that integration and resistance could reinforce each other. The railways, telegraphs, schools, universities, newspapers, statistical agencies, and growing cities (Chapter 44) that tied the empire together also provided activists with tools: print for vernacular literatures, communication across distances, trained professionals, statistics to document their communities, and social settings in which new identities could form. Migration to cities brought peasants into contact with competing languages, and mass literacy made written languages politically consequential. Even policies intended to promote Russian could serve other ends. Jewish students educated in Russian could read socialist literature; Tatar reformers used the empire's postal and railway networks to circulate newspapers; Ukrainian cultural activists borrowed methods from Russian ethnography. The paradox can be summarized this way: many of the institutions that tied the empire more closely together also made it easier for its peoples to imagine, organize, and communicate alternative political communities.

This does not mean that collapse was inevitable. Most national and religious movements before 1905 sought cultural rights, educational freedom, legal equality, or autonomy within the empire rather than independence. Many local elites remained loyal. The imperial army, in which large numbers of non-Russians served, held together through a major war in 1914–1917, and the empire's dissolution came after the strains of that war and the February Revolution (Chapters 48 and 49). Historians debate how far the nationalities problem contributed to the fall of the monarchy; most would say that the mobilization of 1905 revealed the potential for national movements but did not by itself determine the outcome. The fundamental late-imperial problem was therefore not simply diversity itself, but whether an increasingly centralized autocracy could govern diversity without turning cultural and regional difference into political opposition.

Primary Source Window

The Finnish "Great Address" (Suuri adressi), February–March 1899

Creator: Finnish civic organizers and signers · Date: collected February 1899, delivered to St. Petersburg in March 1899 · Type: Mass petition to the sovereign

Context: Following the February Manifesto of 1899, which asserted the emperor's right to legislate on matters of imperial concern without the Finnish Diet's consent, Finnish activists organized a nationwide petition asking Nicholas II to maintain the Grand Duchy's existing laws and constitutional rights. Signatures were gathered in a few weeks, and roughly half a million Finns signed. A delegation of about five hundred people traveled to St. Petersburg to present it, but the emperor declined to receive them. Another petition signed by about a thousand European intellectuals, sometimes called the Pro Finlandia address, also supported the Finnish case.

Source type: A petition addressed to the ruler by his subjects, a form of political action that a monarchy might tolerate more readily than open opposition. Library users should locate a published translation or edition of the full text rather than relying on this summary.

Brief paraphrase (not a verbatim quotation):

In respectful language, the petitioners affirm their loyalty to the emperor as Grand Duke, express anxiety that the new manifesto will undermine the laws and rights on which Finnish society has relied since the beginning of Russian rule, and ask that the existing legal order be preserved and that Finnish institutions be consulted on any changes.

Analysis: The petition does not demand independence. It speaks the language of legality, loyalty, and inherited rights, and its force came from its scale and from the evidence it gave of organization in a society with high literacy and many voluntary associations. This shows how a regional society could defend itself within the imperial framework. As a source, however, it presents a unified Finnish voice that does not capture divisions within Finland, for example between Swedish-speaking and Finnish-speaking elites, between constitutionalists willing to resist and those who preferred compromise, and between those who signed and those who did not, or who signed with varying degrees of enthusiasm. It also reflects an urban and organized perspective more than that of the poorest rural residents. The text cannot tell us what ordinary signers thought beyond the act of signing, nor can it explain the imperial government's motives, which must be studied in official correspondence.

  1. What does the petition ask for, and what does it not ask for?
  2. Who organized it, to whom was it addressed, and why might its authors have chosen this form?
  3. What can historians learn from the number of signatures, and what limits does that kind of evidence have?
  4. What additional evidence (government records, Finnish newspapers, Swedish-speaking elite correspondence) would help evaluate the petition's significance?
Historical Debate

What Was Russification?

The question: Was Russification primarily a modernization policy, a nation-building policy, an imperial-security policy, or an inconsistent combination of all three?

Interpretation A: Security and administrative rationalization. Some historians emphasize that many measures responded to perceived threats in border regions, especially after the Polish rising and in light of German power, and to the aim of standardizing a patchwork administration. On this view, language and school policies served efficiency and control more than any program of making all subjects ethnically Russian.
Interpretation B: Nationalizing impulse. Others stress that after the 1860s a current of Russian nationalism in the press, the bureaucracy, and the court sought to make the empire more Russian in language, religion, and symbols, and that this goal is visible in policies toward Ukrainians, Poles, Baltic Germans, and Finns. The tension between this impulse and the dynastic, multiethnic empire is central to this interpretation.
Interpretation C: Inconsistency and limited capacity. A third body of scholarship, associated with historians such as Theodore Weeks and Alexei Miller, among others, stresses the lack of coordination, the contradictions between goals, the reliance on local elites, and the state's limited reach, arguing that "Russification" names a cluster of uneven and often reactive measures rather than a coherent program. Studies of the Russian Empire as a multiethnic polity by historians such as Andreas Kappeler also emphasize its composite character.

Evidence: Historians use ministry and governor-general correspondence, laws and circulars, school curricula and enrollment statistics, censorship records, newspapers, memoirs, and local petitions. The 1897 census and regional statistics help measure outcomes such as literacy and language use.

Points of agreement: Scholars broadly agree that language, school, and administrative measures after the 1860s restricted several non-Russian cultures, that these measures were applied unevenly and differently from region to region, and that the Russian Empire did not succeed in creating a uniform Russian-speaking population.

Points of disagreement: Historians differ on how coherent the policies were, how much weight to give nationalist ideology compared to security and administrative aims, how effective the measures were, and how much they caused the growth of national movements as opposed to those movements arising from wider social changes.

Why it matters: The answer affects how we understand the empire's character: as a nationalizing state, as a composite dynastic empire adapting poorly to the age of nationalism, or as something between.

Student task: Choose two regions from this chapter and use evidence from the sections to decide which interpretation best fits each. Explain whether a single interpretation can fit both.

Multiple Perspectives

One Empire, Many Experiences: The Language of Schooling

An imperial education official: For officials responsible for schools in the western and Baltic provinces, requiring Russian instruction served several aims: it created a common administrative language, reduced the influence of Polish and German elites, and prepared graduates for state employment. Official reports tended to measure success by the number of Russian-language schools and the share of pupils learning Russian, not by what pupils thought.
A Polish teacher in the Kingdom: For Polish teachers and families, Russian-language instruction in state schools was both a constraint and a spur to organizing alternatives. Private lessons, clandestine reading circles, and "flying" classes passed on Polish literature and history, with the Catholic Church and family networks supporting them. Participation carried risks of arrest or dismissal.
An Estonian or Latvian peasant family: For rural families in the Baltic provinces, schooling had long been dominated by German-language institutions and Lutheran parish schools. Russian-language instruction brought new constraints, but it also created some access to state employment and a way to move beyond the German-dominated order. Many families used schools pragmatically.
A Jewish parent in the Pale: For Jewish families, access to Russian secondary and higher education was limited by quotas, so Russian-language schooling represented opportunity but also exclusion. Some sent children to Russian schools where possible, some to traditional heders, some to schools run by Jewish reform societies, and some abroad.
A Muslim reformer in the Volga region: For Jadid educators, the question was not only Russian versus local language but also what Muslim schools should teach. They created new-method schools teaching modern subjects within an Islamic framework, while also facing criticism from conservative clergy and suspicion from officials.

These perspectives show that "Russification" in education meant different things to different people, and that power was unequal: state officials had the authority to set the rules, while families and communities adapted, resisted, or exploited the openings available.

Cause and Effect

Imperial Integration and the Mobilization of Identities

Long-Term Conditions

An empire assembled over centuries from territories with different laws, religions, and elites; the spread of nationalism and of ideas of administrative uniformity in nineteenth-century Europe; expanding literacy and print culture.

Intermediate Developments

The Polish risings and the security fears that followed; the Great Reforms and universal conscription; railway building and urban growth (Chapter 44); creation of vernacular scholarship, schools, and cultural societies among many peoples.

Short-Term Triggers

Specific measures such as the Valuev Circular and Ems Ukaz, the Baltic judicial and language reforms of the 1880s, the Jewish quotas and May Laws, the February Manifesto of 1899, the Armenian church property decree of 1903.

Effects

Varied: administrative integration in some regions; cultural and political mobilization in others; cooperation by many local elites; emigration; new political parties across the empire (Bund, PPS, RUP, Finnish constitutional movement, Muslim organizations) that became active in 1905.

Alternative pathways: The chain is not simply "Russification → nationalism → revolution." Centralization could provoke resistance, but it could also succeed or be ignored. Education could promote acculturation or national activism or both. Railways and cities could integrate populations and also create new political networks.

Historical complexity: No single cause explains the late imperial nationalities problem: not state policy alone, not social change alone, and not the ideas of national movements alone. Each interacted with the others and varied by region.

Continuity and Change Over Time

The Multinational Empire in 1860 and in 1904

What Stayed the Same

  • The empire remained a composite of regions with different laws, religions, and privileges, governed by an autocratic monarch.
  • The Orthodox Church kept its privileged status, and religion remained a central legal category.
  • Local intermediaries, nobles, clergy, village elders, and merchants, remained essential to governing large areas.
  • Rural populations across the empire remained largely agrarian and for the most part not politically mobilized along national lines.

What Changed

  • The Kingdom of Poland lost most of its distinct institutions after 1863, while Polish society became more industrial and politically differentiated.
  • Finland's autonomy, secure for decades, came under direct challenge from 1899.
  • Baltic German privileges were reduced while Estonian and Latvian movements grew.
  • Railways, cities, and print created new networks: Baku, Łódź, and Odesa grew into major industrial or commercial centers.
  • New political organizations (the Bund, PPS, Zionist groups, Ukrainian and Belarusian political circles, Jadid networks) appeared.
  • Russian-language schooling and administration expanded, although unevenly.

Why the pattern matters: Change was fastest in cities, among educated groups, and in regions with strong institutions; continuity was strongest in villages and nomadic areas. A change of emperor or an individual decree did not alter society overnight.

Everyday Life and Social History

Living With Several Identities

Consider a composite figure, based on common patterns in the evidence rather than on any single documented individual: a clerk in a mid-sized western city around 1900. He may be an Orthodox Christian by baptism, a speaker of Ukrainian at home with his parents and of Russian at work, a literate reader of the Russian press, a member of the townsperson estate, a subject of the emperor, and a resident of a particular province and neighborhood. His wife, whose family came from a Polish Catholic landowning background, may speak Polish in private but use Russian in shops. His neighbor, a Jewish tailor, speaks Yiddish at home, reads a Hebrew or Yiddish newspaper, and may know enough Russian and Polish for business; his legal residence rights depend on his status. The clerk's employer might be a Baltic German or a Russian official.

None of these people's religion, legal estate, town, language, and imperial citizenship maps neatly onto a single modern nationality. People could shift among identities depending on situation: language at work and at home, religious festival and market day. For most, the more important distinctions in daily life concerned work, family, village or neighborhood, and local custom. National identity often became salient when something forced the choice: a school law, a census, a conscription notice, a newspaper campaign, or a violent incident.

Evidence for this kind of life is uneven. Memoirs and literature often come from the educated; police and court records capture episodes of conflict; census categories reflect the state's questions. Peasant and working-class voices are more difficult to recover, and historians should be cautious about assuming that elite activists spoke for them. Gender added further variation: women's access to education, public life, and property differed by religion and community, and women were prominent in some movements (Jewish and Polish socialist circles, Jadid girls' schooling) while excluded elsewhere.

Regional Context

Vilna: A City of Several Peoples

Vilna (Vilnius in Lithuanian, Wilno in Polish, Vilne in Yiddish) shows how the empire looked from outside the capitals. It was the former capital of the Grand Duchy of Lithuania (Chapter 18) and, in the late nineteenth century, a provincial capital in the Northwestern Region, with a population of roughly 150,000 in 1897, of whom about two-fifths were Jews. Poles formed a large part of the city's educated and landowning population, Russians were prominent in the administration and garrison, and Lithuanians and Belarusians lived in the surrounding countryside and, in smaller numbers, in the city. For Jews, Vilna was a major center of scholarship and Jewish culture, often called the "Jerusalem of Lithuania," and in 1897 it was the founding site of the Bund. For Lithuanians, the city's old Polish and Jewish character complicated later claims to it as a national capital.

After the 1863 rising, Governor-General Muravyov enforced the suppression of Polish institutions and the promotion of Russian. The ban on printing Lithuanian in Latin letters (1864–1904) led to a network of book smugglers who brought Lithuanian books printed in East Prussia across the border, and to underground schools; the policy was designed in part to separate Lithuanian speakers from Polish Catholic influence by introducing Cyrillic, but most Lithuanian Catholics refused it. Vilna illustrates how language, script, religion, and class could overlap and how state policy could unintentionally strengthen a cultural movement by turning reading in a particular script into an act of defiance.

Russia and the World

Three Empires and the Problem of Nationalities

The Russian Empire was not alone in facing the challenge of linguistic and national diversity in the nineteenth century. The Habsburg and Ottoman Empires also governed multiethnic, multireligious populations, and all three confronted nationalist movements, demands for representation, and the pressures of modernizing states. Comparison clarifies what was distinctive in the Russian case without suggesting that all three followed an identical path.

The Habsburg monarchy reorganized itself in 1867 into Austria-Hungary, granting Hungary broad autonomy, and gave Poles in Galicia wide self-government. It had a parliament with representation, and language disputes (such as the Badeni language ordinances for Bohemia in 1897) were fought through parliamentary politics. The Ottoman Empire, which had long governed through religious communities (the millet system), attempted the Tanzimat reforms from 1839 to create a more uniform imperial citizenship, and lost Balkan territories to national movements and great-power pressure. Russia, by comparison, had no parliament before 1906 and relied on the autocracy and bureaucracy, which limited the legal channels for the expression of national grievances, though it also governed some regions, such as Finland, through legal systems far more representative than its own center.

The lesson is not that all empires collapsed because of nationalism. Each experienced similar problems of language, representation, administrative centralization, and mobilization, and each responded differently. Their fates were also shaped by war, diplomacy, and economic change. Russian activists also connected with outside networks: Ukrainian activists with Galicia, Jews with Zionist and socialist organizations in Western Europe and America, Poles with Galicia and emigre centers, Muslim reformers with Istanbul and Cairo, Finns with European public opinion. The multinational empire was an international field of action.

Section 45.10

Chapter Review and Historical Thinking Checkpoint

This chapter has examined the Russian Empire as a multinational polity in the decades before 1905. Its central finding is that the empire remained politically unified while becoming more socially connected, educated, mobile, and communicative, and that these processes strengthened imperial integration in some respects and regional institutions, cultural movements, and competing ideas of political community in others. Before proceeding to Chapter 46, students should use the following checkpoint to compare the regional cases rather than merely repeat them.

Transformation and continuity. Between the 1860s and 1904 the empire changed less in its legal architecture than in the society operating within it. Differentiated rule, the privileged Orthodox Church, and reliance on local intermediaries persisted, while railways, cities, schools, and print created new networks and new educated groups. Poland lost most of its distinct institutions but became more industrial and politically varied; Finland kept its autonomy until the confrontation of 1899; the Baltic provinces saw German privilege reduced as Estonian and Latvian movements grew; Jewish communities built institutions and political movements under heavy legal restriction; Caucasian and Central Asian societies were integrated economically through oil, cotton, and railways while governed in contrasting ways.

Causation and evidence. The chapter's explanations were multicausal: security fears after 1863, ideas of administrative uniformity, limited state capacity, local elite strategies, and social and economic change all interacted. Evidence came from very different sources: the census, laws and circulars, petitions such as the Great Address, newspapers, literature, and memoirs. Each reflects the viewpoint of its creator, and the voices of peasants, workers, and many women are harder to recover.

Interpretations and significance. Historians agree that policies were uneven and did not produce a uniformly Russian-speaking population; they disagree about how coherent, how nationalist, and how consequential those policies were. The broader significance is that the empire entered 1905 with many organized communities and distinct grievances, but also with loyal elites and largely unpoliticized rural populations, so neither collapse nor national liberation was predetermined.

Central Checkpoint Question.

Did late imperial policies fail because they pursued too much uniformity, because they never achieved enough integration, or because a multinational empire could not resolve these competing goals through administrative policy alone? There is no single required answer. A strong response compares at least three regions, distinguishes among kinds of Russification, and avoids treating either national liberation or imperial collapse as inevitable. Students should also consider how continuity (the persistence of differentiated rule, rural social structure, and local intermediaries) interacted with change (cities, railways, print, and new parties), and where the available evidence is strongest and weakest.

Chapter Summary

This chapter examined the Russian Empire between the 1860s and the eve of the 1905 revolution as a multinational, multireligious, multilingual polity. Its central problem was whether the empire could achieve greater administrative and political unity without erasing the regional, religious, linguistic, and historical distinctions through which many subjects understood themselves. The 1897 census, which counted language and religion but not nationality, served as an entry point: it displayed both the state's categories and the diversity they could not fully capture.

Section 45.1 established the framework of differentiated rule. The empire had been assembled over centuries and left many local laws, elites, and churches in place, because it needed intermediaries and could not afford to replace them. The state pursued security, taxation, administrative efficiency, loyalty, religious hierarchy, and, increasingly, cultural integration. Imperial government had long survived by recognizing difference, while late nineteenth-century centralization increasingly sought to reduce it.

In Poland, the January Uprising of 1863–64 led to the dismantling of most distinct institutions, to Russian-language administration and schooling, and to a land settlement favoring peasants. Yet Polish society remained vibrant and became more industrial and differentiated, and Polish nationalism itself changed from insurrectionary Romanticism to positivist organic work, socialism, and national democracy. Class divisions meant that Poles did not respond to imperial policy as a single bloc.

For Ukrainians and Belarusians, the chapter stressed that imperial officials often saw three branches of one Russian people, while activists argued for distinct languages and histories. The Valuev Circular and Ems Ukaz restricted Ukrainian print, but enforcement varied and activity shifted partly to Galicia. Language restriction did not mean mass nationalism; language use, ethnographic identity, cultural activism, and political nationalism were distinct. Belarusian identity, with weaker institutions and a population divided by religion and class, developed more slowly and should not be judged by later outcomes.

The comparison of Finland and the Baltic provinces showed that two regions with strong privileges experienced centralization differently. In Finland the major confrontation came under Nicholas II, with the February Manifesto of 1899, and was met by mass petitions and passive resistance; Finnish politics were also shaped by the internal language question. In the Baltic provinces, Russification weakened the Baltic German elite while constraining Estonian and Latvian cultural movements, creating a triangular rather than binary relationship.

The Jewish population of the empire, concentrated in the Pale of Settlement, lived under residence, educational, and occupational restrictions, and suffered violence, but also created religious, literary, commercial, philanthropic, and political institutions. Haskalah, Hasidism, Yiddish and Hebrew literature, Hibbat Zion and Zionism, the Bund, revolutionary activism, and mass emigration were different responses to the same conditions, so the history cannot be reduced to victimization.

In the Caucasus and Central Asia, the chapter turned from conquest to post-conquest governance. The Caucasus was divided among Georgians, Armenians, Azerbaijanis, North Caucasian peoples, and settlers; Tiflis and Baku became multilingual centers, and policies toward churches and schools varied. Central Asia was ruled through a military-led system that mostly preserved local institutions, including Islamic courts, while railways and cotton integrated its economy into the empire. These cases show that Russification was not applied uniformly, and they warn against projecting later national categories backward.

Muslims lived across several regions and were governed through recognized clerical institutions such as the Orenburg Assembly. State recognition both integrated Muslims and provided structures for independent organization. Jadid reformers such as Ismail Gasprinski promoted new schools and print, and students were asked to distinguish Islamic reform, Pan-Islamic ideas, Turkic cultural movements, and later political nationalism.

Section 45.9 synthesized the regional cases. Russification was a bundle of administrative, linguistic, educational, religious, and symbolic measures that were not always implemented together, and outcomes ranged from effective administrative integration to cultural mobilization, local cooperation, and minimal impact. The paradox of integration was that railways, schools, print, and cities that tied the empire together also gave activists tools to organize alternative political communities. The historiographical debate over whether Russification was modernization, nation-building, security policy, or an inconsistent mixture remains open, with broad agreement on unevenness but disagreement about coherence and causation.

The chapter prepares for Chapter 46 by showing which society entered the crisis of 1904–1905: an empire with many organized communities, new parties, and distinct grievances, but also with many loyal elites and largely unpoliticized rural populations. The events of 1905 and after (Chapter 47) tested whether the monarchy could accommodate these differences; the later revolutions and wars (Chapters 48–52) determined how, and in what forms, the empire's peoples would emerge. Nothing in the chapter requires that the outcome was predetermined.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows which society entered the crisis of 1904–1905. The empire was not a Russian nation-state with a few minorities, but a composite polity whose subjects were distinguished by law, religion, language, estate, and region, and whose government increasingly sought uniformity in an age of expanding communication and mobilization. That tension shaped the revolutionary period, in which workers, peasants, national and religious movements, and liberal and socialist parties pressed their demands at once (Chapter 46).

What continued afterward was the problem of governing diversity under changing political systems. The Duma monarchy (Chapter 47) allowed legal parties, press, and representation, and the First World War (Chapter 48) stressed the empire's frontier regions through occupation, deportation, and mobilization. In 1917 many of the movements described here, along with new ones, claimed autonomy or independence, while the Bolsheviks later built a federal structure organized by nationality (Chapters 50–53). The relation between those developments and the late empire is a matter of continuity and change, not simple inheritance: Soviet nationality categories, for example, drew on imperial classification and scholarship but also reconfigured them.

The chapter also reveals larger themes of the textbook: the importance of regional and frontier perspectives, the need to distinguish evidence from later memory, the interaction of state policy and social change, and the caution required when applying modern national categories to earlier periods. Several questions remain open: how far Russification policies shaped identities as opposed to reflecting existing divisions; how best to recover the views of rural and working-class populations; and whether concepts such as colonialism or nation-building clarify or obscure particular regions. Students should carry these questions into Chapter 46 without assuming that the outcome of 1917 or 1991 was already determined in 1900.
