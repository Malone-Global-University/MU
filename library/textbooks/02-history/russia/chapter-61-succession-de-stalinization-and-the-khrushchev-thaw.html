Part XI — The Post-Stalin Soviet Union
61

Succession, De-Stalinization, and the Khrushchev Thaw

A History of Russia: From the Eurasian World to the Russian Federation

Chapter Opening

Between 1953 and 1962 the Soviet Union dismantled much of the machinery of mass terror and began to speak about Stalin's rule in ways that would have been fatal a few years earlier. It did so without surrendering the Communist Party's monopoly on power. This chapter asks how that was possible, and why the result was so incomplete.

On March 9, 1953, Moscow buried Joseph Stalin. For three days his body had lain in state in the Hall of Columns, and hundreds of thousands of mourners had converged on the center of the capital. The crowds were enormous and poorly managed, and in the streets around Trubnaya Square people were crushed by the press of bodies and by cordons of trucks and soldiers. No reliable count of the dead was ever published; later estimates range from hundreds upward, and the uncertainty is itself evidence of how tightly the regime controlled information. Foreign observers and Soviet witnesses alike described real grief among many mourners, alongside fear, bewilderment, and in some cases private relief. For a generation that had known no other supreme leader, the question "what happens now?" had no obvious answer.

Barely three weeks later, on March 27, the government announced an amnesty that opened the gates of labor camps for roughly a million prisoners. In early April, Pravda told its readers that the "Doctors' Plot," announced in January with great fanfare as a conspiracy of Kremlin physicians, had been a fabrication, and that the investigators had used illegal methods. By midsummer the most feared man in the country, Lavrenty Beria, chief of the combined security and police ministry, had been arrested by his own colleagues. By December he had been shot. These events show that the dismantling of Stalinism began in the first months after the dictator's death, years before the better-known speech of 1956.

That speech is nonetheless central. On the night of February 25, 1956, at the close of the Twentieth Congress of the Communist Party of the Soviet Union, First Secretary Nikita Khrushchev addressed a closed session of delegates and denounced Stalin's abuse of power, chiefly his persecution of loyal Communists. The text was read aloud to party and Komsomol meetings across the country in the following weeks, but it was not published in the USSR until 1989. Its effects spread far beyond the delegates: it shook communist parties abroad, encouraged returning prisoners and their families to speak, and forced Soviet citizens to reconsider what they had been taught about the recent past. Yet the speech was also carefully limited. It attacked a "cult of personality" and spoke of violations of Leninist norms; it did not question the one-party state, the October Revolution, collectivization, or the party's right to decide what could be said.

This chapter therefore studies a paradox. The Soviet Union moved away from Stalin's personal dictatorship and from the system of mass arrests, quotas, and executions that had defined the late 1930s, and it did so under leaders who had themselves served Stalin. The camps shrank; prisoners were released and many convictions reversed; the security police were reorganized and placed under greater party control; writers, filmmakers, and poets explored subjects that had been closed; and a younger generation gained access to ideas and foreign contacts unknown to its parents. At the same time, the party retained the power to censor, to prosecute dissent, to close churches, and, as at Novocherkassk in June 1962, to open fire on striking workers. De-Stalinization was real, uneven, contested, and bounded.

The chapter's subjects are of three kinds. The first is political: a struggle among Stalin's lieutenants, ending with Khrushchev's ascendancy and the defeat of rivals such as Malenkov, Molotov, and Kaganovich, and the sidelining of a popular war hero, Marshal Georgy Zhukov. The second is institutional and social: the transformation of the Gulag and the security police, and the difficult return of millions of prisoners and deported peoples to a society that often did not know how to receive them. The third is cultural: the Thaw, named after a 1954 novella by Ilya Ehrenburg, which gave Soviet readers, viewers, and listeners new themes and new voices while leaving the boundaries of permitted expression in the hands of the party.

The evidence for these events is unusually varied and unusually contested. Party records opened after 1991 allow historians to reconstruct the Presidium's deliberations; prisoners' memoirs, camp records, and rehabilitation files document the Gulag's transformation; the text of the Secret Speech, a leaked version of which was published in the West in 1956, can be compared with the official Soviet text; and the Thaw left a rich record in journals, films, poems, and letters. But each of these sources was produced with purposes in mind, and some, particularly later memoirs by the principal actors, must be read with caution. Questions about motive, such as why Khrushchev chose to denounce Stalin, remain open to disagreement.

This chapter is thematic rather than strictly chronological. It overlaps with Chapter 62, which treats Khrushchev's agricultural, housing, and administrative programs, the space program, and the Cold War crises of his era, and it follows Chapter 60's account of late Stalinism and Stalin's death. It also looks ahead: the limits of the Thaw prepare the ground for the dissident movement examined in Chapter 64, and the unfinished business of de-Stalinization resurfaces during the glasnost of Chapter 65.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why Stalin's death in March 1953 created a crisis of succession in a highly personalized and centralized political system.
  2. Describe the structure of the collective leadership and the relationships among the Presidium, the Central Committee, the Council of Ministers, and the security apparatus.
  3. Analyze the reforms of 1953, including the repudiation of the Doctors' Plot and the amnesty, as evidence that de-Stalinization began before Khrushchev's consolidation of power.
  4. Assess the fall of Beria and the reasons his colleagues acted against him, distinguishing documented facts from disputed reconstruction.
  5. Trace the transformation of the Gulag through amnesty, prisoner resistance, administrative reform, and rehabilitation, distinguishing these as separate processes.
  6. Compare the treatment of the deported peoples restored to their homelands with that of communities denied equivalent rights of return.
  7. Interpret the Secret Speech as a historical source, identifying what it acknowledged, what it omitted, and the political purposes it served.
  8. Evaluate competing explanations of why Khrushchev and the leadership chose to confront Stalin's legacy.
  9. Explain how Khrushchev defeated the "anti-party group" and Marshal Zhukov and why the party, rather than any individual or the army, remained the center of authority.
  10. Describe the cultural Thaw across literature, film, poetry, and the arts, and identify the boundaries imposed by censorship and campaigns against writers and artists.
  11. Distinguish de-Stalinization from democratization, and assess whether it reformed the Soviet system or only the methods by which that system exercised power.
  12. Connect the political, social, and cultural developments of 1953–1962 to the later dissident movement, the Brezhnev era, and Gorbachev's reforms.

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  9. Did de-Stalinization transform the Soviet political system, or did it reform the methods through which that system exercised power?

Historical Context

By the winter of 1952–1953 the Soviet Union was a victorious but exhausted great power. It had won the war against Nazi Germany at a cost of some twenty-six to twenty-seven million dead by commonly cited estimates, rebuilt much of its devastated western territory, and tested its first atomic weapon in 1949 (Chapter 60). It was also a society in which fear was structural. The terror of 1937–1938 (Chapter 57) had killed several hundred thousand people in a little over a year, and the camp and colony system of the Gulag had grown into a vast economic and punitive institution. At Stalin's death in early 1953, roughly 2.5 million people were held in camps and labor colonies, and millions more lived in "special settlements" as deportees, among them whole nationalities removed from their homelands during the war (Chapter 58).

Stalin's late years had been marked by a revival of repression: the so-called Leningrad Affair of 1949–1950, in which senior officials were executed; anti-cosmopolitan campaigns that targeted Jewish intellectuals; the Mingrelian case in Georgia; and, in January 1953, the announced Doctors' Plot, in which a group of mostly Jewish physicians were accused of murdering Soviet leaders. Historians continue to debate whether a new purge of the top leadership was imminent. What is clear is that the senior figures around Stalin, including Molotov, Mikoyan, and Beria, had grounds for anxiety, and that Stalin had restructured the party leadership at the Nineteenth Congress in October 1952 in ways that diminished some of his oldest associates.

The institutional structure that Stalin left behind was formally elaborate and substantively personal. The Communist Party was the ruling organization; its Central Committee and the smaller Politburo, renamed the Presidium in 1952, nominally directed policy. The Council of Ministers administered the state and economy. The security police, through the Ministry of State Security (MGB) and the Ministry of Internal Affairs (MVD), controlled the camps, surveillance, and investigation of political crimes. Yet in practice Stalin had made decisions personally, played subordinates against one another, and reduced regular party institutions such as congresses and plenums to occasional rituals. When he died, the formal institutions remained but the person who had made them function was gone.

The successors shared a common problem and a common limit. The problem was how to govern without a dictator while preventing any one of them from becoming the next. The limit was that all of them had been complicit in Stalinist policy, and any extensive account of repression would implicate them. This is the central tension of the chapter. The leaders needed to relax some of the most dangerous features of Stalinism, not least the terror that threatened themselves, but could not repudiate the system that gave them power. Their choices were also shaped by the international environment: the Korean War was ending, the Cold War continued, and the Soviet position in Eastern Europe depended on pliant communist governments that themselves faced popular discontent.

The society that responded to the first reforms was also changing. Wartime and postwar experiences had produced millions of veterans, a growing urban population, and a rising number of educated specialists. Many peasants and workers hoped that Stalin's death would bring relief from the harsh postwar regime of procurements, low wages, and compulsory loans. Intellectuals who had survived the late-Stalinist ideological campaigns were cautious but alert to signals. Historians such as Elena Zubkova have emphasized that expectations of change were already building in Soviet society in the postwar years, which helps explain why signals from above produced such rapid responses below.

Division I — Stalin's Death and the Struggle for Succession, 1953–1955 · Section 61.1

61.1 — Stalin's Death and the Crisis of Succession, March 1953

Stalin suffered a stroke at his dacha at Kuntsevo on the night of February 28–March 1, 1953, after an evening with Beria, Malenkov, Khrushchev, and Bulganin. His guards, unwilling to disturb him, did not summon help until the next day, and physicians reached him only later; many of the best-known specialists had been arrested in the Doctors' Plot and were not available to treat him. He died on the evening of March 5. Accounts of those days come largely from the memoirs of participants and from later investigations, and some details, including claims that Beria or others deliberately delayed medical care, rest on testimony that cannot be fully verified. What the evidence does show is that the people closest to Stalin were frightened of him even as he lay incapacitated.

Public announcements were made in stages, beginning on March 4 with a bulletin on his illness and followed by the announcement of his death on March 6. The press and radio presented the loss in the language of national catastrophe, and mourning was both organized and, for many people, sincere. Soviet citizens had been taught for two decades to attribute victory in the war and the achievements of industrialization to Stalin's leadership. For many, his death produced real alarm about the future, particularly among those who associated him with wartime victory and social advancement. Others, including survivors of the camps and families of the repressed, reacted differently, though they had every reason to conceal their feelings in public. Surviving evidence of private reactions comes from diaries, letters, memoirs, and later oral histories, and it shows a wide range of responses rather than a single mood.

The funeral on March 9 was marked by the crowd disaster in central Moscow already mentioned. Authorities offered no public accounting, and the number of victims remains uncertain. The event is a reminder that the regime's capacity to organize mass ritual exceeded its ability, or willingness, to protect the participants. The body was placed in the Mausoleum beside Lenin's, now bearing both names, a juxtaposition that would be reversed in 1961 (§61.15).

The deeper problem was institutional. Stalin had occupied two formal offices, General Secretary of the party (a title in practice largely overshadowed by his personal authority) and Chairman of the Council of Ministers, but his power derived from decades of accumulated control over appointments, the security services, and the ideological interpretation of Marxism-Leninism. No rule specified how this authority should pass. Lenin's death in 1924 had provoked a prolonged struggle (Chapter 54), and the memory of that conflict, as well as the fates of those who lost it, loomed over the successors. They resolved that no one person should for the moment hold all the main levers.

Within hours of Stalin's death, a combined meeting of the Central Committee, the Council of Ministers, and the Presidium of the Supreme Soviet approved a sweeping reorganization. The leaders were conscious of the risk of popular disorder and of rivalry among themselves. They also acted with unusual speed to consolidate the security apparatus, merging the MGB into the MVD under Beria. The measure concentrated formidable powers in one man's hands, which would prove consequential (§61.4). The immediate message to the public was continuity: "the party and the government" would continue Stalin's course. What no one could say was whether that would be true.

Section 61.2

61.2 — Collective Leadership and the Reorganization of Soviet Power

The arrangement announced in March 1953 established a ten-member Presidium of the Central Committee, reduced from the much larger body Stalin had created in October 1952. Its members were Malenkov, Beria, Molotov, Voroshilov, Khrushchev, Bulganin, Kaganovich, Mikoyan, Saburov, and Pervukhin. The government was headed by Georgy Malenkov as Chairman of the Council of Ministers, with Beria, Molotov, Bulganin, and Kaganovich as first deputy chairmen. Molotov returned as Minister of Foreign Affairs, Bulganin became Minister of Defense, with Marshal Zhukov among his deputies, and Kliment Voroshilov became chairman of the Presidium of the Supreme Soviet, in effect the ceremonial head of state. Beria headed the new, merged Ministry of Internal Affairs. Within days, on March 14, Malenkov left his position as a party secretary, leaving Nikita Khrushchev as the leading figure in the Central Committee's Secretariat.

The arrangement is commonly called collective leadership. Its significance lies less in the label than in the distribution of power it implied. Malenkov, who had been Stalin's closest deputy in the central apparatus, appeared the heir but held the state post rather than the party one. Beria controlled the security services and the camps, and also oversaw atomic and other strategic projects. Khrushchev, a less prominent figure, controlled the party apparatus, the network of regional and republic secretaries whose careers depended on the Central Committee Secretariat. Molotov and Kaganovich brought the prestige of the old guard. Each constituency, the government, the police, the party, and the elder statesmen, had its representative, and none could easily act without the others.

This structure made the relationship among institutions a political issue. In theory the Communist Party led the state: Central Committee plenums elected the Presidium and Secretariat, and party organizations supervised ministries and local soviets. In practice during the Stalin years the line between party and state had blurred, and the security police had often acted as an independent power. The successors' task was to decide where authority lay. Several of them, including Khrushchev and Malenkov, had reason to favor stronger party control over the police, since the police had been the instrument of terror against party officials themselves. Others, including Beria, preferred a state apparatus unified under strong executive leadership. The rivalry among these conceptions of power would shape the next four years.

Historical Vocabulary
Collective Leadership and the Party-State

Definition: Collective leadership refers to the Soviet principle, publicly proclaimed after Stalin's death, that policy should be made jointly by the senior members of the Presidium rather than by one individual. The party-state describes a system in which a single ruling party controls the institutions of government, the economy, the armed forces, and the security services, so that formal state offices operate under party direction.

Historical context: After March 1953 the term was used to contrast the new leadership with Stalin's "cult of personality." It was never a stable constitutional arrangement. Within months, the party leader Khrushchev and the state head Malenkov, as well as Beria, competed for primacy, and by 1957 the competition had resolved in favor of the First Secretary.

Why it matters: The phrase helps students avoid two errors: treating the post-Stalin period as already "Khrushchev's regime" from March 1953, and treating collective leadership as a genuine power-sharing democracy. It was a temporary balance among rival members of one ruling party, held together by shared fear of a return to the terror and by mutual suspicion.

Section 61.3

61.3 — The First Retreat from Stalinism: Policy Changes in 1953

The first months of 1953 saw an unusually rapid series of changes in policy, many of them associated with Beria and Malenkov, though others in the leadership supported or accepted them. The most dramatic was the reversal of the Doctors' Plot. On April 3–4, 1953, the press announced that the physicians arrested in connection with the "plot" were innocent, had been freed, and that the officials responsible for the case had used impermissible investigative methods. The statement also announced the arrest of the former deputy minister of state security Mikhail Ryumin as the main fabricator. This was an extraordinary admission, since it amounted to acknowledging that the security organs had fabricated a political case at the highest level. It also blunted the antisemitic implications of the affair, although the broader official hostility to Jewish cultural life did not disappear.

The leaders also moved against other late-Stalinist cases. The Mingrelian affair in Georgia was reviewed, and some officials purged in connection with it were restored. Some investigations of the "Leningrad Affair" were reopened over the following months, and after the trial of Viktor Abakumov, Stalin's last state security minister, in December 1954, further rehabilitations of its victims followed. In the sphere of public pronouncements, the press shifted tone: editorials criticized the "cult of personality" in general terms, particularly in the months after Stalin's death, and Stalin's name appeared less often. In September 1953 the Special Boards (OSO) of the security police, which had sentenced people administratively without trial, were abolished, removing a principal tool of extrajudicial repression.

The question of motive is important. Beria is often presented as the driving force of the first reforms. His memoranda to the Presidium in March 1953 recommended the amnesty, proposed ending a number of costly Gulag construction projects, and criticized some security practices. Some historians interpret these as the actions of a pragmatic administrator aware of the economic inefficiency of mass forced labor and eager to win favor with the elite and the public. Others note that Beria had been the organizer of the Great Terror's final stages and that his reform proposals were consistent with a bid to secure his own power. Both can be true. The leaders as a group, however, had incentives to reduce the arbitrary police power that had endangered them, and Malenkov and Khrushchev also supported many of the measures. The retreat was a leadership-wide response to a shared insecurity, not simply one man's initiative.

It was also, from the beginning, limited. Statements favoring legality did not extend to dismantling the one-party system, and the regime continued to treat "counterrevolutionary" crimes as a distinct category. The press did not discuss the scale of repression. The leaders' public posture was that there had been mistakes by individual officials, not a systematic policy. These constraints would shape every subsequent step.

Section 61.4

61.4 — Beria's Fall: Security Power and Political Conspiracy

Lavrenty Beria had headed the NKVD from late 1938 and had overseen the Soviet atomic project and large parts of the Gulag economy. After March 1953 he held the merged MVD, which controlled the police, border troops, the camps, and the intelligence services. He used his position to push a series of initiatives. In addition to the amnesty and the reversal of the Doctors' Plot, he supported a changed nationality policy in the republics, recommending that local cadres rather than Russians be promoted in places such as Lithuania, Ukraine, and Belorussia, and that the use of local languages be encouraged. He also reportedly argued for a more flexible Soviet approach to Germany, though the evidence for his specific proposals is thin and has been used selectively by both his accusers in 1953 and later historians. In June 1953, widespread unrest in East Germany culminated in strikes and demonstrations on June 17 that were suppressed with Soviet military force; Beria's rivals later cited the crisis as evidence of his mismanagement.

His colleagues had several reasons for alarm. He controlled the only armed forces other than the army that were directly under Presidium-level command. He possessed files on every leading figure, including themselves, and he had been the executor of Stalin's terror. His reform proposals could be read either as signs of liberalism or as preparations to secure support for a seizure of power. Khrushchev, Malenkov, Molotov, and others concluded that he had to be removed, and they enlisted military leaders, notably Marshal Zhukov and General Kirill Moskalenko, to ensure that Beria's guards could be neutralized.

Beria was arrested on June 26, 1953, at a Presidium meeting in the Kremlin. The details are disputed. Moskalenko's later memoir and Khrushchev's recollections differ on who gave which orders and how Beria was seized, and some accounts, based on later claims, hold that he was killed at the time of arrest rather than after a trial. Historians consider the basic sequence, a coordinated action by the Presidium and the military, to be well established, while the particulars rest on memoirs written long afterward by participants with reasons to emphasize their own roles. The arrest was announced on July 10 after a Central Committee plenum that denounced him as an enemy of the party and the state. A special tribunal presided over by Marshal Ivan Konev tried him and six associates in December in a closed proceeding. They were convicted of treason, terrorism, and conspiracy against the state, and shot on December 23.

The episode set an important precedent. For the first time since the revolution, the leading members of the Soviet government had removed a rival by arrest without his having been defeated in a party vote, but the removal was carried out by the collective leadership acting together and defended in the name of the party. It also fixed an official interpretation: the terror was attributed to Beria and a few associates, not to the system or to Stalin. This allowed the leaders to blame individual villains and to retain the principle that the party itself was blameless. Beria's fall also marked a turning point in the status of the security police, which lost its autonomous leader and was subordinated more fully to party oversight (§61.8).

Section 61.5

61.5 — Malenkov, Khrushchev, and the Contest for Leadership, 1953–1955

With Beria gone, the principal rivalry lay between Malenkov and Khrushchev. Malenkov's base was the government. In August 1953 he told the Supreme Soviet that the country should expand the production of consumer goods and reduce taxes on the peasantry, a program journalists called the "New Course." Its aim was to improve living standards, which remained low and uneven, and to relieve pressure on a countryside strained by high procurement quotas. He also spoke publicly of the danger of nuclear war to civilization in 1954, a statement that departed from Soviet orthodoxy that war would destroy only capitalism.

Khrushchev's base was the party. After March 1953 he used his control over the Secretariat to place allies in regional and republic party posts, and in September 1953 a Central Committee plenum named him First Secretary, a title that replaced General Secretary and reflected the leaders' intention to avoid recreating Stalin's position. At the same plenum he presented a critical review of Soviet agriculture and secured increases in state procurement prices and reductions in obligations on peasant households, issues that would grow into the programs treated in Chapter 62. Khrushchev also launched a campaign in 1954 to bring virgin and fallow land in Kazakhstan and Siberia into cultivation, which both strengthened his reputation as an energetic reformer and linked him to the party organization that would carry it out.

The leadership struggle took the form of a policy debate over priorities. Khrushchev and his allies accused Malenkov of favoring light industry at the expense of heavy industry and defense, the traditional priorities of the Soviet economy and of the military and industrial managers. At a Central Committee plenum in January 1955 Malenkov was criticized for these positions, and on February 8, 1955, he submitted his resignation as Chairman of the Council of Ministers, acknowledging a lack of experience, according to the published statement. He was replaced by Nikolai Bulganin, an ally of Khrushchev, while Zhukov became Minister of Defense. Malenkov stayed in the Presidium as a deputy premier and was assigned to the electric-power sector. The irony of the episode, in which Khrushchev opposed a consumer-oriented program and then adopted elements of it himself in later years, has been widely noted by historians.

Several features of the contest are worth noting. First, it was not a contest between "reformers" and "conservatives" in any simple sense: Malenkov was more consumer-oriented, but Khrushchev was more willing to proceed with rehabilitation and later criticism of Stalin. Second, the outcome depended on control of institutions, particularly the party's personnel system, not on public appeal. Third, the decisive force was the Central Committee, whose members, chiefly regional secretaries, valued security of tenure and had little interest in a premier-centered system that reduced the party's role. This foreshadowed the outcome of 1957 (§61.16). Fourth, the defeated were not executed, imprisoned, or even, at this point, expelled from the leadership. This was itself an important change from Stalin's era: political defeat no longer meant physical destruction, which made it possible for rivals to survive and return to fight again.

Division II — The Retreat from Terror and the Transformation of Soviet Justice · Section 61.6

61.6 — The 1953 Amnesty and the Beginning of the Gulag's Transformation

On March 26, 1953, Beria sent the Presidium a memorandum observing that the camps and colonies held about 2.5 million people, that many were serving short sentences for non-dangerous offenses, and that holding them was costly and economically inefficient. He proposed releasing a large group. The decree was issued the next day, March 27, in the name of the Presidium of the Supreme Soviet. It freed prisoners sentenced to terms of up to five years, reduced by half the sentences of those serving longer terms, and released categories of prisoners regardless of sentence, among them minors, pregnant women, women with young children, the elderly, and the seriously ill. It also halted pending cases against people accused of certain offenses. Archival figures indicate that roughly 1.2 million people were released under the amnesty, though totals vary by source and by how the categories are counted.

The amnesty was broad in numbers but narrow in its coverage of political prisoners. It excluded those convicted of "counterrevolutionary" crimes under Article 58 of the criminal code, of banditry, of premeditated murder, and of large-scale theft. Because political prisoners typically had long sentences, few of them benefited. The great majority of those released were convicted of ordinary offenses, including many sentenced under harsh Stalin-era laws on workplace absenteeism, petty theft, and similar offenses. Their release had social consequences: contemporaries and later historians, notably Miriam Dobson, have documented public anxiety about crime in 1953 and afterward, with rumors blaming the amnesty for disorder, even though the available evidence for a large surge is mixed.

The amnesty also began to alter the Gulag as an economic system. The camps had contributed to mining, construction, forestry, and industrial projects in remote regions, and a sudden removal of labor forced managers to find alternatives. Beria had also moved to halt some Gulag construction projects whose value was doubtful, and in 1953 administrative responsibility for the camps was briefly shifted to the Ministry of Justice, an arrangement that did not last. Economists in the leadership, and some administrators, had concluded that forced labor was less productive than free labor with incentives. The transformation was gradual: camp populations declined over the following years, as sentences were reviewed, shortened, or ended, and as the regime shifted toward colonies and ordinary prisons, but the formal central administration of the Gulag persisted until 1960. The system's economic logic and its political one were in tension, and both pushed toward change.

Section 61.7

61.7 — Resistance Inside the Camps: Norilsk, Vorkuta, and Kengir

The amnesty left the political prisoners in place, and in the months after Stalin's death and Beria's arrest, many of them concluded that conditions might change. Their expectations met a camp administration that was uncertain of its own authority. The result was a series of strikes and uprisings, the largest in the history of the Gulag, in 1953 and 1954. They were possible partly because the camps of the postwar years contained many more political prisoners with military experience, including veterans and members of the nationalist underground from Ukraine and the Baltic states, who had organizational skills and in some camps well-developed networks.

The first major action occurred at Norilsk, in the Arctic north of Krasnoyarsk Krai, where prisoners in the Gorlag complex mined nickel and copper. In late May 1953, after guards shot prisoners in one camp division, strikes spread across several camps and continued into the summer. Prisoners organized committees, put forward demands, and for a time held parts of the camp zone. At Vorkuta, in the Komi Republic, a large coal-mining complex, a strike began in mid-July at one mine and spread to numerous others. In early August, guards shot into a group of strikers, killing dozens according to most accounts. At Kengir, a camp division of Steplag near Dzhezkazgan in the Kazakh SSR, a revolt began on May 16, 1954, after guards fired on prisoners. Inmates took control of the camp zone, expelled the guards, and for about forty days held it, creating a governing structure that included committees for defense, propaganda, and services. On June 26, 1954, troops with tanks retook the camp. Official figures for deaths in the suppression are lower than those given by former prisoners, and the exact toll remains uncertain.

The prisoners' demands were notably concrete. They called for review of their cases, shorter sentences, the removal of numbered uniforms and barred windows, an end to restrictions on correspondence, an eight-hour working day, and punishment for guards who had shot prisoners. Many of the strikers explicitly insisted that they were loyal Soviet citizens wrongly convicted, a position that fit the regime's own language about rectifying "mistakes." In several camps, commissions of senior officials from Moscow came to negotiate, and in some cases made partial concessions. The authorities used force when negotiations failed, and organizers were prosecuted or transferred to harsher regimes.

The strikes mattered for several reasons. They demonstrated that the camp system could no longer be run on the same terms, because prisoners, many of them skilled and organized, would resist and because the administration could not count on unquestioning obedience. They produced reforms: revisions to camp regimes, relaxation of the isolation of prisoners, higher wages for some forced laborers, and, most importantly, the establishment of review commissions to examine political convictions. The uprisings thus belong in the history of de-Stalinization as one of its causes, not only as a result. They also show that the transformation of the Gulag was not simply a gift from the leadership but resulted in part from pressure by those held inside.

Multiple Perspectives

The Camp Uprisings, 1953–1954

Prisoners: Many political prisoners, including former officers, nationalists, and ordinary people convicted under Article 58, had expected release after Stalin's death. They saw the amnesty's exclusions as proof that the injustice continued. Their demands for case review and better conditions reflected both survival needs and a claim to recognition that they had been wrongly treated. Camp administrators and guards: Officials faced contradictory signals from Moscow, which was releasing some prisoners while insisting on discipline. Others saw the strikes as a threat requiring decisive force. The government did both, negotiating in some cases and using troops in others.

These perspectives were unequal in power. Prisoners' accounts come mostly from later memoirs, while the official record reflects the perspective of those who suppressed the strikes; each type of evidence must be tested against the other.

Section 61.8

61.8 — Reforming the Security Apparatus and Restoring Socialist Legality

After Beria's arrest, the combined MVD was divided again. On March 13, 1954, the government created the Committee for State Security, the KGB, as a body attached to the Council of Ministers, and appointed General Ivan Serov, a veteran of the security services, as its chairman. The new organization took over intelligence, counterintelligence, and the investigation of political crimes, while the MVD retained ordinary policing and, for a time, the camps. The change reduced the concentration of power that Beria had held and signaled that the security organs were to serve the party and government, not rule independently.

The reorganization went beyond structure. Following the abolition of the Special Boards in 1953, political cases returned in principle to the courts, and the regime emphasized "socialist legality," the idea that officials and security organs must follow written law. The Procuracy, which supervised legality, received greater authority to review the work of the police, and the party's Central Committee took a more direct role in overseeing security appointments. In 1954–1955 numerous officials associated with the worst abuses were dismissed, and some were tried: Ryumin, the architect of the Doctors' Plot, was executed in 1954, and Abakumov was tried and shot in December 1954 for his role in the Leningrad Affair. These trials, held in closed or limited settings, served to assign blame to particular individuals.

The KGB's capacity to punish political opposition remained intact. The new Criminal Code of the Russian republic, adopted in 1960, replaced the infamous Article 58 with narrower provisions, but retained offenses such as "anti-Soviet agitation and propaganda" (Article 70) and treason, which carried severe penalties, and the other republics adopted parallel codes. The security services expanded their surveillance and developed new methods, such as preventive "warnings" and psychological pressure, intended to deter dissent without mass trials. The difference from Stalin's era was real, as the numbers of political prisoners fell dramatically and arbitrary mass arrests ended, but it was a difference of method and scale rather than of principle.

Cause and Effect

Why the Security Apparatus Was Reorganized After Beria

Long-Term Conditions

The terror of the 1930s and late 1940s had made the security police a source of fear for the party elite itself, and the scale of the camp system imposed economic and administrative strain.

Intermediate Developments

The creation of a merged MVD under Beria in March 1953 concentrated police, camp, and intelligence functions; the amnesty and the Doctors' Plot reversal weakened the security organs' credibility.

Short-Term Triggers

Beria's arrest in June 1953 and the camp strikes of 1953–1954 highlighted the danger of an autonomous police chief and the fragility of control over the camps.

Effects

Abolition of the Special Boards, creation of the KGB in 1954, stronger party supervision, trials of selected police officials, and continued capacity for political prosecution.

Historical complexity: The reorganization served the leaders' self-protection, party control, and economic rationalization at once. No single motive explains it, and none implied that political dissent would be tolerated.

Section 61.9

61.9 — Review, Release, and Rehabilitation of Political Prisoners

The central question for the political prisoners left in the camps was whether their convictions would be reviewed. From 1954, the government established commissions to examine the cases of prisoners convicted of counterrevolutionary crimes, and the pace accelerated after the camp strikes. In 1955 and especially 1956, mobile commissions traveled to camps, reviewed files, and recommended release for tens of thousands of prisoners. Following the Twentieth Congress, review became broader, and by 1956–1957 most surviving political prisoners were free, though many remained in exile or restricted residence. The reviews varied widely in thoroughness and speed. Some commissioners took the process seriously; others processed cases quickly, and applied political tests, including whether a prisoner had acknowledged guilt or had been convicted of what officials considered serious crimes, such as collaboration with the Germans or participation in nationalist insurgencies. Prisoners from Ukraine, Lithuania, Latvia, and Estonia who had fought in armed anti-Soviet movements were typically treated as less eligible, and many were released later or on different terms.

Rehabilitation was a separate matter. It meant a legal determination that the original conviction had been unjustified because no crime had been committed, usually followed by restoration of civil rights, and sometimes of party membership, rank, or property. Many rehabilitations were posthumous, since the person had been executed or had died in custody, and families received a certificate stating that the case had been closed for lack of evidence. The process was bureaucratic and often slow. It was commonly not accompanied by a public acknowledgment, explanation, or compensation beyond limited payments, and officials who had fabricated cases generally went unpunished. Rehabilitation was also selective: leading figures from the early revolutionary opposition such as Nikolai Bukharin, Trotsky's contemporaries in the 1930s trials, remained officially condemned, and Bukharin's rehabilitation did not come until 1988 (Chapter 65).

Historical Vocabulary
Amnesty, Release, Rehabilitation, and Exoneration

Definition: An amnesty is a general act of clemency that ends or shortens sentences without determining that convictions were wrong. Release means that a prisoner leaves custody, whether through amnesty, completion of a sentence, or review. Rehabilitation (reabilitatsiya) in Soviet usage was a legal act annulling a conviction as unfounded and restoring rights. Exoneration, in common English, is an acknowledgment of innocence; Soviet rehabilitation often functioned as a more limited bureaucratic finding.

Historical context: In 1953 the amnesty released many prisoners without rehabilitating anyone. In 1955–1957 many political prisoners were released, and many convictions annulled, but formal restoration of reputation, employment, housing, and social standing often lagged, and returnees continued to face suspicion.

Why it matters: The distinctions explain why the experience of people leaving the camps differed so widely, and why historians avoid treating "release" and "rehabilitation" as interchangeable. They also show how the regime could correct individual injustices without acknowledging systematic responsibility.

The return of millions of people to Soviet society was one of the most significant social consequences of de-Stalinization. Returnees faced difficulties finding housing and work, often in towns where they had been residents before arrest and where their rooms had been given to others. Family relationships had changed: spouses had remarried, children had grown up as the offspring of "enemies of the people." Neighbors and coworkers varied in their responses, some sympathetic, others hostile or uncomfortable, and some had denounced or testified against the person now returning. Miriam Dobson's research on returnees shows that popular attitudes were mixed, that public fears of returning criminals mingled with sympathy for the wrongly convicted, and that these tensions shaped official policy. Memoirs by returnees, such as those later published by Memorial and other archives, describe both the joy of reunion and the persistent stigma. In some cases the restoration of rights occurred long after release.

The partial character of rehabilitation became a theme of Soviet cultural life, particularly after 1956, when writers and readers began to explore the experience of return. It also set the terms of a continuing debate. Those who had been rehabilitated demanded a fuller public reckoning; the authorities preferred to close the topic. The tension surfaces repeatedly in the cultural and political events of the following years, and it reappears in the 1980s when glasnost reopened the question of the victims' memory.

Section 61.10

61.10 — The Return of Deported Peoples and the Limits of Nationality Rehabilitation

During and after the Second World War the Soviet government deported entire nationalities from their homelands on collective charges of disloyalty, including Chechens and Ingush, Karachai, Balkars, Kalmyks, Crimean Tatars, Volga Germans, and Meskhetian Turks, among others (Chapter 58). The deportees were sent mostly to Kazakhstan, Central Asia, and Siberia, lived under the special-settlement regime, and suffered high mortality in the first years. Their autonomous republics and oblasts were abolished, their territories reassigned, and their histories and languages suppressed in public life. At Stalin's death, the deportees numbered in the millions, and the special-settlement regime remained in force.

After 1953, the regime began to relax the controls. In 1954–1956 it ended administrative supervision and removed many restrictions, and a decree in April 1956 lifted special-settlement status for several groups, including Crimean Tatars, Volga Germans, and Meskhetians, but without the right to return home or to reclaim confiscated property. In his Secret Speech Khrushchev explicitly criticized the deportations of the Karachai, Kalmyks, Chechens, Ingush, and Balkars as arbitrary acts, and in January 1957 the government restored the Chechen-Ingush autonomous republic, the Kalmyk autonomous oblast (raised to a republic in 1958), the Kabardino-Balkar republic (with the Balkars), and the Karachai within a joint Karachai-Cherkess autonomous oblast. Many of these peoples returned in 1957–1958.

The criteria for restoration were political and geographic, not simply moral. The Caucasian peoples and Kalmyks were returned to territories that were still comparatively accessible and that the government wished to resettle with a loyal population; the restoration of their autonomies could be presented as the correction of an injustice by Stalin. Crimean Tatars, by contrast, were not permitted to return to Crimea, which had been resettled with Russian and Ukrainian populations and from 1954 belonged administratively to the Ukrainian SSR. They were rehabilitated from the charge of treason only in a decree of 1967, and large-scale return began only in the late 1980s. Volga Germans received a decree in 1964 lifting the accusation of collective guilt, but their autonomous republic on the Volga was not restored. Meskhetian Turks, deported from Georgia in 1944, were not allowed back. The difference is the key point: de-Stalinization restored some national rights and left others unrestored, depending on the leadership's strategic interests and the political cost of displacing the new inhabitants.

Regional Context

The Return to the North Caucasus

For Chechens and Ingush, return after 1957 meant reclaiming a homeland that had been reorganized in their absence. Houses were occupied by settlers from neighboring regions; part of the Ingush territory, the Prigorodny district, had been transferred to North Ossetia and was not returned. Returnees competed with resident populations for land, housing, and jobs, and local authorities sometimes obstructed return. In 1958 in Grozny, violent disorder broke out between Russian residents and returnees, which the authorities suppressed. The unresolved status of the Prigorodny district later became a source of conflict between Ingush and Ossetians, which erupted violently in 1992 (see Chapters 67 and 68 for the post-Soviet period, including the Chechen wars).

These episodes show that rehabilitation did not end the consequences of deportation. Returning peoples had to reconstruct communities, recover property, and restore languages and institutions, while the memory of the deportation became a founding part of their modern national identities.

Division III — The Secret Speech and the Politics of De-Stalinization · Section 61.11

61.11 — The Twentieth Party Congress and the Decision to Confront Stalin's Legacy

The Twentieth Congress of the CPSU met in Moscow from February 14 to 25, 1956, the first congress since Stalin's death and the first since 1952. In its public sessions, delegates heard reports that revised several established ideological positions. Khrushchev's report for the Central Committee argued that war with capitalist states was not inevitable, that different countries might take different roads to socialism, and that peaceful coexistence was the basis of Soviet foreign policy. Anastas Mikoyan, speaking in the open sessions, criticized aspects of Stalin's leadership, including his role in the party's history, and he did so in terms the foreign delegations noticed. These were significant revisions of Stalin-era doctrine, but they did not yet constitute a public account of repression.

The decision to address repression directly was made in the months preceding the congress, and it was not unanimous. The returning prisoners and the rehabilitation process had created pressure. Party veterans released from camps, many of them with high-level connections, were writing to the Central Committee, and the review commissions were turning up evidence of fabricated cases. At the end of 1955, the Presidium established a commission headed by Pyotr Pospelov, a party historian and secretary of the Central Committee, to investigate the repression of party members in the 1930s, particularly of the delegates to the Seventeenth Congress of 1934. The commission reported in early 1956 on the scale of arrests and executions among party cadres and on the falsification of evidence. The numbers it gave, which concern mostly members of the party elite, were by later standards partial, but they were shocking to the leadership.

Within the Presidium, Molotov, Kaganovich, and Voroshilov resisted a public denunciation, partly because they had signed execution lists and partly from conviction that attacking Stalin would damage the party's legitimacy. Khrushchev, supported by Mikoyan and others, argued that the leadership should speak first, before the facts emerged in other ways, and that silence was riskier. A compromise was reached: the issue would be presented not in the public sessions but in a closed meeting after the congress's official work was over. Khrushchev would deliver a report on the "cult of personality" based on the Pospelov commission's findings.

Section 61.12

61.12 — Khrushchev's Secret Speech, February 25, 1956

On the night of February 24–25, 1956, after the congress had formally concluded its business and elected the new Central Committee, Khrushchev addressed delegates in a closed session. Delegates from foreign communist parties were not present. The speech, officially titled "On the Cult of Personality and Its Consequences," lasted several hours. It was not a spontaneous outburst but a prepared report that drew on the Pospelov commission's work and on Khrushchev's own additions, and it was delivered largely from a written text.

The core of the speech was a criticism of Stalin's treatment of the party. Khrushchev began by citing Marxist-Leninist condemnations of personality cults and then invoked Lenin's last writings, in which Lenin had expressed doubts about Stalin's temperament and proposed that he be removed as General Secretary (Chapter 54). He then described the mass arrests of the 1930s, noting that a large majority of the delegates to the Seventeenth Congress of 1934, and of the Central Committee members and candidates elected there, had been arrested and in many cases shot. He cited the figures that 1,108 of 1,966 delegates were arrested on charges of counterrevolutionary crimes, and 98 of 139 members and candidates of the Central Committee were shot. He described the murder of Sergei Kirov in 1934 as the pretext for repression, said that Stalin had approved methods of torture in investigations, and discussed the Leningrad Affair and the Doctors' Plot as products of fabricated evidence. He accused Stalin of reckless unpreparedness for the German invasion in 1941 and of ignoring warnings, of directing the war from a globe rather than from experience, and of arbitrarily deporting entire nations, naming the Karachai, Kalmyks, Chechens, Ingush, and Balkars. He criticized Stalin's break with Yugoslavia and the cult that had surrounded him, including the glorified accounts of his role in the party's history.

The speech's omissions are equally important for historians. Khrushchev focused chiefly on the repression of party members and leading cadres, not on the far larger numbers of peasants, workers, and ordinary citizens arrested and killed. He did not discuss collectivization, dekulakization, or the famine of 1932–1933 (Chapter 56), nor the repression of national minorities other than the deported peoples he mentioned, nor the early terror under Lenin. He did not question the party's monopoly of power, and he presented Stalin's rule as a distortion of Leninist norms, not as a product of the system's own logic. He also avoided discussing his own role and that of other leaders present in the hall, who had participated in policies he condemned. He thus gave an account of Stalin's crimes that implicated Stalin and his henchmen, especially Beria, while sparing the party and, to a significant extent, its senior officials.

Reports from participants indicate that the hall was largely silent and that some delegates were shaken, though the contemporaneous record is thin. The official text was circulated as a closed document to party organizations. A version reached Western governments and was published by the New York Times in June 1956, after it was obtained in part through Eastern European channels; the Soviet Union did not publish its own text until 1989. Students should note that the speech exists in several versions, and historians compare the leaked and later official texts.

Historical Debate

Why Did Khrushchev Denounce Stalin?

The question: Why did a leader who had served Stalin loyally for decades, and who risked his position by doing so, deliver a speech that attacked the dictator's record?

Interpretation A — Political calculation: Some historians stress that the speech served Khrushchev's struggle for power. By raising Stalin's crimes, he weakened rivals more closely tied to the repression, such as Molotov, Kaganovich, and Malenkov, and presented himself as the defender of the party against its former tyrant. On this reading, the decision was driven by factional advantage and the need to manage the consequences of returning prisoners and rehabilitation.
Interpretation B — Conviction and moral reckoning: Others, including Khrushchev's biographer William Taubman, give weight to personal belief. Khrushchev appears to have been genuinely disturbed by what the Pospelov commission found and by the stories of returning comrades, and he insisted on speaking despite resistance from colleagues. On this view, political advantage and personal conscience reinforced one another.
Interpretation C — Systemic need: A third approach emphasizes the needs of the elite as a class. Party and state officials wanted security from arbitrary terror and an end to Stalin's unpredictability, and the system needed a more efficient and legitimate way to rule. Denouncing the cult allowed the elite to make a break while protecting the system. Scholars working from this perspective note that the speech limited its criticism precisely to protect the institutions that sustained the leadership.

Evidence: Historians use records of Presidium discussions, the Pospelov commission's materials, memoirs by Khrushchev and associates, the speech text and its drafts, and evidence of the leadership's actions before and after 1956.

Points of agreement: Most agree that the speech was a deliberate and calculated act, that its focus on party victims reflected the limits of what the leadership would acknowledge, and that political considerations were important. Most also accept that the pressure of released prisoners and rehabilitation material mattered.

Points of disagreement: Historians differ over how much weight to give personal conviction relative to calculation, and over whether the leadership expected the speech to remain closed or anticipated its wide circulation.

Why it matters: The answer shapes whether de-Stalinization is understood as a genuine moral turning point, a tactical maneuver, or a defensive reform of the system.

Section 61.13

61.13 — The Reception of the Secret Speech in the Soviet Union and Abroad

Within days, the Central Committee organized readings of the speech at party and Komsomol meetings throughout the Soviet Union, including in workplaces, institutes, and military units. Millions of people heard it, though not every citizen, since ordinary non-party members were not systematically included, and the text itself was not distributed for retention. The meetings were controlled, but they did not always stay on script. Participants asked questions that the speaker could not answer, such as why those present had not known earlier, why Khrushchev and others had not acted, and why the speech criticized Stalin but left the party unchanged. Reports of such discussions were collected by party and security officials. Reactions varied: many listeners, including former prisoners and relatives of the repressed, felt vindicated; some were disillusioned; others, including some veterans and younger people who identified with Stalin's wartime leadership, were angered or confused.

The most violent reaction occurred in Soviet Georgia, Stalin's homeland, where he was an object of national pride as well as of the cult. In early March 1956, on the anniversary of Stalin's death, demonstrations in Tbilisi turned into protests that included demands for the preservation of his reputation and, in some accounts, for Georgian national rights. On March 9, troops fired on a crowd, killing and wounding people; estimates of the dead range from a few dozen to more. The episode illustrates the tangled meanings the Stalin question could have: for Georgians the denunciation could be felt as an insult to the nation, not just a political event.

Elsewhere, the speech encouraged critical discussion among some students and intellectuals, and the party responded with warnings against going beyond the official line. A Central Committee resolution of June 30, 1956, "On Overcoming the Cult of Personality and Its Consequences," was published and offered an authorized version: the cult had been a deviation, caused by specific circumstances and Stalin's personal qualities, that had not changed the nature of the Soviet system. The resolution urged against "slander" of the party and system. Thus the public version was more restrictive than the speech itself, and the leadership relied on the resolution to channel debate.

Russia and the World

Responses Within International Communist Movements

The speech's content was transmitted to foreign communists, partly through briefings to leaders of fraternal parties and partly through the Western press. For parties outside the Soviet bloc, such as those in France and Italy, the revelations created a crisis of credibility, since they had defended Stalin and the Soviet system for decades. Many members left; others pressed for greater independence. Palmiro Togliatti, leader of the Italian Communist Party, gave an interview in June 1956 that argued the Soviet system should be understood as one model among several, an idea later summarized as "polycentrism." In Western Europe and the United States, the speech contributed to a decline in communist membership and the rise of a "new left."

In Eastern Europe, where Soviet-backed governments ruled, the effects were direct. In Poland, the death of Stalinist leader Bolesław Bierut in Moscow in March 1956 and the Poznań workers' protests in June contributed to a political change in October. In Hungary, the revolt of October–November 1956 followed the loosening of controls and was suppressed by Soviet forces (treated in Chapter 62). The Chinese leadership under Mao Zedong praised some aspects of the criticism of the cult but objected to how Khrushchev had done it and to the damage to the standing of Stalin and the communist movement; this was an early element of the Sino-Soviet disagreement that grew in the following years. Thus a speech meant to resolve a domestic problem triggered a series of crises across the Soviet sphere.

Section 61.14

61.14 — De-Stalinization in Institutions, Symbols, and Public Memory

De-Stalinization appeared in many forms. In official culture, it meant a changed treatment of Stalin: his portraits were removed from many public buildings, his name was dropped from the national anthem in the 1950s (the anthem continued without words for years), and his works were reissued less often. Historians of the party revised the official history, and the Short Course of the history of the Communist Party, the canonical text of 1938, fell out of official use. New textbooks and encyclopedias reduced Stalin's presence and adopted more cautious language. Memoirs by marshals of the Second World War began to describe wartime events differently, though within limits. Rehabilitation continued in a legal sense, and more victims were publicly named, mostly posthumously.

Yet these changes were selective. The leadership did not abolish the system of ideological control, and the official line continued to claim that Stalin had made a decisive contribution to building socialism and winning the war. Monuments to him remained in many places in the 1950s, and in his native Georgia his reputation remained strong. Fundamental institutions of the Stalinist economy, including collective farms, central planning, and the priority of heavy industry, were retained, though modified. The difference between symbolic repudiation and structural reform is a key analytical distinction. Symbolic acts changed the language in which the Soviet past could be discussed; structural reform would have required changes in how political power was held and exercised, which the leadership declined.

The leadership also managed memory through control of archives and publication. Information about repression was available to the party leadership and to review commissions but not to the public, and historians had little access to sensitive materials. The effect was that Soviet citizens learned a great deal about Stalin's personality and about the repression of party members, and very little about the broader mechanisms and the numbers. Polly Jones's study of the Soviet debate on the Stalinist past emphasizes that discussion in these years was both energetic and tightly bounded, and that people drew on private networks, memoirs, and literature, rather than official history, to understand what had happened.

Section 61.15

61.15 — The Twenty-Second Party Congress and the Second Wave of De-Stalinization, 1961

By 1961 Khrushchev was stronger politically than in 1956, having defeated the anti-party group (§61.16), and he returned to the issue of Stalin at the Twenty-Second Congress, which met from October 17 to 31. The congress adopted a new party program promising that the USSR would build the foundations of communism within about two decades, and new party rules that required regular turnover of leadership bodies. It also featured open criticism of Stalin and of Molotov, Malenkov, and Kaganovich, who were denounced by name as participants in the repression. Delegates spoke of Stalin's crimes in public sessions, and one, the old Bolshevik Dora Lazurkina, claimed that she had received advice in a dream from Lenin that he should not remain beside Stalin. The speech was an emotional illustration of the congress's tone and also a gesture toward what the party leadership wanted: the removal of Stalin's body from the Mausoleum.

The congress voted to do so, and on the night of October 31–November 1 the body was removed and reburied in the necropolis by the Kremlin wall. In the following weeks, Stalin monuments were taken down across the Soviet Union and in Eastern Europe, and places bearing his name were renamed. On November 10, Stalingrad, the city whose defense had become the emblem of Soviet victory, was renamed Volgograd, and Stalino in Ukraine became Donetsk. These acts had considerable symbolic power, and some officials and veterans opposed the renaming of Stalingrad, which was associated with wartime sacrifice and not just with Stalin. The Chinese delegation, led by Zhou Enlai, left the congress early after criticizing the attack on Albania's leadership and the public treatment of Stalin, signaling the widening Sino-Soviet split.

The second wave also had limits. The congress did not provide a full account of the victims' number. Khrushchev proposed, and the congress endorsed, the idea of erecting a monument to the victims of Stalinist repression in Moscow, but it was not built in his time. Public discussion remained controlled, and in 1962 the publication of Solzhenitsyn's camp novella (§61.20) occurred with the leader's personal approval but was not followed by a full opening of the archives. The 1961 congress also showed how the leadership linked the anti-Stalin message with a vision of future prosperity, which tied the credibility of de-Stalinization to the economic promises treated in Chapter 62. When those promises later faltered, the entire reform project became vulnerable.

Division IV — Khrushchev's Consolidation and the Limits of Political Reform · Section 61.16

61.16 — The Anti-Party Group Crisis of 1957

The years 1956–1957 strained Khrushchev's position. The Hungarian crisis and unrest in Poland (Chapter 62) raised fears that de-Stalinization had gone too far, and critics inside the leadership blamed him for the loss of control. His economic initiatives, including a plan announced in 1957 to replace most industrial ministries with regional economic councils (sovnarkhozy), threatened the position of central ministry officials. His style, impulsive and given to improvisation, irritated colleagues. A group within the Presidium, including Molotov, Malenkov, Kaganovich, and later Bulganin and Voroshilov, concluded that he should be removed.

The confrontation came at a Presidium meeting on June 18, 1957. The group had a majority: by most accounts seven of the eleven members present voted to relieve Khrushchev of his post as First Secretary and to give him a lesser job, as Minister of Agriculture. Khrushchev refused to accept the decision, arguing that only the Central Committee, which had elected him, could remove him. His allies, among them Mikoyan, Suslov, and Marshal Zhukov, worked to summon the Central Committee, with Zhukov arranging military aircraft to bring members from the provinces. The plenum met from June 22 to 29, and its members, most of them regional party secretaries and officials who owed their positions to Khrushchev's patronage and who were wary of the old guard, supported him. The plenum condemned Molotov, Malenkov, and Kaganovich as a factional "anti-party group," to which Dmitri Shepilov was said to have "joined" them, and removed them from the Presidium and Central Committee.

The consequence was a major shift in the distribution of power. The leaders who had been most closely associated with Stalin's repression were removed, and Khrushchev's authority was enhanced. Yet the manner of the outcome was significant. A party leader had been challenged within the Presidium and had appealed successfully to the larger Central Committee. This suggested that the Central Committee was becoming an effective arbiter, and that the party's apparatus, not the security police or the army, controlled the outcome. It also established that defeat in a leadership struggle did not lead to execution. Molotov was sent as ambassador to Mongolia, Malenkov to manage a hydroelectric station in Kazakhstan, and Kaganovich to direct a potash plant in the Urals. They were formally expelled from the party in 1962, after the Twenty-Second Congress.

Section 61.17

61.17 — The Removal of Marshal Zhukov and the Ascendancy of Party Authority

Marshal Georgy Zhukov had been the most prominent Soviet soldier of the war. Stalin had sent him to provincial commands after 1946, and Khrushchev recalled him after Stalin's death. He had helped arrest Beria in 1953, became Minister of Defense in 1955, and assisted Khrushchev in June 1957, after which he was promoted to full membership of the Presidium, the first professional soldier to hold such rank. His popularity and prestige were unrivaled, and his service to Khrushchev gave him considerable influence.

That influence became a concern. Zhukov favored a strong professional military and was reported to have reduced the role of political officers in the armed forces and to have resisted party interference in military affairs. In October 1957, while he was visiting Yugoslavia and Albania, the Presidium decided to remove him. On his return he was dismissed from the Ministry of Defense, and in late October a Central Committee plenum removed him from the Presidium and Central Committee. The accusations included reducing the party's role in the army, "adventurism" in foreign policy, and building a personality cult around himself. Historians generally consider the charge of personality cult to have been an exaggeration and judge that Khrushchev and his colleagues acted from fear that an independent military figure could threaten collective party control. He was retired and lived in obscurity until after Khrushchev's own fall.

The episode is important for three reasons. It reflects a pattern that continued through the Soviet period: the armed forces were subordinate to the party, even when a military leader had played a crucial role in a political crisis. It demonstrates that Khrushchev, once he no longer needed Zhukov, was willing to remove him. And it shows the contrast with Stalin's treatment of rivals: Zhukov lost power but not liberty or life. Marshal Rodion Malinovsky, a Khrushchev ally, replaced him as defense minister.

Section 61.18

61.18 — Khrushchev as Party Leader and Premier, 1958 Onward

On March 27, 1958, Bulganin resigned as Chairman of the Council of Ministers, and Khrushchev took the post while remaining First Secretary. He thereby held both the principal party office and the head of government, the combination that Stalin had held after 1941 and that Malenkov had been forced to give up in 1953. In December 1958 Aleksandr Shelepin, a former Komsomol leader, replaced Serov as head of the KGB, reinforcing the party's ties with the security organs. Khrushchev also promoted younger figures into the leadership, such as Leonid Brezhnev, Frol Kozlov, and Alexei Kosygin, while removing others he regarded as obstacles.

His leadership differed from Stalin's in several respects. He traveled extensively, within the USSR and abroad, delivered long speeches, and sought direct contact with workers and farmers. He did not rely on mass terror, and the Presidium continued to meet and decide collectively on many questions, though his influence was considerable. He faced criticism and sometimes accepted it. The Central Committee met more regularly than under Stalin, and the 1961 party rules sought to institutionalize turnover in leading bodies. Yet his personal style could become domineering. He launched campaigns, such as the administrative reorganizations and agricultural experiments discussed in Chapter 62, with little consultation and often reversed them, and his habit of making sweeping pronouncements alienated officials who valued stability. A modest personality cult also developed, with adulatory press coverage on his birthdays.

The distinction between his leadership and Stalin's personal dictatorship should be neither exaggerated nor minimized. The most important difference lay in the use of coercion against the elite: officials who lost their positions were removed, not shot, and the security police were subordinated to party control. The most important continuity lay in the structure of the party-state, in which the First Secretary appointed senior officials, controlled information, and set the limits of debate. This arrangement created both the capacity for reform from above and the vulnerability to the leader's errors. When Khrushchev's policies encountered difficulties after 1960, the same Central Committee that had protected him in 1957 would remove him in 1964 (Chapter 62).

Historical Vocabulary
The Thaw (Ottepel')

Definition: The "Thaw" is the conventional name for the period of relaxed cultural and intellectual controls in the Soviet Union after Stalin's death, usually dated from about 1953–1954 to the early to mid-1960s. It takes its name from Ilya Ehrenburg's 1954 novella The Thaw.

Historical context: The term has been applied variously to literature, art, film, and social life, and to the political climate more broadly. Its chronology is contested: some scholars see it ending with the Manege affair of 1962–1963, others with Khrushchev's removal in 1964, and others extend it to the late 1960s.

Why it matters: The metaphor suggests warming and seasonal change, which can obscure the fact that the thaw advanced and retreated unevenly and that the authorities controlled its limits. Historians use the term cautiously, as a convenient label for a period of expanded but bounded possibilities.

Section 61.19

61.19 — One-Party Rule, Political Dissent, and the Limits of Reform

The expansion of freedom of expression occurred within a system that retained the means to punish dissent. After the Hungarian revolution and the unrest of 1956, authorities reacted to what they viewed as dangerous tendencies. In 1957 and 1958, the KGB and the courts prosecuted people for "anti-Soviet agitation," including students, workers, and intellectuals who had circulated critical statements or discussed events in Hungary. The prosecutions were smaller in scale than in the 1930s, but they showed that the boundaries of permissible discussion were enforced. The 1960 criminal code retained political offenses, introduced a broad definition of "especially dangerous crimes against the state," and, in 1961, extended the death penalty to additional economic offenses, a measure applied retroactively in a notorious case of currency speculators. Lesser forms of control included the use of "anti-parasite" laws against people without regular employment, expulsion from universities or workplaces, and confinement of dissenters in psychiatric hospitals, a practice that grew in later years.

Censorship remained institutionalized in the main censorship agency, Glavlit, and in editorial offices that were themselves subject to party supervision. Independent organizations, whether political, religious, or cultural, were not permitted; the Komsomol, trade unions, and writers' and artists' unions functioned as party-controlled bodies. Travel abroad and contacts with foreigners were restricted, though contacts increased, as the World Festival of Youth and Students held in Moscow in 1957 brought thousands of foreign visitors and exposed Soviet young people to unfamiliar fashions, music, and ideas.

The sharpest demonstration of the boundaries occurred in June 1962 at Novocherkassk, an industrial city in the Rostov region. In response to a government announcement of increases in retail prices for meat and butter on June 1, combined with reductions in wage rates at the Novocherkassk electric locomotive plant, workers went on strike, and demonstrations spread. On June 2, troops and security forces fired on a crowd near the party headquarters. Archival records reveal that 26 people were killed and dozens wounded, though lower and higher estimates have been cited. Senior leaders, including Anastas Mikoyan and Frol Kozlov, were sent from Moscow. Afterward, authorities tried participants, sentencing seven to death and many others to long prison terms, and kept the event secret for decades. It became publicly known only in the late 1980s and 1990s.

Novocherkassk was a direct challenge to the regime's claim to represent the working class, and its suppression shows that the post-Stalin leadership was prepared to use lethal force to preserve order. The economic background, including the price changes and conditions in Soviet industry, belongs to Chapter 62's treatment of Khrushchev's domestic programs. The essential point here is political: de-Stalinization had reduced the scope of terror but had not created channels for independent expression of grievances.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Political Repression Under Stalin and Khrushchev

What Changed

  • Mass arrests and quotas for executions ended; extrajudicial Special Boards were abolished.
  • The Gulag camp population fell sharply, and large numbers of prisoners were released and many rehabilitated.
  • Leaders who lost political struggles were demoted, not killed.
  • The security police were subordinated to party supervision.

What Remained

  • The one-party monopoly and the party's control of appointments, media, and organization.
  • Censorship and prosecution for political speech, including "anti-Soviet agitation."
  • The willingness to use lethal force against unrest, as at Novocherkassk.
  • The refusal to publicize the scale of repression or to hold officials accountable.

When and for whom: Change was fastest between 1953 and 1957. Party officials experienced the greatest improvement in personal security, and political prisoners, returnees, and some deported peoples gained significantly. Ordinary workers, religious believers, and dissenting intellectuals experienced more continuity. The pattern shows a reform of methods rather than a change in the fundamental distribution of political power.

Division V — The Cultural Thaw, Society, and the Contradictions of Reform · Section 61.20

61.20 — The Literary Thaw and the Reopening of Soviet Cultural Debate

Literature was the arena in which the changes of the early 1950s first became visible to the public. Soviet writers had lived under the supervision of the Writers' Union and the strictures of socialist realism (Chapter 58), and after Stalin's death many of them sought a broader range of subjects and a more truthful treatment of life. In December 1953, the journal Novy Mir published an essay by the critic Vladimir Pomerantsev, "On Sincerity in Literature," which criticized the falseness of much official writing. In 1954, Ilya Ehrenburg's novella The Thaw, published in the journal Znamya, portrayed the emotional constraints of a provincial factory director and his circle, and gave the era its name. The Second Congress of Soviet Writers in December 1954 revealed divisions between conservative and reform-minded writers. Official criticism of Novy Mir led to the dismissal of its editor Aleksandr Tvardovsky in 1954, but he was restored in 1958 and made the journal the leading forum for relatively frank writing until 1970.

After the Secret Speech, the possibilities expanded. In 1956, Novy Mir serialized Vladimir Dudintsev's novel Not by Bread Alone, which portrayed an inventor's struggle against an entrenched bureaucracy. It was widely read and discussed, defended by many readers, and attacked by officials as one-sided, and Dudintsev was criticized in the party press. The anthology Literary Moscow (1956) published poems and essays that tested limits, and it too drew official condemnation. Writers began to address themes such as bureaucratic careerism, the treatment of returning prisoners, and the moral compromises of life under Stalin. Poets who had survived the years of silence, such as Anna Akhmatova, found new opportunities to publish or circulate work, and works of the 1920s that had been suppressed returned to print in limited numbers.

The most dramatic publication of the Thaw occurred in November 1962, when Novy Mir printed Aleksandr Solzhenitsyn's One Day in the Life of Ivan Denisovich, a short novel describing a single day of a prisoner in a Stalin-era labor camp. Solzhenitsyn, a mathematics teacher who had spent eight years in camps and then in exile, had submitted the manuscript to Tvardovsky, who recognized its significance and worked to obtain permission. Khrushchev read the text, and the Presidium discussed it, and approval was given, reflecting the leader's desire to support the anti-Stalin campaign launched at the Twenty-Second Congress. The work was received as a revelation by many readers, and it was widely discussed. Its publication shows both the Thaw's possibilities and the role of political authorization: a major work on the camps could appear only because the leader sanctioned it, and the permission was not a precedent. Solzhenitsyn's later works were refused publication in the USSR, and he eventually became a leading dissident and was expelled in 1974 (Chapter 64).

Section 61.21

61.21 — Film, Theater, Poetry, and the New Soviet Cultural Generation

Soviet cinema had produced very few films in the last years of Stalin's life, and the cultural loosening of the mid-1950s brought a revival. Films treated the war with a new emphasis on personal experience rather than heroic command. Mikhail Kalatozov's The Cranes Are Flying (1957), a story of a young woman's love and loss during the war, won the Palme d'Or at the Cannes Film Festival in 1958, and Grigory Chukhrai's Ballad of a Soldier (1959) portrayed a young soldier's brief journey home. These works emphasized private feeling, moral ambiguity, and the toll of war on individuals, and they reached large audiences at home and abroad. Directors also made films about contemporary life, and the Moscow and other film studios experimented with new visual styles.

Poetry became a public event. Poets such as Yevgeny Yevtushenko, Andrei Voznesensky, Bella Akhmadulina, and Robert Rozhdestvensky attracted large audiences at readings in Moscow's Polytechnic Museum and in stadiums, and informal gatherings began around the Mayakovsky monument in Moscow, unveiled in 1958. Yevtushenko's poem "Babi Yar" (1961), which criticized antisemitism and the failure to commemorate the Jewish victims of the massacre near Kyiv in 1941, appeared in the Literaturnaya Gazeta and provoked official criticism as well as public acclaim. His "Stalin's Heirs," published in Pravda in October 1962, warned of the persistence of Stalinism in the country's elite. Theater too experimented: new companies and productions, such as the Sovremennik theater in Moscow founded in 1956, sought a more naturalistic style and addressed contemporary subjects.

The audience for these works was a new generation, born in the 1930s, shaped by wartime and postwar experience, and educated in the expanding school and university system. Its members included young scientists and technicians in the growing research cities as well as humanities students and literary enthusiasts. Many of them were sincere believers in the socialist project who wanted to renew it, a generation later called the "sixtiers" (shestidesyatniki). Their relationship to official ideology was complex: they sought authenticity within the Soviet framework while often drawing on forbidden or neglected sources, including pre-revolutionary and foreign culture. Vladislav Zubok has emphasized the "Zhivago generation's" combination of loyalty and search for alternatives. Some of the cultural currents of the Thaw would later feed the dissent movement, while others were absorbed into official culture.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Soviet Student's Cultural World, c. 1957–1962

The following is a generalized portrait, drawn from the kinds of experiences reported in memoirs and studies of the period, not an account of one individual. A university student in a large Soviet city in the late 1950s might share a crowded dormitory room or live with family in a communal apartment, attend lectures that combined technical or humanistic study with compulsory courses in Marxism-Leninism, and belong to the Komsomol. Leisure was increasingly varied. The student might queue at a library for the latest issue of Novy Mir, attend a poetry reading, see The Cranes Are Flying in a cinema, and listen to jazz records or foreign broadcasts, which authorities jammed with limited success. The 1957 Moscow festival had brought foreigners into view; in the following years, fashions, music, and informal styles spread, including among youth known by the authorities as stilyagi, criticized for their imitation of Western dress.

Such experiences differed by place and background. Students in the capitals and larger cities had access to theaters, libraries, and informal circles unavailable in villages or small towns, and rural youth experienced the Thaw mostly through radio, the press, and the arrival of returned prisoners. Young women participated in higher education in large numbers but faced expectations to combine careers and household work. The opportunities of the era coexisted with ideological supervision: Komsomol meetings could discipline students for "unhealthy" views, and expulsion from university was a possible penalty. Evidence of everyday life comes from memoirs, letters, interviews, and police and Komsomol reports, each with its own bias.

Section 61.22

61.22 — Censorship, Artistic Controversies, and the Boundaries of Expression

The same leadership that permitted new subjects also enforced limits, and it did so through public campaigns. The most prominent case was that of Boris Pasternak. The poet and translator completed his novel Doctor Zhivago, a sweeping account of the intelligentsia's experience of revolution and civil war, in the mid-1950s. Novy Mir's editors rejected it in 1956, with a letter explaining that its view of the revolution was unacceptable. Pasternak allowed the manuscript to be taken abroad, and it was published in Italy in November 1957 by the publisher Feltrinelli, and then in many languages. In October 1958 the Swedish Academy awarded Pasternak the Nobel Prize in Literature. The Soviet response was immediate: Pasternak was expelled from the Writers' Union, denounced in the press and at public meetings by many who had not read the book, and threatened with exile. He sent a message declining the prize and wrote to Khrushchev, asking not to be forced to leave his country. He remained in the USSR, under pressure, until his death in 1960. The novel was not published in the Soviet Union until 1988. The Western reception of the book was also shaped by Cold War politics, and later disclosures showed that the Central Intelligence Agency had been involved in distributing the Russian-language text abroad, which the Soviet side cited as evidence of foreign manipulation.

The Pasternak affair established a model of permissible and forbidden publication: works that criticized Stalin's abuses from within the Soviet framework might be tolerated, but works that questioned the revolution's legitimacy, or that were published abroad without permission, were treated as hostile. The phenomena of samizdat (self-published, typed and circulated texts) and tamizdat (works published abroad) emerged in this context and became central to the dissident movement (Chapter 64).

Visual arts were subject to similar controls. In 1962 the party and artists' union organized an exhibition at the Manege in Moscow to mark thirty years of the Moscow branch of the Artists' Union. It included a section of works by younger artists, some in abstract or modernist styles that departed from socialist realism. On December 1, 1962, Khrushchev visited the exhibition and reacted with anger, denouncing the abstract works in crude language. The following weeks brought meetings between party leaders and intellectuals at which writers and artists were criticized, and a more conservative cultural policy under the party ideologist Leonid Ilyichev. These events brought to an end the most open phase of the Thaw's cultural policy, though publishing and artistic life did not return to the conditions of the early 1950s. Both liberal and conservative factions within the intelligentsia drew conclusions from the episode.

Historical Debate

Was the Thaw a Political Transformation or a Controlled Relaxation?

The question: Did the changes of 1953–1962 fundamentally alter the Soviet political and cultural system, or did they adjust the methods by which the party maintained control?

Interpretation A — Controlled relaxation: Many historians emphasize that the party retained control of publication, appointments, and the definition of acceptable ideas, and that reforms were granted from above and withdrawn when they threatened the system. On this view, the Thaw adjusted the instruments of control, and the Pasternak and Manege affairs, Novocherkassk, and the retention of political crimes in the criminal code show the boundaries.
Interpretation B — Transformation of society and expectations: Other scholars stress that the Thaw changed Soviet society even if it did not alter the formal political system. The return of millions of prisoners, access to information, the growth of educated, critical readers, informal networks, and new attitudes toward the past created expectations that the regime could not fully contain, and which shaped the dissident movement and later reform ideas, including those of the Gorbachev era.

Evidence: Historians use publication records, censorship files, letters from readers, memoirs, police reports, and records of party discussions, as well as comparisons with the pre-1953 and post-1964 periods.

Points of agreement: Scholars broadly agree that the party-state remained intact, that the Thaw was uneven and reversible, and that it altered the experience of many people.

Points of disagreement: They differ on how much weight to give long-term social and cultural change relative to institutional continuity, and on how directly the Thaw prepared the later dissident and reform movements.

Why it matters: The question bears on whether the Soviet system was capable of self-reform, a problem that returns in Chapters 63 through 66.

Student task: Using two examples from §§61.19–61.22, show how the same event can support both interpretations, and explain what additional evidence would help decide between them.

Section 61.23

61.23 — Religion, Historical Memory, and the Uneven Thaw

The Thaw did not extend to religious life. During the Second World War and the early postwar years, the state had made an accommodation with the Russian Orthodox Church, reopening churches and allowing a patriarch (Chapter 60). After Stalin's death, the party continued to regard religion as incompatible with socialist ideology. A resolution of the Central Committee in November 1954 criticized crude and insulting propaganda against believers while reaffirming the need for scientific-atheist education, and for a few years the church enjoyed relative calm. From 1958, however, Khrushchev's leadership launched a new antireligious campaign, including pressure on parish clergy, new regulations that reduced clergy influence over parish administration, the closure of many churches and monasteries, and reduction in the number of seminaries. The number of functioning Orthodox churches fell by roughly half between the late 1950s and the mid-1960s. Other communities also suffered: Baptists, Pentecostals, Jews, Muslims, and Catholics all faced restrictions, closures, and in some cases prosecution of leaders. The campaign was justified by the party's confidence that religion was dying out and by its renewed ideological emphasis on building communism.

The memory of Stalinism followed a similar pattern. The leadership's account of the past was selective, and alternative memories existed: families of the repressed, returnees, survivors of famine and deportation, veterans who revered Stalin's wartime leadership, and Georgians who regarded criticism of him as an affront. The regime allowed some memory, mostly that of party victims and literary representations such as Solzhenitsyn's, and suppressed other kinds. Collectivization and the famine were not discussed, and local memories of those events remained largely private. In the non-Russian republics, the experience of the Thaw varied: in the Baltic republics and western Ukraine, for example, returnees and cultural revival intersected with national memory and with the suppressed history of Soviet annexation, while in the Central Asian republics the return of exiles and the growth of local intelligentsias took a different form. The Thaw was thus not a single experience, but different for urban and rural people, for the educated and the less educated, for Russians and non-Russians, and for believers and nonbelievers.

By 1962, the Soviet Union had changed substantially from the state Stalin left. The terror had been curtailed, millions of prisoners and deportees had been freed, the leadership had publicly criticized the dictator, and the cultural sphere offered new possibilities. But the political structure remained a party monopoly, censorship and prosecution persisted, religious life was under pressure, and the full history of repression remained undisclosed. These contradictions, and the ways they would be exploited and contested by dissidents, by conservative officials, and eventually by Gorbachev's reformers, are the subject of the chapters that follow.

Section 61.24

61.24 — Chapter Review and Historical Thinking Checkpoint

This chapter has followed the Soviet Union from the death of a dictator to the early 1960s. Its central question was how the country moved away from Stalin's personal terror while preserving the Communist Party's monopoly of power, and why the resulting liberalization stayed incomplete. Four lines of reasoning, developed across §§61.1–61.23, offer an answer.

Transformation. Real change occurred. Mass arrests ended; the Special Boards disappeared; the Gulag population shrank; millions of prisoners and deportees were released; many convictions were annulled; the security police were reorganized under party supervision; the highest leaders could lose power without losing their lives; and writers, filmmakers, and poets worked in a changed atmosphere. These changes were large enough that contemporaries, including victims, recognized a new era.

Continuity. The political system's foundations remained. The party kept a monopoly on organization, appointments, and information. The state retained censorship, political prosecution, and the capacity for lethal force. The scale of repression remained officially undisclosed, and rehabilitation was uneven and bureaucratic. The Secret Speech blamed Stalin and a few associates, protected the party, and omitted collectivization and the broader victims of terror.

Causation. No single cause explains the pattern. The leaders' fear of the police; the economic inefficiency of mass forced labor; the pressure of prisoners and returnees; the logic of the succession struggle; and the leaders' own sense of the system's needs all contributed. The camp uprisings show that those at the bottom of the system influenced events. Khrushchev's motives remain debated (§61.12), and the outcomes depended on contingent decisions, such as the choice to speak in 1956 and the Central Committee's support in 1957.

Evidence and interpretation. The Secret Speech, the amnesty decree, literary texts, and party resolutions are valuable and partial sources, each shaped by purposes and silences. Later memoirs by participants require corroboration. Historians differ on motives and on how to characterize the Thaw (§§61.12, 61.22), and students should hold these interpretations alongside the evidence.

Central review question: Did de-Stalinization fundamentally transform the Soviet political system, or did it reform the methods through which that system exercised power? A well-supported answer should use specific evidence from several sections, distinguish between elite security, institutional structure, and social experience, and acknowledge that different groups, such as party officials, returnees, deported peoples, writers, believers, and striking workers, experienced the period differently.

Chapter Summary

This chapter examined how the Soviet Union responded to the death of Stalin in March 1953. The central problem was how a state built around one man would be governed without him, and how leaders who had served him could change the system without destroying the source of their own authority. The answer unfolded across overlapping processes between 1953 and 1962: a succession struggle, the retreat from mass terror, a reinterpretation of Stalin's rule, a consolidation of Khrushchev's authority, and a cultural Thaw.

The succession began with a collective leadership in which Malenkov led the government, Beria the merged police ministry, and Khrushchev the party secretariat. The leaders acted quickly to reverse some of Stalin's last policies, repudiating the Doctors' Plot and issuing an amnesty. These early steps, many associated with Beria, were driven by shared fear of the police, the cost of mass imprisonment, and uncertainty about popular reactions. Beria's arrest in June and execution in December 1953 removed the most feared figure and established that the party, not the police, would control the state.

The Gulag's transformation had several components. The amnesty released large numbers of non-political prisoners and left most political prisoners in place. Prisoner strikes and uprisings at Norilsk, Vorkuta, and Kengir demonstrated that the camp system could not be run as before and pushed the government to review cases. The reorganization of the security apparatus created the KGB under closer party supervision and ended the extrajudicial boards, while leaving the machinery for political prosecution intact. Review and rehabilitation brought release and legal exoneration to many, though the process was selective, bureaucratic, and incomplete.

The treatment of deported peoples showed the political criteria governing reform. Chechens, Ingush, Karachai, Balkars, and Kalmyks regained their autonomies and largely returned in 1957–1958, while Crimean Tatars, Volga Germans, and Meskhetian Turks were released from special settlement without the right to return home or recover their territories. The return generated conflict over land and property in the North Caucasus whose consequences extended into the post-Soviet period.

The Twentieth Party Congress and Khrushchev's Secret Speech of February 1956 were the central acts of de-Stalinization. The speech detailed Stalin's persecution of party members and denounced the cult of personality, but omitted collectivization, the broader scale of repression, and the leaders' own responsibility, and it did not challenge the one-party state. Its reception ranged from relief to disillusion and, in Georgia, violent protest, and it shook communist parties abroad and contributed to crises in Eastern Europe. The Twenty-Second Congress of 1961 renewed the campaign by removing Stalin's body from the Mausoleum and renaming Stalingrad, but again stopped short of a full accounting.

Khrushchev consolidated power through the defeat of the anti-party group in June 1957, which depended on the Central Committee, the dismissal of Marshal Zhukov in October 1957, and the assumption of the premiership in March 1958. His leadership differed from Stalin's in that rivals were demoted rather than killed and the Presidium continued to function, but it preserved the party-state's structure of appointment and control.

The limits of political reform appeared in continued censorship, prosecutions for anti-Soviet agitation, the retention of political offenses in the 1960 criminal code, and the lethal suppression of the Novocherkassk strike in June 1962. The antireligious campaign launched in 1958 reduced the number of churches and pressured other communities, showing that the Thaw did not include freedom of conscience.

The cultural Thaw was real and significant. Writers such as Ehrenburg, Dudintsev, and Solzhenitsyn, poets such as Yevtushenko and Voznesensky, filmmakers such as Kalatozov and Chukhrai, and a new generation of readers and viewers expanded the boundaries of discussion. But the Pasternak affair and the Manege episode showed that the party defined the limits. The period's effects were uneven, varying with place, social group, nationality, and religion.

Historians disagree about Khrushchev's motives and about how to characterize the Thaw, and the evidence for both questions is incomplete. The chapter's overall lesson is that the Soviet Union moved away from Stalin's system of personal terror without abandoning the one-party state, and that the Thaw expanded possibilities while preserving substantial constraints. Chapter 62 turns to Khrushchev's economic, scientific, and international programs; Chapter 64 follows the dissident currents that grew from the Thaw's unfinished business; and Chapter 65 returns to the question of Stalinism's legacy under glasnost.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a repressive system changed without ceasing to be itself. The Soviet leaders after 1953 ended mass terror, released millions of people from camps and settlements, and publicly criticized the dictator. Those steps altered the lives of millions and changed what Soviet citizens could say about the recent past. They also created a new kind of political security for the elite, who no longer feared arrest and execution as the price of defeat.

What continued was equally important. The party's monopoly, censorship, political prosecution, and the refusal to document the full scale of repression endured. The unfinished reckoning left a gap between what people knew from experience and what the state would acknowledge. That gap shaped the dissident movement examined in Chapter 64, the debates of the Brezhnev years in Chapter 63, and the explosion of historical memory under glasnost in Chapter 65. It also shaped different national experiences: deported peoples, Georgians, Ukrainians, Balts, and others related the Thaw to their own histories.

The chapter reveals several themes of the textbook: the relationship between center and periphery, the importance of institutions in explaining political change, the role of those at the bottom of the system in shaping policy, and the way memory is managed by states. It also cautions against teleology. Nothing in 1953–1962 made the later stagnation or the collapse of 1991 inevitable. Khrushchev's reforms could have taken different paths, and the leaders and publics of the time did not know where they would lead.

Questions remain. How much did individual conviction matter compared with institutional interest? How should historians weigh the experiences of different groups when characterizing the period? And how did the partial, controlled criticism of Stalin affect later Soviet and post-Soviet attitudes toward the past? Chapter 62 will examine Khrushchev's economic, scientific, and international policies, which both complemented and complicated the political changes studied here.
