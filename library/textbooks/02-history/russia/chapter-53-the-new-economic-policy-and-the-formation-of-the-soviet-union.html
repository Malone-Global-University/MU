Part IX — Revolution and the Birth of the Soviet Union
53

The New Economic Policy and the Formation of the Soviet Union

A History of Russia: From the Eurasian World to the Russian Federation

Chapter Opening

In March 1921 Lenin and the Tenth Party Congress argued, in effect, that the revolution's survival required retreating from compulsory grain requisition in order to preserve a workable relationship with the peasant majority. — a paraphrase of the reasoning behind the tax in kind, not a direct quotation.

In the second week of March 1921, two very different scenes unfolded within a few hundred kilometers of each other. In Moscow, several hundred delegates to the Tenth Congress of the Russian Communist Party (Bolsheviks) debated how to govern a country that the party had just won in a civil war and that was now close to economic collapse. Some two hundred miles to the northwest, in the Gulf of Finland, the naval fortress of Kronstadt, once celebrated by the Bolsheviks as a bastion of the revolution, was in open revolt against them. On 7 March government artillery opened fire on the island; between 16 and 18 March, Red Army columns, some of them reinforced by delegates who had left the congress hall in Moscow, crossed the frozen sea in white camouflage and stormed the fortress. The revolt was crushed within days. The Congress, meanwhile, adopted two measures that would define the next several years: a retreat from the requisition of grain from peasants, which opened the way to what became known as the New Economic Policy (NEP), and a ban on organized factions inside the Communist Party itself.

This chapter studies the settlement that followed the Civil War. Chapter 52 explained how the Bolsheviks defeated their opponents and built the coercive institutions of War Communism; this chapter begins with the crisis those policies and that war had produced and ends in 1924, when the Union of Soviet Socialist Republics (USSR) had a constitution, a federal structure, and a mixed economy under Communist political control. The period is conventionally remembered as the "NEP," but that label captures only a part of it. In the same years the government suppressed rival socialist parties, expelled intellectuals, invested heavily in literacy and schooling, tolerated avant-garde experiment in art and theater, debated the abolition of the traditional family, promoted non-Russian languages and local cadres, and negotiated, sometimes bitterly, the constitutional form of a new multinational state.

The central problem of the chapter is one of apparent contradiction. The party relaxed its control over the economy while tightening its control over politics. It created a formally federal union that included a nominal right of secession while maintaining a centralized ruling party. It sponsored national cultures while insisting on the supremacy of a Moscow-based leadership. It proclaimed the transformation of social life while presiding over a society impoverished by seven years of war, revolution, and civil conflict. These were not accidental inconsistencies; they were the structure of the early Soviet system, and understanding that structure is essential to understanding why later choices, examined in Chapters 54 through 57, took the form they did, and why they were choices rather than inevitabilities.

The chapter also gives voice to people who are easily lost in a narrative of party congresses. Tambov villagers who first supported and then fought against grain requisition, Kronstadt sailors who wrote resolutions demanding free elections to the soviets, Volga peasants who survived the famine of 1921–1922 on substitutes for bread, private traders (known as Nepmen) who reopened shops and market stalls, women workers who were among the first laid off as industry reorganized, homeless children who filled railway stations, Muslim Communists in Tatar and Central Asian towns who sought a Soviet path for their own societies, and Georgian Communists who objected to the way Moscow handled their republic all appear here as participants in the making of the Soviet order rather than as background to it.

The evidence for this period is unusually varied. Party congress stenographic records, decrees, and the private correspondence of Bolshevik leaders survive alongside petitions from peasants, resolutions passed by sailors and factory assemblies, GPU (secret police) summaries of popular mood, foreign relief-agency reports, newspapers, memoirs, early Soviet films and literature, and the 1926 census. Many of these sources were produced by the party-state itself and reflect its categories and priorities; others, such as the Kronstadt resolutions or the letters collected by relief workers, preserve the words of people the regime did not control. Archives opened after 1991 have added a great deal, particularly on Tambov, the famine, and the debates over the Union, and have prompted historians to revise older interpretations on all sides.

The period also raises questions of interpretation that remain open. Was Kronstadt a spontaneous popular revolt, a counterrevolutionary conspiracy, or something in between? Was NEP a tactical retreat that the party always intended to reverse, a genuine strategic alternative that was later abandoned, or a fragile compromise whose internal contradictions made its end likely, though not predetermined? Did the creation of a federal union with national republics build national institutions that later outlived the system itself, or was it a facade for Russian-dominated centralism? This chapter presents the evidence for competing answers and asks students to weigh it.

This chapter is also about the wider world. The Bolshevik leadership lived in expectation of European revolution that did not arrive, and NEP coincided with the opening of limited trade and diplomatic relations with Britain, Germany, and others; with an unprecedented international humanitarian operation during the famine; and with the Communist International's attempts to coordinate revolutionary movements abroad. Soviet Russia was neither isolated nor integrated, but occupied an uneasy intermediate position. By the chapter's end, students should be able to explain how and why the Bolsheviks changed course in 1921, what kind of economy, polity, society, and state resulted by 1924, and why historians continue to dispute how far that settlement was a retreat from the revolution and how far it was a reconstruction of it.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain the economic, demographic, social, and political crisis of 1920–1921 and why it threatened the Bolshevik regime despite military victory.
  2. Analyze the Tambov rebellion and the Kronstadt revolt as distinct responses to War Communism, and evaluate how each shaped party policy.
  3. Distinguish the decision to introduce the NEP from the simultaneous ban on party factions, and explain why economic liberalization coincided with political centralization.
  4. Describe how the tax in kind, legalized market exchange, and state control of the "commanding heights" worked together as a mixed economy.
  5. Assess the causes, regional variation, and consequences of the famine of 1921–1922, including international relief and the church-valuables conflict.
  6. Evaluate the experiences of peasants, Nepmen, workers, women, and the unemployed under NEP, and explain the structural tensions in the peasant economy.
  7. Interpret party rule, the suppression of rival socialist parties, censorship, and the expulsion of intellectuals as the political counterpart to economic relaxation.
  8. Evaluate early Soviet cultural, educational, and family policy as a field of experiment constrained by scarcity and by party supervision.
  9. Explain Soviet nationality policy and korenizatsiya, distinguishing institutional nation-building from central political control.
  10. Trace the debate over autonomization versus union, the Georgian Affair, and the formal creation of the USSR, and distinguish the USSR from the Russian SFSR.
  11. Critically assess historiographical disputes over Kronstadt, NEP, and the nature of the Soviet federation using evidence of different types.
  12. Connect the settlement of 1921–1924 to the succession struggle and later transformations examined in Chapters 54 through 56 without treating those outcomes as inevitable.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did the Bolsheviks face a crisis of legitimacy in 1921 after winning the Civil War, and which groups expressed that crisis most forcefully?
  2. How did Tambov and Kronstadt differ in participants, grievances, and the regime's response, and what did they have in common?
  3. Why did the Tenth Party Congress combine economic retreat with a ban on factions, and what does that pairing reveal about how Bolshevik leaders understood the relationship between economics and politics?
  4. In what sense was NEP a "mixed economy," and what mechanisms allowed market exchange and state control to coexist?
  5. What were the principal causes of the famine of 1921–1922, and how did the government and foreign organizations respond?
  6. What structural tensions between the state and the peasant economy emerged under NEP, and why should they not be read as making collectivization inevitable?
  7. How did NEP change everyday life for peasants, workers, women, traders, and children, and who gained or lost?
  8. How did the government's approach to culture, family, religion, and nationalities combine experiment with control?
  9. Why was the USSR created as a formally federal union rather than by absorbing the republics into the Russian SFSR?
  10. What political, economic, and constitutional system existed by 1924, and what alternatives were still open?

Historical Context

Chapter 52 traced the path from the dissolution of the Constituent Assembly and the Treaty of Brest-Litovsk through the Civil War, foreign intervention, War Communism, and the Red and White terrors to the Bolshevik victory. Military victory came at the end of seven years of almost continuous conflict that began with mobilization for the First World War in 1914 (Chapter 48). The cumulative human cost is impossible to state precisely. Battle deaths, deaths from epidemic disease (above all typhus), famine, executions, and mass emigration all contributed, and published estimates of excess mortality across the former empire between 1914 and 1922 range across several millions to well over ten million depending on the period, territory, and method counted. Historians therefore tend to emphasize the order of magnitude and its sources of uncertainty rather than a single figure.

The economy that emerged from this period was radically contracted. Large-scale industry produced only a fraction of its 1913 output, commonly estimated at about one-fifth or less; transport was crippled by destroyed locomotives, bridges, and track; cities such as Petrograd and Moscow had lost large shares of their prerevolutionary populations as workers returned to villages in search of food; and a collapsing currency, printed in vast quantities to finance the state, had lost most of its purchasing power. Grain harvests had fallen to roughly half of prewar levels in 1920. Under the system known as War Communism, discussed in Chapter 52, the state had requisitioned grain and other produce from peasants through a system called prodrazvyorstka, nationalized industry down to small workshops, restricted private trade, and distributed goods through rationing. Historians still disagree about how far these policies were improvised responses to wartime emergency and how far they reflected an ideological commitment to rapid movement toward a non-market economy. Contemporary Bolshevik writing contained both interpretations, and many party members in 1920 believed that War Communism was a direct route to socialism.

The political landscape was equally unsettled. The Bolsheviks, who in 1917 had called for land to the peasants and for workers' control, now ruled through a party-state in which soviets had become increasingly subordinate to party committees. Rival socialist parties had been marginalized, harassed, or banned in stages during the Civil War; the Cheka (the Extraordinary Commission for Combating Counter-Revolution and Sabotage) operated as an instrument of repression with wide powers. Yet in 1920–1921 the party leadership also faced an open challenge from within and below, as rank-and-file Communists and workers questioned the militarization of labor, the bureaucratization of administration, and the treatment of the peasantry. These disputes took place against a background of widespread rural revolt, from the Tambov region to Western Siberia, as well as urban strikes.

The geographic and national setting also matters. The collapse of the Russian Empire (Chapters 49 and 50) had produced independent states in Finland, Poland, Estonia, Latvia, and Lithuania, and Soviet governments in Ukraine, Belarus, Transcaucasia, and Central Asia that were militarily and politically tied to Moscow in varying ways (Chapter 52, §52.9). Between 1920 and 1921 the Red Army established Soviet power in Azerbaijan, Armenia, and, in February 1921, Georgia, where the Menshevik-led government had been independent since 1918. The peace treaty with Poland signed at Riga in March 1921 fixed a western frontier that left large Ukrainian and Belarusian populations on the Polish side. Meanwhile, Bukhara and Khorezm in Central Asia remained formally separate people's republics under Soviet influence. The Russian Soviet Federative Socialist Republic (RSFSR) was the largest Soviet state, but it was not yet the Soviet Union, and the relationship among these states was undefined and contested.

Dating needs clarification. Soviet Russia adopted the Gregorian (New Style) calendar in February 1918 (Chapter 49 and Chapter 51 discuss earlier Old Style dates). All dates in this chapter are therefore New Style unless otherwise noted. The party's name was the Russian Communist Party (Bolsheviks) (RKP(b)) throughout this chapter; it was not renamed the All-Union Communist Party (Bolsheviks) until 1925, a reminder that the ruling party of a multinational union carried an explicitly Russian name for its first years.

Section 53.1

The Crisis of 1921: A Revolution at the Breaking Point

By the winter of 1920–1921 the Bolsheviks had defeated the main White armies, but victory exposed rather than resolved the regime's problems. Demobilization began to send millions of soldiers back to a countryside that could not absorb them and a transport system that could barely carry them. Industry had collapsed for lack of fuel, raw materials, skilled labor, and functioning railways; many factories had closed or operated for a few weeks a year, and workers survived by bartering, stealing materials to make goods for sale, or leaving for villages. The populations of Petrograd and Moscow had fallen sharply, and the proportion of industrial workers in the urban population was far smaller than in 1917, a fact that complicated the party's claim to rule in the name of the proletariat.

Food shortages were severe and the means of acquiring food had become coercive. Under War Communism, the state requisitioned grain according to quotas assigned to provinces, districts, and villages, and these quotas were often based on estimated rather than actual production. Peasants responded in several ways: reducing the area they sowed, hiding grain, selling to illegal traders (known as "bagmen"), and resisting requisition detachments, sometimes violently. The state's response, including hostage-taking and punitive expeditions, deepened resentment. The currency had lost nearly all value, so formal wages meant little; goods were distributed in kind through rations that varied by occupation and social category, giving the system a pervasive inequality of access that sat uneasily with revolutionary egalitarianism.

Discontent took multiple forms and reached different parts of the political spectrum. In rural areas, uprisings broke out in Tambov province, the middle Volga, Ukraine, the North Caucasus, and Western Siberia, where in early 1921 insurgents temporarily disrupted the Trans-Siberian Railway and food deliveries to the cities. In urban areas, strikes spread in Moscow and especially Petrograd in February 1921, driven by cuts in bread rations, cold, and a lack of fuel. Within the party itself, an unusually sharp debate over the role of trade unions, begun in late 1920, exposed disagreements about the entire direction of policy. Taken together, these developments formed what many party leaders, including Lenin, openly described as a political crisis more dangerous than any military threat of the preceding years.

The crisis was therefore not simply economic; it was a crisis of legitimacy and consent. The regime had won the war by mobilizing an enormous coercive apparatus and by persuading enough peasants that the Whites represented a threat to their land. Once that threat receded, the reasons peasants had tolerated the Bolsheviks diminished while the burdens of requisition remained. The historical question for this chapter is therefore not why the Bolsheviks changed policy, which is relatively clear, but how they changed it: which parts of the wartime system they dismantled, which they retained, and what they did to prevent a loosening of economic policy from becoming a loss of political power.

Section 53.2

Tambov and the Peasant Rebellion

Tambov province, in the central black-earth region about 400 kilometers southeast of Moscow, was a densely populated agricultural area that supplied grain to the center and had been heavily affected by requisition. In August 1920, after the arrival of detachments seeking to fulfill quotas that villagers considered impossible, local resistance developed into a sustained insurgency. Its leadership included Aleksandr Antonov, a former Socialist Revolutionary (SR) who had served as a local militia official, and the movement acquired a political program through the Union of Working Peasants, which called for a constituent assembly, equality of rights among citizens, and an end to requisition and to Communist monopoly rule. Historians disagree about how coherent the rebels' organization was; the evidence suggests a mixture of village-level self-defense, networks of former soldiers and SR activists, and a rudimentary command structure that at its peak commanded tens of thousands of fighters, though estimates vary.

The rebellion succeeded for months partly because it was embedded in villages. Rebels drew on local support, warning networks, and the difficulty regular troops had in distinguishing insurgents from the civilian population. They attacked Soviet officials, requisition agents, and Communist party members, and they disrupted rail and communications. The regime's initial responses, which relied on local forces, were insufficient. In spring 1921, as the center recognized the scale of the problem, it sent additional forces, appointed Mikhail Tukhachevsky to command operations from May, and created a plenipotentiary commission headed by Vladimir Antonov-Ovseenko (no relation to the rebel leader) to combine military and political measures. Well over one hundred thousand troops, together with security units and armored vehicles, were eventually committed.

The counterinsurgency combined repression and concession, and its methods are documented in orders that survive in Russian archives. Two orders should be distinguished. Tukhachevsky's Order No. 130 of 12 May 1921 ordered Red Army forces to destroy the insurgent bands rapidly, called on rebels to surrender, and prescribed the arrest of the families of rebels who failed to surrender, confiscation of their property, and eventual deportation if the rebel remained at large. Rules issued by the Plenipotentiary Commission the same day elaborated the procedures for family arrest, confiscation, and deportation. The Commission's Order No. 171 of 11 June escalated collective reprisals: people refusing to identify themselves could be shot without trial; villages concealing weapons could have hostages taken and executed if the weapons were not surrendered; discovery of hidden weapons could lead to execution of the eldest worker in the household; and families sheltering rebels could be arrested and deported, with their property confiscated and the eldest worker executed. Thousands of people were held in camps. On 12 June, Tukhachevsky and his chief of staff issued Order No. 0116 authorizing the use of asphyxiating gas against insurgents hiding in forests. Historians accept the authenticity of these orders. Archival research indicates that chemical weapons were actually used but only to a very limited extent, although uncertainty remains about individual incidents and their effects; the episode should not be described as extensive gas warfare. At the same time, authorities offered amnesty to those who surrendered and, crucially, began to abolish requisition, which removed the principal grievance. By mid-1921 the rebellion's main forces had been broken; Antonov was killed in a confrontation with security forces in June 1922. Estimates of the number of people killed, imprisoned, or deported cannot be stated precisely, and published figures vary considerably.

Tambov matters beyond its military history. It showed the party leadership that the peasant majority, whose acquiescence was essential, could be driven to organized armed resistance, and that suppression alone was costly and slow. It also set a precedent for the coordinated use of collective punishment, camps, and hostage-taking in counterinsurgency that recurs in later chapters of Soviet history. Finally, Tambov complicates the common narrative that NEP was simply a response to Kronstadt: the abandonment of requisition was shaped by the combined pressure of rural insurgency and urban unrest, and the regime continued to repress those who had resisted even as it conceded their central economic demand.

Section 53.3

Kronstadt: Revolt at the Revolutionary Stronghold

Kronstadt, a fortified island in the Gulf of Finland about thirty kilometers from Petrograd, was the principal base of the Baltic Fleet. Its sailors had been among the most radical supporters of the Bolsheviks in 1917 and had participated in the July Days and the October seizure of power (Chapters 50 and 51). By January 1921 more than 50,000 military personnel and civilians lived at Kronstadt, including nearly 27,000 sailors and soldiers stationed at the naval base and fortress. Civil War mobilization and replacement had changed the personnel, and recruits from rural areas, including Ukraine, had joined the fleet. Historians dispute the extent of the transformation: the older Bolshevik explanation implied that the revolutionary sailors of 1917 had largely been replaced, whereas some scholarship, notably Israel Getzler's study, argues that substantial continuity with the earlier revolutionary garrison remained. In either case, the sailors were aware of conditions in their home villages and in Petrograd.

The immediate trigger was the wave of strikes in Petrograd in late February 1921, which the authorities met with arrests, martial law, and the closure of some factories. Sailors from the battleships Petropavlovsk and Sevastopol sent a delegation to investigate and, after hearing its report, adopted a resolution on 28 February that was endorsed by a large meeting at Kronstadt on 1 March. It demanded new elections to the soviets by secret ballot and with free campaigning, freedom of speech and press for workers, peasants, anarchists, and left-wing socialist parties, freedom of assembly and of trade unions, the release of political prisoners, the abolition of party political departments in the armed forces, equalization of rations, and the right of peasants to farm their land and keep livestock, provided they did not use hired labor. A Provisional Revolutionary Committee, chaired by Stepan Petrichenko, took control of the fortress on 2 March.

The government's response was swift and uncompromising. It portrayed the revolt as a counterrevolutionary plot led by a former tsarist general serving as an artillery specialist at Kronstadt, supported by the Whites and foreign powers, and it issued an ultimatum on 5 March. The first assault, on 7 March, failed. A second, larger operation began on the night of 16 March under Tukhachevsky and involved reinforcements that included party delegates from Moscow; Red troops crossed the ice and reached the fortress on 17–18 March. Casualties were heavy on both sides; several thousand rebels were captured, and the repression that followed was severe. Later published records indicate that at least about 2,100 people received death sentences and more than 6,000 received lengthy prison sentences, while roughly 8,000 sailors, soldiers, and civilians escaped to Finland. Exact totals and categories vary among sources.

Kronstadt was historically significant out of proportion to its size. Its demands came from within the revolutionary milieu, using language of soviets and workers' rule to criticize the Bolsheviks, and therefore posed a legitimacy challenge that rural revolt did not. The party's reaction combined brutal military suppression with policy concessions: the tax in kind was announced during the same days. Most importantly, Kronstadt reinforced the conviction among leaders that political pluralism, if conceded in a moment of weakness, could bring down the regime. Historians still dispute the revolt's nature (see the Historical Debate feature), but they generally agree that it was not primarily a White plot, that it had a mass base among the sailors, and that the party's decisive response shaped the political character of the early Soviet state.

Section 53.4

The Tenth Party Congress: Economic Retreat and Political Discipline

The Tenth Congress of the Russian Communist Party (Bolsheviks), held in Moscow from 8 to 16 March 1921, took place as the Kronstadt rebellion was under way, and its delegates were acutely conscious of both the military emergency and the broader crisis. The congress had been preceded by months of controversy over the role of trade unions in a workers' state. Leon Trotsky had argued for fusing unions with state economic management and applying militarized labor methods; Lenin, Grigory Zinoviev, and others defended a more limited role for unions as protectors of workers and "schools of communism"; Nikolai Bukharin proposed a compromise. Two further groups raised more fundamental challenges: the Workers' Opposition, led by Aleksandr Shliapnikov and Aleksandra Kollontai, demanded that trade unions manage industry and criticized the bureaucratization of the party, while the Democratic Centralists, including Timofei Sapronov and Vladimir Osinsky, attacked the concentration of power in appointed officials.

The congress's two best-known decisions were announced in close succession. First, it approved the replacement of grain requisition with a fixed tax in kind, the foundation of the NEP, on a proposal presented by Lenin. The decision was initially presented as an emergency measure to restore the peasant alliance, and its implications for trade and industry only became clear over the following months. Second, it adopted a resolution "On Party Unity" that prohibited the formation of factions, defined as groups with separate platforms and their own internal discipline, and authorized the Central Committee, by a two-thirds vote, to expel even its own members for factional activity. A further resolution condemned the Workers' Opposition as a "syndicalist and anarchist deviation." A confidential clause on expulsion was not published at the time.

The pairing of economic liberalization with political tightening was not a coincidence but a conscious strategy, defended by Lenin on the grounds that retreating in economic policy while the party was divided would risk disaster. The ban on factions was initially presented as a temporary response to a crisis, and it did not formally ban discussion of policy or criticism in party meetings. In practice, however, it greatly strengthened the Central Committee and its secretariat against internal opponents, and it became a powerful tool in later leadership struggles (Chapter 54). The party also conducted a membership review (chistka) in 1921, which expelled or caused the departure of roughly a quarter of its members, many of them accused of careerism, passivity, or social unreliability.

Historians disagree about the long-term implications. Some interpret the 1921 ban as a decisive step toward the monopolization of power that later enabled Stalin; others stress that it reflected a crisis response and that significant debate within the party continued through the 1920s, even if constrained. What is clear is that the Tenth Congress did not change the party's formal political objectives: the dictatorship of the party, now justified in the language of the "dictatorship of the proletariat" and the "alliance of workers and peasants," remained the foundation of the regime, even as the economic basis of that alliance was reconstructed.

Section 53.5

From Requisition to Tax in Kind: The New Economic Policy

The Tenth Congress's decision was given legal form in the decree of 21 March 1921, which replaced requisition with a tax in kind (prodnalog). The principle was simple but its consequences were radical. Instead of taking all surplus above a minimum needed for subsistence, the state would demand a fixed proportion of the harvest, announced in advance and set lower than the requisition quotas; commonly cited figures suggest the first year's tax was substantially smaller than the grain the state had planned to requisition. After the tax was paid, peasants could dispose of their remaining produce as they wished, including by selling it. The tax was graduated according to harvest and household size, and was gradually converted into a money tax by 1924.

Legalizing sale of surplus necessarily implied legalizing trade. In the spring and summer of 1921, a series of decrees and regulations extended private exchange: small-scale trade was permitted, cooperatives were authorized to operate more freely, and bazaars reopened openly. By the middle of 1921 the government had also allowed the leasing of nationalized enterprises to private individuals, and the denationalization of many small workshops. The state retained control of large industry, transport, banking, and foreign trade. The party leadership debated the meaning of these changes: Lenin described them at various times as a "retreat," a form of "state capitalism" under proletarian political control, and a strategy for building economic links between town and countryside. In 1922, at the Eleventh Party Congress, he declared that the retreat had gone far enough and that the task was now to learn to trade and manage effectively.

NEP is best understood neither as a restoration of capitalism nor as the abandonment of socialist goals. The party continued to define itself as building socialism; the "commanding heights" of the economy, a phrase Lenin used in late 1922, remained in state hands; and political power remained a Communist monopoly. But NEP legalized market exchange, accepted a large private agricultural and commercial sector, and required the state to compete for peasant produce through prices rather than simply seize it. For many Bolsheviks this was ideologically painful, and some saw it as betrayal; for others it represented realism. Debates over how long NEP should last, how fast the state sector should grow, and how to relate to the peasantry became central issues in the succession struggle after Lenin's illness (Chapter 54).

Historical Vocabulary
Prodnalog (Tax in Kind)

Definition: A fixed tax paid by peasant households in agricultural produce, mainly grain, introduced in March 1921 to replace prodrazvyorstka, the requisition of "surplus" produce. After paying the tax, peasants could sell their remaining output.

Historical context: The tax in kind was the first legal measure of the New Economic Policy. It was converted into a single agricultural money tax by 1923–1924 as monetary stabilization progressed.

Why it matters: The change from requisition to tax altered incentives: peasants could now expand sowing without fear that all additional output would be seized. The term should not be confused with modern taxation by a stable state: the tax was set by a regime with limited statistical information and wide discretion, and local implementation varied.

Section 53.6

The Famine of 1921–1922 and International Relief

The introduction of the tax in kind came too late to prevent a major famine. The harvest of 1921 failed across the middle and lower Volga, the southern Urals, parts of southern Ukraine, Crimea, and Kazakhstan. The immediate cause was severe drought, but the catastrophe was shaped by earlier developments: peasants had sown less in 1920 and 1921 after years of requisition, livestock and draft animals had been lost to war and requisitioning, seed stocks had been consumed, and the transport system could not move grain effectively to affected areas. Reports of people eating substitutes for bread, slaughtering draft animals, abandoning villages, and, in some districts, resorting to cannibalism reached Moscow and foreign observers. Millions of people were displaced, and refugees spread typhus and other epidemics.

Estimates of famine mortality vary widely. Some scholarship places famine and famine-related mortality at about two million, while other major estimates place it around five million or higher; weak vital statistics, migration, and the difficulty of separating starvation from epidemic disease prevent a precise total. The government at first attempted to manage the crisis internally. In July 1921, the writer Maxim Gorky issued an international appeal for help, and a public committee that included non-Bolshevik figures (Pomgol) was authorized to organize relief, but it was dissolved within weeks and several members were arrested, illustrating the regime's suspicion of independent organization even during a catastrophe.

International relief nonetheless became decisive. In August 1921, the American Relief Administration, led by Herbert Hoover, signed an agreement with the Soviet government at Riga (negotiated by Walter Lyman Brown for the ARA and by Maxim Litvinov for the Soviet side) that allowed its personnel to operate in Soviet Russia under conditions of independence in distributing food. Other organizations, including the International Committee of the Red Cross, the Quaker (Friends) relief service, and a committee led by the Norwegian explorer and humanitarian Fridtjof Nansen, also provided aid. By the summer of 1922 the American Relief Administration was feeding nearly eleven million people a daily meal; grain shipments and the effects of the 1922 harvest allowed the program to end in 1923. The Soviet government cooperated while remaining wary, and accompanied relief with propaganda about the failure of capitalist states to alleviate hunger at home.

The famine also triggered a major confrontation between the state and the Russian Orthodox Church. In February 1922 the government decreed the confiscation of church valuables, officially to purchase grain for the starving. Patriarch Tikhon agreed to the donation of some items but objected to the confiscation of consecrated vessels; violent incidents occurred in several towns, including Shuia in March 1922. In a confidential letter to Politburo members that month, Lenin argued that the moment was favorable for a ruthless campaign against the clergy. Trials followed, including the prosecution of clergy in Moscow and Petrograd, several death sentences, the arrest of Patriarch Tikhon, and the state's encouragement of a reformist "Living Church" faction. The episode belongs to the history of both famine and religious policy, and is revisited in §53.13 and Chapter 58. Students should note that church property campaigns also had broader goals of weakening the church as an institution, and that the relationship between the famine and the campaign was a matter of debate then and since.

Section 53.7

Agriculture and the Peasant Economy under NEP

The countryside that NEP governed had been transformed by the revolution. The land decree of 1917 and subsequent local repartition had eliminated most large estates and redistributed land to peasant households, producing a countryside dominated by small family farms. Average farm size fell and the differences between wealthy and poor households narrowed, though they did not disappear. The village commune (mir or obshchina), which the Stolypin reforms of 1906–1911 had attempted to undermine (Chapter 47), was reinforced by the revolution and became the main institution allocating land, organizing agricultural calendars, and mediating relations with the state. The Land Code adopted in late 1922 formalized peasant land use, allowed households to leave the commune under certain conditions, and permitted limited leasing of land and hiring of labor, though with restrictions.

Agricultural recovery after 1921 was substantial. Sown area expanded each year as peasants regained confidence that they could keep their produce, and by the mid-1920s overall output approached prewar levels. The composition of production changed, however: peasants shifted somewhat toward industrial crops and livestock products and consumed more of their own grain, while the share of the harvest sold on the market remained well below the prewar proportion, since large estates and wealthy farms that had supplied much of the marketed grain no longer existed. This produced a structural problem for the state. Cities and the army needed grain, and so did exports; yet small peasant households responded to prices and conditions by holding grain back if terms of trade were unfavorable.

Contemporaries classified rural households into categories: poor peasants (bedniaks), middle peasants (seredniaks), and wealthier peasants whom Communists called kulaks, together with landless agricultural laborers (batraks). These categories were political as well as economic, and historians debate how precisely they describe rural stratification, since household fortunes changed as families grew and divided. The party's village presence was weak: rural party membership was small, village soviets were often dominated by local peasants, and the state relied heavily on taxation, credit, and cooperatives. Agricultural cooperatives, which Lenin commended in one of his last articles as a route to socialism through voluntary peasant participation, expanded in credit and marketing but remained mostly outside the collective-farm model later imposed.

Students should be careful about retrospective interpretation. Later Soviet writers, and many historians, treated NEP's agricultural difficulties as proof that the peasant economy was incompatible with industrialization, thereby justifying collectivization. But the grain problem of the mid-1920s was shaped by specific policy choices about prices, investment, and tax, and by competing views within the party about how to resolve it. Some party leaders, notably Bukharin later in the decade, believed the problem could be managed through market incentives; others advocated coercive procurement. Which approach prevailed depended on political conflict, international perceptions of threat, and individual decisions examined in Chapters 54 through 56, not on any single structural necessity.

Section 53.8

Markets, Private Trade, and the Nepmen

Legalization of exchange brought a rapid revival of commerce. Bazaars reopened in cities and towns; fairs, including the famous Nizhny Novgorod fair, were restored; peddlers, shopkeepers, restaurateurs, repairers, and small workshop owners reappeared in public view. By 1923 private traders handled a large majority of retail trade, although their share declined over the following years as cooperatives and state trade expanded and as taxation and regulation increased. Private wholesalers and intermediaries filled gaps in a distribution system that state agencies could not yet cover, especially in connecting peasants with city markets.

Private traders were known as Nepmen, a term that quickly acquired derogatory overtones. Nepmen included former merchants and artisans, demobilized soldiers, peasants who moved into trade, and former officials; they also included people of various national and religious backgrounds, and Jewish traders in particular faced stereotyping. The regime taxed Nepmen heavily, restricted their access to credit and state supplies, and disenfranchised many of them, since the 1918 constitution had denied voting rights to people who employed hired labor or lived on non-labor income. Popular attitudes were ambivalent: Nepmen provided goods that were otherwise scarce, but their visible consumption in a country recovering from famine provoked resentment, and satire, press campaigns, and party discourse depicted them as parasites.

The existence of the Nepmen illustrated a core contradiction. A party that claimed to build socialism had legalized private accumulation, and rank-and-file Communists, many of whom had spent the Civil War fighting the bourgeoisie, were uncomfortable with the new order. The regime's position was that private trade was a temporary necessity while state and cooperative institutions grew stronger. In practice, the boundary between legal and illegal activity was unstable. Entrepreneurs relied on informal relationships with state officials, who sometimes colluded with private traders, producing corruption scandals that became a theme in party self-criticism. The state also issued limited concessions to foreign companies, such as the Lena Goldfields mining concession, though foreign investment remained modest.

Historical Vocabulary
Nepmen

Definition: The private traders, small manufacturers, shopkeepers, and service providers who operated legally under NEP. The term was used in official and popular speech, usually with negative overtones.

Historical context: The Nepmen flourished between roughly 1922 and 1927, then were squeezed by higher taxes, credit restrictions, and administrative pressure as the party turned away from NEP. Many were later arrested, expropriated, or disenfranchised.

Why it matters: The Nepmen show that NEP was a real market economy at the retail level, and that it generated social tensions and political suspicion. The term is not synonymous with "capitalist": most Nepmen operated on a small scale and were under constant state supervision.

Section 53.9

State Industry, Finance, and the “Commanding Heights”

While markets operated in trade and agriculture, the state retained control of large-scale industry, railways, banking, and foreign trade, the sectors Lenin called the "commanding heights." Under NEP, however, these state enterprises were reorganized. Many were grouped into trusts, which were expected to operate on the principle of khozraschet, or cost accounting: they had to cover costs from sales, could be closed if unprofitable, and dealt with each other and with suppliers through contracts and money rather than allocations. From 1922 trusts also formed syndicates to coordinate marketing. A few small and medium enterprises were leased or returned to private owners. Planning institutions existed, including the State Planning Commission (Gosplan), founded in 1921, and the electrification program known as GOELRO, but they did not direct the economy as in the later Five-Year Plans (Chapter 55); NEP planning was mainly forecasting and coordination.

Monetary stabilization was one of the most consequential achievements. Wartime inflation had made the old paper currency (sovznaki) almost worthless, and the regime initially had no way to collect meaningful taxes in money. As the tax in kind was replaced by money taxation and the state budget came under the control of Finance Commissar Grigory Sokolnikov, a new currency, the chervonets, was introduced in late 1922. It was issued by the State Bank, in denominations of ten rubles and backed partly by gold and stable foreign currency, and it circulated alongside the old money before replacing it in a currency reform completed in 1924. The chervonets became a stable unit of account and restored confidence in money, though it was convertible internationally only in limited ways.

The most dramatic economic difficulty of the period was the Scissors Crisis of autumn 1923. As industry recovered, state and trust monopolies raised prices for manufactured goods, while agricultural prices, held down by a good harvest, fell. Trotsky used the image of scissors at the Twelfth Party Congress in April 1923 to describe the diverging price curves; by autumn the gap was wide enough that peasants reduced purchases of industrial goods and sold less grain, threatening the exchange on which NEP rested. The government responded with price controls, forced reductions in industrial prices, credit for trade, and administrative pressure on trusts and syndicates. The crisis eased in 1924, but it showed how fragile the market linkage between city and countryside was, and it sparked debates about industrial investment, pricing, and the pace of economic development that would shape later leadership struggles.

By the middle of the decade, industrial output had recovered to approximately prewar levels. This recovery was driven largely by restarting existing capacity rather than building new plants, which meant that further growth would require large investment, creating the central dilemma of later NEP: where would capital come from? Possible answers, including heavier taxation of the peasantry, foreign loans, or higher industrial profits, were contested. Students should recognize NEP as a genuine mixed economy under Communist political control: markets set many prices and organized much exchange, but the state owned the principal industrial assets, controlled foreign trade through a monopoly, and could change the rules by decree.

Section 53.10

Workers, Cities, and Everyday Life under NEP

The industrial working class that had been the Bolsheviks' declared social base had been decimated by war, hunger, and flight. Under NEP, urban populations began to recover as migrants returned and factories reopened. The Labor Code of 1922 regulated hiring, working hours, and collective agreements; wages were again paid in money, and differentials among workers widened as skilled workers were rewarded and managers were given authority to hire and dismiss. Trade unions, whose role had been the subject of fierce party debate, were retained as organizations that represented workers in negotiations with management and administered social insurance, but they remained subordinate to the party. Strikes occurred, particularly in 1922–1923 over delayed wages, and the authorities suppressed independent dissident workers' groups in 1923.

NEP created unemployment as a new feature of Soviet society. Cost accounting led trusts to shed surplus labor, and demobilized soldiers and peasants seeking work added to the labor market. Registered unemployment rose through the mid-1920s to well over a million people in some counts, and the true figure was probably higher. Women, young people, and unskilled workers were disproportionately affected. Labor exchanges and insurance provided limited relief, and many unemployed relied on family networks, casual work, and trade at bazaars. The experience contrasted with revolutionary promises of work and security and was a source of resentment among workers who saw Nepmen and managers prospering.

Urban life was shaped by scarcity of housing, which had been worsened by neglect and by the revolutionary redistribution of apartments. The policy of "densification" (uplotnenie) assigned multiple families to apartments formerly occupied by single wealthy households, producing the communal apartment (kommunalka) that became a defining feature of Soviet urban life. Shops and restaurants reopened; consumer goods reappeared, though prices were high relative to wages. Cities also faced social problems: crime, begging, and prostitution, which authorities and reformers attributed to unemployment, poverty, and the legacy of war. The number of homeless children (besprizornye), orphaned or displaced by war, epidemics, and famine, reached into the millions in the early 1920s; a government commission chaired by Felix Dzerzhinsky in 1921 organized children's homes, labor colonies, and security-service involvement, but resources were inadequate, and many children survived through street trade and petty crime.

NEP therefore produced winners and losers. Peasants who could sell surpluses, traders, skilled workers, and managers often saw their positions improve; unskilled workers, women, the unemployed, and many state employees saw little benefit. Party officials faced the challenge of defending a policy that allowed visible inequality. The tension between revolutionary egalitarianism and market incentives produced widespread cynicism and moral debate that appears in the literature and press of the period. For historians, urban life under NEP offers evidence that the early Soviet order was neither a simple restoration of prerevolutionary society nor the egalitarian society envisioned by revolutionary theory, but a distinctive hybrid.

Section 53.11

Party Rule and Political Repression

Whatever economic freedoms NEP allowed, the political system remained a one-party dictatorship. The party leadership viewed organized political opposition as a mortal threat, particularly after Kronstadt, and during 1921–1923 it completed the marginalization of the rival socialist parties. The Mensheviks, whose leaders such as Julius Martov had emigrated or been constrained, were subjected to arrests and trials and effectively eliminated as a legal political force; many Menshevik leaders were exiled or imprisoned. The Socialist Revolutionaries, whose party had been the largest in 1917, faced the most dramatic show of state power: a public trial in Moscow from June to August 1922 of thirty-four SR leaders on charges of anti-Soviet activity and terrorism. Twelve were sentenced to death, but the sentences were suspended on condition that the party renounce armed struggle, a form of hostage arrangement that drew protests from European socialists.

The state's political police changed form but not purpose. In February 1922 the Cheka was abolished and replaced by the State Political Directorate (GPU), formally attached to the People's Commissariat for Internal Affairs of the RSFSR; with the founding of the USSR the GPU became the Unified State Political Directorate (OGPU) in 1923. The reorganization was presented as a limitation of extraordinary powers and a transfer to judicial procedure, and it was accompanied by new criminal and procedural codes, but the GPU retained broad powers of arrest, investigation, and administrative sentencing. A system of detention camps, including the Solovki camp on the White Sea islands, expanded from 1923, forming a precursor of the later Gulag (Chapter 57).

Intellectual and cultural life was also supervised. In June 1922, the state created Glavlit, the central censorship authority, to review publications. Despite this, there was considerable range of publishing, including non-party writers, private presses, and journals, much more than in the Stalin era. In autumn 1922 the GPU arrested writers, scholars, and professionals considered ideologically hostile. The best-known deportation ships carried close to 160 passengers, including family members, while additional intellectuals were expelled or exiled by other routes. Among those forced abroad were Nikolai Berdyaev, Ivan Ilyin, and Pitirim Sorokin. This episode, sometimes called the "philosophers' ships," was presented as a humane alternative to execution. It showed a regime that was willing to remove prominent critics without a sustained campaign of terror.

The central analytical point is the contrast between economic relaxation and political tightening. Some historians interpret this as a deliberate Leninist strategy of securing power while retreating on the economy; others emphasize that the leadership's own debates and the party's uneven control of provincial institutions meant that political repression was less comprehensive than later. NEP-era repression was serious and often arbitrary, but its scale, methods, and targets differed from the mass operations of the 1930s examined in Chapter 57. Students should avoid reading the later terror backward into these years, while also avoiding the mistaken impression that NEP was a liberal interlude.

Section 53.12

Education, Literacy, and Revolutionary Culture

Education was central to the Bolshevik project, and the party invested in it even in scarcity. Anatoly Lunacharsky, People's Commissar of Enlightenment, headed a ministry that promoted a unified labor school, adult education, workers' faculties (rabfaks) that prepared workers for higher education, and a campaign against illiteracy (likbez, from the Russian phrase for "liquidation of illiteracy") begun by a decree of 1919 and expanded in the 1920s through voluntary societies. Funding shortages, the lack of teachers, and a collapsing school system limited the early results. The 1897 imperial census had recorded a literacy rate of about 28 percent for those aged nine and over; the 1926 Soviet census recorded about 56 percent, with large gaps between men and women, cities and villages, and Russians and some non-Russian groups.

Cultural life under NEP was unusually varied. State and party institutions sponsored official art, but they also tolerated a range of groups. Proletkult, an organization led by Aleksandr Bogdanov and others, advocated creating a distinctive proletarian culture and often rejected inherited forms; Lenin and the party criticized its claims to autonomy in 1920, and it declined. Avant-garde artists in the Constructivist and Futurist movements, including Vladimir Mayakovsky and Vladimir Tatlin, sought to link art to industrial production and revolutionary life, and organizations such as the Left Front of the Arts (LEF) were formed in 1923. The Serapion Brothers and other "fellow travelers," writers who accepted the revolution but not party discipline, produced prose that explored the violence and dislocation of the period. Early Soviet film developed under directors such as Dziga Vertov and Lev Kuleshov, with the state recognizing cinema as a vehicle for mass communication. Some works, such as Yevgeny Zamyatin's novel We, could not be published in the Soviet Union.

The party was divided about cultural policy. Trotsky's Literature and Revolution (1924) argued against a distinct proletarian literature and for a pluralistic approach under party guidance; other Communists wanted a more controlling approach. The leadership generally preferred supervision over prescription, expecting that the new generation would build socialist culture. Censorship through Glavlit and the party's press and publishing apparatus meant that open criticism of the regime was impossible, but stylistic experiment was often tolerated. Historians caution against projecting the later doctrine of socialist realism, examined in Chapter 58, backward onto the 1920s: it was formulated in the 1930s, and the earlier decade featured a range of competing styles and organizations.

The achievements and limits of educational and cultural policy deserve joint consideration. The expansion of schooling, literacy, and access to books and theater gave millions of people new opportunities, particularly young people from worker and peasant backgrounds who entered rabfaks and technical training. At the same time, class-based admission criteria disadvantaged children of former elites, school budgets lagged, and local variation was large. Cultural experimentation coexisted with political discipline and material scarcity.

Section 53.13

Women, Family, Religion, and Social Experimentation

The revolution had promised legal equality for women, and early Soviet law went further than most contemporary states. Decrees of 1917–1918 established civil marriage, made divorce easy to obtain, removed legal distinctions between legitimate and illegitimate children, and gave women formal rights to property and employment. In 1920 abortion was legalized in state hospitals, the first major country to do so, although it was presented as a temporary measure necessary because of poverty and the lack of contraception. The Women's Department of the party (Zhenotdel), headed in succession by Inessa Armand, Aleksandra Kollontai, and others, organized women's delegate assemblies, literacy and political education, and campaigns to bring women into soviets and unions.

Material conditions limited the reality of these changes. Under NEP, women were often the first laid off in industry as cost-conscious managers reduced employment; unemployment among women was high and the shortage of childcare restricted participation. Communal laundries, canteens, and nurseries, imagined as ways of liberating women from household labor, were scarce. In villages, women's legal equality was unevenly recognized, especially in areas where customary law governed marriage and property. Debates among Bolsheviks about sexuality, marriage, and family were open and sometimes bold, with Kollontai arguing for new forms of intimate relationships and others, including many party leaders, warning against promiscuity and neglect of children. The issue of "free love" was often exaggerated in public discourse. Divorce rates rose and abandoned women and children faced hardship; these social problems fed later debates leading to the 1926 Family Code and, in the 1930s, a shift toward stronger family policy (Chapter 58).

Religious policy combined legal separation, secularization, and conflict. A decree of 1918 had separated church from state and school and deprived religious organizations of legal status and property. Under NEP, antireligious propaganda increased, including satirical "Komsomol Christmases," while the state continued the campaign against the Russian Orthodox Church described in §53.6 and encouraged the "Living Church" reformist movement to divide the clergy. Other faiths were also affected: Islamic endowment lands and Shari'a courts in Muslim regions were curtailed in the early 1920s, Jewish religious institutions and Hebrew-language education came under pressure from the party's Jewish Section (Yevsektsiia), and Protestant groups experienced both restriction and, in some cases, relative tolerance. Evidence shows that popular religion persisted widely, particularly in villages, and that the effects of secularization were uneven across regions and generations.

Early Soviet social experimentation was therefore unusually ambitious, but it should not be seen as a program fully implemented. Communes, children's colonies, and new social institutions were created in some places; many were small, short-lived, or unrealistic in conditions of scarcity. The persistence of older practices, including patriarchal households, religious observance, and customary law, shows that Bolshevik ideas about transforming everyday life met limits. Historians stress that the revolution changed legal categories and institutional opportunities faster than it changed daily habits.

Section 53.14

Nationality Policy and Korenizatsiya

The Bolsheviks inherited the problem of governing a former empire whose population was roughly half non-Russian (Chapter 45). Before 1917 the party had criticized "Great Russian chauvinism" and endorsed national self-determination in principle, though Lenin and others opposed federalism as a general ideal and Stalin, as the party's nationalities specialist, favored autonomy within a unitary state. During the Civil War, the party discovered that national movements were politically powerful, that non-Russian peasants and elites could be mobilized by either side, and that the Red Army needed local allies (Chapter 52, §52.9). Following the military settlement, leaders concluded that Soviet power would be stable only if it appeared less Russian and more national in form.

The policy that emerged was formalized at the Twelfth Party Congress in April 1923 and is known as korenizatsiya, from the Russian word koren (root), often translated as "indigenization" or "nativization." It sought to build Soviet institutions "rooted" in each nationality by promoting local languages in schools, courts, and administration; recruiting local people into party and state positions; supporting national newspapers, publishing, and cultural institutions; and creating national territorial units, from union republics down to autonomous regions and even local national soviets. In Ukraine, "Ukrainization" expanded the use of Ukrainian in education and press under leaders such as Oleksandr Shumsky and Mykola Skrypnyk; in Belarus, a parallel program developed; in Central Asia, Transcaucasia, and the Volga region, languages that had been unwritten or written in Arabic script were standardized, and some, such as Azerbaijani, began to move to Latin alphabets.

Korenizatsiya operated alongside the centralized party. Local Communists were expected to follow central directives, and the party continued to treat national deviation as a danger. Mirsaid Sultan-Galiev, a Tatar Muslim Bolshevik who had argued that Muslim societies could pursue a distinct path to socialism, was arrested in 1923, the first major party figure to be so treated, on charges of "national deviation." Other Communists from national regions debated whether Russian "great-power chauvinism" or local nationalism was the greater danger, a dispute that continued throughout the decade. In Central Asia, the national-territorial delimitation of 1924, which created the Uzbek and Turkmen Soviet republics and other units from the former Turkestan Autonomous Republic and the Bukhara and Khorezm people's republics, reorganized peoples with overlapping identities into administrative nations, with lasting consequences.

Historians disagree about the nature of this policy. Some, emphasizing its institutions, describe the Soviet Union as an "affirmative action empire" that actively promoted national forms; others stress that the content of national culture was limited by censorship and party control, and that the policy was a means of securing Soviet rule rather than a commitment to national autonomy. The best approach for students is to recognize both elements: korenizatsiya created durable national institutions, elites, and standard languages, and it also constrained political expression. The later "retreat from korenizatsiya" in the 1930s is examined in Chapter 58, §58.5.

Historical Vocabulary
Korenizatsiya

Definition: The early Soviet policy of "rooting" the party and state in non-Russian nationalities by promoting local languages, recruiting national cadres, and creating national territorial units. Often translated as "indigenization."

Historical context: Formally endorsed at the Twelfth Party Congress in 1923 and implemented unevenly through the 1920s; its emphasis shifted in the 1930s.

Why it matters: The policy shows that the early Soviet state defined nations as legitimate political and cultural categories and institutionalized them, while retaining a centralized party. The English translation can mislead: it did not mean the end of Russian influence or the autonomy of national governments.

Section 53.15

The Federal Question: Autonomy, Union, and the National Republics

By 1922 the Soviet republics were linked by a patchwork of military, economic, and party arrangements. Ukraine, Belarus, Azerbaijan, Armenia, and Georgia had Soviet governments with their own Communist parties and commissariats, but their relations with the RSFSR were governed by bilateral treaties signed in 1920–1921 that left many functions to Moscow in practice. In March 1922, Georgia, Armenia, and Azerbaijan were combined in the Transcaucasian Federation, a step Georgian Communist leaders resisted. The People's Commissariat for Nationalities, headed by Stalin, and the party's Orgburo (Organizational Bureau) were deeply involved. Many officials found the arrangement confusing and inefficient, especially in finance, trade, and railways.

In August 1922 a party commission formulated what became known as Stalin's "autonomization" plan: the independent Soviet republics would formally enter the RSFSR as autonomous republics, so that the RSFSR's institutions would become the state's central organs. Supporters argued that this would simplify administration and end contradictions. Several national Communists, including Georgian leaders and some in Ukraine, objected that this would eliminate their republics' statehood. Lenin, who was recovering from an earlier stroke, criticized the plan in September, arguing that it was hasty and risked offending non-Russian peoples. He proposed instead that Russia, Ukraine, Belarus, and the Transcaucasian republics form a new union of equals, in which the RSFSR would be one member among several. The revised plan, accepted by the Central Committee in October 1922, preserved formal republican status and created new union institutions above the existing republics.

The dispute coincided with the Georgian Affair. In the autumn of 1922, tensions between Grigory Ordzhonikidze, the Transcaucasian party leader and a close ally of Stalin, and the majority of the Georgian Communist Central Committee, led by Budu Mdivani and Pilipe Makharadze, grew over the pace and form of integration and over issues of land and national policy. In October, Ordzhonikidze physically assaulted a Georgian Communist, Akaki Kabakhidze; the Georgian Central Committee resigned in protest. A commission headed by Felix Dzerzhinsky investigated and largely backed Ordzhonikidze. Lenin, whose illness limited his participation, dictated notes in late December 1922 criticizing the treatment of Georgia and Stalin's role, arguing that a Russian should be especially careful toward smaller nationalities. The further course of that criticism, and Lenin's role in the succession struggle, belongs to Chapter 54; here the key point is that the Georgian Affair exposed disagreements about how far central party authority should override republican communists.

The federal structure that resulted was therefore a compromise. It reflected the party's belief that national forms were necessary to secure loyalty, the practical needs of a unified economy and army, and concerns about Russian dominance. It also reflected a political calculation: a formal union with the declared right of secession could attract Communists in other states and distinguish the new state from the Russian Empire. Historians disagree about how much weight to give each factor. Some interpret the federation as primarily a response to non-Russian communists' pressure; others see it as Moscow's design from the start. The most defensible reading is that decisions emerged from conflict among leaders with different priorities, rather than from a single blueprint.

Section 53.16

The Formation of the Soviet Union

On 30 December 1922, the First Congress of Soviets of the USSR, meeting in Moscow, approved a Declaration and a Treaty on the formation of the Union of Soviet Socialist Republics. The founding republics were the Russian Soviet Federative Socialist Republic (RSFSR), the Ukrainian Soviet Socialist Republic, the Belarusian Soviet Socialist Republic, and the Transcaucasian Soviet Federative Socialist Republic, which combined Georgia, Armenia, and Azerbaijan. The Far Eastern Republic, a buffer state that had existed in eastern Siberia, had been absorbed into the RSFSR in November 1922. The declaration justified the union as a voluntary association created in response to the dangers of the capitalist world and the need for economic unity, and it declared that membership was open to other Soviet republics.

The constitutional framework was elaborated in the Union Constitution, approved in stages in 1923 and ratified by the Second Congress of Soviets of the USSR on 31 January 1924, days after Lenin's death on 21 January (examined in Chapter 54). The Constitution established a bicameral Central Executive Committee with a Union Council and a Council of Nationalities, a Council of People's Commissars of the USSR, and a Supreme Court. Certain commissariats, including foreign affairs, defense, foreign trade, transport, and communications, were all-union; others were unified, with both union and republican levels; and some, such as education, agriculture, and internal affairs, remained republican. Each republic retained a formal right of secession, which had no procedural mechanism and was never exercised during the early decades but later acquired real significance. The first Chairman of the USSR's Council of People's Commissars was Lenin, though his illness prevented him from serving in practice; Mikhail Kalinin, one of the co-chairmen of the Presidium of the union's Central Executive Committee, became its most prominent public figure.

The constitutional structure did not describe the real distribution of power. The Communist Party, which was a single organization rather than a federation of national parties, controlled appointments and decisions across republics through its Politburo, Secretariat, and local committees. The OGPU, created in 1923, operated across union territory. Republican institutions had real authority in areas such as education, language, and local administration, and national elites gained positions, but key decisions on the economy, the army, and security were made in Moscow. The RSFSR remained the largest republic and had no separate party organization or capital distinct from Moscow, a peculiarity that blurred the distinction between Russian and union institutions even as the constitution formally separated them. The national-territorial delimitation of Central Asia in 1924 created the Uzbek and Turkmen Soviet Socialist Republics; they were formally admitted to the USSR by the Third Congress of Soviets in May 1925.

By 1924, the system that had emerged combined several features that appeared inconsistent but were mutually dependent: a mixed economy under state control of the "commanding heights"; a monopoly political party that tolerated internal debate within limits; a formally federal, multinational state with institutionalized national territories and languages; an extensive state commitment to education and cultural change; and a security apparatus that could suppress opposition. This was not a finished system, nor one whose later evolution was determined. The question of NEP's future, the party's internal politics after Lenin's illness, and the shape of industrialization remained open, which is why the next chapter turns to the struggle for power that followed.

Primary Source Window

Resolution of the Petropavlovsk Crew and the Kronstadt Meeting (28 February–1 March 1921)

Creator: Sailors of the battleship Petropavlovsk, endorsed by a mass meeting at Kronstadt · Date: 28 February–1 March 1921 · Type: Collective political resolution

Context: After a delegation visited striking Petrograd factories, sailors on the Petropavlovsk adopted a list of political and economic demands, which a large assembly at Kronstadt endorsed on 1 March. The text was published in the rebels' newspaper and circulated in Petrograd; it is available in English and Russian document collections, and readers should consult a reliable edition and translator.

Paraphrase of the main demands (not a verbatim translated quotation):

The resolution called for new soviet elections by secret ballot with free preelection campaigning; freedom of speech and press for workers, peasants, anarchists, and left socialist parties; freedom of assembly and of trade unions and peasant associations; the release of socialist political prisoners; the abolition of party political departments in the armed forces and of party privileges in workplaces; equal rations for all working people; and the right of peasants to work their land and keep livestock so long as they did not hire labor.

Analysis: The resolution is direct evidence of what a mass meeting of sailors and soldiers asked for in their own name, and it shows that the demands were framed within the vocabulary of soviet democracy rather than monarchist or White restoration. It does not show the views of all garrison members, how many people attended and voted, or how far some leaders held more radical or moderate aims than the text suggests. The document also reflects peasant concerns and the influence of anarchist and socialist ideas. Government sources portrayed the revolt very differently, which is why the resolution should be read together with Bolshevik broadcasts, party reports, and accounts by participants who emigrated.

Historical Debate

What Was Kronstadt?

The question: Was the Kronstadt uprising a spontaneous revolt of sailors and workers against Bolshevik policy, a counterrevolutionary conspiracy, or a more complicated event involving elements of both?

Interpretation A (the official Soviet account): Soviet leaders and historians described the revolt as a White Guard conspiracy, organized by a former tsarist general and supported by émigrés and foreign governments, and exploiting the discontent of backward elements in the garrison. This view was used to justify the suppression and was repeated in Soviet textbooks.
Interpretation B (spontaneous popular revolt): Historians such as Paul Avrich, using the rebels' own publications, émigré memoirs, and Soviet archival materials available to him, argued that the revolt arose from shared grievances among sailors and Petrograd workers and was not planned from outside. On this view, émigré groups tried to exploit it after it began, but did not originate it.
Interpretation C (qualified or revisionist views): Other scholars emphasize changes in the garrison's composition, links between Kronstadt and Petrograd unrest, the role of anarchist and socialist currents, and the Bolsheviks' reasonable fear that a revolt at a strategic base could encourage broader collapse, while rejecting the conspiracy narrative. Some stress that the rebels' political aims were not uniform.

Evidence: Historians use the rebels' resolutions and newspaper (Izvestiia of the Provisional Revolutionary Committee), Bolshevik broadcasts and reports, memoirs by participants and émigrés, party and military records, and Finnish and émigré documents. Each type reflects its creator's position and context.

Points of agreement: Most scholars today agree that the revolt had a broad base among the garrison and that the conspiracy account is not supported by the available evidence as the origin of the uprising. They agree that it was crushed with substantial force and had a major influence on party policy.

Points of disagreement: Scholars disagree about the garrison's composition, the extent of outside contacts, the movement's political coherence, how far the leadership could have negotiated, and how much it influenced the adoption of NEP compared with Tambov and the broader crisis.

Why it matters: The answer affects how historians understand the relationship between the early Soviet regime and its original revolutionary constituency.

Student task: Select one claim from each interpretation and identify the type of evidence that best supports or challenges it, explaining the limits of that evidence.

Multiple Perspectives

Experiencing NEP

Peasant households: For most peasant families, the end of requisition meant that increased sowing was no longer automatically penalized, and the revival of markets allowed them to sell surpluses. Yet the famine of 1921–1922, the burden of taxation, uneven prices, and the weakness of rural credit limited the benefits, and wealthier households that expanded were politically suspect.
Nepmen and small traders: Traders and artisans could legally operate and often prospered, but they faced heavy taxes, civic disabilities, and public hostility, and understood that their legal position depended on the party's continued tolerance.
Communist officials and rank-and-file party members: Many party members who had fought in the Civil War experienced NEP as a retreat or even betrayal, particularly when they saw visible private wealth. Others, including managers and economic officials, considered it a practical necessity that allowed recovery.
Women workers and the unemployed: Women were disproportionately affected by layoffs under cost accounting, and the limited availability of childcare and social services meant that the legal equality proclaimed by the regime coexisted with economic insecurity.

These perspectives show why NEP cannot be summarized as simply a success or a failure: it benefited some groups, disappointed others, and created tensions within the party itself.

Cause and Effect

The Adoption of the New Economic Policy

Long-Term Conditions

Seven years of war and revolution; a small-farm peasant majority; a weakly industrialized, transport-dependent economy; and a party that had come to power with a minority base.

Intermediate Developments

The Civil War requisition system; the collapse of industry, currency, and cities; the revival of rural resistance as the White threat receded; and the internal party dispute over unions and labor policy.

Short-Term Triggers

The Tambov and Western Siberian revolts, the Petrograd strikes and Kronstadt rebellion, and the poor harvest prospects of early 1921.

Effects

The tax in kind and market exchange; recovery of agriculture and industry; monetary stabilization; social tensions around Nepmen; and the preservation of political monopoly through the ban on factions and the suppression of rivals.

Historical complexity: No single trigger explains NEP. Kronstadt dramatized the crisis, but Tambov and the wider rural revolt were important, and leaders had begun to discuss alternatives to requisition before March 1921.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From War Communism to NEP

What Changed

  • Requisition gave way to a fixed tax, and private trade became legal.
  • State enterprises operated on cost-accounting principles, and a stable currency replaced rationing as the main mechanism of exchange.
  • The state developed a nationality policy that promoted local languages and cadres and built a formally federal union.

What Remained

  • The party's political monopoly, the political police, and censorship.
  • State ownership of large industry, transport, banking, and foreign trade.
  • The ideological goal of building socialism and the expectation that NEP was temporary.

Why the pattern matters: The change was rapid in economic policy (months) and gradual in social and cultural life (years); peasants and traders experienced the greatest change, while the party leadership and security institutions experienced the greatest continuity.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Village under NEP

For a typical peasant household in central Russia or the Volga region, the early 1920s brought a series of changes in rapid succession: requisition detachments, then the tax in kind, then in many districts a famine year, then recovery. Families farmed small strips allocated by the commune, relied on household labor, and combined subsistence with sales at local markets. Cash needs for taxes, salt, kerosene, tools, and cloth drove sales, and village women's labor in textile and household production remained essential. In famine-struck districts, families sold livestock, tools, and household goods to survive, and some migrated.

The village was not a uniform community. Wealthier households hired labor and leased land, while the poor depended on wage work and the commune. Young men returning from the Red Army brought new ideas and sometimes Communist party membership, and the Komsomol and local soviets began to challenge older authorities. Priests, village elders, and customary courts continued to matter. Evidence about village life comes from state reports, statisticians, ethnographers, and memoirs, and each reflects the views of outsiders as much as of villagers, so students should treat generalizations carefully.

Regional Context

Beyond Moscow: Ukraine, Georgia, the Volga, and Central Asia

The settlement of 1921–1924 looked different outside Moscow. In Ukraine, the Soviet republic contained a large peasant population affected by requisition and famine, and Ukrainian Communists pressed for a federal structure and for the use of the Ukrainian language, shaping the policy of Ukrainization. In Georgia, a Soviet government imposed by the Red Army in 1921 confronted a Communist party divided over its relationship to Moscow and an urban population that remembered independence under a Menshevik government. In the Volga region, the autonomous Tatar and Bashkir republics combined national institutions with the burdens of the famine. In Central Asia, long-established Muslim societies, nomadic and settled, experienced the end of the Basmachi rebellion's most intense phase, changes in land and water policy, and the territorial delimitation of 1924, which drew new national boundaries. In each case, the central government's decisions were refracted through local conditions and local Communists, who sometimes resisted Moscow and sometimes exploited its policies.

Russia and the World

NEP, Famine Relief, and the International System

NEP coincided with the end of Soviet Russia's complete diplomatic isolation. The Anglo-Soviet trade agreement of March 1921 opened limited commerce with Britain, and the Treaty of Rapallo, signed with Germany in April 1922, restored relations and led to economic and, secretly, military cooperation. The famine brought a large international humanitarian operation, in which the American Relief Administration, Quakers, the Red Cross, and others worked in a state whose government they did not recognize. At the same time, the Communist International continued to promote revolutionary movements abroad, and foreign governments were wary of Soviet intentions. The result was a hybrid relationship of trade, diplomacy, relief, and suspicion rather than either integration or isolation. The Soviet leadership also followed European politics closely, expecting that revolution elsewhere might alter Russia's position; the failure of such revolutions by 1923 reinforced the importance of consolidating the state at home, a theme that returns in Chapter 54.

Section 53.17

Chapter Review and Historical Thinking Checkpoint

This chapter has argued that the years 1921 to 1924 were not simply a retreat from revolution. The Bolsheviks retreated economically because War Communism had become unsustainable and had provoked rural revolt and urban unrest, and the tax in kind, legal markets, and a stable currency allowed agriculture, trade, and industry to recover. At the same time they banned factions, suppressed rival socialists, strengthened the political police, and kept the "commanding heights" in state hands. They also built new institutions, including a federal union with national republics, and they sponsored schooling, literacy, cultural experiment, and legal changes affecting women and the family.

Causation here was layered. Long-term devastation, the structure of the peasant economy, and the Bolsheviks' narrow social base created the conditions; Tambov, Kronstadt, and the party's internal disputes forced action; and decisions by leaders such as Lenin, Stalin, and local Communists shaped outcomes. Evidence of different kinds, from party records to rebel resolutions and relief-agency reports, shows that the regime's own accounts were incomplete. Competing interpretations of Kronstadt, NEP, and the Union reflect different weighting of this evidence rather than simple political bias. Students should also remember that much remained open in 1924: the future of NEP, the relationship between party and republics, and the leadership after Lenin were unsettled, which is the subject of Chapter 54.

Chapter Summary

This chapter examined the transition from the Civil War and War Communism to the New Economic Policy and the formation of the USSR. Its central problem was how a regime that had won militarily but faced rural revolt, urban unrest, and economic collapse reconstructed its authority without relinquishing political monopoly.

The crisis of 1921 combined demographic loss, industrial collapse, currency breakdown, food shortage, and growing resistance to requisition. The Tambov rebellion showed that the peasant majority could mount sustained armed opposition, and the regime's response combined mass repression, including hostage-taking and camps, with the amnesty and abandonment of requisition. The Kronstadt revolt showed discontent among the sailors and the workers of Petrograd, framed in the language of soviet democracy, and was crushed by force.

The Tenth Party Congress paired two decisions: a retreat from requisition and a ban on factions. The tax in kind and the legalization of trade formed the basis of NEP, which created a mixed economy in which markets operated in agriculture and retail trade while the state retained large industry, banking, transport, and foreign trade. Trusts, cost accounting, the chervonets, and the resolution of the Scissors Crisis illustrated both the achievements and the fragility of the arrangement.

The famine of 1921–1922 revealed the limits of the economy and prompted an international relief effort alongside a conflict with the Orthodox Church over church valuables. Peasant agriculture recovered but remained small-scale and difficult for the state to procure from, and the Nepmen and unemployment generated social tensions that were visible in urban life, housing, and the plight of homeless children.

Political relaxation did not accompany economic relaxation. The regime suppressed Mensheviks and Socialist Revolutionaries, replaced the Cheka with the GPU and then the OGPU, created censorship institutions, and expelled intellectuals. Cultural policy, by contrast, tolerated considerable diversity, and educational campaigns raised literacy. Family law and women's policy were innovative but constrained by poverty.

Nationality policy and the creation of the USSR reflected the party's recognition that national forms were necessary to rule a multinational territory. Korenizatsiya promoted local languages and cadres, while the debate over autonomization and the Georgian Affair revealed conflict over how much authority Moscow should hold. The union founded on 30 December 1922 and confirmed in the 1924 Constitution was formally federal but politically centralized through the party and security apparatus.

Regional and international perspectives complicate any Moscow-centered account. Ukraine, Georgia, the Volga, and Central Asia experienced the period differently, and Soviet Russia's engagement with Britain, Germany, and international relief organizations placed NEP in a wider context.

Historiographically, the chapter showed disputes over the character of Kronstadt, the meaning of NEP as retreat or alternative, and the nature of the Soviet federation. These disputes depend on evidence and interpretive emphasis rather than simple partisanship, and the chapter's overall argument is that the early Soviet system was defined by interdependent contradictions.

These developments set the stage for Chapter 54, which examines Lenin's illness and death, Stalin's position as General Secretary, and the struggle for leadership, and for later chapters on industrialization and collectivization, which were consequences of decisions made after 1924 rather than of inevitable developments within NEP.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a revolutionary regime stabilized itself after victory by changing course in some respects and holding firm in others. The settlement of 1921–1924 did not resolve the contradictions it contained; it organized them into a working system in which markets, one-party rule, national republics, and cultural experiment coexisted, and which produced recovery, tension, and new institutions at once.

Many features of this settlement persisted. The party's political monopoly, the security apparatus, and the state's hold on the economic "commanding heights" remained, while the federal and national institutions created in these years outlived NEP and shaped Soviet history through 1991, as Chapters 58 and 66 show. Others did not: NEP was abandoned later in the decade, korenizatsiya was reshaped, and the relative cultural diversity of the 1920s narrowed.

The period reveals larger themes of the textbook: the relationship between state and peasantry, the management of a multinational territory, the interaction of ideology and material constraint, and the importance of evidence produced by people outside the regime. It also warns against hindsight. Contemporaries did not know that collectivization, rapid industrialization, or the terror lay ahead, and those developments emerged from choices examined in Chapters 54 through 57.

Questions remain open. Historians continue to debate how much real alternative NEP offered, how far the federation constrained Moscow, and how to estimate the human costs of the period. These questions return as the textbook turns to the struggle for leadership after Lenin.
