Part IX — Revolution and the Birth of the Soviet Union

The Provisional Government, Soviets, and the Revolutionary Year

A History of Russia: From the Eurasian World to the Russian Federation

Chapter Opening

A note on dates: unless marked otherwise, dates in this chapter follow the Julian calendar then used in Russia (Old Style, O.S.), which ran thirteen days behind the Gregorian calendar (New Style, N.S.) in 1917. The "February" and "October" revolutions are named by the Old Style calendar.

In the first days of March 1917 (O.S.), two bodies occupied different parts of the same building in Petrograd, the Tauride Palace, once the residence of Catherine II's favorite Grigory Potemkin and since 1906 the seat of the State Duma. In one wing, members of the Duma's Temporary Committee, liberal and moderate politicians mostly drawn from the propertied and educated classes, negotiated the formation of a cabinet. In another, hundreds of workers' and soldiers' deputies crowded into meetings of the Petrograd Soviet, elected in factories and barracks, debating how far the revolution should go and whether they should hold power at all. The government would soon move to the Mariinsky Palace, and the Soviet would later relocate to the Smolny Institute, but the proximity of the two bodies in those first days was a fitting image of what followed. Russia after the Romanovs had not one source of authority but several, and none was able to command general obedience.

Chapter 48 explained why the imperial system entered terminal crisis during the First World War, and Chapter 49 traced how that crisis became a revolution that ended the monarchy between 23 February and 2 March. This chapter examines a different problem: why the new political order created in February could not establish stable authority between March and October 1917. It studies a period of roughly seven months in which Russia was governed by four successive cabinets, remained at war with Germany and Austria-Hungary, saw its army transformed by committees and mass desertion, witnessed a vast peasant movement against landed estates, experienced strikes and the emergence of workers' control in factories, and watched national movements in Ukraine, Finland, and elsewhere press claims that no central authority was equipped to answer.

The chapter's subject is not a single event but a field of competing institutions, political movements, and social demands. The Provisional Government, formed by liberal and moderate leaders, claimed to be the lawful successor to the imperial government. The Petrograd Soviet and the soviets that spread across the empire represented a rival conception of revolutionary legitimacy, rooted in the workers, soldiers, and, increasingly, peasants who had made the revolution. Beneath and between them operated soldiers' committees, factory committees, trade unions, peasant land committees, city dumas and zemstvos, national councils, and Cossack assemblies. Historians often summarize this situation as "dual power," but, as Section 50.2 explains, the phrase captures only part of a more complicated reality.

The chapter also asks students to resist two temptations. The first is to read the period backward from October, treating each crisis as a step on a road leading to Bolshevik rule. The Bolsheviks were participants in some of the episodes studied here, especially the April crisis, the July Days, and the Kornilov Affair, but they were for most of this period a minority within the revolutionary movement, and the story of their rise belongs to Chapter 51. The second temptation is to treat the Provisional Government as simply doomed. Its leaders faced extraordinary constraints, including a war they had inherited, an economy near breakdown, and a society whose expectations had been raised enormously. They also made choices, such as postponing land reform and the Constituent Assembly and continuing the war, whose consequences must be assessed rather than excused or condemned in advance.

The evidence for this period is unusually abundant and unusually partisan. Government decrees, Soviet resolutions, newspapers of every political orientation, soldiers' and peasants' petitions, factory committee minutes, diplomatic dispatches, and memoirs by participants including Aleksandr Kerensky, Pavel Milyukov, Nikolai Sukhanov, and Leon Trotsky all survive in quantity. The memoirs, however, were mostly written afterward by people explaining defeat or justifying victory, and they must be read with the questions this textbook has asked since Chapter 1: who wrote this, when, for whom, and why? Local archives, petitions from villages and barracks, and contemporary press reports allow historians to see the revolution from outside Petrograd as well, though the capital still dominates the surviving record.

This chapter also places Russia in a wider setting. The Provisional Government's decisions were shaped by pressure from Britain and France, by the entry of the United States into the war in April, by German and Austro-Hungarian military and propaganda strategy, and by a European socialist movement divided over whether to support national war efforts. The February Revolution was watched, and in some cases actively exploited, by governments abroad. At the same time, the revolutionary experiment of 1917, with its councils, committees, and attempts to define democratic authority in wartime, belongs to a broader upheaval in which empires from the Baltic to the Pacific were coming under strain.

By the chapter's end, students should be able to explain the structure and limits of dual power, trace the major crises of the revolutionary year, assess the competing social movements of soldiers, peasants, workers, and national communities, interpret the July Days and the Kornilov Affair as contested historical episodes, and explain why the political center created in February had become so fragile by autumn. They should also understand why that fragility made a further transfer of power more likely without making any particular outcome inevitable.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain the problem of revolutionary authority created by the collapse of the monarchy in March 1917.
  2. Define dual power and distinguish it from a formal division of governmental responsibilities.
  3. Analyze the composition, program, and sources of legitimacy of the Provisional Government and the reasons its legitimacy was inherently limited.
  4. Explain why Mensheviks and Socialist Revolutionaries, rather than Bolsheviks, led the soviets in spring and early summer 1917.
  5. Evaluate Order No. 1 and the debate over its role in the transformation of the army.
  6. Trace the April crisis, the formation of the first coalition, and the June Offensive as stages in a crisis over war aims and military cohesion.
  7. Describe the peasant revolution, the movement for workers' control, and the national movements as separate, overlapping social revolutions.
  8. Distinguish among soviets, trade unions, factory committees, soldiers' committees, and land committees.
  9. Interpret the July Days and the Kornilov Affair using competing historical explanations and the available evidence.
  10. Assess the combination of causes that eroded the Provisional Government's authority, including contingent decisions as well as structural pressures.
  11. Distinguish the failure of the February political order from the question of Bolshevik victory, which Chapter 51 addresses.
  12. Connect developments in Russia to Allied and German policy and to wider upheavals in Europe and Eurasia.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. When a monarchy disappears during a war, what are the possible sources of legitimate authority, and which of them were available in Russia in March 1917?
  2. Why did the leaders of the Petrograd Soviet decline to take governmental power in the spring of 1917, and what were the consequences of that choice?
  3. Was Order No. 1 a cause of the army's disintegration, a symptom of changes already under way, or something more complicated?
  4. Why did the question of war aims prove so much more destabilizing than the question of the form of government?
  5. How did the peasant revolution, the workers' movement, and national movements each pose distinct challenges to the central government?
  6. How should historians interpret the July Days: as a failed coup, a spontaneous uprising, or an interaction of rank-and-file militancy and hesitant leadership?
  7. What does the Kornilov Affair reveal about trust, communication, and political polarization in 1917, and why do historians disagree about it?
  8. Which causes of the Provisional Government's loss of authority were structural, and which depended on decisions that might have been made differently?
  9. How did the experiences of Petrograd differ from those of villages, the front, and the borderlands?

Historical Context

The political landscape of March 1917 had been shaped by experiences that stretched back well before the war. The Revolution of 1905, examined in Chapter 46, had produced the first soviets, the first mass political parties, and the Duma. The Duma monarchy of 1906 to 1914, examined in Chapter 47, had given liberal and conservative politicians parliamentary experience while leaving real executive power with the emperor and his ministers. The Fundamental Laws of 1906 and a revised electoral law of 1907 had weighted representation heavily toward landowners and urban propertied voters. Socialist parties, meanwhile, had been driven underground or into exile, and many of their leaders spent the war years in Siberia or abroad. When the monarchy fell, therefore, Russia possessed a parliamentary tradition limited in scope, a vigorous public sphere of zemstvos, city dumas, cooperatives, and professional associations, and socialist parties with deep roots but little recent experience of governing.

The First World War, examined in Chapter 48, had expanded the role of those public organizations. Zemstvo and municipal unions, the War Industries Committees, and voluntary associations had taken on tasks of supply and relief that the imperial bureaucracy could not manage. By 1917 the state's administrative capacity, never extensive at the local level in a country of Russia's size, had been further strained by mobilization of millions of men, inflation, transport breakdown, and food shortages. The mobilized army was overwhelmingly composed of peasants, commanded by an officer corps that had been transformed by wartime casualties and by the rapid commissioning of men of lower social origin. The urban workforce had grown during the war, especially in armaments industries in Petrograd, Moscow, and the Donbas, and the capital's garrison had swollen to include large numbers of reserve troops who dreaded being sent to the front.

The February Revolution itself, examined in Chapter 49, was the product of bread riots, strikes, and mutinies that overwhelmed the capital's authorities in a few days. The Duma politicians who formed the Provisional Government did so partly to channel the revolution and partly out of fear that it might otherwise become uncontrollable; the socialists who formed the Petrograd Soviet did so out of a tradition, dating from 1905, in which soviets served as organs of working-class action. Neither body had planned the revolution, and neither had a worked-out program for governing in its aftermath. They were improvising within a war that most politicians, liberal and socialist alike, believed Russia could not simply abandon.

Finally, the revolutionary year unfolded in a multinational empire, examined in Chapter 45, in which the collapse of centralized authority immediately raised the question of the status of non-Russian territories. Poland was under German and Austro-Hungarian occupation, Finland possessed its own constitution and Diet, Ukraine and the Baltic provinces had active national movements, the Caucasus and Central Asia had large Muslim and Christian populations with their own political agendas, and Siberia and the steppe had indigenous and settler populations whose relationship to the capital was distant. The Provisional Government's decisions about the empire's structure were therefore decisions about much more than Petrograd politics. Students should keep in mind throughout this chapter that "Russia" in 1917 was a contested political space rather than a settled national territory.

Section 50.1

Russia After the Monarchy: The Problem of Revolutionary Authority

Nicholas II abdicated on 2 March 1917 in favor of his brother Grand Duke Mikhail Aleksandrovich, and on 3 March Mikhail deferred assuming supreme power unless and until a Constituent Assembly confirmed it. Russia thereby ceased to have a monarch without having decided what would replace the monarchy. The empire's ministers were under arrest, the police and gendarmerie had dissolved in the capital, and provincial governors were being replaced by commissars, usually the chairmen of local zemstvo boards or city dumas. The ministries, the railways, the post and telegraph, the courts, and most local administration continued to function, but they did so on the basis of habit and the cooperation of officials rather than a clear chain of legitimate command.

What had not changed was as important as what had. The war continued, with millions of men in the trenches on a front stretching from the Baltic to the Black Sea and the Caucasus. Food and fuel were scarce, and the transport system that carried them was overstrained. The social conflicts that predated the war, including peasant land hunger, labor disputes, and national grievances, were not ended by the monarchy's fall and were, in many cases, intensified by the sudden lifting of restraints. The tens of thousands of public meetings, resolutions, and elections of March and April reflected a widespread belief that the "days of freedom" opened the way to a rapid settlement of every long-delayed question.

The central problem was one of legitimacy. Under the old order, authority had flowed, in principle, from the emperor. In March 1917, at least four sources of claimed legitimacy competed. The State Duma, whose Temporary Committee had taken the initiative in forming a government, had been elected under a restrictive franchise and had been dissolved by imperial decree; it claimed continuity with representative institutions but could hardly claim to speak for the whole nation. The Petrograd Soviet claimed to represent the revolutionary people, especially workers and soldiers, but it was a self-constituted body of one city. The army's command structure claimed the loyalty of the armed forces and the duty to defend the country. And a future Constituent Assembly, elected by universal suffrage, was promised by nearly everyone as the sole body entitled to decide Russia's permanent political and social order. Until it met, every government would be provisional, and every major decision about land, the empire's structure, and the constitution could be said to lack final authority.

Two further features of the situation deserve emphasis. First, almost every major political party in March accepted that the revolution was, in its immediate historical task, a "bourgeois-democratic" revolution, meaning one that would establish civil liberties and parliamentary government rather than socialism. This view, held with particular conviction by Mensheviks, shaped the Soviet leadership's reluctance to take power itself. Second, the leaders of both the government and the Soviet feared that disorder would bring military defeat or counterrevolution, and this fear encouraged cooperation even where their aims diverged. The rest of the chapter examines how that cooperation worked, and how it eventually broke down.

Section 50.2

Dual Power

Contemporaries and later historians have used the Russian term dvoevlastie, usually translated "dual power," to describe the coexistence of the Provisional Government and the Petrograd Soviet from March to the autumn of 1917. The phrase was used by Lenin in April, who contrasted the government's formal authority with the Soviet's actual power, and it became standard in later Soviet and Western writing. It is a useful shorthand, but it can mislead if it suggests two governments formally dividing responsibilities, each with its own territory or functions, in a stable constitutional arrangement. What existed instead was an unstable and informal relationship in which each side depended on the other and neither fully controlled the situation.

The foundation of the relationship was a set of understandings reached on 1 and 2 March between the Duma Committee and the Soviet's Executive Committee. The Soviet leaders, who commanded the loyalty of the capital's workers and garrison, agreed not to claim the government for themselves and to support the new cabinet provided it carried out a program that included an immediate political amnesty, civil liberties including freedom of speech, press, assembly, and strikes, the abolition of legal restrictions based on class, religion, and nationality, the replacement of the police by an elected people's militia, elections to local government bodies, preparation for a Constituent Assembly, and a guarantee that the military units that had taken part in the revolution would not be disarmed or removed from Petrograd. The Soviet's leaders expressed their policy as conditional, supervisory support: they would back the government as long as it served the revolution, and they would monitor it through a Contact Commission.

The practical balance of power was uneven but not fixed. The government held the formal machinery of the state, including the ministries, the diplomatic corps, the financial system, and the recognition of the Allied powers. The Soviet held the allegiance of the garrison and the factories, and with it effective control of the means by which orders were enforced in the capital. Railway, postal, and telegraph workers, who sympathized with the Soviet, could in practice determine whether government messages were transmitted. The government could not issue an order to the army that the Soviet would not tolerate, and the Soviet could not carry out major policies without ministries staffed by officials the government appointed. Soldiers and workers, meanwhile, often chose between the two bodies depending on the question. A common pattern in the spring was to accept the government's authority in principle while following the Soviet's guidance in practice.

The most important question about dual power is why the Soviet leadership, with greater popular support than the government, declined to govern. Several explanations have been offered, and they are not mutually exclusive. The Menshevik and many Socialist Revolutionary leaders held that a bourgeois revolution required bourgeois government, and that a socialist cabinet would be isolated in a country where the propertied classes and the officer corps controlled much of the state's machinery. They feared that a seizure of power would invite civil war and possibly allow the Germans to exploit Russian disarray. They also lacked, in March, administrative experience and a trained staff. Others, including some Bolsheviks, argued that this reluctance was a political mistake; Chapter 51 considers their reasoning. Whatever the reasons, the result was that the Soviet exercised power without responsibility, while the government carried responsibility without commanding the instruments of power.

Dual power also looked different outside the capital. In many provincial towns, local soviets coexisted with committees of public organizations, city dumas, and government commissars, and in some places the commissar's authority was minimal. In villages, the relevant institutions were often village assemblies and land committees. In the army, it was soldiers' committees and, increasingly, commissars. A student should therefore think of dual power as a pattern of overlapping and uncertain authority across a variety of institutions rather than a single contest in Petrograd.

Historical Vocabulary
Dual Power (dvoevlastie)

Definition: The informal coexistence, from March 1917, of the Provisional Government, which held formal governmental authority, and the Petrograd Soviet and other soviets, which held substantial practical authority over soldiers, workers, and communications.

Historical context: The term became common after Lenin's April writings, though contemporaries described the situation in related language from March. Its application varied across the empire: in some regions soviets dominated, in others local dumas, zemstvos, or commissars did.

Why it matters: The concept explains why neither the government nor the soviets could make or enforce decisions on war, land, or economic regulation without the other's cooperation. It should not be read as a constitutional division of powers; it was an unstable improvisation.

Section 50.3

The Provisional Government: Liberal Revolution and Political Legitimacy

The first Provisional Government, formed on 2 March (15 March N.S.) and publicly proclaimed on 3 March (16 March N.S.), was headed by Prince Georgy Lvov, a zemstvo leader with a reputation as a reformer and administrator, who also served as minister of the interior. Pavel Milyukov, the leader of the Constitutional Democrats (Kadets), became foreign minister; Aleksandr Guchkov, an Octobrist and industrialist who had been a prominent critic of the court, became minister of war and the navy; Aleksandr Konovalov, a textile manufacturer, took trade and industry; Mikhail Tereshchenko, a sugar magnate, became finance minister; and Andrei Shingarev, a Kadet physician, took agriculture. The only socialist was Aleksandr Kerensky, a lawyer and Duma deputy associated with the Trudovik and Socialist Revolutionary milieu, who became minister of justice and, uniquely, was also a vice-chairman of the Petrograd Soviet. Kerensky had accepted his post at the Soviet on 2 March, in a speech which won him prominence as a mediator between the two centers, though the Soviet leadership did not formally authorize participation by socialists in the government at this stage.

The cabinet's makeup reflected the political and social weight of the liberal and moderate public that had long demanded constitutional reform: landowners, industrialists, professionals, and zemstvo and municipal leaders. Its early record was substantial. It proclaimed freedom of speech, press, assembly, and association, abolished the death penalty (later restored in the front zone for specified grave military offenses in July), declared a political amnesty, dissolved the political police, replaced local police with a militia, and ended legal restrictions on the basis of religion, nationality, and social estate on 20 March, which for the first time removed the formal legal disabilities that had burdened the empire's Jewish population. It restored Finland's constitution, issued a statement of principle in favor of an independent Poland in ethnically Polish territory, and on 20 July approved women's participation in the Constituent Assembly elections, making Russia the first major power to grant women both the vote and the right to hold office at the national level. These were real changes, and they gave Russia, for a time, some of the freest political conditions of any major belligerent.

The government's central difficulty was that it was provisional by its own declaration. It had not been elected, and it said so; it promised to govern until a Constituent Assembly, elected by universal, equal, direct, and secret ballot, could determine the country's permanent form of government. Its members treated this as a reason for caution. Major social questions, especially the redistribution of land, were to await the Assembly, on the argument that a government without a mandate should not prejudge them and that sweeping changes in the middle of a war could disrupt the army and food supply. Preparation of the Assembly had been delayed for months. On 14 June the government formally scheduled elections for 17 September; on 9 August, citing the difficulties of preparing electoral lists and machinery across a vast country at war, it postponed them to 12 November (25 November N.S.). The delay reinforced the impression, among soldiers, peasants, and workers pressing for change, that the government was delaying.

A second difficulty was administrative. The government inherited a state apparatus that had been strained to the limit, and it had few instruments of enforcement of its own. The new militias were improvised and local; commissars appointed in the provinces often lacked the means to compel compliance; and the army, as Section 50.5 shows, was increasingly governed by committees. The government relied on cooperation by officials, public organizations, and the Soviet, and its authority was, accordingly, most effective when its decisions accorded with popular expectations and least effective when they did not.

Finally, the government's ideological outlook shaped its choices. Liberal leaders such as Milyukov regarded the revolution as an opportunity to prosecute the war more effectively and to preserve the empire's integrity. They also believed that social reforms should be orderly, lawful, and constitutional. This outlook was not dishonorable or irrational, and it was shared by many socialists in the early weeks. But it placed the government at odds with the expectations of much of the population, who understood "freedom" to include an end to the war, land for the peasants, and improved conditions for workers. The tension between these views structures most of the rest of this chapter.

Section 50.4

The Petrograd Soviet and the Spread of Soviets

Chapter 49 described the creation of the Petrograd Soviet of Workers' and Soldiers' Deputies on 27 February and 1 March. This section examines how it functioned and why, in spring and early summer, it was led by moderate socialists. The Soviet's membership expanded quickly, as factories and, after the merger of the workers' and soldiers' sections on 1 March, regiments elected representatives; its membership eventually reached the thousands. Because plenary meetings were large and unwieldy, power resided in an Executive Committee in which party delegates from the socialist parties and elected deputies sat together. The Executive Committee's chairman was the Georgian Menshevik Nikolai Chkheidze, with Matvei Skobelev and Kerensky as vice-chairmen. Its leading political figure from the end of March was another Georgian Menshevik, Irakli Tsereteli, who returned from Siberian exile and became the principal architect of cooperation with the government.

The Soviet was dominated in these months by Mensheviks and Socialist Revolutionaries, with a Bolshevik minority. The reasons are significant for avoiding hindsight. The Socialist Revolutionaries had the allegiance of much of the peasantry, who made up most of the soldiers, and the party's language of land and freedom had broad appeal. The Mensheviks had organizational experience in trade unions, cooperatives, and the 1905 soviet, and many of their leaders were prominent intellectuals. Both parties were identified with the moderate view that the revolution should be defended through cooperation with liberal elements. The Bolsheviks, who had been repressed and divided during the war, were smaller, and many of their early leaders in the capital had only recently returned from exile or prison. At the First All-Russian Congress of Soviets, which met from 3 to 24 June, the Socialist Revolutionaries and Mensheviks had a large majority; the Bolsheviks held roughly one-eighth of the delegates, though they were growing.

The Soviet leadership's conception of the revolution is often summarized by the phrase "revolutionary democracy," which denoted the workers, soldiers, and peasants and their socialist parties, in contrast to "propertied society" (tsenzovoe obshchestvo), the landowners, industrialists, and educated professionals represented by the Kadets and other liberals. The Soviet's moderate leaders sought to unite the two, hoping that the revolution would remain a broad national alliance against the old regime. In practice, the policy of conditional support required them to restrain popular demands, including those of soldiers and workers, in the interests of preserving the alliance and the war effort, and this restraint was one source of tension between the leadership and the rank and file.

Soviets multiplied across the empire. Within weeks of the February Revolution, soviets, or similar councils, existed in most large towns, and in the following months they spread to smaller towns, garrisons, and the countryside, where peasant soviets and later volost-level institutions took form. The All-Russian Congress of Peasants' Deputies, held in Petrograd in May and dominated by Socialist Revolutionaries, created its own executive. The First Congress of Soviets in June elected an All-Russian Central Executive Committee (VTsIK), which claimed authority to coordinate soviets nationwide. Soviets differed enormously in composition and character. Some were dominated by moderate socialists, some by local Bolsheviks or other radicals, some by national parties; some focused on political supervision, others on economic regulation, food supply, or administration. Students should therefore treat "the soviets" as a family of institutions rather than a single organization, and should note that non-Russian soviets and national councils did not always cooperate.

Section 50.5

Order No. 1 and the Democratization of the Armed Forces

Few documents of 1917 are as famous, or as contested, as Order No. 1 of the Petrograd Soviet, issued on 1 March (14 March N.S.). It emerged from a particular context. Soldiers of the capital's garrison had mutinied against their officers on 27 February, and many feared reprisal if the movement failed. When the Soviet's soldier deputies met on the evening of 1 March, they demanded guarantees; a group including the lawyer Nikolai Sokolov drafted a text addressed to the garrison of the Petrograd military district. The order directed units to elect committees of elected representatives of the lower ranks, to send delegates to the Soviet, to follow the Soviet's orders on political questions, and to obey the orders of the Duma's Military Commission only where they did not conflict with those of the Soviet. It placed weapons under the control of unit committees and not officers, abolished certain forms of deference and demeaning address, and extended civil rights to soldiers when off duty, while insisting on strict discipline during duty.

The order was addressed to Petrograd, but it was soon telegraphed and distributed across the front and rear, where many units took it as applying to themselves. The Soviet and later the government attempted to clarify its scope, and on 11 May (24 May N.S.) the war minister Kerensky issued a Declaration of Soldiers' Rights that incorporated civil liberties while attempting to preserve the authority of commanders. By then, however, soldiers' committees existed in most units, at the level of regiment, division, army, and front, and in the fleet. In the Baltic Fleet the revolution had been accompanied by killings of officers, including Admirals Adrian Nepenin and Robert Viren, in early March.

Officers' memoirs and later conservative and émigré writing often described Order No. 1 as the act that destroyed the Russian army. The war minister Guchkov complained at the time of the damage it did to discipline, and many officers experienced the changes as an attack on their authority and status. The evidence, however, points to a more complicated picture. The army's cohesion had been deteriorating before February under the strain of casualties, shortages, and rumors of corruption and betrayal at the top. The officer corps had been transformed by the war: tens of thousands of junior officers drawn from the educated middle and lower classes sat uneasily between a conservative senior command and the peasant soldiers. The soldiers' demand for dignity and for a say in decisions affecting their lives expressed grievances that long predated the order, and in many units committees initially worked with officers to maintain order and even to support continued fighting.

Historians therefore generally view Order No. 1 as one factor among several, and as a symptom as well as a cause. It accelerated the loss of traditional hierarchies and legitimated the idea that soldiers could contest orders, particularly orders that threatened their lives or were perceived as serving the old regime. But the larger forces behind military collapse included the war's length, the expectation that the revolution would soon bring peace, the soldiers' ties to villages seething with land hunger, and the failure of the government to offer a persuasive account of why the war should continue. Historians such as Allan Wildman, whose studies of the army remain central, have emphasized the gradual, uneven, and politically structured character of the army's transformation rather than a sudden collapse in March.

Section 50.6

War, Peace, and the April Crisis

The war was the revolution's central contradiction. The Provisional Government, committed to its allies, intended to continue the war until victory, and its foreign minister Milyukov hoped that Russia would obtain the long-sought control of Constantinople and the Straits as part of a victorious settlement. Many in revolutionary society, by contrast, understood the revolution as a chance to change the character of the war, and the Soviet declared on 14 March in an appeal "To the Peoples of the Whole World" for a democratic peace without annexations or indemnities, based on the self-determination of peoples. Between these positions emerged the stance known as revolutionary defensism: support for defending the revolution and Russia against German and Austro-Hungarian attack, combined with active efforts to bring about a negotiated peace.

Historical Vocabulary
Revolutionary Defensism

Definition: The position, held by most Mensheviks and Socialist Revolutionaries in 1917, that Russia must defend itself and the revolution against military defeat while pursuing a general democratic peace without annexations or indemnities.

Historical context: Revolutionary defensists distinguished themselves both from "defencists" of the old regime, whose aims included territorial gains, and from "internationalists," including the Bolsheviks, who called for ending the war immediately and rejected defense of a government of the propertied classes.

Why it matters: The position bound the soviet leadership to the government's military effort, and made them responsible for the consequences when the war grew less tolerable to soldiers and workers.

The government's declaration of 27 March asserted that the aim of a free Russia was not domination over other peoples but a lasting peace based on national self-determination, language that satisfied the Soviet only partly because it omitted a clear repudiation of earlier Allied secret agreements. The Allies themselves pressed Russia to continue fighting and, after the United States entered the war in April, anticipated a coordinated effort in the spring. Milyukov, after a delay, sent a note to the Allied governments on 18 April (1 May N.S.) reaffirming Russia's determination to fight to a decisive victory and to honor its obligations. Its reference to "sanctions and guarantees" was read by many as a repudiation of the declared peace-without-annexations policy.

The reaction was immediate. On 20 and 21 April, soldiers and workers marched in Petrograd, some demanding Milyukov's removal, others supporting the government, and counter-demonstrations by middle-class residents appeared as well. General Lavr Kornilov, then commanding the Petrograd military district, wished to use troops against the demonstrators, and the Soviet, which regarded troop movements without its sanction as a threat, ordered that soldiers should not leave their barracks under arms without its authorization. The incident illustrated dual power in action: a military commander subject to the government was effectively constrained by a body outside it. Violence was limited, but the confidence of liberals in the stability of the arrangement was shaken.

The crisis forced a reconstruction. Guchkov resigned on 30 April, followed by Milyukov on 2 May, and after negotiations the Soviet leadership agreed that socialists should join the cabinet. The first coalition government, announced on 5 May, retained Lvov as head, made Kerensky minister of war and the navy and Tereshchenko foreign minister, and brought in the Menshevik leaders Tsereteli and Skobelev and the Socialist Revolutionary leader Chernov, who took agriculture. The Soviet leadership had accepted shared responsibility, hoping to strengthen the government, restrain the army's disintegration, and press the Allies toward a peace conference. Instead, it tied the socialist parties to a government whose decisions on war, land, and the economy would be judged by increasingly impatient constituents. The coalition's formation also shows that the Bolsheviks, who rejected participation, remained outside the central bargain; their role in these events is the subject of Chapter 51.

Section 50.7

The June Offensive and the Crisis of the Army

The most consequential decision of the first coalition was to resume offensive operations. Kerensky, as war minister, believed that the revolution could renew the army's purpose and that Russia owed its allies a military effort; he toured the front in May and June giving speeches urging soldiers to defend the revolution and take the offensive. Aleksei Brusilov, who had commanded the successful offensive of 1916, replaced Mikhail Alekseev as supreme commander at the end of May. Allied governments pressed for action to relieve their own fronts, and many Russian politicians feared that a passive army would lose all remaining credibility with their allies and with the Germans. The decision was also made against the background of a strong movement of the Soviet leadership toward ending the war by negotiation, which the government hoped to support from a position of strength.

The offensive began on 18 June (1 July N.S.), with the main attack on the Southwestern Front in Galicia. Meanwhile, in Petrograd, the Soviet organized a large demonstration the same day, in which many marchers carried banners with slogans against the "capitalist ministers" that reflected the growing influence of the Bolsheviks and other radicals. In Galicia, the Eighth Army, under Kornilov, broke through Austro-Hungarian lines and advanced toward Halych and Kalush, and for a few days it appeared that the revolutionary army might succeed. The offensive also encouraged the use of volunteer shock formations intended to restore morale. Among the most famous was Maria Bochkareva's Women's Battalion of Death, which joined the wider offensive effort and later fought near Smorgon, becoming a powerful symbol for advocates of renewed military discipline.

The advance did not hold. Many units refused to attack after the first days, or held meetings to debate orders; committees sometimes voted not to advance; and units that did attack suffered casualties without lasting gains. On 6 July German forces, reinforced and well prepared, counterattacked near Tarnopol, and the Russian front on that sector broke. Soldiers retreated, in some cases in disorder, abandoning positions and equipment, and there were reports of looting and violence against civilians in the territory they evacuated. Casualty figures are uncertain and vary by source, but the damage was heavy in both lives and morale, and the German and Austro-Hungarian advance recovered much of the territory the Russians had gained.

The government's response showed the strain. On 12 July, it restored the death penalty in the front zone for specified grave military offenses, and on 19 July Kornilov replaced Brusilov as supreme commander, partly because of Kornilov's reputation for firmness and partly because he was the favorite of those demanding a restoration of discipline. Commissars of the government and soldiers' committees, who attempted to mediate between officers and men, were blamed by officers for weakness and by soldiers for compromise. The deeper issue, however, was political rather than tactical. The offensive had been justified as a means of strengthening the revolution; its failure suggested that the army could not be trusted to fight, while the government's attempts to restore discipline alienated the soldiers who had expected change. The war, for many soldiers, workers, and peasants, was now clearly the government's war, and its continuation increasingly became a measure of the government's legitimacy.

Section 50.8

Land and the Peasant Revolution

Most of the empire's population were peasants, and for them the revolution's central question was land. Peasant communities had long resented the concentration of landholding in the hands of nobles, the crown, the church, and, increasingly, wealthy peasants and capitalist estates, and they had expected since the emancipation of 1861, discussed in Chapter 42, that the land would eventually be redistributed to those who worked it. The revolutions of 1905 and the Stolypin reforms of 1906 and after, examined in Chapter 47, had left many of these grievances unresolved. In 1917, with the police gone, the landlords' protectors discredited, and the village's young men returning from or still serving at the front, peasants saw an opportunity to settle the question themselves.

The Provisional Government's position was that fundamental land reform must await the Constituent Assembly. Its ministers feared that wholesale redistribution during the war would disrupt grain supply and the army, many of whose officers were landowners, and that unilateral decisions would be illegitimate. In April it created a network of land committees, from a main committee in Petrograd to volost and district committees, to collect information and mediate disputes. Peasants, however, treated the committees as instruments through which communities could regulate rents, seize unused land, and prepare for redistribution. Chernov, as the Socialist Revolutionary minister of agriculture, sought to steer the movement toward orderly change and attempted in July to limit land sales, but he faced opposition from liberal colleagues and lacked means to enforce policy.

The peasant revolution took many forms. Villages refused to pay rents or reduced them by decision of the community; they cut timber and used pastures belonging to estates; they seized livestock and tools; they pressed landlords to hand over land; and, in some regions, they attacked and burned manor houses and drove out their owners. Most of this activity was organized by village assemblies and communal institutions, with a strong sense of legality grounded in the idea that the land belonged to those who worked it. Violence increased as the year went on, especially in the autumn, and was most intense in the central agricultural provinces, the Volga region, and parts of Ukraine, where landlord estates were concentrated and peasant land shortage most acute. The picture differed regionally: Cossack lands had a different structure of landholding, Siberia had more available land, and the Baltic provinces, Central Asia, and the Caucasus had distinct patterns of ownership and conflict, including grievances of Kazakh and other nomadic or semi-nomadic populations over settler colonization.

Soldiers played an important part. Deserters and furloughed men returned to villages with weapons, news, and political ideas; soldiers' letters, petitions, and visits linked the army's fate to the countryside's. Peasant petitions and resolutions of 1917, many collected in the summer by the All-Russian Congress of Peasants' Deputies, expressed a widespread demand for the abolition of private property in land and its transfer to the community. The Socialist Revolutionary Party, whose program called for socialization of land, benefited from this support, but its leaders in government were constrained by coalition and by the government's insistence on legality. The result was a growing gap between the central parties and peasants who increasingly acted without waiting for the Constituent Assembly.

The central point for this chapter is that the peasant revolution was occurring largely outside Petrograd, often independently of the capital's political dramas, and that the government could neither stop it nor satisfy it. The delay of land reform did not make the peasant movement hostile to the revolution as such; on the contrary, it was part of the revolution. But it deepened the sense that the government was failing to deliver on expectations, and in doing so it weakened the legitimacy of a cabinet whose most important ministers belonged to parties that had promised land to the peasants.

Section 50.9

Workers, Factory Committees, and the Economic Crisis

Industrial workers were a smaller share of the population than peasants but concentrated in large factories in Petrograd, Moscow, the Urals, and the Donbas, and they exercised influence disproportionate to their numbers, particularly in the capital. Their demands in 1917 combined economic grievances with political aspirations. The eight-hour day, long a central demand of the labor movement, was introduced in many Petrograd factories in March through an agreement between the Soviet and the Association of Trade and Industry, and workers sought wage increases to offset inflation, better conditions, the dismissal of unpopular managers, and a voice in the running of enterprises. Women workers, who made up a large share of the workforce in textiles and other industries and in service sectors such as laundries, pressed their own demands, often in strikes.

New institutions of workers' representation appeared alongside the older ones. Trade unions, which had been legalized, grew rapidly, and were often led by Mensheviks, who favored organized bargaining. Soviets represented workers as part of the wider revolutionary democracy, and engaged in political and economic questions. Factory committees, elected by workers within individual enterprises, began as bodies to maintain order, supervise hiring and firing, and ensure fair treatment, and soon claimed a role in monitoring and sometimes controlling management. The first Petrograd conference of factory committees, held in late May and early June, signaled their growing organization, and many committees formed armed workers' militias, the beginning of the Red Guard.

Historical Vocabulary
Factory Committee and Workers' Control

Definition: A factory committee was a body elected by the workers of a single enterprise to represent their interests and supervise aspects of work, hiring, and management. "Workers' control" referred to the claim that workers should monitor or direct production, finances, and the dismissal of managers.

Historical context: Factory committees emerged in March 1917 and expanded through the year, often cooperating with, but distinct from, trade unions (which organized workers by occupation or industry) and soviets (which represented workers and soldiers politically across a locality).

Why it matters: The term shows that 1917 produced several overlapping institutions of popular power. Workers' control meant different things in different settings, from supervising managers to taking over abandoned enterprises, and should not be confused with later Soviet state-run planning.

The economy deteriorated steadily. Inflation outpaced wages, shortages of fuel, raw materials, and food reduced output, and the railway system, strained by military demands, delivered supplies unreliably. Some employers, citing shortages, losses, or hostility from workers, reduced production, laid off workers, or closed plants, and workers often suspected owners of deliberate sabotage; in some cases they were correct, though in many others closures reflected genuine material shortages. Managers complained of loss of authority, and committees sometimes took over administration of abandoned enterprises to keep them running. Government attempts at regulation, including a grain monopoly established in March and the creation of economic bodies to coordinate supplies, were hampered by weak administration and by the interests of producers, merchants, and peasants.

The workers' movement was not uniformly radical, nor was it monolithic. Many workers in the spring supported the Soviet leadership and the policy of cooperation, and Menshevik and Socialist Revolutionary influence in unions and factories remained substantial. Over the summer, however, disillusionment grew, particularly after the June Offensive and the July events, and the Bolsheviks, who rejected coalition and advocated workers' control, gained support in many factory committees and workers' meetings. Chapter 51 examines that process in detail. For present purposes, students should note that the economic crisis and the conflict over control of workplaces helped erode confidence in the government's ability to manage the country, and that workers acted through a variety of institutions rather than a single party.

Section 50.10

Nationality Movements and the Unraveling of Imperial Authority

The fall of the monarchy raised an immediate constitutional question: should the post-imperial state remain centralized, become a federation, or permit national self-determination, and who was entitled to decide? The imperial government had claimed sovereignty over a multinational territory; the Provisional Government, which lacked an electoral mandate, argued that fundamental questions of the state's structure should be decided by the Constituent Assembly. National movements, by contrast, wished to settle their regions' status without waiting. The tension between these positions, examined in more general terms in Chapter 45, became acute in 1917.

In Ukraine, activists formed the Central Rada in Kyiv on 4 March (17 March N.S.), with the historian Mykhailo Hrushevsky as chairman. It began as a coalition of Ukrainian parties, cultural societies, and, increasingly, soldiers' and peasants' organizations, and it gradually claimed to speak for Ukraine. On 10 June, the Rada's First Universal, a proclamation addressed to the Ukrainian people, declared Ukraine's autonomy within a future federal Russia, though it did not claim independence. A delegation of three ministers, Kerensky, Tereshchenko, and Tsereteli, traveled to Kyiv at the beginning of July and negotiated an arrangement; the Second Universal of 3 July accepted the Provisional Government's authority and acknowledged a role for the Constituent Assembly while recognizing the Rada's role in Ukrainian affairs. The agreement provoked the resignation of three Kadet ministers, who objected that the government had exceeded its powers, and thereby contributed to the cabinet crisis of early July. Ukraine's own politics were complex, involving Ukrainian national parties, Russian, Polish, and Jewish communities, and urban workers and peasants with differing interests. The Regional Context feature below returns to this subject.

In Finland, the Provisional Government restored the Grand Duchy's constitution, which had been curtailed under Nicholas II, but the Finnish Diet, dominated by Social Democrats, went further. In July it passed a law asserting Finnish authority over internal affairs, leaving foreign and military matters to Russia. The government rejected the law and, at the end of July, dissolved the Diet, an action carried out with the support of Russian troops stationed in Finland. In Poland, the government in March recognized the right of Poland to independence in ethnically Polish territory, subject to a military alliance with Russia, but Poland was under German and Austro-Hungarian occupation, so the declaration had limited practical effect. In the Baltic provinces, the government granted Estonia autonomy in the spring, unifying Estonian-inhabited areas of Estland and Livland; Latvian national organizations pressed similar claims, and the Latvian riflemen, a military force of Latvian soldiers, became politically important. German forces captured Riga on 21 August (3 September N.S.), changing the strategic situation in the region.

In the Caucasus, the government created a Special Transcaucasian Committee in March, but it soon faced competition from national councils and soviets among Georgians, Armenians, and Azerbaijanis. Muslim political organization developed across the empire; the First All-Russian Muslim Congress met in Moscow from 1 to 11 May (N.S.; 18 to 28 April O.S.), with delegates from the Volga, Crimea, the Caucasus, Central Asia, and Siberia, debating whether to seek a federal structure or territorial autonomy, and including a substantial number of women delegates. Kazakh, Bashkir, Crimean Tatar, and other leaders convened their own assemblies. Jewish communities benefited from the abolition of legal restrictions but also faced uncertainties and, in some regions, hostility. Cossack hosts convened assemblies to defend their traditional autonomy.

The government's difficulty was not simply that it lacked answers but that the questions pitted two principles against each other. To concede autonomy or independence quickly risked fragmenting the war effort and creating facts on the ground before the Assembly could act; to refuse risked alienating national movements and driving them toward more radical positions. Most national movements in 1917 sought autonomy within a reformed Russian state rather than outright independence, and several, including the Ukrainian leadership, initially hoped for a democratic federation. The central authorities' reluctance to move faster, combined with their weakness, contributed to a pattern in which regional institutions assumed functions of government in practice. This was another dimension of the broader loss of authority described in Section 50.14.

Section 50.11

The July Days

The July Days, 3 to 5 July (16 to 18 July N.S.), were a series of armed demonstrations in Petrograd that brought the question of power to a violent head. Several developments converged. Tensions had been rising since the June Offensive; the government had ordered units of the capital's garrison, including the First Machine Gun Regiment, to prepare for service at the front, which many soldiers regarded as a threat to the revolution's defenders and a breach of the March understanding that revolutionary units would remain in the capital. Workers at large factories such as the Putilov works were frustrated by prices and the economic situation. On 2 July, the three Kadet ministers resigned over the government's agreement with the Ukrainian Rada, leaving the cabinet weakened.

On 3 July, soldiers of the First Machine Gun Regiment, joined by other units and workers, took to the streets and sent delegations to the Tauride Palace demanding that the Soviet take power. Sailors from Kronstadt arrived in force on 4 July, and a large and heavily armed crowd moved through the city, with shooting in the streets and scores of deaths. Tsereteli and other Soviet leaders confronted the marchers, while a crowd seized the Socialist Revolutionary minister Chernov outside the Tauride Palace and was prevented from harming him only through the intervention of the Soviet leaders, including Trotsky, then associated with the Inter-District organization that soon merged with the Bolsheviks. The Soviet's Executive Committee refused to take power, regarding the crowd's demands as a threat.

The role of the Bolsheviks is the heart of the controversy. The party's Military Organization had agitated among soldiers and was involved in the mobilization, and Bolshevik slogans, especially "all power to the soviets," were prominent. But the party's Central Committee had decided against an armed demonstration, and Lenin, who was ill and resting outside the capital, returned on 4 July. Party leaders were unsure how to respond to a movement that appeared likely to proceed with or without them; on the evening of 4 July, they decided to try to give it a peaceful and organized character and, on 5 July, to end it. This hesitation, followed by an attempt to guide a movement they had helped to encourage, makes the party's role difficult to characterize as simple instigation or simple spontaneity.

The government and Soviet leadership called loyal troops to Petrograd, and by 5 July the demonstrations had dissipated. On the evening of 4 and 5 July, the government and military counterintelligence released material alleging that Lenin and other Bolsheviks had taken German money and acted as German agents. The claim, which was publicized in the press, was based largely on circumstantial and disputed evidence, and later scholarship has found no proof that Lenin acted as a German agent, though Germany did, in various ways, seek to exploit Russia's revolutionary disorder and some Bolshevik associates had financial ties that remain disputed. The accusations nonetheless turned many soldiers and others against the Bolsheviks. The party's newspaper offices were wrecked, Bolshevik leaders including Trotsky and Lev Kamenev were arrested, Lenin went into hiding and later to Finland, and units that had taken part were disarmed or sent from the capital.

Politically, the July Days had immediate consequences. Prince Lvov resigned on 7 July, and Kerensky became minister-president on 8 July. The moderate socialists in the Soviet, who had sided with the government in suppressing the demonstrations, were strengthened in the short term, but at the cost of identifying themselves more closely with a government whose policies were failing. The Bolsheviks were damaged, though not destroyed; their position recovered quickly, as Chapter 51 explains. Students should understand the July Days as neither a Bolshevik coup attempt in any simple sense nor simply a spontaneous riot: they were a confrontation involving rank-and-file militancy, party agitation, uncertain leadership, and a government and Soviet unwilling to share or transfer power.

Section 50.12

Kerensky, the Second Coalition, and the Search for Authority

Kerensky's emergence as the central figure of the government in July reflected both his talents and the lack of alternatives. He was a gifted orator who had been prominent in March as a bridge between the government and the Soviet, and he enjoyed support from moderates in both camps. As minister-president, he also retained the portfolios of war and the navy. The second coalition, announced on 24 July after extended negotiations, included Kadets, Mensheviks, Socialist Revolutionaries, and others. It sought to combine the restoration of discipline with continued socialist participation, but its social base was narrower than that of the first: the Kadets and the propertied circles that supported them wanted order and firmer action, while the socialists in the cabinet were increasingly criticized by their own constituents.

Kerensky's government took measures to restore authority. The death penalty had been reintroduced in the front zone for specified grave military offenses; newspapers associated with the Bolsheviks were closed or restricted; arrests of Bolsheviks continued; and Lavr Kornilov, who had made his reputation through the offensive and his firmness in the army, became supreme commander on 19 July. Kornilov, in turn, submitted proposals to the government for restoring military discipline, extending the death penalty to the rear, and placing certain industries and railways under military regulation. The government's discussions of these proposals revealed disagreements: the prime minister and his socialist colleagues were reluctant to adopt measures that would appear to eliminate revolutionary gains, while generals and the propertied circles regarded such measures as necessary to save the army and the state.

The Moscow State Conference, held at the Bolshoi Theatre from 12 to 15 August (25 to 28 August N.S.), was intended to display national unity. It brought together delegates from the Duma, the soviets, trade unions, cooperatives, business and professional groups, the army, and others. Instead, it displayed polarization. The Bolsheviks organized a strike in Moscow on the opening day to protest the conference, and the seating, speeches, and applause revealed deep divisions between delegates of "revolutionary democracy" and those of propertied society. Kornilov's arrival was greeted with ovations from the right, while the left regarded him with suspicion. Speeches by Kerensky, Kornilov, Milyukov, Tsereteli, and others showed that no shared view existed on discipline, war, land, or the Assembly.

The weeks that followed intensified anxieties on all sides. The military situation worsened with the German capture of Riga on 21 August, an event that conservatives blamed on indiscipline and that fed rumors of treachery. Many officers, businessmen, and conservative politicians began to see a military dictatorship as a possible solution, while many socialists feared that propertied elements and generals aimed to destroy the revolution's gains. Kerensky, positioned between these groups, sought a way to strengthen his government without becoming dependent on either. This precarious balance, in which each side believed the other aimed to overthrow the government, set the stage for the Kornilov Affair.

Section 50.13

The Kornilov Affair

The Kornilov Affair, from 25 to 31 August (7 to 13 September N.S.), was a confrontation between the supreme commander and the head of government that ended in the collapse of Kornilov's movement and a decisive weakening of the political center. Its outlines are clear, though its interpretation remains contested. In mid-August, Kornilov, with encouragement from the deputy war minister Boris Savinkov and others, was developing plans to move troops to the capital region, ostensibly to enforce order and to protect the government from a possible Bolshevik uprising. Kerensky had discussed with Kornilov and Savinkov the idea of bringing reliable forces to Petrograd and placing the capital under martial law. The two men appear to have differed, however, on what would follow and on who would control the force.

An intermediary complicated matters. Vladimir Lvov, a former procurator of the Holy Synod (and not to be confused with Prince Georgy Lvov), traveled between Kerensky and Kornilov on 22 to 26 August carrying messages whose content each man later interpreted differently. Lvov told Kornilov that Kerensky was prepared to accept a reconstruction of the government under Kornilov's influence, and he told Kerensky that Kornilov demanded that Kerensky come to army headquarters or hand over power. Kornilov, meanwhile, ordered the Third Cavalry Corps under General Aleksandr Krymov to move toward Petrograd. On 27 August, Kerensky, convinced that Kornilov was attempting a coup, dismissed him by telegram and announced that he had committed treason; Kornilov refused to submit and issued his own appeal accusing the government of acting under the influence of the Bolsheviks and German interests. The two communicated by telegraph in the course of these events, but misunderstandings were not resolved.

The response to Kornilov's movement came largely from below. Soviet leaders, the moderate and radical socialists alike, formed a Committee for Struggle against Counterrevolution; railway workers and their union diverted or blocked trains carrying Krymov's troops; telegraph and postal workers delayed or intercepted messages; soldiers' committees and agitators, including representatives of Muslim organizations who talked to men of the Caucasian Native Division, persuaded many units to halt. Workers in Petrograd formed Red Guard units, and the government, for the first time, distributed weapons to them. Krymov's advance disintegrated without a battle. Krymov himself went to Petrograd, met Kerensky, and shot himself on 31 August. Kornilov was arrested on 1 September and held at Bykhov in Belorussia, along with several other generals. General Aleksei Alekseev briefly became Kerensky's chief of staff.

The consequences were substantial. The right was discredited: the officer corps appeared tainted by association with an attempted coup, and the propertied circles that had flirted with the idea of a general's dictatorship lost credibility with the revolutionary left. Kerensky's standing with conservatives and many officers collapsed, since he had first appeared to endorse and then denounced the supreme commander, while his dependence on the soviets grew. The Kadet ministers had resigned during the crisis, and the cabinet was replaced on 1 September by a five-member Directory, which proclaimed Russia a republic, and later by a third coalition on 25 September, which included few Kadets and little standing with any constituency. Armed workers, with weapons from the government's own distributions, and revolutionary organizations that had led the resistance gained influence and credibility. The Bolsheviks, whose leaders had been arrested or in hiding after July, were released or returned to political activity and benefited from the sense that they had been among the staunchest defenders against counterrevolution. Chapter 51 follows that process.

Historians disagree about the nature of the affair. Some argue that Kornilov planned a military coup to establish a dictatorship, and that he moved troops toward Petrograd with that aim. Others contend that the affair was largely the product of misunderstanding and mutual suspicion, in which Kerensky, Kornilov, Savinkov, and Lvov each acted on assumptions about the others' intentions. Still others see an effort by a government under pressure to enlist military support for authoritarian measures that went wrong. The Historical Debate feature below examines the evidence, including the testimony and memoirs of participants, which were produced afterward and in self-justifying circumstances.

Section 50.14

Why the Revolutionary Government Lost Authority

This section synthesizes the chapter's evidence. It asks why the political settlement created in February was so fragile by September and October, and it deliberately does not ask why the Bolsheviks won, which is the question of Chapter 51. The explanation must be assembled from several interacting causes, some structural and others contingent.

First, the government's legitimacy was inherently limited. Unelected and provisional by its own declaration, it governed in the name of a Constituent Assembly that had not yet met, and it asked a population with raised expectations to wait for decisions on land, the empire's structure, and the war. Each delay of the Assembly, although defensible on technical and political grounds, magnified the gap between expectation and delivery. Second, the war was unresolved. The government's commitment to its allies, the soviet leadership's revolutionary defensism, and the failure of efforts to secure a negotiated peace tied the revolution's fate to a military effort that soldiers, workers, and many peasants increasingly resented. The June Offensive and its collapse made the contradiction visible.

Third, the army was transformed and, in important respects, disintegrating. Order No. 1 and the committee system were part of this change, but so were war weariness, casualties, the soldiers' ties to a village revolution, and the failure to articulate a persuasive purpose for continued fighting. Fourth, land reform was delayed while peasants took action themselves. Fifth, economic breakdown, including inflation, shortages, and conflict between workers and management, undermined confidence in the government's ability to manage the economy, and factory committees and soviets increasingly assumed functions the state could not perform. Sixth, nationality demands showed that central authority could not command obedience in the borderlands, and reluctance to concede autonomy deepened the problem.

Seventh, repeated cabinet crises reduced the capacity of the political center to act: the April crisis, the early July crisis, the formation of coalitions in May, July, and September, and the resignations during the Kornilov Affair made it hard to sustain any policy for long. Eighth, soviets, committees, and other bodies became increasingly autonomous. They were not simply instruments of party leaders, and as their memberships responded to local grievances, they shifted away from the moderates who had led them in the spring. Ninth, the July Days polarized politics, as the left feared repression and the right demanded firmness. Tenth, the Kornilov Affair destroyed mutual trust: the government's conservative supporters felt betrayed, its socialist supporters felt that it had flirted with counterrevolution, and the political center lost its most important constituencies.

None of these causes alone is sufficient. The government faced extraordinary constraints, but its choices also mattered: the decisions to continue the war, to launch an offensive, to postpone the Assembly and land reform, and to rely on a fragile coalition were not predetermined. A government that had obtained a negotiated peace, or an earlier land settlement, or a more rapid Assembly election might have faced a different course, though it is difficult to know whether any such alternatives were realistically available given Allied pressure, German military power, and the divisions within Russian society. What can be said with confidence is that by early autumn the political center had little remaining authority.

The chapter's central conclusion is a distinction that students should keep firmly in mind. The failure of the Provisional Government made another transfer of power increasingly possible; it did not make Bolshevik victory inevitable. Alternatives, including a more broadly based socialist government, a military regime, a negotiated settlement among parties, and the continued fragmentation of authority, remained conceivable in September and October. How and why one particular outcome occurred is the subject of the next chapter.

Primary Source Window

Order No. 1 of the Petrograd Soviet (1 March 1917, O.S.)

Creator: Soldiers' section and Executive Committee of the Petrograd Soviet; drafted with the participation of Nikolai Sokolov · Date: 1 March 1917 (14 March N.S.) · Type: Political directive to a military garrison

Context: Issued the day after the garrison's mutiny, when soldiers feared reprisals and the Soviet sought to secure the troops' loyalty to the revolution. It was distributed by hand and telegraph and soon read as relevant to units far beyond Petrograd.

Source type: An order addressed to the lower ranks of the capital's garrison, published and widely reproduced. Students should consult a reliable edition in translation, such as those in standard documentary collections of the Russian Revolution, for exact wording.

Brief paraphrase (not a verbatim translation):

The order instructs units of the Petrograd garrison to elect committees of lower-rank representatives and to send delegates to the Soviet. It directs that, in political matters, units obey the Soviet and their own committees, and that orders of the Duma's military commission be followed only when they do not contradict Soviet orders. Weapons are to be kept under the committees' control rather than officers'. Soldiers are to enjoy ordinary civil and political rights outside duty, honorific forms of address are abolished, and rudeness toward soldiers is forbidden, while strict military discipline during duty is affirmed.

Analysis: The order is evidence of soldiers' demands for dignity, protection, and a voice, and of the Soviet's attempt to establish its authority over the garrison. It also shows the leadership's assumption that the army would remain a disciplined fighting force under new rules. Its limitations are significant: it was written in haste for a particular local situation, its intended scope was Petrograd, and how units across the front interpreted it varied widely. It cannot by itself show how soldiers actually behaved or why discipline eroded. Officers' perspectives are absent, as are the views of soldiers who were not represented in Petrograd's committees.

Questions for students:

  1. What does the order say, and what did it ask soldiers and officers to do?
  2. Who created it, and for what possible purposes in the circumstances of 1 March?
  3. What can historians learn from it about the Soviet's claim to authority and about soldiers' grievances?
  4. What additional evidence, such as soldiers' letters, officers' reports, or committee minutes, would help evaluate its effects?
Historical Debate

The Kornilov Affair: Coup, Misunderstanding, or Something Else?

The question: Did General Kornilov plan to overthrow the Provisional Government and install a military dictatorship, or was the affair the result of mutual misunderstanding between Kornilov and Kerensky?

Interpretation A, a planned military intervention: Some historians argue that Kornilov, backed by conservative and business circles, intended to use military force to suppress the soviets and impose a strong government, and that his order to move the Third Cavalry Corps toward Petrograd reflected that aim. Soviet historiography and many contemporary socialists adopted this view, and it is supported by Kornilov's own political statements, his proposals for restoring discipline, and the support he received from some officers and politicians.
Interpretation B, a tragedy of miscommunication and mutual suspicion: Others stress the ambiguous communications through Savinkov and Vladimir Lvov, the fact that Kerensky had discussed bringing troops to Petrograd and may have expected to control them, and the speed with which each man concluded the other was betraying him. This reading, associated with several Western and émigré accounts, holds that Kornilov may have believed he had Kerensky's approval and that Kerensky may have misread Kornilov's aims.
Interpretation C, a government seeking authoritarian support: A further view emphasizes that Kerensky himself explored measures to strengthen executive power and was prepared to use military force against radical left threats, and that the affair arose when the aims of the military and the head of government diverged.

Evidence: Telegraph records, orders, testimony before the later Extraordinary Investigation Commission, the memoirs of Kerensky, Kornilov's associates, and others, press accounts, and the movements of troops. Most participant testimony was produced to defend reputations.

Points of agreement: Historians agree on the basic sequence of events, that Kornilov did order troops toward the capital, that Kerensky dismissed him, that resistance came from below, and that the political consequences were severe for the center and the right.

Points of disagreement: They disagree on the intentions of Kornilov and Kerensky, the extent of conspiracy among officers and civilians, and how far the affair was avoidable.

Why it matters: The interpretation affects how one judges the government's decisions in the summer and the origins of the polarization that followed.

Student task: Select two items of evidence and explain how each supports or complicates one of the interpretations, noting the author and date of each source.

Multiple Perspectives

The Land Question in 1917

Landowners and estate managers: Many saw rent strikes, timber cutting, and seizures as theft and a threat to their livelihood and, in wartime, to grain supply. Some appealed to the government for protection; others sold or abandoned estates. Moderate landowners could support land reform in principle if conducted through the Constituent Assembly and with compensation.
Peasant communities: Village assemblies regarded the land as rightfully theirs and saw the revolution as the moment to act. Resolutions and petitions describe demands for the abolition of private property in land and for local control over redistribution. Poorer and richer peasants, and different regions, held differing interests.
Soldiers and deserters: Peasant soldiers feared that waiting at the front would cost them a share when land was divided, and returning soldiers carried weapons and authority into village disputes, linking the army's crisis to the countryside.
The Provisional Government and its land committees: Ministers sought lawful procedure and continuity of food supply. Socialist Revolutionary ministers, such as Chernov, were caught between party commitments and coalition constraints.

These perspectives were not equally powerful: landowners had legal and institutional advantages that eroded quickly, while peasant communities could increasingly act without the state's permission.

Cause and Effect

The Loss of Authority of the February Order

Long-Term Conditions

Wartime strain on the state, economy, and army; unresolved land and labor grievances; a restricted parliamentary tradition; weak local administration; a multinational empire with active national movements.

Intermediate Developments

Collapse of the monarchy and police; dual power; coalition governments; spread of soviets and committees; delay of land reform and the Assembly; the April crisis and the June Offensive.

Short-Term Triggers

The July Days, the cabinet crisis over Ukraine, the fall of Riga, the Moscow State Conference, and the Kornilov Affair.

Immediate and Long-Term Consequences

A weakened center, armed workers' units, discredited conservatives, and a polarized politics. Longer-term consequences are examined in Chapters 51 and 52.

Historical complexity: No single cause explains the loss of authority. The war, the economy, the army, land, nationalities, and political choices reinforced one another, and some depended on contingent decisions.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Institutions and Experience, February to October

What Changed?

  • Civil liberties, legal equality, and women's suffrage replaced imperial restrictions.
  • Soldiers' committees, factory committees, soviets, and land committees became sources of local authority.
  • The balance within the soviets shifted from moderate socialists toward more radical forces over the summer.

What Remained?

  • The war, the Allied alliance, food scarcity, and the transport system continued.
  • Many officials, ministries, and judges remained in office, and key questions of land and the empire awaited the Constituent Assembly.
  • Villages continued to organize through communal assemblies, even as their goals changed.

Why the pattern matters: Change was fastest in institutions of local popular power and slowest in the state's ability to deliver land, peace, or economic stability. Experiences differed: soldiers, women workers, peasants, officials, and national activists encountered different rhythms of change.

Everyday Life and Social History

Meetings, Queues, and Committees

For many ordinary people, the revolutionary year was marked by an extraordinary expansion of public speech and organization. Meetings in barracks, factory yards, and village squares debated resolutions, and newspapers and pamphlets proliferated. Elections to committees and soviets taught millions to vote, often for the first time. At the same time, daily life grew harder: urban residents queued for bread and other goods, prices rose, fuel was scarce, and crime increased as policing weakened. Soldiers at the front debated orders, fraternized with enemy soldiers in some sectors, and worried about families and land at home.

Women's experiences were varied. Women workers struck over wages and conditions, women demonstrated in favor of suffrage in the spring, and the government's electoral law enfranchised women in July. In villages, women managed households while men served and sometimes participated in land disputes. Regional and class differences were large, and surviving evidence, drawn heavily from urban, literate, and politically active people, should not be taken as representative of all experiences.

Regional Context

Ukraine and the Central Rada

From Kyiv in 1917, the revolution looked less like a Petrograd drama and more like a negotiation over authority in a diverse region with large Ukrainian, Russian, Polish, Jewish, and other populations. The Central Rada emerged from Ukrainian cultural and political organizations; soldiers' and peasants' congresses gave it a mass base, and its socialist leadership combined national demands with social programs, including support for land socialization. It claimed autonomy in June and negotiated with the Provisional Government in July, while rival institutions, including Russian-dominated soviets and city councils, claimed authority in cities such as Kyiv, Kharkiv, and Odesa.

Students should treat Ukrainian politics in 1917 on its own terms rather than as a footnote to Petrograd. Its aims, in 1917, were primarily autonomy within a democratic Russian federation. What later happened, including declarations of independence and military conflict, belongs to later chapters, and the 1917 record should not be read in the light of subsequent national narratives, whether Russian or Ukrainian.

Russia and the World

Allies, Enemies, and the Socialist International

Russia's revolutionary politics were entangled with the wider war. Britain and France, dependent on the Eastern Front, pressed the Provisional Government to continue fighting and welcomed the entry of the United States in April, while their own war aims, partly secret, complicated Soviet calls for a democratic peace. Germany and Austria-Hungary sought to exploit Russian disarray, through military pressure, propaganda, and in some cases support for antiwar movements, though the extent and effect of German financial support for Russian revolutionaries remains disputed. European socialists were divided over whether to support national war efforts; plans for an international socialist conference at Stockholm foundered amid Allied opposition and disagreement. Russia's revolution thus both influenced and was shaped by wider developments, including growing strikes and mutinies in other belligerent countries during 1917.

Chapter Summary

This chapter examined why the political order created by the February Revolution failed to produce stable authority between March and October 1917. The monarchy's fall left a vacuum of legitimacy: the State Duma, the Petrograd Soviet, the army command, and the promised Constituent Assembly each offered a different source of authority, and none could command general obedience.

The Provisional Government, led first by Prince Lvov and later by Kerensky, enacted far-reaching civil liberties and legal reforms, but it was unelected, provisional by its own declaration, and committed to postponing major social decisions. The Petrograd Soviet, led by Mensheviks and Socialist Revolutionaries, supported the government conditionally and declined to take power, producing the informal and unstable pattern called dual power.

Order No. 1 and the growth of soldiers' committees changed relations between officers and soldiers, though historians treat it as one factor among several in an army already strained by war. The April crisis showed that the war-aims question could bring crowds into the streets, and the first coalition tied socialist leaders to government responsibility.

The June Offensive tested the revolutionary army and failed, damaging the credibility of the government and intensifying disputes over discipline. Meanwhile, peasants began seizing land and regulating rents through village institutions, workers formed factory committees and pressed for control over production amid economic decline, and national movements in Ukraine, Finland, the Baltic, the Caucasus, and among Muslim communities pressed demands the center could neither satisfy nor ignore.

The July Days brought armed soldiers, sailors, and workers into the streets demanding Soviet power. The episode was neither a simple Bolshevik coup nor merely spontaneous disorder; it was suppressed, and its aftermath brought Kerensky to the head of government. The Moscow State Conference displayed the polarization of society.

The Kornilov Affair, whose interpretation remains contested, ended in the collapse of the supreme commander's movement through resistance from below. It discredited the military right, armed workers' units, destroyed trust between the government and its conservative supporters, and left the political center badly weakened.

The explanation for this outcome combined structural pressures, including war, economic breakdown, and the multinational empire, with contingent decisions about the war, the Assembly, and land. The chapter's central distinction is that the government's failure made another transfer of power more possible but did not make Bolshevik victory inevitable.

These conclusions lead directly to Chapter 51, which examines the Bolsheviks' rise and the October seizure of power, and to Chapter 52, which examines the Constituent Assembly, the Civil War, and the violence that followed.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows that revolutions do not end when a regime falls. The months between February and October 1917 were a period of improvisation in which institutions, expectations, and wartime constraints interacted unpredictably. Reading them only as a prelude to October obscures the real options, choices, and constraints faced by people at the time.

The period left lasting legacies: soviets and committees as models of popular authority, a peasant revolution that reshaped rural society, the question of how to manage multinational diversity, and a political culture shaped by war and polarization. These themes recur in Chapters 51 and 52 and in the early Soviet state, where they were reinterpreted and often suppressed.

Questions remain open, including how far alternatives to coalition government were available, how to interpret the Kornilov Affair, and how much weight to give structural conditions versus decisions. Students should carry forward the habit of explaining outcomes without treating them as inevitable.
